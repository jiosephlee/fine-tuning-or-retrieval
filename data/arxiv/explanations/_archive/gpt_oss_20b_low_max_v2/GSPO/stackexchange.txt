Answer:
**Why the token‑level importance ratio in GRPO is a misuse of importance sampling**

The core idea of importance sampling is that if we want to estimate an expectation under a *target* distribution $\pi_{\rm tar}$ but we only have samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution $\pi_{\rm beh}$, we re‑weight each sample with the likelihood‑ratio  

\[
\frac{\pi_{\rm tar}(z)}{\pi_{\rm beh}(z)} ,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\rm tar}}[f(z)]
   =\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\rm beh}}\!\left[
       \frac{\pi_{\rm tar}(z)}{\pi_{\rm beh}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

This weighting works because, for every *sample* \(z\), the factor \(\frac{\pi_{\rm tar}}{\pi_{\rm beh}}\) corrects the *distributional mismatch*. In practice we only use this idea when we have **many** samples from the behavior policy so that the weighted sum of the $f(z)$’s can be estimated reliably. Crucially, each data point is weighted by a **single** factor that accounts for how far its entire probability mass under the target policy is from its mass under the behavior policy.

---

### What GRPO does

GRPO samples a batch of responses \(\{y_i\}\) from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). For every token \(y_{i,t}\) it then uses a **token‑level** ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

This ratio is multiplied by an *advantage* that is identical for all tokens in \(y_i\):
\[
\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_{i}.
\]

Consequently, each token’s contribution to the gradient becomes  
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\).

---

### Why this is a mis‑application

1. **The ratio estimates the change of a single token’s probability, not that of an entire response.**  
   The reward \(r(x,y_i)\) is given to the whole sequence \(y_i\). Thus, from an importance‑sampling standpoint, we would need a weight that reflects how likely the whole sequence would be under the new policy relative to the old one:

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}.
   \]

   Token‑level ratios only look at one step in the generative process; they ignore how earlier tokens affect later ones and therefore do not capture the joint likelihood that matters for the reward.

2. **Single‑token weights are highly variable and uncorrelated with rewards.**  
   Each token \(y_{i,t}\) is drawn from its own conditional distribution; there is no guarantee that its individual likelihood ratio reflects how unlikely or likely the entire sequence will be under the new policy. Some tokens will receive very large ratios because their probability under $\pi_\theta$ happened to increase greatly, even though the overall sequence may be far less likely overall. Others may get tiny ratios simply because a single rare token was emitted. This mismatch injects large variance into the gradient estimate.

3. **The weighting scheme breaks importance sampling’s guarantees.**  
   Importance sampling requires that for every sample we use *the same* weight as would be applied if we were estimating expectations over that complete sample. Token‑level weighting is effectively performing *biased* re‑weighting: it multiplies each log‑likelihood term by a factor derived from *that very same term*, so the expectation no longer equals the target expectation. The estimator becomes inconsistent; its variance blows up as sequence length grows (each token contributes another independent noisy weight), which precisely explains why longer responses in GRPO lead to catastrophic collapse.

4. **Clipping aggravates the problem instead of alleviating it.**  
   In PPO/GRPO clipping merely limits how much any single ratio can deviate from 1 for stability; but if most ratios are already ill‑defined (because they come from different tokens), clipping cannot recover correctness – it simply discards some gradient contributions and leaves highly noisy ones untouched.

---

### Contrast with GSPO

GSPO replaces these token‑wise ratios with a **sequence‑wise ratio**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(
  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
  \right)^{1/|y_i|}
  = 
  \exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}
           \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
           \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
         \Bigr].
\]

Because this ratio averages over all tokens in one exponent, it faithfully represents how much more (or less) probable an entire response becomes under $\pi_\theta$. Every token in \(y_i\) receives an identical scaling factor \(s_i(\theta)\), so variance does not accumulate across tokens and clipping operates on a coherent signal that matches the unit of reward.

---

### Bottom line

GRPO’s token‑level importance ratio misuses importance sampling because it attempts to correct for off‑policy sampling at an atomic level (a single token) while the objective—and thus the reward—belongs to whole sequences. This inconsistency produces high‑variance, biased gradients that grow with sequence length and ultimately cause training instability and model collapse. Using sequence‑level ratios—as GSPO does—restores validity to importance sampling, yields lower variance estimates, and aligns optimization with rewards, thereby explaining GSPO’s superior stability and performance.
