\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Model Finetuning

Large foundation models—such as GPT‑3, BERT, Vision Transformers, and Stable Diffusion—have become the backbone of modern artificial‑intelligence applications. Their sheer size and breadth of knowledge make them invaluable. Nevertheless, training these models from scratch demands gigantic amounts of compute, energy, and data: training GPT‑3 required hundreds of petaflop‑seconds on high‑end GPUs and costs estimated in the tens of millions of dollars. Because most researchers and practitioners do not have access to such resources, the focus in the field has shifted to **adapting** already pre‑trained models to new downstream tasks.  

Model finetuning is a lightweight way to preserve the knowledge encoded in a foundation model while steering its behavior toward a particular objective. Instead of re-training millions or billions of parameters from scratch, we keep the vast majority frozen and only modify a small set of trainable weights. This approach is attractive because it dramatically reduces both memory consumption and training time, while still enabling state‑of‑the‑art performance on a wide spectrum of tasks.

In this chapter we set the stage for understanding how orthogonal finetuning (OFT) fits into the larger ecosystem of parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT). We will first describe why large foundation models are expensive to train, then outline three principal categories of PEFT (model finetuning, adapter tuning, prompt tuning), explain the guiding principle that all methods share—preserving pretrained knowledge—and finally delve into OFT’s core idea: transforming neurons with an orthogonal matrix to maintain pairwise angles.

---

## Large Foundation Models and Training Cost

A *foundation model* is a neural network trained on a huge corpus of data that can be subsequently leveraged—sometimes with minimal effort—to address many downstream tasks. The recent surge in model scale has been largely driven by two powerhouses: deep language models (e.g., GPT families, BERT flow) and large vision transformers (e.g., CLIP, ViT). Their success stems from learning complex statistical regularities across modalities.

However, this success comes at an enormous computational cost:

1. **Parameter count** – GPT‑3 contains 175 billion parameters; Stable Diffusion encodes several hundred million parameters across multiple submodules.
2. **Training data** – Models ingest terabytes or petabytes of text or images; this volume divides by GPU hours in thousands.
3. **Hardware** – Training requires clusters of state‑of‑the‑art GPUs or TPUs interconnected with high‑bandwidth NVLink or GDDR6i memory buses.
4. **Time & energy** – Scaling laws predict that loss on downstream benchmarks drops logarithmically with model size; thus, going from 1 billion to 10 billion parameters might improve performance substantially but also increases training cost by an order of magnitude.

Given such figures, it is simply infeasible for most labs—or even many industry teams—to redeploy full training pipelines for every new task. The alternative is *fine‐tuning*: starting from a pre‑trained checkpoint and tweaking only a handful commands or new modules while keeping the bulk frozen.

---

## Categories of Parameter–Efficient Finetuning

Researchers have converged on three broad strategies for PEFT, each with distinct properties concerning computational overhead at inference time, flexibility across architectures, and how they modify the pre‑trained model.

| Category | Core Idea | What is learned | Inference overhead | Typical use cases |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|--------------------|------------------|
| **Model finetuning** | Directly optimize a subset (or all) weights subject to constraints like small learning rate or structural regularization | Some (or all) parameters near original values | None (the updated weights are directly used) | Vision & language transfer learning |
| **Adapter tuning** | Insert lightweight modules (e.g., small residual bottlenecks) between existing layers | Parameters inside adapters only; base weights remain frozen | Small layer-wise extra computation | Multi‐task learning across modalities |
| **Prompt tuning** | Append learnable tokens or prefixes to inputs without touching weights | Only prompts; core weights fixed | None; only input manipulation required | Few‐shot language generation, cross-domain prompting |

These categories differ mainly in *where* we inject learnability:
- In model finetuning we alter existing connections.
- In adapter tuning we augment architecture lightly.
- In prompt tuning we leave the network structure intact and only tweak inputs.

All three approaches share a common philosophical goal: **preserve as much as possible the semantic knowledge already encoded in the pre-trained weights** while allowing the network to adapt its decision surface to new data.

---

## Principle of Preserving Pretrained Knowledge

A fundamental challenge in fine-tuning lies in *balance*. At one extreme we leave everything frozen; at the other we replace all weights. Classic tricks such as lowering learning rates or reducing batch sizes aim indirectly at maintaining proximity between pre-trained and fine-tuned parameters.

A more principled perspective looks not simply at magnitudes but at *relationships* between neurons:

- The *pairwise angles* between weight vectors determine how activations spread through layers.
- Preserving these angles means keeping relative geometry intact so that learned feature spaces remain stable.

If during adaptation every weight vector is rotated by the same orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), then all pairwise dot products \( \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j\) stay unchanged because
\[
(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i)^\top (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)= \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{w}_j .
\]
Hence an *orthogonal transformation* provides **exact preservation** of neuronal geometry: distances and angles among embeddings are invariant.

This observation leads naturally to **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**: instead of changing each weight vector individually—an expensive operation—we apply one global orthogonal matrix per linear transformation layer (or per part thereof). By learning \(\mathbf{R}\), OFT adjusts directions while leaving magnitudes untouched, thereby retaining much of what made the foundation model powerful.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Concept and Advantages

OFT was initially proposed for controllable text-to-image diffusion models but bears universal appeal for any weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\). The idea proceeds as follows:

1. **Reparameterization**  
   Replace \( \mathbf{W}^0\) with \( \tilde{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\), where \( \mathbf{R}\) is an orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons within that layer. The transform is multiplicative rather than additive (as in LoRA).

2. **Zero initialization**  
   Set \( \mathbf{R} = \mathbbm{I}\); thus early during training \( \tilde{\mathbf{W}}\approx\mathbf{W}^0\). This guarantees stable gradients and prevents catastrophic forgetting.

3. **Off-the-shelf orthogonality preservation**  
   Employing Cayley parameterization (\(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbbm I+\mathbbm Q)(\mathbbm I-\mathbbm Q)^{-1}\)) where \( \mathbbm Q = -\mathbbm Q^\top\) ensures that any gradient step remains in the orthogonal group without expensive projection.

4. **Parameter efficiency via block-diagonal restriction**  
   Since full orthogonal matrices scale quadratically (\(O(d^2)\)), OFT typically uses block-diagonal structures (\(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_1,\dots,\mathbf R_r)\)). Each small block feels like an independent rotation; this reduces parameters to \(O(bd)\), where \(b=d/r\) is block size.

### Advantages
- **Stability**: Orthogonality preserves spectral norms; gradients propagate without exploding/vanishing.
- **Generalizability**: Empirical evaluations show OFT often outperforms additive counterparts like LoRA on unseen data.
- **Zero latency**: After training one simply plugs back the learned orthogonal matrices into existing weights; inference remains unchanged.

### Limitations
- The primary bottleneck lies in scalability: for extremely large models even block-diagonal OFT demands too many trainable parameters compared with LoRA or adapters.
- Moreover, rigid block grouping imposes an inductive bias that may hinder representation flexibility.

The landscape set by these observations motivates devising an orthogonal finetuning scheme that retains expressive transformations yet drastically reduces trainable parameters—a problem addressed head-on by **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** – the core contribution of our reference paper.

--- 

In summary, this chapter laid out why adapting foundation models via fine-tuning is essential, mapped out major PEFT strategies—including their structural inherent trade-offs—and positioned Orthogonal Finetuning as a principled approach rooted in preserving inter-neuron geometry via global orthogonal transformations. Understanding this background equips readers for deeper exploration into parameter-efficient innovations such as BOFT that aim to reconcile expressivity and scalability in modern deep-learning pipelines.
