Chapter 2: # 2. Orthogonal Finetuning: Mechanics and Limitations  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a parameter‑efficient paradigm that updates a frozen pre‑trained weight matrix by sandwiching it with a learnable orthogonal transform. The central idea is to preserve the geometric relations among neurons—specifically, the pairwise angles—while steering the model toward a downstream objective. This chapter unpacks the mechanics of OFT, its re‑parameterization strategy, the use of Cayley parameterization to enforce orthogonality, and how block‑diagonal sparsity reduces parameter count at the possible cost of expressive power. We also discuss why this sparsity pattern may not align with natural inductive biases.

---

## OFT Re‑parameterization

Let us consider a single linear layer in a deep model, represented by the frozen weight matrix  
\[
\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
where \(d\) is the input dimensionality and \(n\) is the output dimensionality. In conventional fine‑tuning, we would adapt \(\bm{W}^0\) directly by adding small perturbations or modifying its entries. Orthogonal Finetuning instead introduces a multiplicative transform that acts on the neuron space of the layer:
\[
\bm{W}^{\text{new}} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 ,\qquad
\bm{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad 
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}_d .
\tag{2.1}
\]
The transformed weight matrix \(\bm{W}^{\text{new}}\) inherits all forward‑pass behavior from \(\bm{W}^0\) but now operates on rotated neuron embeddings. Consequently, given an input vector \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\), the layer’s output becomes
\[
\bm{z}
= (\bm{W}^{\text{new}})^\top\,\bm{x}
= (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top\,\bm{x}
= (\bm{W}^0)^\top\,(\underbrace{\bm{R}^\top}_{=\! \! \!\! \! \!\!\!\! \!\! {\color{purple}\text{\small R transpose}}}\,\!\!\!\!\! \bm{x}) .
\tag{2.2}
\]
Thus, OFT can be viewed as rotating the input space before applying the frozen projector \((\bm{W}^0)^\top.\) Because an orthogonal transformation preserves Euclidean norm and pairwise angles,
the intrinsic geometry of neurons—captured in \(\operatorname{span}\) of columns of \(\bm{W}^0\)—remains unchanged after adaptation. This geometric preservation is believed to maintain the knowledge distilled during pre‑training while still allowing task‑specific modulation.

## Cayley Parameterization for Orthogonality

Enforcing exact orthogonality during stochastic optimization is nontrivial: naive gradient updates break the constraint immediately. The Cayley transform offers an elegant solution by mapping a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \bm{Q}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\),
\( \bm{Q} = -\,\bm{Q}^\top,\)
to an orthogonal matrix:
\[
\boxed{\;\;
    \bm{R}
    = (\,\boldsymbol I_d + \boldsymbol Q\,)\;{\bigl(}\boldsymbol I_d - \boldsymbol Q\,{\bigr)}^{-1}.
    }
    \tag{2.3}
    \]
Because any real skew‑symmetric matrix yields an orthogonal matrix via (2.3), we can simply learn the entries of \( \boldsymbol Q.\) Unlike alternative parameterizations (e.g., exponentials or Householder reflections), Cayley's approach has two practical advantages:

1. **Explicit enforcement** – The mapping guarantees exact orthogonality for all feasible \( \boldsymbol Q,\) so no projection step is needed after each update.
2. **Sparsity reuse** – When \( \boldsymbol Q\) is itself sparse (as will be true for block-diagonal or butterfly forms), (2.3) preserves that sparsity structure, thereby keeping gradients as cheap to evaluate as possible.

In OFT’s original design, the diagonal entry of \( R_{ii}\) starts as \(1,\) and all off–diagonals are initialized to zero so that \( R_0 = I_d.\) This zero initialization in \(Q,\)
\( Q_{ij}=0,\;\forall i,j,\)
ensures that training begins by staying exactly at the pre-trained model before spinning out rotations.

## Block‑Diagonal OFT and its Parameter Count

To reduce the parameter budget from \( d^2- d/2.\) back to something manageable, OFT introduces block‑diagonal sparsity on \( R.\)

### Block‑Diagonal Structure

Let us partition each input neuron into \( r > 1\) equal groups; each group contains \(b=d/r.\)
The orthogonal matrix is then enforced to be block diagonal:
\[
     R
     = {\color {red}
         {\rm diag}\bigl(
          R_1, R_2,\ldots , R_r
        )},
     𝑅𝑖∈ℝ^{b×b}, 𝑅𝑖⊤𝑅𝑖=I_b .
     \tag{2.4}
     }
     \]
Only within each block does rotation occur; there are no cross–block interactions.

### Auxiliary Interpretations

If one imagines rotating neurons in parallel across blocks, an outer encoder part sees each group as independent subspace rotations—very reminiscent of *grouped convolutions* in CNNs or *tensor decomposition* for transformers.

#### Example: Visual Illustration

Suppose $d=8,$ split into two blocks ($r=2,b=4$). The first block rotates vectors $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$ among themselves; likewise for $(x_5,x_6,x_7,x_8).$ No matter what rotation application yields you cannot mix information between these two half subspaces.

## Trade‑off: Expressiveness vs. Parameter Efficiency

**Expressiveness** refers here to how many arbitrary linear transformations $\forall R$ are attainable via our parameterized form when optimized on data.

### Why Expressiveness Decreases

A dense orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ free degrees of freedom—every pairwise angle can move independently. In block–diagonal OFT, you lose all degrees linking distinct blocks: there simply does not exist a mathematical path in that representation connecting groups.

To quantify:

- Full DOFs: $d(d-1)/2,$ where every pair of neurons can rotate relative to each other.
- Block diagonal DOFs: $(b-1)/2$ per block times $r=b^{-1}d$ blocks $\Rightarrow P_{\text {block}} = d(b-1)/(2b).$

Fixing $b$ constant yields linear scaling versus quadratic scaling for dense orthogonal matrices.

Hence every potential rotation that could manifest cross–group correlations must be encoded explicitly through extra parameters; otherwise such transformations cannot appear during finetuning.

### Parameter Efficiency Gains

Despite this loss, **by constraining** the space we avoid having to learn overwhelming numbers of useless weights that may overfit or destabilize training—a crucial consideration when downstream data are few-shot or noisy.

Moreover, restricting rotations to smaller subspaces makes preconditioned optimization (e.g., Adam or momentum SGD) converge more robustly because gradients are less chaotic across block interactions.

### Practical Observations

Empirically:

- **Stability**: Training with block-diagonal OFT typically wins over full OT when using standard learning rates because gradients stay bounded within blocks.
- **Performance**: On tasks where cross-group correlations are vital—say aligning attention heads between language models—a large block size ($b>d^{0.5}$ or even full $b=d$ ) gives better transfer but at huge computational cost.
- **Transferability**: Block-diagonal OFT often generalizes better on low-data regimes due to implicit regularization; but may lag on tasks requiring intricate feature reweighting across entire feature vectors.

## Why Block Groups Lack Natural Inductive Bias

An **inductive bias** reflects our prior belief about useful function classes inherent in a model architecture or parameterization—not just about data distribution but about what transformations “make sense” given domain knowledge.

The underlying question: *Do segregated neuron groups yield rotations that match any natural organization?*

### Observed Issues:

1. **Index-Based Grouping Is Arbitrary**  
   Grouping neurons solely by index order provides no semantic alignment. For example, grouping layers’ hidden states often mixes tokens from different semantic roles arbitrarily rather than respecting propagate patterns essential in transformers or CNNs.

2. **Impossible Acoustic / Spatial Mixing**  
   When modeling visual features spanning multiple scales, we might want rotations that couple low–frequency filters with high–frequency ones—cross-group mixing becomes essential—but BE-block structure prevents such coupling entirely.

3. **Cross-Axis Feature Interaction Loss**  
   In vision transformers pre-trained on ImageNet, attention-head projections heavily intermix features across feature dimensions; restricting rotations within pre-chosen subspaces ignores this intermixing optimal property seen across many architectures e.g., *global* covariance estimation would suggest no fixed groups at all.

4. **Harder Parameter Transfer Across Layers**  
   Developers often wish to reuse optimized seeding parameters from one layer in another; block structures hamper reusability because transformation matrices have mismatched shapes if layers differ drastically in dimension—yet depth-stepping often requires consistent transformations across depths for transfer learning consistency.

Thus, while block-diagonal structure offers scale convenience, it imposes an *artificial partition* that often conflicts with intrinsic logical structures learned during pre-training and latent factorization in downstream tasks. Consequently it may not unfold necessary cross-neuron couplings for highly expressive tasks such as image generation or adversarial robustness fine-tuning where global coherence matters deeply.

---

## Summary

Orthogonal Finetuning presents a principled way to deform pre-trained models conservatively via rotational transformations preserving inter-neuron angular relationships. Using a Cayley-transformed skew-symmetric matrix guarantees strict orthogonality throughout optimization while endowing flexibility through learnable angles unknown at initialization.

To curb parametric overhead—and hence computational burden—Designers originally enforce a *block-diagonal* sparsity pattern on $\mathbf R$, drastically cutting parameters from quadratic to near-linear scale at cost of expressiveness: cross-group couplings vanish entirely and natural inductive bias inherent to many downstream tasks remains unsatisfied because partitions are index-based instead of semantically grounded.

The remainder chapters will continue by exploring solutions that keep rotational expressiveness while still operating with limited parameters—most notably butterfly-factorized OOT introduced later—which mitigates these shortcomings by encoding structured sparse multipliers capable of faithfully spanning the entire orthogonal group under modest parameter budgets.*
