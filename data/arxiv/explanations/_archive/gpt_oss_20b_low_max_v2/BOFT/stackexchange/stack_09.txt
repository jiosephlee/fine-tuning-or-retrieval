\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### "Injective inductive bias" – what does it mean for BF's sparse factorization?
Question:
"Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT” says that sparse factorization may induce an implicit inductive bias similar to other sparse training works. What theoretical intuition or prior results support this claim? How might such bias manifest in practice (e.g., encouraging certain invariances)?
Answer:
**Answer**

The claim that a *sparse* factorization of the orthogonal matrix in BOFT induces an *implicit inductive bias* is grounded in two strands of theory that have emerged in recent years: (1) the *information‑theoretic view* of structured linear operators, and (2) results on *implicit regularisation* of sparse or structured training procedures.  Below I sketch how each of those strands applies to BOFT, and then discuss concrete ways that this bias could manifest itself during finetuning.

---

### 1.  Information‑theoretic intuition: “sparse patterns = low complexity”

BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix $R$ as a product of $\log d$ sparse butterfly matrices:
$$
R = B_{\log d}\,B_{\log d-1}\dots B_1,\qquad
B_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\; \text{with }O(d)\text{ non‑zero entries}.
$$
Each butterfly matrix is a permutation of a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are $2\times 2$ rotation matrices (or, more generally, $b\times b$ rotations).  From the *information‑transmission* view introduced in the paper, every entry in a butterfly component either
- **free** (the parameter can be set arbitrarily), or
- **fixed** (it must be zero to keep orthogonality).

Because orthogonality imposes linear constraints on the non‑zero pattern, only $(b-1)d/2$ degrees of freedom survive per component.  Therefore most entries are *redundant* from the optimization point of view; the network is forced to learn only a low‑dimensional manifold within the full orthogonal group.

This matches the cluster‑encoding intuition behind many sparse or low‑rank structures: if an operator has only few free parameters, the family it can represent has lower *Kolmogorov complexity*.  In learning theory this translates into a smaller hypothesis class and consequently a tighter pessimistic risk bound via covering numbers or Rademacher complexity.  In other words, by forcing the finetuning weight to lie in the set $\mathcal{Q}=\{R: R=\prod_i B_i,\;B_i\text{ butterfly}\}$, BOFT is effectively adding a prior that prefers “structured” rotations—rotations that can be built from successive pairwise mixings.

A concrete prior that arises is **frequency sparsity**.  The butterfly topology is essentially a hierarchical pairwise mixer reminiscent of the Cooley–Tukey FFT plan.  It can exactly represent all linear transforms that are *fast Fourier type*: DFT, DCT, Hadamard, etc.  These transforms have well‑known invariances (e.g., shift equivariance in Fourier space, bit‑reversal symmetry).  Hence, BOFT tends to favor weight updates that resemble those operations even if they do not match any particular known transform—nonetheless they inherit some of its regularising qualities.

---

### 2.  Implicit regularisation through sparsity constraints

Several recent works have shown that training with sparse or structured weight matrices implicitly regularises optimization dynamics:

| Paper | Key idea | How it applies to BOFT |
|-------|----------|------------------------|
| Gunasekar et al., ICML’18 | Gradient descent on overparameterised networks converges to minimum Euclidean norm solutions | In BOFT every parameter lives on an orthogonal manifold; gradients are tangent to this manifold and consequently preserve norms. |
| Li et al., ICML’18 | Structured sparsity patterns induce distributional biases on gradients | The butterfly pattern enforces equal spacing between pairwise mixings; gradients cannot freely assign one neuron to dominate another without affecting all its paired partners horizontally. |
| Liu et al., ICML’19 | Sparse connectivity can act as a form of dropout with low variance | The multiplicative dropout proposed for BOFT masks out whole butterfly components or subblocks.  At each update step many wiring patterns are forgotten temporarily which leads to exploration around locally equivalent rotational modes and reduces overfitting. |

Together these results suggest that *sparsifying* the factorisation does not simply reduce capacity; it also shapes how gradients flow through space.  Since the learning dynamics stay on an orthogonal manifold where norms are preserved, BOFT acts as a **regulariser that favours directions with small norm growth**, which has been shown experimentally to align with improved generalisation in related works (e.g., LoRA vs. full fine‑tuning).

---

### 3.  Practical manifestations: invariance learning and robustness

When you look at what BOFT does during finetuning, several behaviours emerge that match common inductive biases:

1. **Action‐invariance across permutations**  
   Each butterfly stage pairs elements symmetrically and rotates them together.  The network therefore learns transformations that treat permuted indices in a similar way—much like weight sharing in convolutional layers but at the level of internal channels.

2. **Frequency or spectral inductive bias**  
   Because many classical fast transforms are expressible exactly by butterflies, intermediate weight matrices will tend to “lean” toward operations that concentrate energy into few frequencies or super‑positions thereof.  For vision models this translates into better preservation of global patterns (edges spread across network depth). For language models it helps maintain positional encoding structure.

3. **Curse‑of‑dimensionality mitigation**  
   By keeping effective degrees of freedom low ($O(d\log d)$ vs. $O(d^2)$), BOFT reduces variance in parameter estimates when only a small downstream dataset is available—exactly what parameter‑efficient finetuning aims for.

4. **Smooth interpolation between identity and arbitrary rotation**  
   The paper shows an interpolation curve where progressively more butterfly components are turned off gradually: $R(p)=B_{\log d}^{p}B_{\log d-1}^{p}\dots B_{1}^{p}$.  Early steps keep $R(p)\approx I$, thereby preserving pretraining knowledge; later steps allow more expressive rotations while still staying within structured subspace.

These behaviours collectively constitute an **implicit inductive bias**: instead of explicitly regularising with $\ell_2$ penalties or batchnorm statistics, BOFT structurally forces updates toward rotations that respect pairwise mixing hierarchies and spectral sparsity.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

- **Theory**: Orthogonal factorisation via butterflies reduces the hypothesis class size without sacrificing expressiveness outright; it sits between full dense rotations (too flexible) and block‑diagonal ones (too rigid).  
- **Empirical tracebacks**: Experiments in the paper show lower test error under identical budgets for vision CLIP fine‑tuning and language fine‑tuning relative to both LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT—this aligns with an implicit regulariser boosting generalisation.
- **Practical takeaway**: When you apply BOFT you are effectively telling the downstream task “only let me rotate neurons along these fixed pairwise pathways”. That’s precisely many task–agnostic inductive biases such as shift‐equivariance (`FFT`), mean preserving (`orthogonal`), and limited feature interactions (`sparse`). 

Thus we see how theoretical guarantees from manifold optimisation plus empirical insights about sparsity yield a compelling explanation for why *injective inductive bias* appears naturally in BF’s sparse factorization, resulting in better generalisation across multiple downstream tasks without extra latency at inference time.
