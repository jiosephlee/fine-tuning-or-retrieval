\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does the block-diagonal structure in OFT limit expressiveness of the orthogonal matrix?
Question:
In Section 3, the authors mention that a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms, whereas a dense orthogonal matrix can. I understand that a block-diagonal structure partitions the neuron dimensions into independent groups, but I’m unclear on why this fundamentally prevents us from approximating transforms like the discrete Fourier transform or a full random orthogonal matrix. Could someone provide an intuitive explanation or a small example demonstrating this limitation?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix acts independently on a set of disjoint neuron sub‑groups.  
Because the blocks never exchange information, any linear transform that couples any two hidden
dimensions must appear as a non‑zero entry somewhere outside the diagonal blocks.  
Classical transforms that mix all dimensions—such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) or a
generic Haar‑style orthogonal matrix—require exactly such cross‑couplings, so they cannot be
represented by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix of any size.  In contrast, a *dense*
orthogonal matrix has all its off‑diagonal entries potentially non‑zero and can therefore produce
any orthogonal transformation.

Below I unpack this intuition with two concrete examples and then tie it to the paper’s discussion.

---

## 1. Why does independence matter?

Let the weight matrix of a linear layer be $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$.  
OFT rewrites the forward pass as  

$$
z = (R W^0)^{\top}x ,
$$

where $R\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal.  
If $R$ is **block–diagonal**, say  

$$
R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r), \qquad 
R_i\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br = d,
$$

then for every input $x=(x_1^{\top},\dots,x_r^{\top})^{\top}$ with
$x_i\in \mathbb{R}^{b}$, we have

$$
Rx = 
\begin{pmatrix}
      R_1x_1\\
      \vdots\\
      R_rx_r
     \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The transformation acting on block $i$ depends *only* on $x_i$.  
No element of block $i$ can influence block $j(\neq i)$.

Hence any linear map that requires cross–talk between blocks cannot be realised by such an
$R$.  That is exactly what makes classic transforms “non‑local”.

---

## 2. Small‑scale illustration

Take $d=4$ (two blocks of size 2).  

*Block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrices look like

```
[   R1   0 ]
[   0    R2]
```

with $R_1,R_2 \in O(2)$.

A **Fourier** transform in four dimensions is given by

$$
F_{4}= \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\
1 & i & -1 & -i\\
1 & -1 & 1 & -1\\
1 & -i & -1 & i
\end{bmatrix},
$$

which (after removing complex numbers and rescaling) is a real orthogonal matrix with many non‑zero off‑diagonal entries, e.g.

```
[ . . . . ]
[ . . . . ]
[ . . . . ]
[ . . . . ]
```

(Here “.” denotes non‑zero.)  
No matter how you choose $R_1,R_2$, the resulting product will have zero entries wherever
the two subspaces are blocked from communicating: the pairs $(x_1,x_2)$ or $(x_3,x_4)$
never mix.

Trying to force an “approximation” by optimizing over $R_1,R_2$ will leave two diagonal zeros in
every row‐column pair that straddles the block boundary, meaning the DFT cannot be reproduced even approximately.

---

## 3. Random orthogonal matrices

The paper notes:

> “block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms,
> whereas a dense orthogonal matrix can.”

For a random orthogonal matrix $Q \in O(d)$, entries are typically non‑zero.  
Blocking it into subgroups reduces the *manifold dimension*: instead of having $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$
degrees of freedom, a block‑diagonal parameterization has only 
$\displaystyle r\,b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2$ degrees of freedom (with $r=d/b$).  
If we want to match an arbitrary random $Q$, we would need to assign each pair of indices a separate degree of freedom—something impossible if we insist on disjoint blocks.

Mathematically, suppose we wish to solve

$$
Q = \operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)
          \quad (\text{or close to } Q ).
$$

For any index pair where one lies in block $i$, the other in block $j (\neq i)$,
the corresponding entry in the product is always zero regardless of what we learn for 
$R_i,R_j,\dots,R_r.$ Consequently, all such cross–block entries of *any* candidate from this family are exactly zero, whereas *some* entries in $Q$ are non‑zero. Therefore no elementwise approximation is possible: at least one entry will stay zero while it should be non‑zero for closeness metrics such as Frobenius norm.

---

## 4. Connection to BOFT and the paper’s claims

The authors observe that a **butterfly** factorisation can produce a *dense*
orthogonal matrix while still keeping only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters.
A butterfly component mixes pairs of coordinates across intermediate stages:

```
     level k          level k+1
        x                  y             y = B_k x
```

Every stage connects each coordinate to two others in the next level, and after enough stages every input coordinate influences every output coordinate. This ensures that every off-diagonal entry may be “touched” by some product term, which dense matrices need.

So, when OFT uses block-diagonal matrices, it effectively fixes many cross terms to zero; BOFT lifts this restriction by stacking several sparse blocks whose **global composition** becomes dense.

In Section 3 and Theorem 4 of the paper, the authors formalise this idea: BOFT’s butterfly construction sits strictly “above” OFT in expressiveness for the same number of parameters because it allows interaction across all neuron dimensions via repeated small rotations.

---

## 5. Bottom line

* A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix preserves angles **within each group**, but it forbids any interaction between groups.
* Classical transforms such as DFT or an arbitrary full random orthogonal matrix rely on interactions between *every* pair of dimensions.
* Consequently, no choice of blocks can generate those transformations, making the class represented by block-diagonal matrices strictly smaller than that represented by all dense orthogonal matrices.
* The butterfly architecture used in BOFT circumvents this limitation while still being parameter-efficient, exactly because its repeated sparse (but interleaved) multiplications eventually provide full coupling among all coordinates.
