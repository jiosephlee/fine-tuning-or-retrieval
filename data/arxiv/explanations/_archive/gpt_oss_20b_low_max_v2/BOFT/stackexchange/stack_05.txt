\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How is multiplicative dropout implemented in BOFT compared to standard dropout?
Question:
Section 5.1 describes ‘multiplicative dropout’ for BOFT: randomly picking some butterfly components and diagonal blocks and replacing them with identity matrices. How does this differ mathematically from applying standard dropout on the weight updates? Would it be equivalent to zeroing out certain entries, or is it conceptually different because of the multiplicative update rule?
Answer:
**Multiplicative‑dropout in BOFT vs. usual dropout**

In ordinary Dropout (as used in LoRA or vanilla feed‑forward layers) the weights are updated additively, e.g.

$$
W_{\text{fin}} = W^0 + \Delta W,\quad
\Delta W = U V^\top ,
$$

and dropout masks are applied to the low‑rank factors $U$ and $V$.  
Masking a factor simply sets some of its entries to zero; when the masks are
re‑sampled at every forward pass, the effective update $\Delta W$ is a random
sub‑matrix of the low‐rank term. Mathematically this is equivalent to multiplying
the *additive update* by a binary mask:

$$
\Delta W_{\text{mask}} = M \odot \Delta W ,
$$

where $M\in\{0,1\}^{d\times b}\times b\times n$.

BOFT, on the other hand, updates the pretrained weight matrix **multiplicatively**:

$$
W_{\text{fin}} = R\,W^0 , \qquad R=\prod_{i=1}^{m} B_i,
$$

with each $B_i$ an orthogonal butterfly component.  
Because the update is *multiplicative*, applying dropout cannot be done by
masking the entries of the *adopted* orthogonal matrix $R$.  Dropping an entry
of $R$ would break orthogonality and alter all subsequent multiplications in
non‑trivial ways.

Instead, BOFT uses a **multiplicative drop‑out** that operates on the *building blocks*
$B_i$:

1. Randomly choose a fraction $p_1$ of butterfly components.
2. Within each chosen component, randomly choose a fraction $p_2$ of its
   diagonal blocks.
3. Replace these selected blocks by identity matrices (i.e., set them to 1 on
   the diagonal and 0 elsewhere).

After this replacement, each affected component becomes locally an identity.
Because an identity matrix commutes with every other matrix, leaving it in the
product does not change the overall transformation at that level.  Conceptually,
you are “turning off” some of the angle rotations performed by that butterfly
step.

Mathematically, if $B_i = I + E_i$ where $E_i$ contains all learnable
entries of that component, then dropping a block substitutes it by $I + E'_i =
I + 0 = I$.  The product over all $i$ becomes

$$
R_{\text{drop}} = \prod_{i \notin S} B_i \;\bigg(\prod_{i \in S} I \bigg)
                = \prod_{i \notin S} B_i ,
$$

where $S$ is the set of dropped components.  
Thus, multiplicative dropout in BOFT **does not zero out any entries in
\(R_{\text{fin}}\)**; it merely removes entire stages of rotation from the
composition pipeline.

---

### Why this matters

* **Preserves orthogonality** – By never perturbing any non‑identity block,
  every remaining factor stays orthogonal and so does their product.
  Orthogonality guarantees spectral norms are unchanged, which is a key reason
  OFT/BOFT enjoys stable training.

* **Dropout targets sub‑spaces instead of individual weights** – Because each
  butterfly block governs a specific pairwise rotation (or scaling), dropping it
  forces the model to rely on other rotations.  This is more analogous to layer-
  level regularization than entry‑wise dropout.

* **Interpretable and efficient** – Replacing parts of a sparse orthogonal matrix
  by identities is cheaper than generating fresh binary masks for every weight,
  and it keeps alignment with the factorized structure used for parameter efficiency.

In summary, multiplicative Droppout in BOFT acts on *components* of the learned
orthogonal transformation rather than on individual weight entries.  It is thus
mathematically different from standard additive dropout and preserves both the
sparsity pattern and orthogonality that are central to BOFT’s parameter‑efficient
finetuning strategy.
