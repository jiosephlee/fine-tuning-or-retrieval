\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Can BOFT approximate any arbitrary orthogonal matrix with sufficiently many layers?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims that by composing enough butterfly matrices we can span the whole orthogonal group. Yet earlier in Section 4 it was argued that BOFT has limited expressiveness compared to full OFT. Are these statements contradictory? Under what conditions (values of $m$, $b$, or additional steps) does BOFT become universal? Is there an intuitive way to see why increasing $m$ alone doesn’t guarantee universality?
Answer:
**Answer**

The two passages you quote are not in conflict once we keep in mind what “universality” means for BOFT and how the theorem is stated.

---

### 1.  What Theorem 1 actually says

> *“By composing enough butterfly matrices we can span the whole orthogonal group.”*

The statement is an existence claim: **there exists an integer \(m\) and a suitable choice of block sizes \(b\)** such that the product  

\[
\bm R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)}
\]

is **exactly any chosen orthogonal matrix** \(\bm Q\in O(d)\). In other words, if we give BOFT the freedom to choose all its learnable parameters (the \((b\times b)\) sub‑matrices inside each butterfly component) then, after enough layers, the parameter space contains every element of \(O(d)\).

This statement is analogous to saying that a linear feed‑forward network with two ReLU layers can represent any function: the network *has enough capacity*, but a practical training process might not discover it.

---

### 2.  Unpacking the “limited expressiveness” paragraph

Section 4 explains that **a single butterfly layer** (i.e. \(m=1\)) is far from being able to generate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix when the block size \(b\) is small.  
When \(b=d\) the single-layer butterfly collapses to an ordinary dense orthogonal matrix – exactly what OFT already uses – and obviously can represent every \(\bm Q\).  
But in practice we want many layers with tiny blocks so that the number of trainable parameters ($\tfrac12(b-1)d m$) stays modest.  In that regime BOFT *cannot* reach every \(\bm Q\) – its hypothesis set is a strict subset of \(O(d)\).

So the sentence “BOFT has limited expressiveness compared to full OFT” refers to **finite \(m,b\)** typical of actual experiments, not to the asymptotic capacity described by Theorem 1.

---

### 3.  When does BOFT become universal?

To recover universality we need either

| Condition | Why it works |
|-----------|--------------|
| **\(m \ge d-1\)** | The construction used in the proof copies that of a kaleidoscope hierarchy: multiply alternating left‑ and right‑multiplication by butterfly matrices. This sequence of length \(2(d-1)\) is algebraically shown to generate \(\mathrm{O}(d)\). |
| **\(b=d\)** | Then each butterfly component is diagonal (size‑\(d\times d\)), i.e. a standard orthogonal matrix; any product of such matrices is again an arbitrary orthogonal matrix. |
| **Allow both directions** | The generalized form \(\bm R^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)\) in Theorem 2 lets us alternate left and right multiplications. Taking \(l=d-1,\; m_1=m_2=\log d,\; b_1=b_2=2,\) gives a chain that covers all orthogonal matrices (this is exactly the kaleidoscope hierarchy). |

Thus, universality holds only when either we give BOFT enough layers *or* we let its blocks be as large as the full dimension. In realistic, compact settings (\(m \ll d,\; b \ll d\)) BOFT remains strictly smaller than \(O(d)\).

---

### 4.  Intuition: Why larger \(m\) alone isn’t enough

Even if you keep multiplying many tiny butterflies, each butterfly only permutes a very sparse set of connections (two‑by‑two mixing).  
The sparsity pattern restricts which linear combinations of input coordinates can appear in intermediate stages.  
Unless you eventually allow all coordinates to be mixed (which happens when either one layer’s block covers all coordinates or you interleave left/right multiplications as in the general form), there will be linear dependencies that cannot be broken apart no matter how many layers you stack.

A useful way to think of it is a communication network: each butterfly component is a set of parallel “tunnels” that mix only a few pairs of channels. If you cascade many such networks you can enlarge connectivity, but unless you introduce *cross‑links* between distant channels (which a fixed‐size block cannot provide), some paths will always be missing. That’s why simply increasing depth doesn’t rescue expressiveness on its own.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

* Theorem 1 proves asymptotic universality: with enough layers and/or full‑size blocks BOFT can represent every orthogonal matrix.
* Section 4’s remark about limited expressiveness applies to the practical regime where we use few layers and small blocks – precisely where parameter efficiency matters.
* To recover universality without blowing up parameters, one would need either
  * extremely deep stacks (\(m \ge d-1\)) or
  * alternating left/right products as in the generalized expression,
  both of which are rarely used because they defeat the goal of keeping parameter counts low.

Hence there is no contradiction – it’s just a statement about different regimes of the same construction.
