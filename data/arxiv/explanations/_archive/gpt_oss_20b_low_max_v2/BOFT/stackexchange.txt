\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does the block-diagonal structure in OFT limit expressiveness of the orthogonal matrix?
Question:
In Section 3, the authors mention that a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms, whereas a dense orthogonal matrix can. I understand that a block-diagonal structure partitions the neuron dimensions into independent groups, but I’m unclear on why this fundamentally prevents us from approximating transforms like the discrete Fourier transform or a full random orthogonal matrix. Could someone provide an intuitive explanation or a small example demonstrating this limitation?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix acts independently on a set of disjoint neuron sub‑groups.  
Because the blocks never exchange information, any linear transform that couples any two hidden
dimensions must appear as a non‑zero entry somewhere outside the diagonal blocks.  
Classical transforms that mix all dimensions—such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) or a
generic Haar‑style orthogonal matrix—require exactly such cross‑couplings, so they cannot be
represented by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix of any size.  In contrast, a *dense*
orthogonal matrix has all its off‑diagonal entries potentially non‑zero and can therefore produce
any orthogonal transformation.

Below I unpack this intuition with two concrete examples and then tie it to the paper’s discussion.

---

## 1. Why does independence matter?

Let the weight matrix of a linear layer be $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$.  
OFT rewrites the forward pass as  

$$
z = (R W^0)^{\top}x ,
$$

where $R\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is orthogonal.  
If $R$ is **block–diagonal**, say  

$$
R = \operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r), \qquad 
R_i\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br = d,
$$

then for every input $x=(x_1^{\top},\dots,x_r^{\top})^{\top}$ with
$x_i\in \mathbb{R}^{b}$, we have

$$
Rx = 
\begin{pmatrix}
      R_1x_1\\
      \vdots\\
      R_rx_r
     \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The transformation acting on block $i$ depends *only* on $x_i$.  
No element of block $i$ can influence block $j(\neq i)$.

Hence any linear map that requires cross–talk between blocks cannot be realised by such an
$R$.  That is exactly what makes classic transforms “non‑local”.

---

## 2. Small‑scale illustration

Take $d=4$ (two blocks of size 2).  

*Block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrices look like

```
[   R1   0 ]
[   0    R2]
```

with $R_1,R_2 \in O(2)$.

A **Fourier** transform in four dimensions is given by

$$
F_{4}= \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\
1 & i & -1 & -i\\
1 & -1 & 1 & -1\\
1 & -i & -1 & i
\end{bmatrix},
$$

which (after removing complex numbers and rescaling) is a real orthogonal matrix with many non‑zero off‑diagonal entries, e.g.

```
[ . . . . ]
[ . . . . ]
[ . . . . ]
[ . . . . ]
```

(Here “.” denotes non‑zero.)  
No matter how you choose $R_1,R_2$, the resulting product will have zero entries wherever
the two subspaces are blocked from communicating: the pairs $(x_1,x_2)$ or $(x_3,x_4)$
never mix.

Trying to force an “approximation” by optimizing over $R_1,R_2$ will leave two diagonal zeros in
every row‐column pair that straddles the block boundary, meaning the DFT cannot be reproduced even approximately.

---

## 3. Random orthogonal matrices

The paper notes:

> “block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot approximate classic linear transforms,
> whereas a dense orthogonal matrix can.”

For a random orthogonal matrix $Q \in O(d)$, entries are typically non‑zero.  
Blocking it into subgroups reduces the *manifold dimension*: instead of having $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$
degrees of freedom, a block‑diagonal parameterization has only 
$\displaystyle r\,b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2$ degrees of freedom (with $r=d/b$).  
If we want to match an arbitrary random $Q$, we would need to assign each pair of indices a separate degree of freedom—something impossible if we insist on disjoint blocks.

Mathematically, suppose we wish to solve

$$
Q = \operatorname{diag}(R_1,\dots,R_r)
          \quad (\text{or close to } Q ).
$$

For any index pair where one lies in block $i$, the other in block $j (\neq i)$,
the corresponding entry in the product is always zero regardless of what we learn for 
$R_i,R_j,\dots,R_r.$ Consequently, all such cross–block entries of *any* candidate from this family are exactly zero, whereas *some* entries in $Q$ are non‑zero. Therefore no elementwise approximation is possible: at least one entry will stay zero while it should be non‑zero for closeness metrics such as Frobenius norm.

---

## 4. Connection to BOFT and the paper’s claims

The authors observe that a **butterfly** factorisation can produce a *dense*
orthogonal matrix while still keeping only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters.
A butterfly component mixes pairs of coordinates across intermediate stages:

```
     level k          level k+1
        x                  y             y = B_k x
```

Every stage connects each coordinate to two others in the next level, and after enough stages every input coordinate influences every output coordinate. This ensures that every off-diagonal entry may be “touched” by some product term, which dense matrices need.

So, when OFT uses block-diagonal matrices, it effectively fixes many cross terms to zero; BOFT lifts this restriction by stacking several sparse blocks whose **global composition** becomes dense.

In Section 3 and Theorem 4 of the paper, the authors formalise this idea: BOFT’s butterfly construction sits strictly “above” OFT in expressiveness for the same number of parameters because it allows interaction across all neuron dimensions via repeated small rotations.

---

## 5. Bottom line

* A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix preserves angles **within each group**, but it forbids any interaction between groups.
* Classical transforms such as DFT or an arbitrary full random orthogonal matrix rely on interactions between *every* pair of dimensions.
* Consequently, no choice of blocks can generate those transformations, making the class represented by block-diagonal matrices strictly smaller than that represented by all dense orthogonal matrices.
* The butterfly architecture used in BOFT circumvents this limitation while still being parameter-efficient, exactly because its repeated sparse (but interleaved) multiplications eventually provide full coupling among all coordinates.

### How does the information transmission framework translate a butterfly factorisation into a sparse graph?
Question:
The paper introduces an ‘information transmission view’ where an orthogonal matrix $m{R}$ is seen as transmitting information over a $d	imes(m+1)$ grid graph. I’m struggling to connect this view to the concrete butterfly factorisation $	ilde{m{B}}(d,2^k)$. Specifically, how does a butterfly matrix correspond to directed edges in this grid, and how many edges are required to guarantee dense connectivity? A diagram or explicit mapping would help.
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s *information‑transmission view* is an abstract way to look at a matrix factorisation  

\[
\mathbf R \;=\; \mathbf B_m\mathbf B_{m-1}\dots \mathbf B_1 ,
\qquad
\mathbf B_i\in \mathbb R^{d\times d},
\]

as a communication network that we can “unroll” into *\(m+1\)* horizontal layers of \(d\) nodes each.  
Layer \(0\) contains the *sources* (the \(d\) columns of the identity), layer \(m\) contains the *sink* (the columns of \(\mathbf R\)), and each factor \(\mathbf B_i\) specifies the directed edges that connect layer \(i-1\) to layer \(i\): an entry \((p,q)\) of \(\mathbf B_i\) is non‑zero iff there is an edge from node \(q\) in layer \(i-1\) to node \(p\) in layer \(i\).  

A **dense** orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\) therefore requires that for every pair of source–sink nodes there is at least one directed path that goes through the intermediate layers.  

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### From butterfly factorisation to the graph

A butterfly matrix is a product of *butterfly factors*.  
For a power‑of‑two dimension \(d=2^N\), a single butterfly factor \(\mathbf B^F(k)\) (with block size \(k=2^r,\;r<N\)) is

\[
\mathbf B^F(k)=
  \begin{bmatrix}
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1)&
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2)\\[2pt]
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3)&
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)
  \end{bmatrix},
  \qquad
  \|\mathbf d_i\|_0=\frac{k}{2},
\]
so the factor has exactly **\(k/2+k/2= k/\!1 = k/2\times 2 = k** zero entries everywhere except on four diagonal submatrices – in other words, it has exactly **\(k<d^2\)** non‑zero entries.

When we build a depth‑\(m=\log_2 d - 1\) butterfly matrix

\[
\bR = 
   \widetilde{\bB}(d,\,2)\;
   \widetilde{\bB}(d,\,4)\;
   \cdots;
   \widetilde{\bB}(d,\,d),
   \qquad
   m=\log_2 d ,
\]

each factor contributes one column of directed edges between successive layers:

* **Layer 0 → Layer 1**: edges described by the first two blocks of size \(2\times 2\).
* **Layer 1 → Layer 2**: edges described by the next two blocks of size \(4\times 4\), etc.
* …  
* **Layer m−1 → Layer m**: edges from the largest block, still only a handful.

In total a butterfly of depth \(m=\log_2 d,\;b=2\,k_{\min}\) has  

\[
E_{\text{butterfly}} = (m-1)d + b(d/ b)=d\, (\log_2 d -1)+b(d/b)
                   = O(d\,{\log}_2 d)
\]

non‑zero **trainable** entries – precisely the number cited in the paper.  
Each non‑zero entry corresponds to one directed edge.  

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How many edges are enough for dense connectivity?

The sparsest factorisation that still yields a dense product satisfies two constraints:

| Requirement | What it means on the graph | Example edge count |
|-------------|----------------------------|--------------------|
| **Full rank per layer** | Every node in level \(i-1\) must have at least one outgoing edge and every node in level \(i$ must have at least one incoming edge | Minimum `d` edges between two adjacent layers |
| **Full reachability** | For every source node there exists at least one path to every sink node | Applied recursively, this yields `∼O(d log d)` total edges |

For a single dense orthogonal matrix (`m=1`) we would need `d²` edges.  
The block‑diagonal OFT requires each block to be independent, so although it has only `d` non‑zeros per block it cannot preserve global dense connectivity—many sink nodes are unreachable from many sources.

By contrast, a butterfly graph with `m = log₂ d` layers succeeds with only `O(d log d)` edges because each level shuffles information across *half* of the current group of nodes, doubling the reachable set at each step. A simple inductive argument shows that after `ℓ` levels the set of nodes reachable from any source contains all nodes iff O(ℓ²)=O(d log d). This matches the known optimal communication cost in computer‑network theory for fully connected topologies.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Concrete example: `d=8`

```
level 0   [0] [1] [2] [3] [4] [5] [6] [7]
               |<--edges from */node 0-->|
level 1   [...]                       ...
```

A depth–3 power‑of‑two butterfly for `d=8` contains three factors:

```
B(8,8):  block size k=8 (splits into two 4×4 blocks)
   │───► all nodes → all nodes               (8×8 sparsity pattern)
B(8,4):  block size k=4 (splits into two 2×2 blocks)
   │─────► half‑nodes → all nodes            (16 non‐zeros)
B(8,2):                                                        (
   │───────► node pairs ↔ connect pairs             (16 non‐zeros))
```

Adding up gives   
`16 + 16 + 8 = 40` trainable entries ≈ `O(8 log₂8)=24`, close to the theoretical lower bound once we subtract fixed `+/−` orthogonality‐imposed “identity” edges.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

* Each butterfly weight matrix is a sparse orthogonal matrix where non‑zero entries encode directed edges between successive grid levels.
* The product of these matrices builds a communication network that guarantees every input neuron can influence every output neuron.
* The minimal number of such directed edges needed for full reachability grows as \(O(d\,{\log}_2 d)\); hence BOFT attains parameter efficiency while maintaining expressivity comparable to unconstrained OFT.

### What guarantees that each butterfly component $	ilde{m{B}}(d,k)$ can be rendered orthogonal?
Question:
Equation (4) defines a butterfly component as a block‑diagonal matrix of $k	imes k$ butterflies, and later the authors say each component can be parameterized as an orthogonal matrix using Cayley transform or $2$‑dimensional rotations. However, for $k>2$ the blocks are not just simple rotations; they involve multiple diagonal sub‑blocks. How exactly do we ensure that the whole $	ilde{m{B}}(d,k)$ is orthogonal? Is there a closed‑form condition on the vectors $m{d}_i$ that guarantees orthogonality for all $k$?
Answer:
In a BOFT layer the *butterfly component*  
\[
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\bm{B}^{F}_{1}(k),\dots ,\bm{B}^{F}_{d/k}(k)\bigr)
\]
is a block–diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are exactly the “butterfly factors’’  
(Equation (9) in the paper)

\[
\bm{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\bm{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{k\times k},
\qquad 
\bm{d}_{i}\in\mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

Because the matrix is diagonal in each half‑column block, each “column” \(j=1,\dots ,k/2\)  
acts only on two indices of the vector \((x_{1},x_{k/2+1}), (x_{2},x_{k/2+2}),\ldots\).
For a fixed \(j\) let  

\[
u_{j}=\bigl[\underbrace{\mathbf e_{\,\,\, j}}_{\text{$k$‑dim}},0,\dots ,0\bigr]\in \mathbb R^{k},
\qquad
v_{j}=\bigl[0,\dots ,0,\underbrace{\mathbf e_{\,\, j}}_{\text{$k$‑dim}},0,\dots ,0\bigr].
\]

The butterfly factor acts on the subspace spanned by \(\{u_{j},v_{j}\}\) as the
\(2\times 2\) matrix

\[
M_{j}= 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    d_{1,j}&d_{2,j}\\[4pt]
    d_{3,j}&d_{4,j}
    \end{bmatrix}\in \mathbb R^{\, 2\times 2}.
\]

Consequently

```
tilde B(d,k) =   ⨂_{j=1}^{k/2} M_j                            (tensor product)
```

Hence \(\tilde{\bm B}(d,k)\) is orthogonal **iff every** \(M_j\) is an
orthogonal \(2\times 2\) matrix.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### When is a \(M_j=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\) orthogonal?

The condition \(M_j^\top M_j=I_2\) is equivalent to

$$
a^2+c^2 = 1,\qquad b^2+d^2 = 1,
$$
$$
ab+cd = 0.
$$

Geometrically this means that \(\bigl[a,c\bigr]^\top=(\cos \theta _j ,\, \sin \theta _j )^\top
   \) and
   \(\bigl[b,d\bigr]^\top=(-\, \sin \phi _j ,\, \cos \phi _j )^\top .
$

A convenient closed‑form parametrisation is

```math
a   = c   =   \cos \theta_j,
b   = - \sin \theta_j,
d   =   \sin \theta_j .
```

Setting \(θ_j∈[0,π)\) for every \(j=1,\dots ,k/2\) guarantees that each
\(M_j∈SO(2)\).  
Thus we can pick any collection of angles and obtain an orthogonal butterfly
factor.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why does this work for any \(k> 2?\)

The definition of a butterfly component explicitly permutes all the independent
\(M_j\)’s across the larger matrix:

* The positions of the non‑zero entries in a butterfly component are just a
permutation of those in the base case where \(k=2\).
* Therefore every butterfly component can be obtained by concatenating several 
independent \(M_j^{(i)}∈SO(₂)\), one for each half‑column.

Because product permutations of orthogonal matrices are themselves
orthogonal, every butterfly component inherits orthogonality.  
The proof follows directly from the fact that:

```
tilde B(d,k)
    = P_σ₁ ⨂_{r} M_r P_σ₂ ,   for some permutation matrices P_σ₁,P_σ₂,
```

and every permutation matrix is itself an element of \(O(d)\).
Consequently

```
tilde B(d,k)^T tilde B(d,k)
     = (P_σ₂ ⊗ I)(P_σ₁ ⊗ I)^T ⋅ I ⋅ (P_σ₁ ⊗ I)(P_σ₂ ⊗ I)^T
     = I .
```

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* Each diagonal block of a butterfly component acts on two coordinates and can  
be written as a single \(S O(₂)\)-rotation defined by an angle \(θ_n∈[0,π)\).
* By choosing such angles independently for every half‑column we obtain a
full orthogonal matrix.
* The whole butterfly component is just a permutation of independent \(SO(₂)\)
blocks; thus it stays orthogonal regardless of how large \(k> ₂​)\).

Therefore BOFT’s claim—“every component can be parameterised as an  
orthogonal matrix using Cayley transform or ²‑dimensional rotations”—holds for all allowed values of \(k.\)

### Why does BOFT(	extit{m},	extit{b}) reduce parameters from $oldsymbol{O}(d^2)$ to $oldsymbol{O}(doldsymbol{	extit{m}})$?
Question:
The paper states that BOFT with parameters $(m,b)$ has 	frac12(b-1)dm effective trainable parameters for a layer of size $d	imes n$, leading to $O(doldsymbol m)$ when $b=2$. Could someone walk through the counting argument? In particular, how many free parameters do we have in each $2b	imes 2b$ block of a butterfly component, and how does this sum over all components?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is learned for a linear layer \(W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is not a dense matrix but a \emph{product} of \(m\) \emph{butterfly components}.  
Each component is a **block‑diagonal** matrix whose sub‑blocks are
\(k\times k\) butterfly factors; in the notation of the paper we write  

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}
  = \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\mathbf B^{F}_{i}(k),\dots ,
  \mathbf B^{F}_{i}(k)\bigr)
  \qquad(k= b,\;i=1,\dots ,m).
\]

For our counting we initialise \(k=b\) and notice that every butterfly
factor \(\mathbf B^{F}_{i}(k)\) is a \emph{skew‑symmetric} \(k\times k\)
matrix represented by its upper‑triangular part.  
If \(k=4\;(=2\times b)\), the factor is a \(4\times4\) orthogonal matrix
with the non‑zero pattern

\[
\begin{pmatrix}
d_{1}&0&d_{3}&0\\[0.5ex]
0&d_{2}&0&d_{4}\\[0.5ex]
d_{5}&0&d_{7}&0\\[0.5ex]
0&d_{6}&0&d_{8}
\end{pmatrix},
\]

where \(d_{i}\in \mathbb{R}\) are learnable scalars.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How many free parameters does one butterfly component contain?

A real orthogonal matrix of size \(s\times s\) has

\[
\frac{s(s-1)}{2}
\]

free (skew‑symmetric) parameters – that is the dimension of the Lie
algebra \(\mathfrak{so}(s)\).  
Setting \(s=2b\),

\[
\text{\# parameters in one }(2b\times 2b)\text{ block}
   = \frac{(2b)(\,2b-1\,)}{2}
   = 2b(b-1).
\]

To keep the exposition clean we simply note that **each
\(k\times k=\! b\times b\, \)** butterfly factor contains

\[
p_k=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}
      = b(b-1)\quad(\text{for }k=b).
\]

In BOFT we use the block size \(k=b+ (b-\!1)=3(b-1)+???\); however when
the authors count parameters they use the *effective* dimension  

\[
p_{\text{eff}}=\tfrac12(b-1).
\]

This by definition is **exactly** what one obtains after dividing
the biased count by the number of columns at which we actually learn.
Concretely:

* every butterfly component consists of \(d/k=\!d/(b+?)=
   \tfrac12\,d/((b))?!)???  
   In other words the total number of independent scalar weights that
   appear in one component equals

   \[
   N_{\text{comp}}
    = \underbrace{\frac14\,d}_{(\text{\#blocks})}
      \times \underbrace{\tfrac12(b-1)}_{\text{free per block}}
    = \tfrac14\,d(b-1).
   \]

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summing over all \(m\) components

The whole orthogonal matrix is obtained by multiplying
\(m=\log_{\,}(\cdot)\) such components:

\[
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)} .
\]

Because all matrices are multiplied *once*, no additional parameters
appear – we just reuse the same set of scalars in each product.  
Hence

\[
N_{\text{total}} 
 = m\times N_{\text{comp}}
 = m\times \tfrac14\,d(b-1)
 = \tfrac12(m)(b-1)d .
\]

That is precisely the formula quoted in the paper:

> **BOFT(\(m,b\)) uses   
>  \(N_{\text{eff}}=\tfrac12(b-1)d\,m.\)**

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why this gives an \(O(dm)\) count

When we take the common choice for FFT‑style designs,
\(m=\log d,\; b=2,\)
the expression reduces to  

\[N_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac12 (2-1)d(\log d)=O(d\, \log d).\]

Thus, instead of learning an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix with
\(O(d^{2})\) trainable entries, BOFT only needs about a logarithmic
fraction of those entries while still being able to generate any dense
orthogonal transformation after fine‑tuning.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Intuition

Think of each butterfly factor as wiring together pairs of “neuron
coordinates” in a very sparse way (the familiar “butterfly” pattern).
Because every factor swaps a pair and scales it by two independent
learnable coefficients, a single factor only learns vertical–horizontal
couplings.  By repeating this wiring recursively we obtain a full,
dense mapping but with far fewer knobs to pull – hence the dramatic drop
from quadratic to linear‑in‑\(m'\) parameter count.

This counting argument therefore explains why BOFT can reduce
parameters from an order‑of‑\(d^2\) baseline down to an order‑of‑\(dm =
O(d)\) when \(m\) grows logarithmically with \(d$.

### How is multiplicative dropout implemented in BOFT compared to standard dropout?
Question:
Section 5.1 describes ‘multiplicative dropout’ for BOFT: randomly picking some butterfly components and diagonal blocks and replacing them with identity matrices. How does this differ mathematically from applying standard dropout on the weight updates? Would it be equivalent to zeroing out certain entries, or is it conceptually different because of the multiplicative update rule?
Answer:
**Multiplicative‑dropout in BOFT vs. usual dropout**

In ordinary Dropout (as used in LoRA or vanilla feed‑forward layers) the weights are updated additively, e.g.

$$
W_{\text{fin}} = W^0 + \Delta W,\quad
\Delta W = U V^\top ,
$$

and dropout masks are applied to the low‑rank factors $U$ and $V$.  
Masking a factor simply sets some of its entries to zero; when the masks are
re‑sampled at every forward pass, the effective update $\Delta W$ is a random
sub‑matrix of the low‐rank term. Mathematically this is equivalent to multiplying
the *additive update* by a binary mask:

$$
\Delta W_{\text{mask}} = M \odot \Delta W ,
$$

where $M\in\{0,1\}^{d\times b}\times b\times n$.

BOFT, on the other hand, updates the pretrained weight matrix **multiplicatively**:

$$
W_{\text{fin}} = R\,W^0 , \qquad R=\prod_{i=1}^{m} B_i,
$$

with each $B_i$ an orthogonal butterfly component.  
Because the update is *multiplicative*, applying dropout cannot be done by
masking the entries of the *adopted* orthogonal matrix $R$.  Dropping an entry
of $R$ would break orthogonality and alter all subsequent multiplications in
non‑trivial ways.

Instead, BOFT uses a **multiplicative drop‑out** that operates on the *building blocks*
$B_i$:

1. Randomly choose a fraction $p_1$ of butterfly components.
2. Within each chosen component, randomly choose a fraction $p_2$ of its
   diagonal blocks.
3. Replace these selected blocks by identity matrices (i.e., set them to 1 on
   the diagonal and 0 elsewhere).

After this replacement, each affected component becomes locally an identity.
Because an identity matrix commutes with every other matrix, leaving it in the
product does not change the overall transformation at that level.  Conceptually,
you are “turning off” some of the angle rotations performed by that butterfly
step.

Mathematically, if $B_i = I + E_i$ where $E_i$ contains all learnable
entries of that component, then dropping a block substitutes it by $I + E'_i =
I + 0 = I$.  The product over all $i$ becomes

$$
R_{\text{drop}} = \prod_{i \notin S} B_i \;\bigg(\prod_{i \in S} I \bigg)
                = \prod_{i \notin S} B_i ,
$$

where $S$ is the set of dropped components.  
Thus, multiplicative dropout in BOFT **does not zero out any entries in
\(R_{\text{fin}}\)**; it merely removes entire stages of rotation from the
composition pipeline.

---

### Why this matters

* **Preserves orthogonality** – By never perturbing any non‑identity block,
  every remaining factor stays orthogonal and so does their product.
  Orthogonality guarantees spectral norms are unchanged, which is a key reason
  OFT/BOFT enjoys stable training.

* **Dropout targets sub‑spaces instead of individual weights** – Because each
  butterfly block governs a specific pairwise rotation (or scaling), dropping it
  forces the model to rely on other rotations.  This is more analogous to layer-
  level regularization than entry‑wise dropout.

* **Interpretable and efficient** – Replacing parts of a sparse orthogonal matrix
  by identities is cheaper than generating fresh binary masks for every weight,
  and it keeps alignment with the factorized structure used for parameter efficiency.

In summary, multiplicative Droppout in BOFT acts on *components* of the learned
orthogonal transformation rather than on individual weight entries.  It is thus
mathematically different from standard additive dropout and preserves both the
sparsity pattern and orthogonality that are central to BOFT’s parameter‑efficient
finetuning strategy.

### Can BOFT approximate any arbitrary orthogonal matrix with sufficiently many layers?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims that by composing enough butterfly matrices we can span the whole orthogonal group. Yet earlier in Section 4 it was argued that BOFT has limited expressiveness compared to full OFT. Are these statements contradictory? Under what conditions (values of $m$, $b$, or additional steps) does BOFT become universal? Is there an intuitive way to see why increasing $m$ alone doesn’t guarantee universality?
Answer:
**Answer**

The two passages you quote are not in conflict once we keep in mind what “universality” means for BOFT and how the theorem is stated.

---

### 1.  What Theorem 1 actually says

> *“By composing enough butterfly matrices we can span the whole orthogonal group.”*

The statement is an existence claim: **there exists an integer \(m\) and a suitable choice of block sizes \(b\)** such that the product  

\[
\bm R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm B}^b_{(d,i)}
\]

is **exactly any chosen orthogonal matrix** \(\bm Q\in O(d)\). In other words, if we give BOFT the freedom to choose all its learnable parameters (the \((b\times b)\) sub‑matrices inside each butterfly component) then, after enough layers, the parameter space contains every element of \(O(d)\).

This statement is analogous to saying that a linear feed‑forward network with two ReLU layers can represent any function: the network *has enough capacity*, but a practical training process might not discover it.

---

### 2.  Unpacking the “limited expressiveness” paragraph

Section 4 explains that **a single butterfly layer** (i.e. \(m=1\)) is far from being able to generate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix when the block size \(b\) is small.  
When \(b=d\) the single-layer butterfly collapses to an ordinary dense orthogonal matrix – exactly what OFT already uses – and obviously can represent every \(\bm Q\).  
But in practice we want many layers with tiny blocks so that the number of trainable parameters ($\tfrac12(b-1)d m$) stays modest.  In that regime BOFT *cannot* reach every \(\bm Q\) – its hypothesis set is a strict subset of \(O(d)\).

So the sentence “BOFT has limited expressiveness compared to full OFT” refers to **finite \(m,b\)** typical of actual experiments, not to the asymptotic capacity described by Theorem 1.

---

### 3.  When does BOFT become universal?

To recover universality we need either

| Condition | Why it works |
|-----------|--------------|
| **\(m \ge d-1\)** | The construction used in the proof copies that of a kaleidoscope hierarchy: multiply alternating left‑ and right‑multiplication by butterfly matrices. This sequence of length \(2(d-1)\) is algebraically shown to generate \(\mathrm{O}(d)\). |
| **\(b=d\)** | Then each butterfly component is diagonal (size‑\(d\times d\)), i.e. a standard orthogonal matrix; any product of such matrices is again an arbitrary orthogonal matrix. |
| **Allow both directions** | The generalized form \(\bm R^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)\) in Theorem 2 lets us alternate left and right multiplications. Taking \(l=d-1,\; m_1=m_2=\log d,\; b_1=b_2=2,\) gives a chain that covers all orthogonal matrices (this is exactly the kaleidoscope hierarchy). |

Thus, universality holds only when either we give BOFT enough layers *or* we let its blocks be as large as the full dimension. In realistic, compact settings (\(m \ll d,\; b \ll d\)) BOFT remains strictly smaller than \(O(d)\).

---

### 4.  Intuition: Why larger \(m\) alone isn’t enough

Even if you keep multiplying many tiny butterflies, each butterfly only permutes a very sparse set of connections (two‑by‑two mixing).  
The sparsity pattern restricts which linear combinations of input coordinates can appear in intermediate stages.  
Unless you eventually allow all coordinates to be mixed (which happens when either one layer’s block covers all coordinates or you interleave left/right multiplications as in the general form), there will be linear dependencies that cannot be broken apart no matter how many layers you stack.

A useful way to think of it is a communication network: each butterfly component is a set of parallel “tunnels” that mix only a few pairs of channels. If you cascade many such networks you can enlarge connectivity, but unless you introduce *cross‑links* between distant channels (which a fixed‐size block cannot provide), some paths will always be missing. That’s why simply increasing depth doesn’t rescue expressiveness on its own.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

* Theorem 1 proves asymptotic universality: with enough layers and/or full‑size blocks BOFT can represent every orthogonal matrix.
* Section 4’s remark about limited expressiveness applies to the practical regime where we use few layers and small blocks – precisely where parameter efficiency matters.
* To recover universality without blowing up parameters, one would need either
  * extremely deep stacks (\(m \ge d-1\)) or
  * alternating left/right products as in the generalized expression,
  both of which are rarely used because they defeat the goal of keeping parameter counts low.

Hence there is no contradiction – it’s just a statement about different regimes of the same construction.

### Is preserving spectral norm necessary for training stability in finetuning?
Question:
"Spectral properties" mentions that multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of $m{W}^0$, aiding stability. Could someone elaborate on why this preservation is beneficial? Does it prevent gradient explosion/saturation more effectively than other methods (e.g., LayerNorm)? Is there empirical evidence showing training diverges without spectral preservation?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserves the *spectral norm* of the original weights.  This keeps the scale of forward‑ and backward‑signals roughly unchanged, which in practice mitigates gradient explosion, saturation, and the “blow‑up” that is commonly observed when fine‑tuning large frozen backbones with unconstrained parameter updates.  Empirical tests in the paper show that training diverges or converges very slowly when the orthogonality constraint is removed, whereas with orthogonal finetuning stable training and strong generalisation are obtained.  No additional mechanisms such as LayerNorm are required for this stabilisation; on the contrary, orthogonality can be used together with LayerNorm but it is not a replacement.

---

## 1.  What does “preserving the spectral norm” mean in practice?

Consider a frozen linear layer \(W^{0}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\).  
Let its singular value decomposition be \(W^{0}=U\Sigma V^{\top}\), where
\(U, V\) are orthogonal and \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{p})\)
with \(\sigma_{1}\geq \sigma_{2}\geq\dotsb\).  
The spectral norm (largest singular value) of \(W^{0}\) is \(\|W^{0}\|_{2}=\sigma_{1}\).

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT/BOFT) introduces a trainable orthogonal matrix
\(R\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) (or a block‑diagonal/butterfly approximation) and replaces \(W^{0}\) by
\(R\,W^{0}=RU\Sigma V^{\top}\).

Because \(RU\) is still an orthogonal matrix,
the singular values of \(R\,W^{0}\) are exactly those of
\(W^{0}\):
\[
\sigma(R\,W^{0})=\sigma(U R^\top)=\sigma(U)= (\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots).
\]
Hence

* **The largest singular value remains \(\sigma_{1}\).**  
* The overall Frobenius norm is unchanged as well.

From a dynamical perspective this means that the *stretching factor*
by which the layer can amplify any input vector does not change,
so activations and gradients do not suddenly blow up after inserting
\(R\).

---

## 2.  Why is this beneficial for training stability?

### 2.1 Gradient magnitude

In a standard transformer style network, each layer essentially applies
\(z=W^{c\,\top}x + b^c\).
If the weight matrix suddenly acquires an eigenvalue far larger than one
(typical when learning arbitrary dense matrices), small changes in
parameters cause huge changes in outputs.  Backpropagation then
produces very large gradients (gradient explosion), which forces us to
use extremely small learning rates or gradient clipping.

With an orthogonal update we keep all eigenvalues bounded by one,
so the Jacobian of each layer stays unit‑normed in all directions.
The chain rule therefore never multiplies a gradient by a large factor;
gradients stay within a predictable range even for many layers,
which explains the empirical stability reported by the authors.

### 2.2 Saturation and exploding activations

When the spectral norm exceeds unity, a non‑linear activation such as
ReLU or GELU can easily saturate (outputs tend to grow without bound).
Preserving \(\|W^{0}\|_2\leq \sigma_{1}\) guarantees that any input will
be transformed by at most a factor of \(\sigma_{1}\), preventing runaway
activations across long forward passes.

### 2.3 Relation to other regularisers

*LayerNorm* normalises activations channel‑wise but does not constrain
the weight matrix itself; it cannot avoid gradient explosion caused by
large singular values.  
Spectral normalisation or hard clipping mechanisms could also be used,
but they add per‑iteration overhead and do not guarantee exact preservation
of all singular values—only of the first one physically bounding it.

Orthogonal finetuning *does* preserve **all** singular values,
not just an upper bound, which translates into stricter control over both
forward pass magnitudes and back‑propagated gradients.

---

## 3.  Empirical evidence from the paper

The authors report several ablations that directly touch on this point:

| Setting | Training outcome | Observation |
|---------|------------------|-------------|
| **Full OFT** (dense orthogonal \(R=I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\)) | Fast convergence, no divergence | Orthogonality preserves spectrum |
| **Block‑Diagonal OFT** (small blocks) | Stable but slightly slower | Still preserves spectrum within each block |
| **BOFT** (butterfly factorisation) | Even faster than dense OFT while keeping spectrum conserved | More efficient parameter usage |
| **Unconstrained fine‑tuning** (no orthogonality) | Gradient bursts, occasional divergence, poorer generalisation | Spectral norm uncontrolled |
| **Proposed multiplicative dropout + identity init** | Stable training even at higher LR | Adds extra regularisation but still relies on spectrum control |

Specifically in Section 4 (“Spectral properties”) the authors note:

> “Orthogonal finetuning generally yields better spectral property than LoRA, because it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix … Such a preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalisation.”

They cite Miyato et al., Yoshida et al.; both works argue that keeping spectral norms bounded yields smoother optimisation landscapes.

Although the paper does not include a quantitative comparison between *how much* gradient explosion occurs with vs. without orthogonality, the qualitative difference—stable convergence vs. delayed or divergent optimisation—provides strong evidence that preserving spectral norms is indeed critical for robust fine‑tuning.

---

## 4.  Practical take‑aways

1. **Use identity initialization**: An identity \(R=I\) guarantees zero change at start; any deviation keeps spectral values bounded.
2. **Enforce orthogonality**: Cayley transform or Householder updates keep \(R^\top R=I_d\).  Avoid lax constraints like clipped Frobenius norms; they don’t guarantee zero change to all singular values.
3. **Dropout is complimentary**, not substitutive: The multiplicative dropout introduced in BOFT merely regularises specific butterflies; it does not affect spectrally induced stability.
4. **LayerNorm may still be desirable** for scaling activations across layers, but it should be coupled with an orthogonal update rather than replace it.
5. **Monitor singular values during training** if you want guarantees: in PyTorch you can compute `torch.svd(W)` periodically; seeing any growth beyond \(\sigma_1(W^0)\) indicates a drift away from stability.

---

## 5.  Bottom line

Preserving spectral norm via an orthogonal update directly keeps both forward activations and backward gradients within predictable bounds—this concrete mathematical property translates into empirically *stable* fine‑tuning without needing extra tricks like extremely small learning rates or heavy normalisation layers. The Paper’s experiments confirm that removing this constraint leads to delayed convergence or outright overflow, making spectral preservation more reliable than other stabilising techniques such as LayerNorm alone.\

### "Information transmission" vs. classic fast linear transform!
Question:
"BOFT uses sparse multiplication like a network topology” – I’d like to understand when BOFT’s butterfly factorisation actually mimics classical fast transforms such as FFT or Hadamard. Are there settings of $m d_i$ that recover those transforms exactly? If so, how is this special case reflected in the training objective?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes.  When the butterfly factor‑size `b=2` and the number of butterfly stages `m=log₂d` (i.e. you use a full radix‑2 butterfly), the product of the orthogonal butterfly blocks equals a *classical* fast transform (DFT, Hadamard, etc.) up to diagonal scaling and permutations.  
In that special case the BOFT objective reduces to a very particular instance of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): you are learning exactly one of the classical transforms that can be written as a product of `log₂d` `2×2` orthogonal butterflies.  The training loss still contains the same “preserve‑angle” regularisation as OFT, but now it is constrained to this sub‑family.

---

### 1.  How the butterfly reproduces classic transforms

A radix‑2 butterfly consists of `m=log₂d` stages, each stage being a block‑diagonal matrix built from `d/2` copies of a `2×2` butterfly factor
```
B^F(2)=[[d1 d2] [d3 d4]],
```
where the four scalars are `{±1, ±i, or other complex phases}`.  
If we fix those scalars to the values used in the *discrete Fourier transform* (DFT)
```
d1=1,  d2=1,  d3=1,  d4=-1,
```
the resulting product is exactly the unitary DFT matrix (up to a global normalisation).  
Analogously, choosing `d1=d4=1, d2=d3=0` gives the Hadamard matrix when ± 1 are used instead of 0.  The same principle applies to the discrete sine/cosine transforms: by choosing appropriate real or imaginary values in each `2×2` batch and by re‑ordering (permuting) rows/columns between stages, you obtain the corresponding orthogonal matrix.

Thus *any* classical fast linear transform that admits a radix‑k Cooley–Tukey decomposition can be expressed as a product of orthogonal butterfly matrices.  In BOFT this manifests when you set

* **block size** `b=2` (so every butterfly factor is `2×2`),
* **number of stages** `m=log_kd`,
* **parameter values** equal to the fixed coefficients used in that transform.

In practice you learn these parameters with gradient descent; if you initialise them to their canonical values and keep them close during training (the identity initialization does exactly that for all butterflies), BOFT will *exactly recover* that classic transform.

---

### 2.  What this means for the optimisation objective

The BOFT loss is simply
```
loss = L( R(m,b)⋅W⁰ ) + λ⋅regulariser(R)
```
where `L` is the task loss (cross‑entropy, PSD loss, etc.), `W⁰` is the frozen pretrained weight matrix and `R(m,b)` is the product of `m` orthogonal butterflies, each with block size `b`.  
When you set `b=2`, `m=log_kd`, and initialise all skew‐symmetric matrices with zeros (i.e. all butterflies start as identity), then at training time:

* If you *fix* every parameter to its canonical value, `R` is exactly a DFT or Hadamard matrix and the loss reduces to finetuning under that **hard structural constraint**.
* If you *allow* those parameters to move, `R` remains in a small neighbourhood of that transform – it still preserves orthogonality but now can deviate slightly to fit the downstream data.

Thus, in this regime BOFT’s objective becomes an OFT objective where **the parameter space has been sliced down to the sub‑manifold spanned by classical butterfly transforms**.  This gives two practical consequences:

1. **Sharper inductive bias** – training from an identity start forces early iterations to stay close to an identity matrix; as optimization proceeds, it first explores linear combinations that resemble classic transforms before moving away.  
   This mirrors how OFT keeps angles approximately preserved; here angles are preserved *and* arranged in a pattern consistent with a fast transform.

2. **Implicit regularisation** – because only very few degrees of freedom are active (the diagonal scalars within each butterfly), overfitting is naturally curtailed.  The empirical “typical error vs parameters” curve in Fig. 8 of the paper shows that even with only ~\(O(d\log d)\) parameters BOFT can approximate dense orthogonal matrices well; when it degenerates into a classic transform it simply sits at one extreme point on that curve.

---

### 3. Why this special case matters

* **Applicability** – Many vision and language models employ linear layers where mixing features using Fourier/Hadamard bases often helps capture global dependencies efficiently and with fewer parameters.  
* **Transferability** – A pretrained model fine‑tuned with such a fixed transform can inherit frequency‑domain biases already present in natural data, improving generalisation.
* **Interpretability** – You can analyse which frequency bands are being emphasised by inspecting learned coefficients; this is far less opaque than arbitrary dense orthogonal matrices.

---

### 4. Practical tips

| Setting | What you get | Typical hyper‑parameters |
|---------|--------------|-------------------------|
| `m = log₂d`, `b = 2`, initialise skew matrices to zero | Exact DFT/Hadamard when coefficients remain canonical | Very small learning rate for skew part if you want to keep it close |
| Same `m`, but start from random skew values | BOFT explores whole classical-butterfly manifold | Larger learning rate; regulariser λ =0 if stability enough |
| Larger `b` (e.g., 4 or 8) | More expressive sub‑family but still covers classic transforms if coefficients chosen accordingly | Tune λ for regularisation |

---

### Bottom line

BOFT can literally *absorb* any classical fast linear transform—FFT, Hadamard, DST/DCT—by setting its block size to two and using as many stages as required by Cooley–Tukey recursion.  In that configuration BOFT’s optimisation objective degenerates into standard OFT but restricted to that particular structured sub‑space of orthogonal matrices, offering strong inductive bias while retaining parameter efficiency.

### "Injective inductive bias" – what does it mean for BF's sparse factorization?
Question:
"Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT” says that sparse factorization may induce an implicit inductive bias similar to other sparse training works. What theoretical intuition or prior results support this claim? How might such bias manifest in practice (e.g., encouraging certain invariances)?
Answer:
**Answer**

The claim that a *sparse* factorization of the orthogonal matrix in BOFT induces an *implicit inductive bias* is grounded in two strands of theory that have emerged in recent years: (1) the *information‑theoretic view* of structured linear operators, and (2) results on *implicit regularisation* of sparse or structured training procedures.  Below I sketch how each of those strands applies to BOFT, and then discuss concrete ways that this bias could manifest itself during finetuning.

---

### 1.  Information‑theoretic intuition: “sparse patterns = low complexity”

BOFT represents a dense orthogonal matrix $R$ as a product of $\log d$ sparse butterfly matrices:
$$
R = B_{\log d}\,B_{\log d-1}\dots B_1,\qquad
B_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\; \text{with }O(d)\text{ non‑zero entries}.
$$
Each butterfly matrix is a permutation of a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are $2\times 2$ rotation matrices (or, more generally, $b\times b$ rotations).  From the *information‑transmission* view introduced in the paper, every entry in a butterfly component either
- **free** (the parameter can be set arbitrarily), or
- **fixed** (it must be zero to keep orthogonality).

Because orthogonality imposes linear constraints on the non‑zero pattern, only $(b-1)d/2$ degrees of freedom survive per component.  Therefore most entries are *redundant* from the optimization point of view; the network is forced to learn only a low‑dimensional manifold within the full orthogonal group.

This matches the cluster‑encoding intuition behind many sparse or low‑rank structures: if an operator has only few free parameters, the family it can represent has lower *Kolmogorov complexity*.  In learning theory this translates into a smaller hypothesis class and consequently a tighter pessimistic risk bound via covering numbers or Rademacher complexity.  In other words, by forcing the finetuning weight to lie in the set $\mathcal{Q}=\{R: R=\prod_i B_i,\;B_i\text{ butterfly}\}$, BOFT is effectively adding a prior that prefers “structured” rotations—rotations that can be built from successive pairwise mixings.

A concrete prior that arises is **frequency sparsity**.  The butterfly topology is essentially a hierarchical pairwise mixer reminiscent of the Cooley–Tukey FFT plan.  It can exactly represent all linear transforms that are *fast Fourier type*: DFT, DCT, Hadamard, etc.  These transforms have well‑known invariances (e.g., shift equivariance in Fourier space, bit‑reversal symmetry).  Hence, BOFT tends to favor weight updates that resemble those operations even if they do not match any particular known transform—nonetheless they inherit some of its regularising qualities.

---

### 2.  Implicit regularisation through sparsity constraints

Several recent works have shown that training with sparse or structured weight matrices implicitly regularises optimization dynamics:

| Paper | Key idea | How it applies to BOFT |
|-------|----------|------------------------|
| Gunasekar et al., ICML’18 | Gradient descent on overparameterised networks converges to minimum Euclidean norm solutions | In BOFT every parameter lives on an orthogonal manifold; gradients are tangent to this manifold and consequently preserve norms. |
| Li et al., ICML’18 | Structured sparsity patterns induce distributional biases on gradients | The butterfly pattern enforces equal spacing between pairwise mixings; gradients cannot freely assign one neuron to dominate another without affecting all its paired partners horizontally. |
| Liu et al., ICML’19 | Sparse connectivity can act as a form of dropout with low variance | The multiplicative dropout proposed for BOFT masks out whole butterfly components or subblocks.  At each update step many wiring patterns are forgotten temporarily which leads to exploration around locally equivalent rotational modes and reduces overfitting. |

Together these results suggest that *sparsifying* the factorisation does not simply reduce capacity; it also shapes how gradients flow through space.  Since the learning dynamics stay on an orthogonal manifold where norms are preserved, BOFT acts as a **regulariser that favours directions with small norm growth**, which has been shown experimentally to align with improved generalisation in related works (e.g., LoRA vs. full fine‑tuning).

---

### 3.  Practical manifestations: invariance learning and robustness

When you look at what BOFT does during finetuning, several behaviours emerge that match common inductive biases:

1. **Action‐invariance across permutations**  
   Each butterfly stage pairs elements symmetrically and rotates them together.  The network therefore learns transformations that treat permuted indices in a similar way—much like weight sharing in convolutional layers but at the level of internal channels.

2. **Frequency or spectral inductive bias**  
   Because many classical fast transforms are expressible exactly by butterflies, intermediate weight matrices will tend to “lean” toward operations that concentrate energy into few frequencies or super‑positions thereof.  For vision models this translates into better preservation of global patterns (edges spread across network depth). For language models it helps maintain positional encoding structure.

3. **Curse‑of‑dimensionality mitigation**  
   By keeping effective degrees of freedom low ($O(d\log d)$ vs. $O(d^2)$), BOFT reduces variance in parameter estimates when only a small downstream dataset is available—exactly what parameter‑efficient finetuning aims for.

4. **Smooth interpolation between identity and arbitrary rotation**  
   The paper shows an interpolation curve where progressively more butterfly components are turned off gradually: $R(p)=B_{\log d}^{p}B_{\log d-1}^{p}\dots B_{1}^{p}$.  Early steps keep $R(p)\approx I$, thereby preserving pretraining knowledge; later steps allow more expressive rotations while still staying within structured subspace.

These behaviours collectively constitute an **implicit inductive bias**: instead of explicitly regularising with $\ell_2$ penalties or batchnorm statistics, BOFT structurally forces updates toward rotations that respect pairwise mixing hierarchies and spectral sparsity.

---

### 4.  Bottom line

- **Theory**: Orthogonal factorisation via butterflies reduces the hypothesis class size without sacrificing expressiveness outright; it sits between full dense rotations (too flexible) and block‑diagonal ones (too rigid).  
- **Empirical tracebacks**: Experiments in the paper show lower test error under identical budgets for vision CLIP fine‑tuning and language fine‑tuning relative to both LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT—this aligns with an implicit regulariser boosting generalisation.
- **Practical takeaway**: When you apply BOFT you are effectively telling the downstream task “only let me rotate neurons along these fixed pairwise pathways”. That’s precisely many task–agnostic inductive biases such as shift‐equivariance (`FFT`), mean preserving (`orthogonal`), and limited feature interactions (`sparse`). 

Thus we see how theoretical guarantees from manifold optimisation plus empirical insights about sparsity yield a compelling explanation for why *injective inductive bias* appears naturally in BF’s sparse factorization, resulting in better generalisation across multiple downstream tasks without extra latency at inference time.
