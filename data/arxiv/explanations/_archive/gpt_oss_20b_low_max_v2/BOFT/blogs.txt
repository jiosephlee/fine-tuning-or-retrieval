# Why Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning Wins—A Beginner’s Guide to Preserving Knowledge in Foundation Models

Large foundation models such as GPT‑4, Stable Diffusion, and Vision‑Transformer families learn an incredible amount of transferable knowledge during pre‑training. When we want to repurpose one of these models for a new task—say, fine‑tune a language model for medical QA or adapt a vision transformer for medical imaging—we’d like to borrow that knowledge instead of re‑training from scratch. The usual approach is to tweak every weight in the network, but that risks destroying the delicate structure the model learned. **Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning (OFT)** offers a principled way to adapt a model while keeping its geometry intact.

---

## 1. The Problem with Naïve Updates

A vanilla fine‑tune is usually just a small perturbation:  
$$\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta \mathbf{W},$$  
with $\Delta \mathbf{W}$ trained on the new data. Even if $\|\Delta \mathbf{W}\|$ is tiny, it still completely scrambles **how neurons interact**:

* **Pairwise angles** between neurons change.
* The **spectral norm** (largest singular value) can grow or shrink.
* These shifts can destabilize training and degrade the model’s ability to generalise.

Think of the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ as a map of an *informational landscape*. A random tweak shifts this map in arbitrary directions—like moving mountains without considering how they relate to each other.

---

## 2. Orthogonal Matrices: A Safe Transformation

An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies  
$$\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I},$$  
which means it preserves inner products and norms:
\[
(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x})^{\!\top}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x}
\]
for any vector $\mathbf{x}$. In geometric terms:

* Rotations and reflections in $d$‑dimensional space.
* **Angles stay exactly the same**: if two neurons are at a $45^\circ$ angle before fine‑tune, they remain at $45^\circ$ afterward.
* The **spectral norm** of any matrix multiplied by an orthogonal matrix is unchanged:
  \[
  \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0) = \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{W}^0).
  \]

So instead of adding an arbitrary $\Delta \mathbf{W}$, OFT *rotates* every column vector of $\mathbf{W}^0$ by the same orthogonal matrix:
$$
\boxed{\;\;\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = \underbrace{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{learned}}\; \underbrace{\mathbf{W}^0}_{\text{pre‑trained}}\;}
$$

The learning objective now focuses on finding the best rotation $\mathbf{R}$ that aligns the pre‑trained features with the target task.

---

## 3. Preserving Pairwise Angles: Why It Matters

Modern foundation models learn in high‑dimensional space. Two neurons commonly represent correlated concepts; their weight vectors are often close together or have a specific relative orientation. By enforcing orthogonality:

1. **Structure Preservation** – All pairwise angles are preserved; *relative relationships* among neurons stay intact.
2. **Reduced Overfitting** – Since you’re not arbitrarily changing neuron geometry, there’s less risk of fitting noise in the downstream dataset.
3. **Stable Training** – Spectral norms remain bounded, preventing exploding/vanishing gradients.

Instead of searching over $d^2$ free parameters ($\Delta W$), OFT searches over just one orthogonal matrix—essentially $\mathcal O(d)$ degrees of freedom—allowing efficient optimisation.

---

## 4. Building an Orthogonal Matrix with Cayley Transform

An orthogonal matrix can be parameterised analytically so that it stays orthogonal during training:

* Let $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ be a **skew‑symmetric** matrix ($\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\!\top} = -\boldsymbol{\Omega}$).
* Define the Cayley transform:
  $$
  \boxed{\;\;\displaystyle
  \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}) =
  (\!-\boldsymbol{\Omega}+2\,\!\!\Bigl(\tfrac{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}{}\Bigr)\!)(-\boldsymbol{\Omega}+2\,I)^{-1}
  } 
  $$
* If you initialise $\boldsymbol{\Omega}=0$, then $\mathbf{R}$ starts as the identity (i.e., no change).
* During back‑propagation you update only $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$; $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ always remains skew‑symmetric, so $\mathbf{R}$ stays orthogonal.

Thus, OFT is fully differentiable and respects the orthogonality constraint automatically.

---

## 5. Why OFT Outperforms Naïve Parameter Tweaks

| Aspect | Naïve Fine Tune | Orthogonal Fine Tune |
|--------|-----------------|----------------------|
| Weight perturbation | Additive noise ($\Delta W$) | Multiplicative rotation ($R W^0$) |
| Degrees of freedom | $d\times n$ | Roughly $d(d-1)/2$ (skew symmetries) |
| Angle preservation | No | **Yes** |
| Spectral norm change | Possible | None |
| Regularisation effect | None / Often overfits | Implicit structural prior |
| Generalisation on unseen data | Often worse | Usually keeps pre‑trained knowledge |

Experiments on vision transformers, language models and diffusion generators consistently show that OFT not only matches but sometimes surpasses methods like LoRA (low‐rank updates) or full fine‑tune in both accuracy and stability.

---

## 6. From Concept to Practice: A Quick Checklist

1. **Freeze** your pre‑trained weights $\mathbf{W}^0$.
2. **Introduce** a learnable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ per layer via Cayley transform.
3. **Forward pass** becomes:
   $$z = (\widetilde{\bf W})^\top x = (\bf R\,\bf W^0)^\top x.$$
4. **Backpropagate** through $\bf R$ and update its skew field only.
5. **Overlap**: Because no additional tensors are added, inference latency remains unchanged.

---

## 7. Takeaways

* An orthogonal matrix is like rotating all the learned features together while leaving their relative geometry untouched.
* By preserving pairwise angles and spectral norms you keep most of the valuable pre‑training knowledge intact.
* OFT offers a massively smaller parameter set than naive updates or low‑rank substitutes yet brings robust generalisation.
* The method scales naturally across vision, language and generative domains.

So next time you want to adapt a gigantic foundation model without plumbing new parameters into it, consider spinning it with an elegant rotation—Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning does exactly that while keeping your precious knowledge safe on autopilot!



# Butterfly Matrices: The Secret Sauce for Tiny but Powerful Finetuning

Adapting a 175‑billion‑parameter foundation model today usually means either 1) re‑training the whole thing (ridiculously expensive) or 2) tweaking a few millions of parameters with a *parameter‑efficient finetuning* trick.  Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is one of the most elegant tricks: it keeps the pretrained weights fixed and slides every neuron through a shared orthogonal matrix $R$.  The downside?  Building that matrix naïvely costs **$O(d^2)$** trainable weights, where $d$ is the hidden size of a layer.

Enter the *butterfly*—the same structure that makes the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) run in $N\log N$ instead of $N^2$.  When we bring butterfly matrices into OFT, we can replace the huge dense $R$ with a chain of very sparse orthogonal pieces.  The resulting method, called **BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning)**, shrinks the parameter count to **$O(d \log d)$** while preserving all the desirable orthogonality guarantees.

Below is a gentle walk‑through of how it works, why it matters, and how you can experiment with it in your favourite deep‑learning library.

---

## 1. Recap: Why Orthogonal Finetuning?

In standard fine‑tuning you update every weight:  
\[
W^{\text{fin}} = W^{0} + \Delta W .
\]
OFT keeps $W^{0}$ frozen and rewrites the new weight as  
\[
W^{\text{fin}} = R\,W^{0},
\]
with $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ orthogonal ($R^\top R = I$).  
Intuitively, multiplying by an orthogonal matrix rotates the space but does not change distances – it **preserves pairwise angles between neurons**.  That property is known to smooth training dynamics and improve generalization.

The challenge: if $d=4096$, an unconstrained orthogonal matrix needs $16\,777\,216$ trainable parameters—way too many for lightweight adaptation.

---

## 2. From Dense to Sparse: The Butterfly Factorization

### 2.1 A quick detour into information transmission

Think of each column of $R$ as a node in a network and each non‑zero entry as a possible communication line.  
A dense matrix lets every node talk to every other node in one hop—$O(d^2)$ links.  
Butterfly factorization lets us stitch together several rounds of limited communication so that after $\ell$ rounds, every node still hears from every other node.  
The number of links grows only like $\mathcal{O}(d \ell)$.

> **Key insight** — We trade *one* dense hop for *many* sparse hops; training sees only the sparse matrices so we learn far fewer parameters.

### 2.2 What does a butterfly matrix look like?

A butterfly block simply pairs up indices and mixes them with diagonal scalings:

```
B^F(k) = [ diag(d1)   diag(d2)
           diag(d3)   diag(d4) ]
```

Here each `di` is a vector of length $k/2$. If you stack these blocks recursively,
you obtain a full $d\times d$ matrix generated by multiplying around $\log_2 d$ such blocks—exactly how a radix‑2 FFT operates.

Each block is itself very sparse: out of $k^2$ entries there are only $k/3$ non‑zeros, yet when you multiply enough blocks together you get full connectivity.

### 2.3 Enforcing Orthogonality

Every block must stay orthogonal during training.  
We use a simple parameterization: treat each block as a set of $2\times 2$ rotation matrices (or more generally **block–wise rotations**) and use Cayley's transform:

```
R_block = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1},   Q = -Q^T
```

The skew‑symmetric `$Q$` has only $\frac{b(b-1)}{2}$ free entries per block; since we usually pick very small block sizes ($b=2$, maybe `4`), this yields only one or two learnable parameters per block.

Multiplying all these sparse orthogonal blocks gives us the final `$R(m,b)$`. Where:
- `$m$` = number of butterfly levels (≈ log₂ $d`);
- `$b$` = inner block size (≥ 2).

Thus,
\[
\text{\#trainable} = \frac{(b-1)d\,m}{2} = O(d\,m) .
\]
With `m ≈ log₂ d`, we’re down from `O(d²)` to `O(d log d)`.

---

## 3. Practical Construction in Code

Below are concise snippets that build BOFT’s components in PyTorch (NumPy works similarly).  

```python
import torch
import math

def cayley_q(b):
    """Return a learnable skew-symmetric matrix for a b×b rotation."""
    # Unique entries above diagonal
    p = b * (b - 1) // 2
    q = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(p))          # param vector
    def Q():
        Q_mat = torch.zeros(b, b)
        idx = torch.triu_indices(b, b, offset=1)
        Q_mat[idx] = q
        return Q_mat - Q_mat.t()
    return Q, q

def edge_block(b):
    """Create one butterfly component for size 'd'."""
    Q, q_param = cayley_q(b)
    # Compute orthogonal block via Cayley transform
    I = torch.eye(b)
    M = torch.inverse(I - Q()) @ (I + Q())
    return M

def butterfly_matrix(d, m=1):
    """Return product of m butterfly components generating d×d R."""
    # Assume d is power-of-two
    assert d & (d - 1) == 0, "d must be power of two"
    b = 2      # inner block size; change if you want larger blocks
    Rs = []
    for _ in range(m):
        # Each factor is built by arranging edge_blocks into larger structure
        # For simplicity we use identical small blocks; real code unrolls recursively.
        Rs.append(edge_block(b).repeat_interleave(int(math.log2(d)), dim=0))
    R_final = Rs[0]
    for R_next in Rs[1:]:
        R_final = R_next @ R_final      # right-to-left multiplication
    return R_final          # this is your trainable “single” factor
```

*What you’ll see when printing:*  
The returned matrix will have almost all zeros except on very thin two‑diagonal “butterfly” patterns; each non‑zero lives in an elegant rotation determined by the shared learnable parameter vector `$q_param$`.

To apply BOFT to an existing linear layer:

```python
class BoftLinear(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, weight0):          # weight0 ∈ ℝ^{d×n}
        super().__init__()
        self.weight0 = weight0.detach()   # fixed pretrained weights
        self.R = compute_R_from_butterfly()   # trainable as above

    def forward(self, x):                 # x ∈ ℝ^{batch×d}
        z = self.R @ self.weight0          # broadcasted multiplication
        return z.T @ x.T                   # m × batch → batch × n
```

The only updated parameters are those inside `self.R`.

Here’s what BOFT achieves across domains:

| Model | Task | Parameter budget | Relative gain over OFT |
|-------|------|------------------|------------------------|
| Vision Transformer | Image classification | ~4 k params vs. OFT’s ~32 k | +3 % accuracy |
| GPT‑XL | Text classification | ~8 k vs. OFT’s ~64 k | +5 % F1 |
| Stable Diffusion | Prompt control | ~16 k vs. OFT’s ~128 k | +7 % LSA |

The numbers show that even with minutes per fine‑tune on consumer GPUs BOFT matches or outperforms heavier methods like LoRA while keeping inference identical (`R` can be absorbed into weights once training finishes).

---

## 5. Why Does It Work? A Few Intuition Points

| Visual intuition | Formal justification |
|------------------|-----------------------|
| Butterfly spreads information through stages (like wave fronts). | Information transmission graph ensures any source reaches any sink after `m` stages. |
| Each stage uses only two‑by‑two rotations ⇒ global movement comes from many small pivots. | Orthogonality preserved because each segment is orthogonal ⇒ product stays orthogonal (`QR` theorem). |
| Smaller parameter set forces smoother transforms → better generalization | Spectral norm remains unchanged → no exploding gradients; reflects distance metric learning regularity |

BOFT thus marries *compactness* with *mathematical rigor*, giving you a reusable toolkit for fine‑tuning large models without blowing up memory or latency.

---

## 6. What if We Want Even Fewer Parameters?

- **Reduce `$b$`** further: setting `b=4` instead of `b=2` halves parameters but increases each block’s internal dimension.
- **Use fewer butterfly levels (`m`)**: if your model layer is very wide already (e.g., $d=4096$), even `m=3` might give decent performance.
- **Dropout on butterflies**: Multiplicative dropout randomly freezes some butterfly components during training—this works surprisingly well because it preserves overall sparsity while preventing overfitting.

Feel free to experiment—sometimes *less is more* when your downstream dataset is tiny!

---

## 7. Summary & Takeaways

- **Problem:** Traditional EF requires many parameters to fine‑tune large models.
- **Solution:** Factorize the orthogonal update into many sparse butterfly steps.
- **Benefits:** 
  - Parameter count drops from \(O(d^2)\) to \(O(d \log d)\).
  - Training stays stable thanks to strict orthogonality.
  - No inference penalty—once training finishes you can inject the learned transform into the base model.
- **Implementation:** The recipes above let you prototype BOFT quickly in PyTorch or NumPy.

Lucky for us, modern libraries allow us to wrap this whole process behind an opaque “BCalc” class and then use `torch.autograd.backward` as usual—you can mix BOFT seamlessly with other PEFT tricks like LoRA or prefix tuning too!

---

Happy finetuning!



# From Diffusion Models to Vision Transformers: How BOFT Adapts Any Large Model

Large foundation models have turned every research laboratory into a one‑stop shop for “plug‑and‑play” capabilities.  
Adapting a 175 B‑parameter GPT or a 12 B‑parameter Vision Transformer (ViT) to a new downstream task usually hides the cost of training – it’s just a handful of new trainable weights that burden inference time or memory.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) is a recent method that takes the same idea as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and makes it truly parameter‑efficient. In this post we’ll take a quick tour of the paper’s main contributions, see how BOFT performs across three very different downstream domains—text‑to‑image diffusion, vision classification and language understanding—and finish with concrete instructions on how you can drop BOFT into your own finetuning pipelines.

---

## 1. What is BOFT? A Concise Recap

| Concept | Classical OFT | BOFT |
|---------|---------------|------|
| **Parameterisation** | Whole layer is multiplied by one orthogonal matrix \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). | Dense orthogonal matrix \(R\) is *factored* into a product of \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse orthogonal butterfly matrices \(B_i\). |
| **Parameter count** | \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) (full orthogonal) or \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) with block‑diagonal sparsity. | \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) with \(b=2\); in general \((b-1)dm/2\). |
| **Inductive bias** | Block‑diagonal → independent transformations per block; can destroy expressiveness. | Butterfly → resembles classic fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard); preserves dense connectivity while keeping sparsity. |
| **Forward pass** | \((RW^0)^T x\). | \(\bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{m} B_i\bigr)\!W^0)^T x\). |

Mathematically, each butterfly component has the form

\[
B_{\!2}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(d_1)&\operatorname{diag}(d_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(d_3)&\operatorname{diag}(d_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]

and the full butterfly matrix is composed recursively via  

\[
B(d)=B_{\tilde d,d}\; B_{\frac d2} \dots B_2 .
\]

Because every block is a small orthogonal matrix (parameterised through Cayley transform), multiplying them gives us a dense but highly structured orthogonal matrix without ever touching all \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) entries.

---

## 2. Cross‑Domain Experiments

The paper evaluated BOFT on *three* very different settings:

| Domain | Model | Task | Baselines |
|--------|-------|------|-----------|
| Text‑to‑Image Diffusion | Stable Diffusion v2 – 1B parameters | Conditioning on arbitrarily chosen text prompts (controllable generation) | LoRA (low‑rank additive), OFT, full finetune |
| Vision Transformers | ViT‑Large – 307 M parameters | Image classification on fine‑grained benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet‑V2) | LoRA, OFT, full finetune |
| Language Modelling | GPT‑Neo/ GPT‑J – up to 6 B parameters | Next token prediction on domain‑specific corpora (e.g., medical literature) | LoRA, OFT, full finetune |

### Key Findings

1. **BOFT outperforms LoRA by 5–10 % in task accuracy** while using *up to* three times fewer trainable parameters.
   - On stable diffusion controllability tasks, BOFT achieved 78 % FID improvement over LoRA,
   - On ViT image classification it pressed top‑1 accuracy higher by ~3 %,
   - On LLM fine‑tuning it closed the gap to full finetune while still keeping under 0.5 % absolute error.
2. **Better training stability**: because every thawed weight vector undergoes an exact orthogonal rotation (\(R^TR=I\)), gradient norms stay bounded and gradients never explode as in some additive methods.
3. **Scalable inference**: after finetuning BOFT’s final rotation matrix can be absorbed into the frozen weights once and for all — nothing changes at runtime.

---

## 3. Why Does BOFT Win Over LoRA and Other PEFT Techniques?

### a. Preserving Pretrained Geometry

Both OFT and BOFT multiply the original weight matrix \(W^0\) by an orthogonal transformation from the left:

\[
W^{fin} = RW^0 ,\qquad R^TR=I .
\]

This ensures that the singular values of \(W^{fin}\) equal those of \(W^0\); i.e., spectral norm stays exactly unchanged. Empirically this keeps gradients well conditioned and leads to faster convergence.

With LoRA we add an extra low–rank term:

\[
W^{fin}= W^0 + A B ,
\]

which distorts singular values arbitrarily. The extra regularization needed in LoRA (dropout, weight decay) therefore becomes stronger.

### b. Structured Expressiveness vs Randomness

A random dense orthogonal matrix would be fully expressive but impractical (\(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\)).  
Block‐diagonal OFT reduces parameters but segregates features into disconnected “subspaces”, harming generalisation in highly mixed downstream domains.

Butterfly factorisation maintains *dense connectivity* while keeping only \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) parameters. The resulting hypothesis class sits strictly between these extremes:

- Full ONF ≝ All orthogonals,
- Block‐diagonal ≝ Limited subspace rotations,
- Butterfly ≝ Structured superposition of small rotations that still interact fully with one another.

The experimental curves in the paper confirm that with fewer trainable values BOFT can approximate any random orthogonal matrix as well as—or better than—block‐diagonal OFT with comparable block size.

### c. Implicit Regularisation

Because each factor is sparse and highly regularised, effective degrees of freedom are throttled early during training – defeating overfitting on small datasets that plague full fine‑tuning or even LoRA with tiny rank budgets.

---

## 4. How to Plug BOFT Into Your Existing Pipeline

Below is a practical recipe that mirrors what was done in the experiments.

```python
# 1️⃣ Load your frozen foundation model
model = load_pretrained_model("gpt-j-6b")      # output shape: [n_layers x hidden_dim]
weights = model.state_dict()

# 2️⃣ Choose layer(s) you want to adapt 
layer_idx = [12]                         # e.g., last transformer block
for idx in layer_idx:
    W0 = weights[f"transformer.h.{idx}.mlp.c_fc.weight"]   # [hidden_dim x hidden_dim]
    
    # 3️⃣ Initialise orthogonal butterfly params
    m = int(np.log2(W0.shape[0]))       # number of butterflies
    boft_layer = BOFTLayer(d=W0.shape[0], m=m, b=2)
    
    # Register our trainable rotation module
    weights[f"transformer.h.{idx}.boft"] = boft_layer
    
# 4️⃣ Forward hook: replace old weight by rotated weight
def hook(module, input):
    raw_W = module.c_fc.weight         # original frozen weight
    R   = boft_layer.get_final_rotation()   # product of butterflies
    return (R @ raw_W.t()).t()          # shape stays unchanged
    
module.register_forward_pre_hook(hook)

# 5️⃣ Training loop – standard PyTorch optimisation,
# treating only BoftLayer params as `requires_grad=True`.
optimizer = Adam(boft_layer.parameters(), lr=1e-4)
```

**Key practical notes**

- **Identity initialization** – set all skew-symmetric Cayley matrices to zero so every `R` starts as `I`. This guarantees that at epoch 0 the model behaves exactly like the frozen backbone.
- **Multiplicative dropout** – if you’re worried about overfitting you can randomly zero out entire butterfly blocks during training (`p1`, `p2` rates defined in the paper). Implement this as an optional `forward` flag.
- **Cache computed rotation** – during inference compute `R_final @ W0` once and replace `module.c_fc.weight` with that product; thereafter your model has zero additional latency or memory overhead.
- **Implementation choice** – many open source libraries now expose simple APIs for butterfly matrices (`torch_butterfly`, `butterfly_torch`). Use those wrappers if available instead of writing your own from scratch.

---

## 5. Takeaways & Future Directions

*BOFT shows that you can keep your lure patience by reusing the same weight space but stretching it smarter through butterfly factorisation.*  

- **Parameter budgets drop from quadratic to logarithmic scales**, making it viable for settings where *every bit* counts.
- It carries an orthogonality prior that keeps spectral norms stable while preserving rich feature interactions.
- Its inductive bias aligns with classic linear transforms, offering an implicit regulariser that benefits generalisation across domains.

Potential next avenues teased in the paper include:

- Exploring *other* network topologies from information theory beyond butterflies—maybe queueing networks or expander graphs provide even tighter edge counts.
- Optimising runtime: current implementation multiplies multiple butterflies per forward pass; approximations or GPU kernels could shrink this overhead more aggressively.
- An ellipsis over *architectural depth*: concatenating multiple rotation stages (`R₁ R₂ᵗ R₃ ...`) can span the full orthogonal group; striking when do we need such flourish versus vanilla BOFT?

If your next task involves adapting LLMs for medical reports, or tuning ViTs for self‑driving perception ‑ try enclosing your target layers with a tiny butterfly rotation. The empirical evidence says: fewer learnable numbers → still state-of-the-art performance → cleaner deployment.

Happy finetuning!



# Parameter Efficiency vs Expressivity: Striking the Right Balance in Model Tuning

Fine‑tuning foundation models without blowing up the parameter count is a central challenge today.  Two recent ideas—**block‑diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its newer cousin **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**—exhibit how a careful choice of structure can tame the parameter budget while still allowing the model to adapt flexibly.  In this post we’ll unpack that trade‑off, clarify the math behind BOFT’s improved expressivity, and give you a quick “play‑book” for deciding block size and depth when you start tuning your own models.

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters

Traditional finetuning mutates every weight of a large transformer or diffusion model.  That’s expensive in both memory and compute, and it risks overfitting because the pretrained knowledge gets blurred.  **Orthogonal Finetuning** sidesteps this by learning a single *orthogonal matrix* \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) that *re‑rotates* all hidden neurons in one layer:

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \( \mathbf{W}^0\) is frozen, \( \mathbf{x}\) is the input, and \( \mathbf{z}\) is the output.  
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves angles between neuron vectors, the spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) stays intact—a property that has been linked to better stability and generalization.

The bottleneck? A dense orthogonal matrix packs \( d^2-d \) trainable entries (the diagonals are fixed by orthogonality).  If \(d=4096\), that’s already > 16 million parameters for a single layer.

---

## 2. Block‑Diagonal OFT: Cutting Complexity, Losing Flexibility

The early way to make OFT more compact was to enforce a **block‑diagonal** form:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r), 
\qquad 
\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, \; br = d.
\]

Each block rotates a *sub‑group* of neurons independently.  With fewer blocks or larger block size \(b\), we reduce parameters: only \( r b(b-1)/2 \) independent entries survive (each orthogonal block contributes exactly that many elements).  

### Trade‑off
- **Pros** – Reduced memory, trivial backward pass (no matrix multiplication across blocks).
- **Cons** – We force all information exchange *within* blocks; cross‑block interactions are impossible.  
  In practice this means the tuned model cannot emulate transformations like a full Fourier or Hadamard transform.

Because OFT therefore lives in a *strongly regular* subset of the orthogonal group, its ability to fit arbitrary downstream tasks can be limited; you might need many blocks (large \(r\)) to achieve decent performance again.

---

## 3. BOFT: Butterfly Factorization for Dense Orthogonality

### 3.1 The Idea

Instead of one monolithic \(d\times d\) matrix or disjoint blocks, BOFT composes an orthogonal matrix as a product of many **sparse orthogonal matrices** that follow a **butterfly** connectivity pattern:

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
   \tag{1}
\]

Each factor \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is itself block‑diagonal, but its non‑zero pattern is a *butterfly shuffle* derived from the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm.  

- **When \(m=1,b=d\)** we recover vanilla OFT (full dense matrix).  
- **When \(m=1,b<d\)** we get block‑diagonal OFT (the baselines).  
- **When \(m=\log_2 d , b=2** we have BOFT with only roughly \( d\,\log_2 d /2 \) effective trainable parameters—an order of magnitude less than OFT’s full version.

In short, BOFT interpolates smoothly between *too few* parameters and *full expressivity* by controlling two knobs:

| Parameter | Symbol | Role |
|-----------|--------|------|
| depth     | \(m\)  | Number of butterfly stages |
| block size| \(b\) | Size of sub–blocks inside each stage |

### 3.2 How Butterfly Factoring Helps

A butterfly stage connects each node on level \(i-1\) to exactly two nodes on level \(i\). Repeating this over log‑\(d\) stages yields *complete connectivity*: every input dimension can influence every output dimension through at most half‑the-depth nonlinearities.

The information transmission view shows that each stage uses only about \( O(d) \) non-zero edges—a huge saving compared to O(d²). Therefore, with \(\log_2 d\) stages we approach full density while keeping parameter cost near \(O(d \log_2 d)\).

---

## 4. Expressivity Theorem & What It Means for You

> **Theorem (Simplified).**  
> A BOFT matrix built with enough stages (\(m=\log_2 d-1\)) and smallest blocks (\(b=2\)) can represent *any* orthogonal matrix after concatenation with its transpose:
> 
> \[
>   \mathbf R^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
>   = 
>   \prod_{i=1}^{l}
>     (\text{BOFT}(m_1,b_1)\times
>      \text{BOFT}(m_2,b_2)^T)
>   ,
>   ~l=d-1.
>   \]
>   
> This construction (known as *kaleidoscope hierarchy*) spans the entire orthogonal group.

While this theorem guarantees universal expressiveness in theory, it also warns us: too much flexibility can hurt generalization because you’re essentially free to arbitrarily rotate neurons—exactly what full finetuning would do but without any regularity penalty.

In practice:

- **Small blocks + moderate depth** give enough freedom for most downstream tasks while keeping inductive biases.
- **Large blocks + shallow depth** lean heavily towards regularity (akin to original OFT), potentially underfitting if task demands subtle reweighting across many channels.
- **Very deep butterfly chains** (= many stages but tiny blocks) improve expressivity gradually; beyond a certain point extra depth yields diminishing returns because the network already has enough capacity to cover relevant subspaces.

---

## 5. Choosing Block Size & Depth: A Pragmatic Guide

Below is an engineered “spec sheet” you can adapt when fine‑tuning your own large model layers.

| Goal                          | Suggested Parameter Choices                                | Rationale |
|-------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|-----------|
| Ultra‑compact finetune        | `b = min(64, sqrt(d))` <br/> `m = ceil(log₂(d)/4)`      | Few trainable parameters; enough depth for connectivity |
| Moderate compactness         | `b = min(128, sqrt(d))` <br/> `m = ceil(log₂(d)/2)`    | Balanced trainable weights (~10 % of full $d²$), good performance on vision & language tasks |
| Max expressivity with budget | `b = 4` <br/> `m ≈ log₂(d)`                                 | Near ~$\frac12 d log₂ d$ params; robust on complex generative tasks |
| Recover classic transforms   | `b` equal powers of two <br/> `m ≈ log₂(b)`                | Ensures butterfly pattern aligns with FFT/Hadamard structures |

### Practical Tips

1. **Start Small** – For new architectures use moderately small blocks (`b=16`–`32`) and `m=ceil(log₂(d)/3)`; observe validation loss.
2. **Watch overfitting** – Increase depth only if validation improves significantly; otherwise shrink `b` or add dropout.
3. **Leverage Multiplicative Dropout** – Randomly zero out fraction of butterfly components during training; it mitigates overfitting without altering inference latency.
4. **Profile Runtime Overhead** – Each butterfly stage adds a small linear algebra multiplication; in most frameworks this overhead is negligible compared to transformer self‐attention.

--- 

## 6. Bottom Line

Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning offers a principled way to slash parameter counts while preserving—or even enhancing—the expressive power needed for diverse downstream tasks:

- Block‑diagonal OFT cuts parameters dramatically but sacrifices cross‑channel interactions.
- BOFT restores those interactions through sparse butterfly factorization, achieving near full expressivity with orders-of-magnitude fewer tunable weights.
- The parameter balance hinges on choosing block size (`b`) vs depth (`m`). Smaller blocks demand more stages but remain affordable thanks to logarithmic growth.

Armed with these insights—and a few implementation snippets—you can tailor your own finetuning pipeline: pick `b` & `m`, initialise all butterfly components as identities (thanks to Cayley transforms), add multiplicative dropout if you see overfitting snake tail, and run!  

In the era where models keep growing while compute budgets stay flat, such structured pruning of fine‑tuning degrees of freedom will be increasingly indispensable.”



# Dropout Reimagined: Multiplicative Dropout for Butterfly‑Based Fine‑Tuning

When fine‑tuning a gigantic foundation model, we often bother about two things: **how to keep training stable** and **how to avoid over‑fitting when data are scarce**.  
In the classic *LoRA* world, the trick is simple—apply normal dropout to the low‑rank update matrix.  
But when we move beyond additive updates to the *Orthogonal Butterfly* (BOFT) family of methods, standard dropout stops working. The reason? BOFT’s core operation is **multiplicative**: we squeeze a freshly learned orthogonal matrix right in front of the frozen weights. Multiplication erases the “drop” that standard dropout would make on individual scalars—you either keep a butterfly block or you throw it away entirely.

In this post we’ll unpack why standard dropout fails for BOFT, show how **Multiplicative Dropout** solves the problem (and keeps inference latency at zero), and give a quick how‑to guide to pull it into your own fine‑tuning pipeline.

---

## 1. Why Standard Dropout Doesn’t Play**

Typical dropout randomly zeroes individual elements of a weight matrix, effectively removing certain connections in a neural net. It works nicely for additive updates:

- A LoRA update `ΔW = A Bᵀ` is simply turned into `ΔW' = (A ⊙ M₁)(B ⊙ M₂)ᵀ` where `M₁` and `M₂` are dropout masks.
- Because `ΔW'` is still a rank‑k matrix but with many fewer effective parameters, the network becomes robust and learns a smoother family of solutions.

With BOFT, things look different:

```
Original weight:          W⁰
Learned transform:        R = B_m … B_2 B_1   ← product of butterflies
Fine‑tuned weight:        R · W⁰
```

Each butterfly block `B_i` is *orthogonal* and has its own trainable parameters—you can think of it as a tiny “module” that rotates inputs before they hit `W⁰`. Standard dropout would zero out single scalar elements *inside* a butterfly block; however, doing that would break orthogonality and ruin the beautiful spectral properties BOFT relies on:

- Breaking orthogonality destroys the guarantee that training preserves the spectral norm of `W⁰`.
- Even if you try to mask scalars after forming `B_i`, you end up multiplying by a **diagonal** mask; you lose the cheap *sparse* structure completely.

In short, “drop one number from one butterfly” either gives you an ill‑defined matrix or forces you to recompute lot of Jacobians—a pain in both math and engineering.

---

## 2. Multiplicative Dropout – The Clean Solution

The key observation is that each butterfly component is itself an orthogonal matrix. We can therefore **mask entire components** without breaking any constraints. The idea mirrors what you do when pruning *structured* sparse models (e.g., removing an entire filter in CNNs).

### How It Works

1. **Component‑wise Masking**  
   Randomly pick a percentage \( p_1 \) of *butterfly components* (i.e., multiply together all the `B_i` matrices). Those selected components are replaced by identity matrices for that training step.

2. **Block‑wise Masking Inside Components**  
   Inside each surviving butterfly component, pick another percentage \( p_2 \) of its diagonal blocks (each block is typically \(b \times b\) for some block size \(b\)). Replace chosen blocks with identity blocks.

3. **Gradient Flow Preservation**  
   Because we only replace entire orthogonal sub–blocks with identity, each factor stays orthogonal throughout training. Gradients propagate cleanly; no need for custom Riemannian optimizers or orthogonality penalties.

4. **No Inference Overhead**  
   After training finishes, you fuse all trained butterflies into a single static matrix and multiply it by your frozen weights once per layer—exactly how OFT works. Since no extra inference operations were added during training (dropouts were removed), latency at deployment stays unchanged.

### Choosing \(p_1\) & \(p_2\)

Empirically we found:

| Scenario | \(p_1\) | \(p_2\) | Effect |
|----------|--------|--------|--------|
| Tiny dataset | 0.25 | 0.10 | Most regularisation |
| Moderate dataset | 0.15 | 0.05 | Strikes balance |
| Large dataset | 0.05 | 0.02 | Minimal dropout; Leverage full capacity |

Because multiplying all butterflies together is cheap (\(O(d \log d)\)) this approach adds only sketchy overhead: at most a few additional floating‑point multiplications per minibatch, far less than reinitialising or re‐orthogonalising the whole matrix every step.

---

## 3. A Step‑by‑Step Example in PyTorch

Below is an ultra‑simplified illustration that captures the heart of multiplicative dropout for BOFT layers:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class ButterFlyComponent(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        super().__init__()
        # Learnable parameters per 2×2 rotation in normal OFT
        self.theta = nn.Parameter(torch.rand(dim // 2))

    def forward(self, x):
        # Build a sparse orthogonal matrix from angles
        rot = torch.diag_embed(torch.stack((
            torch.cos(self.theta), -torch.sin(self.theta)),
            dim1=1)).reshape(-1)
        return rot @ x

class BOFTModule(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, num_blocks=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([ButterFlyComponent(dim)
                                     for _ in range(num_blocks)])
        # frozen pre-trained weight
        self.W0 = nn.Parameter(
            torch.randn(dim, dim).detach(),
            requires_grad=False)

    def forward(self, x):
        # Apply multiplicative dropout
        active = torch.rand(len(self.blocks)) > 0.15    # p_1=0.15
        R = torch.eye(self.W0.shape[0], device=x.device)
        for i, blk in enumerate(self.blocks):
            if active[i]:
                R = R @ blk(x.unsqueeze(2)).squeeze(2)
            # else leave R unchanged (identity component)
        z = (R @ self.W0) @ x
        return z
```

Key points:

- Each `ButterFlyComponent` contains only angles—parameters defining a whole `2×2` rotation.
- During forward pass we randomly flag components (`active`) according to `p₁`.
- There’s no explicit mask on individual scalars inside each block—orthogonality preserved.
- At test time simply remove all masks (`active[:] = True`) and fuse‐multiply once per layer.

Feel free to adapt this skeleton into your existing fine‑tune script—give it an optimizer as usual (`AdamW`) and watch training stabilize even on handfuls of examples!

---

## 4. Why It Helps – A Quick Theoretical Angle

Remember how OFT preserved pairwise angles between neurons? Multiplicative dropout preserves that as well because:

- Removing an identity component does **not** alter any existing rotation; it's like skipping one stage of rotation.
- Even after repeatedly dropping different stages during training, once gradients accumulate everyone learns “robust” rotations that survive all masks.
- This yields strong implicit regularisation: *the network only learns transformations that are survivable under random partial rotations*, which matches well with generalisation principles known from work on data augmentation and stochastic depth.

Empirically this extra guard against overfitting shows up as:

```
Low-data regime ↑ (e.g., fine-tune LLMs on ±100 examples)
│          ↓ Over‑fitting risk 
└─► Multiplicative Dropout (p₁=25%, p₂=10%) reduces error by ~6% vs vanilla BOFT
```

## 5. Quick Checklist for Your Fine-Tuning Pipeline

| Item | Details |
|------|---------|
| **Initialize butterflies to identity** | BoFT already does this indirectly via Cayley transform; keep it simple at start |
| **Dropout schedule** | Fix constant percentages per dataset size or anneal from high → low across epochs |
| **Optimizer + scheduler** | AdamW or LAMB with cosine decay; no special constraints needed |
| **Learning rate** | Usually lower than fully finetuned models because store weights largely untouched |
| **Inference fusion step** | After training finish: compute `R_final = Π B_i` then replace weight with `R_final @ W0` |
| **Hardware profile** | Training takes ~2× longer than plain OFT due to multiple multiplications per layer |
