# Parameter Efficiency vs Expressivity: Striking the Right Balance in Model Tuning

Fine‑tuning foundation models without blowing up the parameter count is a central challenge today.  Two recent ideas—**block‑diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its newer cousin **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**—exhibit how a careful choice of structure can tame the parameter budget while still allowing the model to adapt flexibly.  In this post we’ll unpack that trade‑off, clarify the math behind BOFT’s improved expressivity, and give you a quick “play‑book” for deciding block size and depth when you start tuning your own models.

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters

Traditional finetuning mutates every weight of a large transformer or diffusion model.  That’s expensive in both memory and compute, and it risks overfitting because the pretrained knowledge gets blurred.  **Orthogonal Finetuning** sidesteps this by learning a single *orthogonal matrix* \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \) that *re‑rotates* all hidden neurons in one layer:

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \( \mathbf{W}^0\) is frozen, \( \mathbf{x}\) is the input, and \( \mathbf{z}\) is the output.  
Because \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves angles between neuron vectors, the spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) stays intact—a property that has been linked to better stability and generalization.

The bottleneck? A dense orthogonal matrix packs \( d^2-d \) trainable entries (the diagonals are fixed by orthogonality).  If \(d=4096\), that’s already > 16 million parameters for a single layer.

---

## 2. Block‑Diagonal OFT: Cutting Complexity, Losing Flexibility

The early way to make OFT more compact was to enforce a **block‑diagonal** form:

\[
\mathbf{R} = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r), 
\qquad 
\mathbf{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, \; br = d.
\]

Each block rotates a *sub‑group* of neurons independently.  With fewer blocks or larger block size \(b\), we reduce parameters: only \( r b(b-1)/2 \) independent entries survive (each orthogonal block contributes exactly that many elements).  

### Trade‑off
- **Pros** – Reduced memory, trivial backward pass (no matrix multiplication across blocks).
- **Cons** – We force all information exchange *within* blocks; cross‑block interactions are impossible.  
  In practice this means the tuned model cannot emulate transformations like a full Fourier or Hadamard transform.

Because OFT therefore lives in a *strongly regular* subset of the orthogonal group, its ability to fit arbitrary downstream tasks can be limited; you might need many blocks (large \(r\)) to achieve decent performance again.

---

## 3. BOFT: Butterfly Factorization for Dense Orthogonality

### 3.1 The Idea

Instead of one monolithic \(d\times d\) matrix or disjoint blocks, BOFT composes an orthogonal matrix as a product of many **sparse orthogonal matrices** that follow a **butterfly** connectivity pattern:

\[
\mathbf{R}(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
   \tag{1}
\]

Each factor \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is itself block‑diagonal, but its non‑zero pattern is a *butterfly shuffle* derived from the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm.  

- **When \(m=1,b=d\)** we recover vanilla OFT (full dense matrix).  
- **When \(m=1,b<d\)** we get block‑diagonal OFT (the baselines).  
- **When \(m=\log_2 d , b=2** we have BOFT with only roughly \( d\,\log_2 d /2 \) effective trainable parameters—an order of magnitude less than OFT’s full version.

In short, BOFT interpolates smoothly between *too few* parameters and *full expressivity* by controlling two knobs:

| Parameter | Symbol | Role |
|-----------|--------|------|
| depth     | \(m\)  | Number of butterfly stages |
| block size| \(b\) | Size of sub–blocks inside each stage |

### 3.2 How Butterfly Factoring Helps

A butterfly stage connects each node on level \(i-1\) to exactly two nodes on level \(i\). Repeating this over log‑\(d\) stages yields *complete connectivity*: every input dimension can influence every output dimension through at most half‑the-depth nonlinearities.

The information transmission view shows that each stage uses only about \( O(d) \) non-zero edges—a huge saving compared to O(d²). Therefore, with \(\log_2 d\) stages we approach full density while keeping parameter cost near \(O(d \log_2 d)\).

---

## 4. Expressivity Theorem & What It Means for You

> **Theorem (Simplified).**  
> A BOFT matrix built with enough stages (\(m=\log_2 d-1\)) and smallest blocks (\(b=2\)) can represent *any* orthogonal matrix after concatenation with its transpose:
> 
> \[
>   \mathbf R^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)
>   = 
>   \prod_{i=1}^{l}
>     (\text{BOFT}(m_1,b_1)\times
>      \text{BOFT}(m_2,b_2)^T)
>   ,
>   ~l=d-1.
>   \]
>   
> This construction (known as *kaleidoscope hierarchy*) spans the entire orthogonal group.

While this theorem guarantees universal expressiveness in theory, it also warns us: too much flexibility can hurt generalization because you’re essentially free to arbitrarily rotate neurons—exactly what full finetuning would do but without any regularity penalty.

In practice:

- **Small blocks + moderate depth** give enough freedom for most downstream tasks while keeping inductive biases.
- **Large blocks + shallow depth** lean heavily towards regularity (akin to original OFT), potentially underfitting if task demands subtle reweighting across many channels.
- **Very deep butterfly chains** (= many stages but tiny blocks) improve expressivity gradually; beyond a certain point extra depth yields diminishing returns because the network already has enough capacity to cover relevant subspaces.

## 5. Choosing Block Size & Depth: A Pragmatic Guide

Below is an engineered “spec sheet” you can adapt when fine‑tuning your own large model layers.

| Goal                          | Suggested Parameter Choices                                | Rationale |
|-------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|-----------|
| Ultra‑compact finetune        | `b = min(64, sqrt(d))` <br/> `m = ceil(log₂(d)/4)`      | Few trainable parameters; enough depth for connectivity |
| Moderate compactness         | `b = min(128, sqrt(d))` <br/> `m = ceil(log₂(d)/2)`    | Balanced trainable weights (~10 % of full $d²$), good performance on vision & language tasks |
| Max expressivity with budget | `b = 4` <br/> `m ≈ log₂(d)`                                 | Near ~$\frac12 d log₂ d$ params; robust on complex generative tasks |
| Recover classic transforms   | `b` equal powers of two <br/> `m ≈ log₂(b)`                | Ensures butterfly pattern aligns with FFT/Hadamard structures |

### Practical Tips

1. **Start Small** – For new architectures use moderately small blocks (`b=16`–`32`) and `m=ceil(log₂(d)/3)`; observe validation loss.
2. **Watch overfitting** – Increase depth only if validation improves significantly; otherwise shrink `b` or add dropout.
3. **Leverage Multiplicative Dropout** – Randomly zero out fraction of butterfly components during training; it mitigates overfitting without altering inference latency.
4. **Profile Runtime Overhead** – Each butterfly stage adds a small linear algebra multiplication; in most frameworks this overhead is negligible compared to transformer self‐attention.

--- 

## 6. Bottom Line

Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning offers a principled way to slash parameter counts while preserving—or even enhancing—the expressive power needed for diverse downstream tasks:

- Block‑diagonal OFT cuts parameters dramatically but sacrifices cross‑channel interactions.
- BOFT restores those interactions through sparse butterfly factorization, achieving near full expressivity with orders-of-magnitude fewer tunable weights.
- The parameter balance hinges on choosing block size (`b`) vs depth (`m`). Smaller blocks demand more stages but remain affordable thanks to logarithmic growth.

Armed with these insights—and a few implementation snippets—you can tailor your own finetuning pipeline: pick `b` & `m`, initialise all butterfly components as identities (thanks to Cayley transforms), add multiplicative dropout if you see overfitting snake tail, and run!  

In the era where models keep growing while compute budgets stay flat, such structured pruning of fine‑tuning degrees of freedom will be increasingly indispensable.”
