\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# From Diffusion Models to Vision Transformers: How BOFT Adapts Any Large Model

Large foundation models have turned every research laboratory into a one‑stop shop for “plug‑and‑play” capabilities.  
Adapting a 175 B‑parameter GPT or a 12 B‑parameter Vision Transformer (ViT) to a new downstream task usually hides the cost of training – it’s just a handful of new trainable weights that burden inference time or memory.

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) is a recent method that takes the same idea as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and makes it truly parameter‑efficient. In this post we’ll take a quick tour of the paper’s main contributions, see how BOFT performs across three very different downstream domains—text‑to‑image diffusion, vision classification and language understanding—and finish with concrete instructions on how you can drop BOFT into your own finetuning pipelines.

---

## 1. What is BOFT? A Concise Recap

| Concept | Classical OFT | BOFT |
|---------|---------------|------|
| **Parameterisation** | Whole layer is multiplied by one orthogonal matrix \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). | Dense orthogonal matrix \(R\) is *factored* into a product of \(\mathcal{O}(\log d)\) sparse orthogonal butterfly matrices \(B_i\). |
| **Parameter count** | \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) (full orthogonal) or \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\) with block‑diagonal sparsity. | \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) with \(b=2\); in general \((b-1)dm/2\). |
| **Inductive bias** | Block‑diagonal → independent transformations per block; can destroy expressiveness. | Butterfly → resembles classic fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard); preserves dense connectivity while keeping sparsity. |
| **Forward pass** | \((RW^0)^T x\). | \(\bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{m} B_i\bigr)\!W^0)^T x\). |

Mathematically, each butterfly component has the form

\[
B_{\!2}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(d_1)&\operatorname{diag}(d_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(d_3)&\operatorname{diag}(d_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\]

and the full butterfly matrix is composed recursively via  

\[
B(d)=B_{\tilde d,d}\; B_{\frac d2} \dots B_2 .
\]

Because every block is a small orthogonal matrix (parameterised through Cayley transform), multiplying them gives us a dense but highly structured orthogonal matrix without ever touching all \(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\) entries.

## 2. Cross‑Domain Experiments

The paper evaluated BOFT on *three* very different settings:

| Domain | Model | Task | Baselines |
|--------|-------|------|-----------|
| Text‑to‑Image Diffusion | Stable Diffusion v2 – 1B parameters | Conditioning on arbitrarily chosen text prompts (controllable generation) | LoRA (low‑rank additive), OFT, full finetune |
| Vision Transformers | ViT‑Large – 307 M parameters | Image classification on fine‑grained benchmarks (e.g., ImageNet‑V2) | LoRA, OFT, full finetune |
| Language Modelling | GPT‑Neo/ GPT‑J – up to 6 B parameters | Next token prediction on domain‑specific corpora (e.g., medical literature) | LoRA, OFT, full finetune |

### Key Findings

1. **BOFT outperforms LoRA by 5–10 % in task accuracy** while using *up to* three times fewer trainable parameters.
   - On stable diffusion controllability tasks, BOFT achieved 78 % FID improvement over LoRA,
   - On ViT image classification it pressed top‑1 accuracy higher by ~3 %,
   - On LLM fine‑tuning it closed the gap to full finetune while still keeping under 0.5 % absolute error.
2. **Better training stability**: because every thawed weight vector undergoes an exact orthogonal rotation (\(R^TR=I\)), gradient norms stay bounded and gradients never explode as in some additive methods.
3. **Scalable inference**: after finetuning BOFT’s final rotation matrix can be absorbed into the frozen weights once and for all — nothing changes at runtime.

---

## 3. Why Does BOFT Win Over LoRA and Other PEFT Techniques?

### a. Preserving Pretrained Geometry

Both OFT and BOFT multiply the original weight matrix \(W^0\) by an orthogonal transformation from the left:

\[
W^{fin} = RW^0 ,\qquad R^TR=I .
\]

This ensures that the singular values of \(W^{fin}\) equal those of \(W^0\); i.e., spectral norm stays exactly unchanged. Empirically this keeps gradients well conditioned and leads to faster convergence.

With LoRA we add an extra low–rank term:

\[
W^{fin}= W^0 + A B ,
\]

which distorts singular values arbitrarily. The extra regularization needed in LoRA (dropout, weight decay) therefore becomes stronger.

### b. Structured Expressiveness vs Randomness

A random dense orthogonal matrix would be fully expressive but impractical (\(\mathcal{O}(d^2)\)).  
Block‐diagonal OFT reduces parameters but segregates features into disconnected “subspaces”, harming generalisation in highly mixed downstream domains.

Butterfly factorisation maintains *dense connectivity* while keeping only \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\) parameters. The resulting hypothesis class sits strictly between these extremes:

- Full ONF ≝ All orthogonals,
- Block‐diagonal ≝ Limited subspace rotations,
- Butterfly ≝ Structured superposition of small rotations that still interact fully with one another.

The experimental curves in the paper confirm that with fewer trainable values BOFT can approximate any random orthogonal matrix as well as—or better than—block‐diagonal OFT with comparable block size.

### c. Implicit Regularisation

Because each factor is sparse and highly regularised, effective degrees of freedom are throttled early during training – defeating overfitting on small datasets that plague full fine‑tuning or even LoRA with tiny rank budgets.

## 4. How to Plug BOFT Into Your Existing Pipeline

Below is a practical recipe that mirrors what was done in the experiments.

```python
# 1️⃣ Load your frozen foundation model
model = load_pretrained_model("gpt-j-6b")      # output shape: [n_layers x hidden_dim]
weights = model.state_dict()

# 2️⃣ Choose layer(s) you want to adapt 
layer_idx = [12]                         # e.g., last transformer block
for idx in layer_idx:
    W0 = weights[f"transformer.h.{idx}.mlp.c_fc.weight"]   # [hidden_dim x hidden_dim]
    
    # 3️⃣ Initialise orthogonal butterfly params
    m = int(np.log2(W0.shape[0]))       # number of butterflies
    boft_layer = BOFTLayer(d=W0.shape[0], m=m, b=2)
    
    # Register our trainable rotation module
    weights[f"transformer.h.{idx}.boft"] = boft_layer
    
# 4️⃣ Forward hook: replace old weight by rotated weight
def hook(module, input):
    raw_W = module.c_fc.weight         # original frozen weight
    R   = boft_layer.get_final_rotation()   # product of butterflies
    return (R @ raw_W.t()).t()          # shape stays unchanged
    
module.register_forward_pre_hook(hook)

# 5️⃣ Training loop – standard PyTorch optimisation,
# treating only BoftLayer params as `requires_grad=True`.
optimizer = Adam(boft_layer.parameters(), lr=1e-4)
```

**Key practical notes**

- **Identity initialization** – set all skew-symmetric Cayley matrices to zero so every `R` starts as `I`. This guarantees that at epoch 0 the model behaves exactly like the frozen backbone.
- **Multiplicative dropout** – if you’re worried about overfitting you can randomly zero out entire butterfly blocks during training (`p1`, `p2` rates defined in the paper). Implement this as an optional `forward` flag.
- **Cache computed rotation** – during inference compute `R_final @ W0` once and replace `module.c_fc.weight` with that product; thereafter your model has zero additional latency or memory overhead.
- **Implementation choice** – many open source libraries now expose simple APIs for butterfly matrices (`torch_butterfly`, `butterfly_torch`). Use those wrappers if available instead of writing your own from scratch.

## 5. Takeaways & Future Directions

*BOFT shows that you can keep your lure patience by reusing the same weight space but stretching it smarter through butterfly factorisation.*  

- **Parameter budgets drop from quadratic to logarithmic scales**, making it viable for settings where *every bit* counts.
- It carries an orthogonality prior that keeps spectral norms stable while preserving rich feature interactions.
- Its inductive bias aligns with classic linear transforms, offering an implicit regulariser that benefits generalisation across domains.

Potential next avenues teased in the paper include:

- Exploring *other* network topologies from information theory beyond butterflies—maybe queueing networks or expander graphs provide even tighter edge counts.
- Optimising runtime: current implementation multiplies multiple butterflies per forward pass; approximations or GPU kernels could shrink this overhead more aggressively.
- An ellipsis over *architectural depth*: concatenating multiple rotation stages (`R₁ R₂ᵗ R₃ ...`) can span the full orthogonal group; striking when do we need such flourish versus vanilla BOFT?

If your next task involves adapting LLMs for medical reports, or tuning ViTs for self‑driving perception ‑ try enclosing your target layers with a tiny butterfly rotation. The empirical evidence says: fewer learnable numbers → still state-of-the-art performance → cleaner deployment.

Happy finetuning!
