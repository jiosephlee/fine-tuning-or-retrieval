\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### "ArXiv papers seem ineffective” – could data quality be the culprit?
Question:
- The authors say models trained on arXiv‐only corpora ‘display no notable improvements or even deterioration’. Is this due to tokenization problems (macros, citations) or because arXiv texts are too formal/labeled? Is there evidence that cleaning arXiv LaTeX would improve mathematical reasoning?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
The experiments in *DeepSeekMath* show that, when used in isolation, arXiv‑derived corpora do not significantly improve mathematical reasoning (and sometimes hurt performance). This is not a simple “token‑level” problem of LaTeX macro ambiguity, nor merely a matter of the papers being overly formal, but mainly a matter of *distributional mismatch* and *noisy supervision* that arises when the entirety of an arXiv paper is fed into a language‑model head.

---

### 1. What does the paper actually report?

In the section “ArXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning,” the authors train two models on pure arXiv data sets:

| Model | Size | arXiv corpus used | Result |
|-------|------|-------------------|--------|
| DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B | 1.3B params | MathPile (≈ 8.9 B tokens, > 85 % arXiv) | No notable improvement or even deterioration on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, miniF2F |
| DeepSeek-Coder‑Base‑v1.5 7B | 7B params | ArXiv‑RedPajama (≈ 28 B tokens, clean LaTeX) | Same trend |

The authors highlight three caveats:  
1. **They did not combine arXiv with other data** – the split is “purely arXiv.”  
2. **They didn’t test on every possible math sub‑domain** – e.g., informalisation was untouched.  
3. **Scale limitations** – perhaps larger models would benefit.

Thus the conclusion is *conditional*: on the exact setup employed, arXiv alone is not helpful.

---

### 2. Why is arXiv ineffective when trained alone?

#### a. **Distributional mismatch**

ArXiv papers are long monologues written for a knowledgeable audience; they rarely output clean, step‑by‑step solutions in plain text that an LLM can learn to emulate. In contrast, the math corpora that *did* help (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) were harvested directly from websites that publish *explained* solutions: blogs, forums, AoPS threads, and question–answer pairs where the answer appears in ordinary prose or short code snippets. The language model thus learns a *format style* – short explanations mixed with symbols – that matches what it sees during inference.

When a model is trained only on dense research prose, it over‑internalises syntax like long sentences, heavy notation (`$\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$`), and dense references, which do **not** map well to the short‐form reasoning required on GSM8K or MATH.

#### b. **Noisy tokenisation & macro bleed**

Even after removing preambles and comments (the RedPajama pipeline), many LaTeX source files still contain user macros (`\newcommand`, `\def`, etc.) that diverge wildly across papers and create out-of-vocabulary tokens. The tokenizer (typically a byte‑pair or wordpiece model) has to split these into atomic units that often have no semantic meaning by themselves. During pre-training this creates noisy patterns that are hard for gradient descent to stabilise: `\exp` becomes `[PARSE_TOKEN_0][PARSE_TOKEN_5]...`, wrapped around without context.

Whereas content published directly as HTML (as in our math corpus) already uses rendered math (`\[ \]`, `\( \)`), which tokenises cleanly to operator symbols (`[TOKEN_EVAL]`) that are more directly useful for symbolic manipulation learning.

#### c. **Static vs dynamic presentation**

ArXiv abstracts and sections are static – they do not include interactive tool usage or differentiable code snippets as seen on MathOverflow or Kaggle notebooks. The RL phase of DeepSeekMath relies on reward signals that compare *different completions of the same prompt*. If the model has never seen at least one reasonable completion format during pre‑training,

the reward model will be able to score nevertheless *because it learns from annotated examples*, but the policy will struggle to map tokens back to useful structures because its prior exposure was entirely out of distribution.

#### d. **Benchmark contamination avoidance**

The authors filtered out any segment containing >10‑gram matches with benchmark questions (GSM8K/MATH/CMATH). This rigorous filtering may have removed relatively short but highly informative explanations embedded within longer arXiv texts—e.g., quick derivations inside footnotes or appendices—leaving behind only the highly technical core content that gives little practice to step‑by‑step reasoning.

---

### 3. Is tokenisation alone the culprit?

Not quite. Tokeniser noise certainly hurts convergence speed; however:

* *If you trained a small LLM purely on cleaned LaTeX* (`ArXiv‐RedPajama`), you would still see similar lack of gains even after fiddling with tokeniser boundaries.
* *If you used higher‑level graph or parse encoders* (e.g., tree‐structured transformers applied directly to `\begin{equation} … \end{equation}`), you could sidestep macro noise completely yet still find no big jump in reasoning accuracy unless coupled with explanations interspersed with natural language.

Hence tokenisation is a **symptom**, not a root cause.

---

### 4. What would help if we wanted to make use of arXiv text?

The paper’s own discussion hints at three further directions:

1. **Mixing corpora** – harvesting arXiv text *in tandem* with high‑quality solution corpora alleviates catastrophic forgetting while preserving any general scientific knowledge it might provide.
2. **Fine‐grained fine‐tuning** – LaTeX statements can be parsed into ASTs and then fed as structured input; future work could teach models to read and generate symbolic objects directly.
3. **Scaling up** – Larger models have more capacity to store both high‐quality “solution” patterns and generic scientific language; possibly 70B+ would see improvement when trained on arXiv plus other data.

Until such techniques are applied, pure-arxiv training remains an uphill task for mathematical reasoning benchmarks.

---

### 5. Bottom line

The authors’ empirical evidence suggests that **arXiv text alone is too formal and too noisy in its token structure relative to what current LM training pipelines consume for mathematical reasoning tasks**. A mixture of web-extracted solution texts, careful cleaning of macros, and inclusion of structured code-like snippets provides richer learning signals than raw research prose does.

Consequently, if you want better math reasoning performance today you should prioritize curated web data rather than bulk-arxiv dumps – though future research might uncover ways to extract value from papers via advanced parsing or larger architectures._filtered_
