\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does DeepSeekMath use a fastText classifier for selecting Common Crawl pages, and how does it avoid overfitting to the initial seed corpus?
Question:
In the data collection pipeline, the authors train a fastText model on 500k positive OpenWebMath examples and 500k negative Common Crawl pages. They then use this model to recall more math pages, rank them, and keep the top ones. How does this iterative approach prevent the model from learning only the narrow style of the seed corpus? What mechanisms are in place to ensure diversity in the retrieved pages across iterations?
Answer:
**Why a fastText classifier is used for page selection**

fastText is a shallow, linear model that learns a sparse or dense embedding for words and then projects documents into a low‑dimensional space (here 256 dimensions). Because the goal of the data‑collection pipeline is to sift through *billions* of Common Crawl pages, the classifier has to be:

* **Fast and memory‑friendly** – the whole CC shard can be streamed once, each page’s token sequence passed through the same tiny neural net and immediately scored.
* **Scalable** – training on 1 M examples (500 k positives, 500 k negatives) takes only minutes on a single GPU or even a CPU cluster; once trained, inference is ~O(length) per page.
* **Probabilistically interpretable** – the cosine‑like score can be used as a confidence to rank pages, so the top‑N can be selected without further human effort.

The model therefore acts as a lightweight filter that turns an otherwise intractable search problem into a handful of easy “retrieve‑then‑filter” passes.

---

**How the iterative pipeline avoids over‑fitting to the seed corpus**

1. **Seed diversity is deliberately enlarged in each round**  
   After the first pass only ~40 B tokens are retained because the initial fastText model only knew how to recognise a narrow slice of mathematical style (mostly English texts that look like OpenWebMath). In subsequent rounds the authors explicitly *search for domains* whose pages were largely missed (“over 10 % of their pages were collected” → `mathoverflow.net`, `artofproblemsolving.com`, etc.) and manually annotate many more “math” URLs inside those domains. This new material is added to the positive set before re‑training. Consequently, each iteration exposes fastText to lexical patterns, symbols, and layout conventions that were absent in earlier rounds.

2. **Negative examples are drawn from *all* Common Crawl pages**  
   For every iteration 500 k random Common Crawl pages are sampled as negatives. Because these come from every topic in the crawl, the model learns not only what *is* math but what *is not* math. The more negative examples we have, the less likely fastText will simply collapse onto idiosyncratic features of the seed data (such as specific web‑template boilerplate).

3. **Ranking by confidence removes low‑quality recall**  
   After each model is applied to CC we keep only those pages whose score exceeds a high threshold. This ensures that highly atypical or noisy pages that might have been memorised by fastText do not leak into the next iteration’s training set.

4. **Human annotation after recall feeds back real labels**  
   The top‑ranked pages are manually inspected; correct math pages are added back into the positive pool while false positives are discarded. This “human‑in‑the‑loop” step guarantees that any drift toward overfitting (e.g., the model learning to recognise a particular layout or logo) is caught early and corrected.

5. **Diminishing returns signal convergence**  
   After four iterations almost all remaining uncollected math pages are already in earlier collections (~98 % overlap). At this point further fine‑tuning would provide negligible new signal but could reinforce any remaining bias, so collection stops. The early stopping criterion prevents runaway overfitting.

---

**Mechanisms that maintain diversity across iterations**

* **Domain–level filtering** – by grouping webpages into domains (identical base URL) and tracking which domains yield many false negatives, new seed examples come from fresh topical regions rather than re‑using similar locales.
* **Near‑deduplication of URLs** – before any page ever enters training quick checks for URL similarity ensure that nearly identical copies (e.g., mirrored editions) do not dominate future iterations.
* **Cross‑lingual inclusion** – OpenWebMath is multilingual; during manual annotation additional foreign language math sites are added, yielding a corpus that spans English and Chinese at least.
* **Statistical ranking across passes** – each iteration assigns fastText scores based on its own parameterisation; because models evolve, even descriptions that were previously rejected may now receive higher scores simply because new lexical features have been learned, thereby injecting variety.

Thus, even though each cycle uses a single linear classifier trained only on examples seen so far, the continual enrichment of positives and negatives—combined with strict ranking and human vetting—keeps the class boundary from collapsing onto any narrow stylistic niche of the initial seed while steadily expanding coverage across domains, languages, and mathematical styles.
