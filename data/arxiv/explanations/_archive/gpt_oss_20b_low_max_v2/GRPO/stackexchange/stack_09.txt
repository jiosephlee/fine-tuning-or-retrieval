\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does GRPO avoid over‑optimization of irrelevant tokens when using only final‑token rewards?
Question:
"Outcome supervision only provides a reward at the end of each output" – yet GRPO applies advantage to every token via its min/clipping operator. Are there occasions where tokens before the final one receive disproportionate updates because their probabilities were correlated with the reward? Would adding per‑token minimal reward signals help?
Answer:
**Short‑answer**  
GRPO does not “teleport” its reward to early tokens in an uncontrolled way.  
Because the advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ for every token in an output is set to the *same* value—the normalized final reward of that whole output—every token receives a signal that is proportional to how good (or bad) the *entire* answer is, not to any local evidence. The clipping operator therefore only limits how much any single token can be punished or praised at once, but every token still carries the same weight relative to the group baseline. Consequently, tokens that are strongly correlated with a scoring artifact (e.g., a repeated formula) can receive disproportionate updates, but only if that artifact is part of many high‑reward outputs in a group.  

Whether this hurts training depends on how often such correlations appear. In practice GRPO already includes several safeguards that reduce the risk of over‑optimising irrelevant symbols:

| Guard | How it works | Why it matters |
|-------|--------------|----------------|
| **Group‑relative baseline** | The baseline for each output is the mean reward of other outputs sampled from the *same question* (Section 4.3). | If a token happens to be common among many poor but correlated outputs, its advantage will be down‑scaled because the group mean will already be high. |
| **Reward normalisation** | Rewards are z‑scored within the group before constructing $\hat A_{i,t}$ (Outcome supervision) or summed across steps (Process supervision). | This keeps extreme rewards from dominating and ensures tokens that happen to get high raw scores because of a spurious pattern will be penalised if most other outputs in the group do not share that pattern. |
| **KL penalty on policy** | A explicit $\mathbb D_{\rm KL}(\pi_\theta\Vert\pi_{\rm ref})$ term keeps policy changes close to the SFT reference. | Tokens that cause large deviations early on will quickly incur a KL cost, preventing runaway over‑optimisation of a single token style or frequency. |
| **Iterative reward model retraining** | The reward model is updated every iteration with new samples and a 10 % replay buffer (Algorithm 1). | Short‑term overfitting to artifacts in one batch diminishes as the reward model learns their true quality across many batches. |

These design choices mean that while *theoretically* an early, irrelevant token could get amplified by its correlation with a high final score, in practice it rarely happens because:

1. **Correlation alone is not enough** – GRPO only credits tokens that help distinguish one sample from its peers; if an irrelevant token appears randomly, it will appear evenly across samples and its advantage will average close to zero.
2. **Normalization attenuates spikes** – z‑scoring within each group flattens out extreme values; an isolated high‑reward outlier has no effect on other tokens.
3. **KL regularisation binds the policy** – any sudden change that increases probability mass for a specific irrelevant symbol must pay a KL penalty, discouraging such behaviour unless consistently rewarded.

### When might tokens get “excessive” updates?

There are nevertheless pathological scenarios where early tokens could receive large updates:

| Scenario | What happens? |
|-----------|--------------|
| A *feature drift* test set where all winning answers start with a fixed keyword (e.g., “Answer:”) | That keyword gets repeatedly associated with high rewards across groups, lifting its probability everywhere. |
| Reward model mis‑specification – e.g., scoring only length instead of correctness | Longer continuations containing superfluous wording receive higher scores; all preceding tokens receive inflated reward even though they carry no real signal. |
| Very small group size ($G=1$) → baseline degenerates to zero → $\hat A_{i,t} = \widetilde r_i$ for all $t$ gives huge advantage if $\widetilde r_i\neq 0$. | Tokens will be pushed uniformly up or down based solely on whether one sample was good or bad, amplifying noise. |

GRPO mitigates these via:

* **Choosing sufficiently large $G$** (typically 64 in DeepSeekMath experiments) so baselines are stable.
* **Using well‑trained reward models**, validated by comparing process vs outcome supervision sensitivity.
* **Occasional `reward clipping’** inside each batch if variance gets too high.

---

### Would adding per‑token minimal reward signals help?

A per‑token reward—e.g., annotating each logical step or formula with “high confidence” vs “low confidence”—could bring two benefits:

1. **Finer granularity** – Directly aligning loss to the evaluative signal reduces reliance on group effects; early proof steps that genuinely improve final correctness get stronger positive reinforcement.
2. **Noise suppression** – Even if later steps are noisy or partially correct, earlier correct ones can still receive credit.

However, there are practical trade‑offs:

* **Data collection overhead** – Annotating step‑level quality for thousands of problems is expensive.
* **Reward model complexity** – We would need a model able to predict step quality reliably; error in this model could outweigh its benefits.
* **Risk of over‑fitting local patterns** – Token‑level signals may encourage the policy to overproduce certain syntactic forms (“Let’s compute…”) even if they do not truly improve reasoning.

In many RL for LLMs research papers (e.g., RFT, DPO), authors opt for *process supervision* (step‑level rewards) only when data annotations are available; otherwise they rely on *outcome supervision*. DeepSeekMath’s authors already experiment with process supervision in Section 4.3 and reported it improves results versus outcome alone without extra annotations: GRPO+Process outperforms GRPO+Outcome on GSM8K/MATH.

Thus, adding fine‑grained token rewards could help further reduce over‑optimisation of irrelevant tokens *if* those rewards are reliable and well calibrated. In current deep‐seek experiments, the combination of group‑relative advantage + normalisation + KL regularisation already suppresses most undesirable token amplification, which is why no separate per‑token reward was included.

---

## Bottom line

- GRPO avoids catastrophic overshoot on irrelevant initial tokens by using a group baseline that neutralises token‐level correlations and by clipping ratios during optimisation.
- Typical failure modes involve very small groups or ill‐posed reward functions; these are already mitigated through design choices such as large $G$, z–score normalisation and explicit KL constraints.
- Per‐token rewards could sharpen learning at the cost of new annotation and potential noise amplification; unless such data exists (or process supervision can serve as surrogate), outcome–only GRPO is sufficient for robust training—as demonstrated by DeepSeekMath‐RL’s performance gains without growth in overfitting anywhere along the sequence.
