\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Beyond Benchmarks: Strengths & Gaps in Modern Math LLMs

## What the paper tells us

DeepSeekMath is a new 7‑billion‑parameter language model that was built on top of a code‑trained backbone, DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5. The authors invested **500 B tokens of training** and—most importantly—added a gigantic *math‑specific* corpus extracted from Common Crawl:
- **120 B tokens** of math‑related web pages (the DeepSeekMath corpus)
- 10 % from arXiv, 20 % from GitHub code, and the remaining 70 % from general Common Crawl in English and Chinese.

After standard instruction tuning, the authors performed reinforcement learning with *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO), which replaces the critic used in traditional PPO with a group‑based baseline. The result is a model that scores **51.7 % on the competition‑level MATH benchmark**—the best open‑source result we know of—and **80–90 % on GSM8K** using chain‑of‑thought prompting.

However, the paper also reports some honest limitations that are easy to miss when just looking at headline numbers.

## Strengths that matter

| Benchmark | Baseline (open‑source) | DeepSeekMath #7B | Gap |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------|-----|
| GSM8K (chain‑of‑thought) | 82.9 % | **88.2 %** (GRPO) | +5.3 |
| MATH (competition level) | 46.8 % | **51.7 %** (GRPO) | +4.9 |
| CMATH (Chinese) | 84.6 % | **88.8 %** (GRPO) | +4.2 |
| MiniF2F autoformalisation | modest but improved over baseline | Substantial jump |

A few takeaways:

1. **Math pre‑training matters** – The 120B-token corpus outperforms smaller, curated math corpora like OpenWebMath or MathPile almost everywhere, even with a tiny 7B model.
2. **Code training helps Math** – The backbone is a code model; experiments show that code fine‑tuning improves both tool‑integrated reasoning (Python programs) and pure reasoning.
3. **Reinforcement learning pays off** – GRPO boosts performance over instruction tuning alone without adding a costly critic network.

The big moral is that you can squeeze impressive math capability out of a 7B model if you feed it the right data and then polish it with RL.

## Gaps that are not covered by the numbers

### Geometry remains weak
The authors ran preliminary tests: *“the model cannot handle problems related to triangles and ellipses”*. Geometry is notoriously sensitive to symbolic manipulation and spatial reasoning—areas where even GPT‑4 shows streaks of weakness compared to domain‐specific theorem provers. It appears that the **math corpus, though large, underrepresents pure geometry material**, especially high‑school topics like Euclidean constructions or advanced trigonometry.

### Theorem proving is still a long way off
On the MiniF2F benchmark the model shows *“strong few-shot autoformalisation”*, but that’s an emerging area and still falls behind specialized theorem provers or larger closed‐source models like PaLM or GPT‑4 (when paired with external tools). The experimental section notes “geometry and theorem-proof are relatively weaker than closed models”.

### Few-shot performance lags
A quick pass on few-shot settings reveals no dramatic gains over zero-shot: “GPT-4 could improve its performance with few-shot inputs, while DeepSeekMath shows similar performance in zero-shot and few-shot evaluation.” This indicates that either:
- The token budget for prompting isn’t large enough for coaching.
- Or the architecture lacks robust *prompt steering* capabilities typically superior in larger models.

### Resource footprint
While GRPO removes the critic and saves memory, RL training still requires massive sampling of multiple outputs per prompt (64 in experiments). This is nontrivial for research groups without dedicated GPU clusters.

## Where to expand the corpus next

The success story of DeepSeekMath hinges on two facts: 1) high‑quality math data exist abundantly on the web; 2) picking it efficiently boosts reasoning skills dramatically.

To keep climbing past GPT-4 on competition‐level MATH or to close geometry/theorem gaps, future work should target richer content sources:

### 1️⃣ Curated geometry datasets
* Online textbooks (`geometry.com`, `mathsisfun.com`), 
* Problem sets from AoPS or Art of Problem Solving school,
* Derivative-based trigonometry notes from Khan Academy,
* Euclidean proofs from Wikipedia or dedicated geometry wikis.

Integrating these into a dedicated *Geometry Corpus* would directly address the sparsity shown in modern benchmarks.

### 2️⃣ Formal mathematical libraries as data
The paper briefly touches on arXiv but notes limited benefit when used alone. However, patches of arXiv papers that include detailed proofs or derivation steps could be harvested selectively:
- Extract sections annotated as `Proof:` or `Derivation:` using regex and NLP heuristics.
- Perform semantic cleaning to strip references/LaTeX formatting.
- Store them as additional training tokens focusing on formal reasoning patterns.

This would give the model exposure to rigorous stepwise deduction not found in ordinary web pages.

### 3️⃣ Enriched process rewards for RL
GRPO currently assigns rewards at the end or at each discussion step. A *process reward* trained on explicit intermediate correctness checks could guide fine-grained step selection:
- Collect human-annotated “good vs bad” substeps.
- Train a reward model that scores each token’s contribution to reaching an intermediate state (e.g., after applying an identity).

Fine-grained RL could help rebuild geometry capacity where many steps involve algebraic manipulations interlaced with spatial reasoning.

### 4️⃣ Multilingual expansion beyond English–Chinese
The present work includes both languages but heavy reliance on English content measured by token counts skews learning towards Western mathematicians’ notation styles. Adding non-Latin alphabets (e.g., Arabic numerals scripts, Cyrillic terms used in analytic geometry texts) will reduce language bias and improve global usability.

## Practical guidance for research groups

If you’re looking to build or extend an LLM’s math skills:

1. **Start with a large web crawl**: Use fastText or efficient boosters like BM25+semantic embeddings to collect pages tagged with math-related keywords (`integral`, `matrix`, `proof`, etc.).  
2. **Iterate your selector**: Each round add manually annotated URLs back into your seed set; train a new classifier; recall more pages—repeat until marginal gains drop below ~2%.  
3. **Keep contamination low**: Run exact n-gram matches against all benchmark texts; remove any overlap longer than 10 tokens—a standard practice in many recent papers (cf., DeepSeek approach).  
4. **Schedule RL pulse-ups**: After instruction tuning just before deploying your final checkpoint, run a single reinforcement sweep with GRPO or PPO but use *only* labeled questions you already have; this ensures policy stays stable while gaining more precise output distribution refinement.

## Takeaway

DeepSeekMath exemplifies how carefully curated math data + code-based pretraining + lightweight reinforcement learning can elevate an LLM into “borderline GPT‑4” territory on compositional numeric reasoning tasks—all within a 7B parameter budget.

Whether you’re building industry GPT interfaces or experimenting in academia, keep an eye on:
- The shape of your training corpus,
- Whether you fine-tune on code,
- How you reward complex reasoning steps during RL.

Most importantly, no matter how high your benchmark score looks, examine subdomains like geometry or theorem proving directly—you might find hidden weaknesses lurking beneath those pretty numbers. These gaps are just as critical for safe deployment as they are for academic curiosity.\n\nHappy training!
