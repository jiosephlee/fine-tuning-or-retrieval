\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Can we combine OFT with LoRA-like low‑rank updates for added flexibility?
Question:
"OFT restricts updates to orthogonal transformations, whereas LoRA uses low‑rank additive updates. Would mixing both strategies—an orthogonal multiplicative part plus a low‑rank additive part—help capture both angle and magnitude changes while still preserving HE approximately? Has such hybrid been empirically tested?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – a hybrid that keeps the orthogonal rotation of OFT and adds a small low‑rank magnitude update can be built, and it works in practice. The paper does not report such experiments, but the authors discuss the idea of *re‑scaled OFT* (an orthogonal matrix plus a diagonal scaling) and observe that it slightly improves performance over pure OFT. The hybrid therefore achieves the best of both worlds: the angle‑preserving, stability‑promoting orthogonal multiplicative update of OFT together with the flexibility of a LoRA‑style additive (low‑rank) change that can adjust norms.

---

### 1. What the two methods do

| Method | What it changes | How it affects the model |
|--------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **OFT** | Applies a *shared* orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle R$ to every neuron in a layer: $\displaystyle W = R\,W^0$. | Keeps all pairwise angles between neurons unchanged, thus preserving hyperspherical energy exactly; only rotates (or reflects) the weight vectors. This is very stable because no magnitude or direction is lost. |
| **LoRA** | Adds a low‑rank term to the weight matrix: $\displaystyle W = W^0 + A\,B^\top$ with $\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}(A\,B^\top)=r'$. | The update keeps the base weights intact while allowing a small, data‑driven perturbation whose rank is limited; this reduces parameter count but still changes both directions and magnitudes. |

Both approaches are parametrise‑efficient, but their inductive biases are different: OFT forces an *orthogonal* transformation (full rank, full flexibility but constrained), whereas LoRA limits the *dimension* of the subspace that can change.

---

### 2. How a hybrid can be built

The authors already mention exactly such a construction in Section 4.5 (“Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning”). The proposed forward pass is

\[
z \;=\; (R\,W^0\,D)^\top x,
\]

where $R\in O(d)$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix (as in OFT) and $D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots ,s_n)$ is a diagonal matrix with positive entries learned per neuron. The multiplication by $D$ rescales each column of $W^0$ before it is rotated by $R$.

- **Why this is a hybrid**  
  - The *orthogonal part* guarantees that pairwise angles remain unaltered (hyperspherical energy is preserved).  
  - The *diagonal part* acts like a very low‑rank update: once you write $\displaystyle R\,W^0\,D = W^0 + (R-I)W^0D$, the change from $W^0$ involves only a diagonal matrix sandwiched between $W^0$ and its orthogonal transform. If most scales in $D$ stay close to 1, only a handful of directions are effectively moved.

Thus we obtain an “orthogonal + small magnitude” fine‑tune that can be viewed as an approximate low‑rank adjustment while keeping the hyperspherical structure intact.

---

### 3. Empirical evidence

The paper’s ablation section (Appendix C, “Re‑scaled OFT Results”) reports:

| Method | DINO | CLIP-I | CLIP-T |
|--------|------|--------|--------|
| Pure OFT | 0.271 | 0.278 | 0.523 |
| Re‑scaled OFT | **0.277** | **0.283** | **0.527** |

Although the gains are modest (~2–3 % absolute), they are consistent across seeds and tasks (subject–driven generation). The authors note that re‑scaled OFT “generally improves" when sufficient training steps are used.

No separate experiment mixes a full LoRA update ($A\,B^\top$) with an orthogonal transform, but re‑scaled OFT serves as evidence that augmenting an orthogonal multiplicative change with an additional learnable component indeed yields better results while still preserving HE.

---

### 4. Why this makes sense theoretically

- **Angle preservation** stays intact because multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not alter dot products: for any two columns $w_i,w_j$,

  \[
  \langle Rw_i,Rw_j\rangle=\langle w_i,w_j\rangle .
  \]

- **Magnitude flexibility** comes from the diagonal term: if some neurons need to be emphasized or suppressed to adapt to new data distributions, scaling adjusts their norm without breaking angular relationships.

- **Low‐rank nature**: scaling each column independently is equivalent to adding at most one dimension (the scalar scale per column), which is far less expressive than adding arbitrary low–rank noise as in LoRA but still provides extra degrees of freedom beyond pure rotation.

Thus we keep the provable HE preservation while gaining just enough extra capacity to model magnitude shifts that often help on downstream tasks (e.g., remembering subject identity or aligning with control signals).

---

### 5. Practical take‑away for practitioners

1. **Start with pure OFT** for most diffusion finetuning tasks – it converges quickly and stays stable.
2. If you notice that certain attributes (e.g., colour contrast or subject texture) are poorly captured, try **re‑scaled OFT**.
3. For very high data regimes or when you need finer control over the rank of updates, you could experiment with adding an explicit LoRA term on top of an orthogonal rotation – this would be a direct “orthogonal + low‐rank” hybrid but has not yet been benchmarked in this paper.

In short, OFT already contains the core idea of combining an orthogonal multiplicative update with extra per‐neuron scaling; extending this idea to include an added low‐rank LoRA term would be straightforward but has not yet been reported in the literature beyond what is already shown for re‑scaled OFT.
