Answer:
**Short answer**

The paper shows that a diffusion model’s “semantic knowledge” is largely encoded in the *angles* between neurons in each linear layer.  If you change those angles arbitrarily you shred that knowledge, even if the weights stay close in Euclidean distance.  Hyperspherical energy (HE) is simply the sum of pairwise angular similarities of all neurons in a layer; it is invariant under any orthogonal transformation of that layer’s weight matrix.  Therefore, if we finetune only by applying an orthogonal matrix to every neuron (so the HE stays the same) we can rotate the entire set of neuron directions while preserving all pair‑wise angles.  This keeps the internal geometry that the pretrained model learned and, as experiments show, keeps its generative and semantic quality intact.

---

### What is hyperspherical energy?

For a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) let  
\(\hat w_i=w_i/\|w_i\|\) be the \(i\)-th neuron projected onto the unit sphere.  
The *hyperspherical energy* is

\[
\text{HE}(W)\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}
            \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\!\bigl(1-\hat w_i^T\hat w_j\bigr)^{-\frac12},
\]

which depends only on all pairwise cosine similarities \(\hat w_i^T \hat w_j\).  
Thus HE captures how *uniformly* the neurons are spread on the sphere: a high HE means most neurons are far apart (large angular separation).

---

### Why does preserving HE matter for diffusion models?

Diffusion models are essentially a stack of transformer layers that map a latent vector to its denoised version.  
Each linear transform inside these layers has already found a set of direction vectors \(\{w_i\}\) that, together with their dot‑product relationships, encode high‑level content (styles, shapes, semantics).  When we finetune a pretrained model, we want to keep *those relationships* so that:

1. **Semantic consistency** – the model still “understands” concepts it was trained on.
2. **Generalisation** – forgetting those relationships would force the model to relearn them from little data and would lead to mode collapse or catastrophic forgetting.

Because HE is exactly the fingerprint of these pairwise angular relationships, keeping it constant guarantees that the *geometry* of the space inside each layer remains unchanged.  Even if individual weight vectors move around on their sphere, as long as all pairwise angles stay the same, the layer behaves exactly like it did before.

---

### How does an orthogonal transformation preserve HE?

Let \(R \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) be any orthogonal matrix (\(R^\top R=I\)).  
If we replace \(W\) by \(W'=WR^T\), then each neuron is rotated by \(R^T\):

\[
w'_i = R\,w_i .
\]

Because rotations preserve dot products,
\(w'_i{}^T w'_j = w_i^T w_j\), every cosine similarity stays identical.  
Hence `HE(W') == HE(W)` automatically.

This invariance property is what enables Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT):  

- **Step 1:** learn an orthogonal matrix \(R_\ell\) for each layer \(\ell\).  
- **Step 2:** apply it to all neurons in that layer during forward passes.  

No gradient term is needed to keep HE; it follows from orthogonality itself.

---

### Why changing angles *only* matters

Changing angles means rotating neurons while keeping their norms unchanged—essentially moving points on a hypersphere without stretching or compressing them.  
If we were to change magnitudes as well (e.g., by scaling some neurons), the inner‑product relationships would shift because magnitude contributes to dot products. This would also change HE if magnitudes differ across neurons.

In practice:

- The authors add a small *diagonal* scaling after rotation (Re‑scaled OFT).  
  Because scaling cancels out when normalising each neuron for HE, this extra freedom does not break invariance but gives enough freedom to adapt subtle details without touching angular relations.
- They also add an ε‑constraint (`‖R−I‖≤ε`) in Constrained OFT (COFT) to bound how far one can rotate from the original configuration—this keeps training stable when data are scarce.

---

### Intuition: angles ≈ “phase”, magnitudes ≈ “energy”

The paper draws an analogy with 2‑D Fourier analysis: magnitude carries no semantic information; phase (angle) carries almost all visual structure.  
In high‑dimensional networks, each neuron acts like a basis vector pointing in some direction on a hypersphere; its angle relative to others determines what combination of semantic features it can activate.  

By rotating these basis vectors together we are simply re‑orienting our internal coordinate system—like rotating your view of an already carved sculpture—but we still see exactly the same shapes and textures because those shapes are determined by relative angles, not by absolute coordinates.

---

### Empirical evidence

- **Subject‑driven generation**: OFT/COFT keep subject identity much better than LoRA or DreamBooth while converging faster.
- **Controllable generation**: control consistency scores are higher for OFT/COFT.  
- **Finite training data**: both methods work well with tiny datasets (few images per subject).

These results show that preserving HE preserves high‑level knowledge while allowing enough flexibility to adapt new downstream signals.

---

### Bottom line

1. **HE encodes pairwise semantic relations within each layer.**  
2. **Orthogonal transforms leave all such relations intact** because they preserve all dot products/angles.  
3. **Thus finetuning only by learning orthogonal matrices keeps those learned relations**, ensuring that pre‑trained style and content knowledge survive while still permitting efficient adaptation to downstream control tasks.

So keeping hyperspherical energy unchanged guarantees that you can twist and turn neuron directions without losing what made the diffusion model powerful in the first place—its ability to generate diverse, high‑quality images conditioned on text or other signals.
