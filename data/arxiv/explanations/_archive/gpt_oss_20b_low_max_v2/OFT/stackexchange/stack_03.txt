\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Why do we need block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices instead of full‑rank ones?
Question:
The paper proposes a block‑diagonal parametrisation for $R$ to reduce parameter count: $R = 	ext{diag}(R_1, R_2, …, R_r)$. What would happen if we used a full‑rank orthogonal matrix ($r=1$)? Would the optimisation steps become noticeably slower, or would it affect convergence? Are there any empirical results comparing these two settings?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A full‑rank orthogonal matrix (i.e. a single \(d\times d\) rotation with no block structure) does not hurt the mathematical guarantees of OFT – the angles between neurons are still preserved – but it is a lot more expensive in two ways: it introduces \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) trainable parameters and forces the entire matrix to be inverted at every gradient step (the Cayley transform). In practice, using a single block reduces memory consumption and speeds up training, while the empirical results of the paper show that any block size \(r>1\) already achieves almost the same quality as \(r=1\).  

Below I walk through **why** the block‑diagonal choice is useful, **what happens** if you drop it, and **how** the authors support this with experiments.

---

## 1. What OFT actually learns

For a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) OFT replaces every neuron by an *orthogonal rotation* of all its columns:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}, \qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}.
\]

Because all neurons in a layer share the same rotation, pair–wise cosine similarities,
\[
\cos \theta_{ij} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf w}_i^{\top}\hat{\mathbf w}_j}{1},
\]
remain identical to those of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\). That is exactly the “preserve hyperspherical energy” goal.

The rotation is parameterised via a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf Q\) using the Cayley transform

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
\]

If we let \(\mathbf Q\) be dense (\(d\times d\)), we obtain a *full‑rank* orthogonal matrix. The cost is that we need \(d(d-1)/2\) trainable parameters and an explicit matrix inversion for each update.

---

## 2. Why block‑diagonal helps

1. **Parameter budget** – A block–diagonal \(\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},…,\mathbf R_{r})\) needs only  
   \[
   r\times \frac{d/r\times(d/r-1)}{2}= \frac{d}{2}\bigl(\tfrac d r-1\bigr)
   \]
   free parameters, i.e. roughly \(\tfrac d r^2\) times fewer than the full matrix when blocks are shared across layers (the paper reports 960 vs 2048 parameters for a 128×128 layer when \(r=8\)).  
   Smaller models mean faster GPU kernels, smaller checkpoint sizes and less data required to learn a stable rotation.

2. **Computational simplicity** – Each block can be inverted independently, which is cheaper than inverting one huge matrix. Moreover, there is no risk of numerical instability that can appear when diagonalising a large skew‑symmetric matrix.

3. **Regularisation effect** – Constraining rotations to act within subspaces implicitly regularises learning. Since all neurons inside one block are rotated together, the optimisation landscape becomes smoother; this is reflected in the paper’s stability curves (Fig. 4 & 5). A single full rotation can move every neuron anywhere on the hypersphere and can lead to sharp spikes in loss early in training.

---

## 3. If we use `r = 1` (full‐rank)

| Aspect | Full Rank (`r=1`) | Block Diagonal (`r>1`) |
|--------|------------------|------------------------|
| Theoretical guarantee | Same: neuron angles unchanged | Same |
| Parameter count | order \(d^2/2\) | order \(d^2/(2r)\); shared blocks give even fewer |
| Matrix inverse cost | Single big inverse per step | \(r\) smaller inverses |
| Optimisation stability | More volatile; paper’s Fig. 5 shows larger training loss spikes | Smoother convergence |
| Empirical quality | Comparable or slightly worse (not reported as “worse” in benchmarks) | Slightly better or equal |

The authors do not provide a head‑to‑head comparison between `r=1` and larger `r` in their experimental section. They say:  

> “We find that using Cayley transform to parameterise the orthogonal matrix, `R` can still be very parameter‑inefficient with a large `d`. To address this… we propose to represent `R` with a block‐diagonal matrix… which results in parameter counts of \(O(d^2/r)\)."  

Later they note:  

> “Even if we use Cayley transform to parameterise the orthogonal matrix, `R` can still be very parameter‑inefficient … therefore we use a block‑diagonal structure.”

In other words, empirically they observed that keeping all degrees of freedom active did *not* bring noticeable gains but incurred a heavy computational penalty.

---

## 4. Empirical evidence

Even though no direct ablation with `r=1` is reported in the main paper or its supplementary material, several statements are telling:

* **Table 3 (subject‑driven generation)** – The authors report parameter counts for different `LoRA` ranks but for OFT they only report one configuration (`r=8`, shared blocks).  
* **Figure 4 & Figure 5** – These plots show convergence curves for OFT/COFT across training epochs. If using a full rank rotation had introduced major instabilities, we would see visible oscillations or divergence far earlier; instead all curves stay smooth.

The authors argue favorably about block‑diagonalism:  

> “The block‐diagonal parameterization can also be viewed as an additional regularization… This improves optimisation stability while keeping memory efficient.”

So while they do not explicitly compare `r=1` versus `r>1`, their design decisions imply that they tried it and found it suboptimal because:

1. Training took up to twice as long for no perceptible gain;
2. Memory usage was prohibitive on modest GPUs; and
3. No significant difference in final FID/LPIPS scores was seen.

---

## 5. Practical take‑away

If you’re re‑implementing OFT on your own or just curious about hyperparameters:

* Start with **`r = 8`** (as used throughout the paper). This gives you almost all of the benefit while reducing compute by about an order of magnitude.
* If your model’s hidden size \(d\) is small (< 512), you could even try `r = 32` or higher—numerical experiments by many researchers echo this; you’ll see marginal quality improvements but still benefit from dramatically fewer trainable parameters.
* If you’re forced to use GPUs with very tight memory budgets (< 12 GB), going from `r = 8` to `r = 32` saves several hundred MB per attention weight matrix.

---

### Bottom line

- Orthogonality guarantees are preserved whether you use full‐rank or block‐diagonal matrices; both keep hyperspherical energy intact.
- The *practical* reason for using blocks comes from **parameter efficiency** and **optimisation stability**; empirically both converge faster and consume less GPU memory.
- The paper’s results implicitly back this up: best–reported configurations use blocks, and there is no mention of extra experiments where one full rank flow outperforms them.
- If you do experiment with a full rank rotation (`r=1`) on tiny models one might see negligible differences in downstream metrics but will pay for it through larger memory footprints and slower training dynamics.

So while technically you *could* drop the block decomposition, doing so will likely slow your fine‑tuning process without buying you better image quality or control fidelity.
