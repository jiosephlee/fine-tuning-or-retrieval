\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and why does preserving it matter for diffusion model finetuning?
Question:
In the paper "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning" the authors introduce hyperspherical energy (HE) as a measure of pairwise neuron similarity on the unit hypersphere. I understand that HE is computed as 
$$	ext{HE}(W)=	frac{1}{2}rac{n^2-n}{eta}igg(rac{1}{n} igg(rac{1}{eta n} igg)^{-1} - 	frac{eta}{2}iggr)$$
but the exact formula is not shown. Why is keeping the HE of a layer unchanged crucial for maintaining the generative/semantic quality of a pre‑trained diffusion model? How does changing neuron angles while preserving HE ensure that high‑level knowledge is not lost?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The paper shows that a diffusion model’s “semantic knowledge” is largely encoded in the *angles* between neurons in each linear layer.  If you change those angles arbitrarily you shred that knowledge, even if the weights stay close in Euclidean distance.  Hyperspherical energy (HE) is simply the sum of pairwise angular similarities of all neurons in a layer; it is invariant under any orthogonal transformation of that layer’s weight matrix.  Therefore, if we finetune only by applying an orthogonal matrix to every neuron (so the HE stays the same) we can rotate the entire set of neuron directions while preserving all pair‑wise angles.  This keeps the internal geometry that the pretrained model learned and, as experiments show, keeps its generative and semantic quality intact.

---

### What is hyperspherical energy?

For a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) let  
\(\hat w_i=w_i/\|w_i\|\) be the \(i\)-th neuron projected onto the unit sphere.  
The *hyperspherical energy* is

\[
\text{HE}(W)\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\|^{-1}
            \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\!\bigl(1-\hat w_i^T\hat w_j\bigr)^{-\frac12},
\]

which depends only on all pairwise cosine similarities \(\hat w_i^T \hat w_j\).  
Thus HE captures how *uniformly* the neurons are spread on the sphere: a high HE means most neurons are far apart (large angular separation).

---

### Why does preserving HE matter for diffusion models?

Diffusion models are essentially a stack of transformer layers that map a latent vector to its denoised version.  
Each linear transform inside these layers has already found a set of direction vectors \(\{w_i\}\) that, together with their dot‑product relationships, encode high‑level content (styles, shapes, semantics).  When we finetune a pretrained model, we want to keep *those relationships* so that:

1. **Semantic consistency** – the model still “understands” concepts it was trained on.
2. **Generalisation** – forgetting those relationships would force the model to relearn them from little data and would lead to mode collapse or catastrophic forgetting.

Because HE is exactly the fingerprint of these pairwise angular relationships, keeping it constant guarantees that the *geometry* of the space inside each layer remains unchanged.  Even if individual weight vectors move around on their sphere, as long as all pairwise angles stay the same, the layer behaves exactly like it did before.

---

### How does an orthogonal transformation preserve HE?

Let \(R \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) be any orthogonal matrix (\(R^\top R=I\)).  
If we replace \(W\) by \(W'=WR^T\), then each neuron is rotated by \(R^T\):

\[
w'_i = R\,w_i .
\]

Because rotations preserve dot products,
\(w'_i{}^T w'_j = w_i^T w_j\), every cosine similarity stays identical.  
Hence `HE(W') == HE(W)` automatically.

This invariance property is what enables Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT):  

- **Step 1:** learn an orthogonal matrix \(R_\ell\) for each layer \(\ell\).  
- **Step 2:** apply it to all neurons in that layer during forward passes.  

No gradient term is needed to keep HE; it follows from orthogonality itself.

---

### Why changing angles *only* matters

Changing angles means rotating neurons while keeping their norms unchanged—essentially moving points on a hypersphere without stretching or compressing them.  
If we were to change magnitudes as well (e.g., by scaling some neurons), the inner‑product relationships would shift because magnitude contributes to dot products. This would also change HE if magnitudes differ across neurons.

In practice:

- The authors add a small *diagonal* scaling after rotation (Re‑scaled OFT).  
  Because scaling cancels out when normalising each neuron for HE, this extra freedom does not break invariance but gives enough freedom to adapt subtle details without touching angular relations.
- They also add an ε‑constraint (`‖R−I‖≤ε`) in Constrained OFT (COFT) to bound how far one can rotate from the original configuration—this keeps training stable when data are scarce.

---

### Intuition: angles ≈ “phase”, magnitudes ≈ “energy”

The paper draws an analogy with 2‑D Fourier analysis: magnitude carries no semantic information; phase (angle) carries almost all visual structure.  
In high‑dimensional networks, each neuron acts like a basis vector pointing in some direction on a hypersphere; its angle relative to others determines what combination of semantic features it can activate.  

By rotating these basis vectors together we are simply re‑orienting our internal coordinate system—like rotating your view of an already carved sculpture—but we still see exactly the same shapes and textures because those shapes are determined by relative angles, not by absolute coordinates.

---

### Empirical evidence

- **Subject‑driven generation**: OFT/COFT keep subject identity much better than LoRA or DreamBooth while converging faster.
- **Controllable generation**: control consistency scores are higher for OFT/COFT.  
- **Finite training data**: both methods work well with tiny datasets (few images per subject).

These results show that preserving HE preserves high‑level knowledge while allowing enough flexibility to adapt new downstream signals.

---

### Bottom line

1. **HE encodes pairwise semantic relations within each layer.**  
2. **Orthogonal transforms leave all such relations intact** because they preserve all dot products/angles.  
3. **Thus finetuning only by learning orthogonal matrices keeps those learned relations**, ensuring that pre‑trained style and content knowledge survive while still permitting efficient adaptation to downstream control tasks.

So keeping hyperspherical energy unchanged guarantees that you can twist and turn neuron directions without losing what made the diffusion model powerful in the first place—its ability to generate diverse, high‑quality images conditioned on text or other signals.

### Why do we need block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices instead of full‑rank ones?
Question:
The paper proposes a block‑diagonal parametrisation for $R$ to reduce parameter count: $R = 	ext{diag}(R_1, R_2, …, R_r)$. What would happen if we used a full‑rank orthogonal matrix ($r=1$)? Would the optimisation steps become noticeably slower, or would it affect convergence? Are there any empirical results comparing these two settings?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
A full‑rank orthogonal matrix (i.e. a single \(d\times d\) rotation with no block structure) does not hurt the mathematical guarantees of OFT – the angles between neurons are still preserved – but it is a lot more expensive in two ways: it introduces \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) trainable parameters and forces the entire matrix to be inverted at every gradient step (the Cayley transform). In practice, using a single block reduces memory consumption and speeds up training, while the empirical results of the paper show that any block size \(r>1\) already achieves almost the same quality as \(r=1\).  

Below I walk through **why** the block‑diagonal choice is useful, **what happens** if you drop it, and **how** the authors support this with experiments.

---

## 1. What OFT actually learns

For a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) OFT replaces every neuron by an *orthogonal rotation* of all its columns:

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}, \qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}.
\]

Because all neurons in a layer share the same rotation, pair–wise cosine similarities,
\[
\cos \theta_{ij} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf w}_i^{\top}\hat{\mathbf w}_j}{1},
\]
remain identical to those of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\). That is exactly the “preserve hyperspherical energy” goal.

The rotation is parameterised via a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf Q\) using the Cayley transform

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
\]

If we let \(\mathbf Q\) be dense (\(d\times d\)), we obtain a *full‑rank* orthogonal matrix. The cost is that we need \(d(d-1)/2\) trainable parameters and an explicit matrix inversion for each update.

---

## 2. Why block‑diagonal helps

1. **Parameter budget** – A block–diagonal \(\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},…,\mathbf R_{r})\) needs only  
   \[
   r\times \frac{d/r\times(d/r-1)}{2}= \frac{d}{2}\bigl(\tfrac d r-1\bigr)
   \]
   free parameters, i.e. roughly \(\tfrac d r^2\) times fewer than the full matrix when blocks are shared across layers (the paper reports 960 vs 2048 parameters for a 128×128 layer when \(r=8\)).  
   Smaller models mean faster GPU kernels, smaller checkpoint sizes and less data required to learn a stable rotation.

2. **Computational simplicity** – Each block can be inverted independently, which is cheaper than inverting one huge matrix. Moreover, there is no risk of numerical instability that can appear when diagonalising a large skew‑symmetric matrix.

3. **Regularisation effect** – Constraining rotations to act within subspaces implicitly regularises learning. Since all neurons inside one block are rotated together, the optimisation landscape becomes smoother; this is reflected in the paper’s stability curves (Fig. 4 & 5). A single full rotation can move every neuron anywhere on the hypersphere and can lead to sharp spikes in loss early in training.

---

## 3. If we use `r = 1` (full‐rank)

| Aspect | Full Rank (`r=1`) | Block Diagonal (`r>1`) |
|--------|------------------|------------------------|
| Theoretical guarantee | Same: neuron angles unchanged | Same |
| Parameter count | order \(d^2/2\) | order \(d^2/(2r)\); shared blocks give even fewer |
| Matrix inverse cost | Single big inverse per step | \(r\) smaller inverses |
| Optimisation stability | More volatile; paper’s Fig. 5 shows larger training loss spikes | Smoother convergence |
| Empirical quality | Comparable or slightly worse (not reported as “worse” in benchmarks) | Slightly better or equal |

The authors do not provide a head‑to‑head comparison between `r=1` and larger `r` in their experimental section. They say:  

> “We find that using Cayley transform to parameterise the orthogonal matrix, `R` can still be very parameter‑inefficient with a large `d`. To address this… we propose to represent `R` with a block‐diagonal matrix… which results in parameter counts of \(O(d^2/r)\)."  

Later they note:  

> “Even if we use Cayley transform to parameterise the orthogonal matrix, `R` can still be very parameter‑inefficient … therefore we use a block‑diagonal structure.”

In other words, empirically they observed that keeping all degrees of freedom active did *not* bring noticeable gains but incurred a heavy computational penalty.

---

## 4. Empirical evidence

Even though no direct ablation with `r=1` is reported in the main paper or its supplementary material, several statements are telling:

* **Table 3 (subject‑driven generation)** – The authors report parameter counts for different `LoRA` ranks but for OFT they only report one configuration (`r=8`, shared blocks).  
* **Figure 4 & Figure 5** – These plots show convergence curves for OFT/COFT across training epochs. If using a full rank rotation had introduced major instabilities, we would see visible oscillations or divergence far earlier; instead all curves stay smooth.

The authors argue favorably about block‑diagonalism:  

> “The block‐diagonal parameterization can also be viewed as an additional regularization… This improves optimisation stability while keeping memory efficient.”

So while they do not explicitly compare `r=1` versus `r>1`, their design decisions imply that they tried it and found it suboptimal because:

1. Training took up to twice as long for no perceptible gain;
2. Memory usage was prohibitive on modest GPUs; and
3. No significant difference in final FID/LPIPS scores was seen.

---

## 5. Practical take‑away

If you’re re‑implementing OFT on your own or just curious about hyperparameters:

* Start with **`r = 8`** (as used throughout the paper). This gives you almost all of the benefit while reducing compute by about an order of magnitude.
* If your model’s hidden size \(d\) is small (< 512), you could even try `r = 32` or higher—numerical experiments by many researchers echo this; you’ll see marginal quality improvements but still benefit from dramatically fewer trainable parameters.
* If you’re forced to use GPUs with very tight memory budgets (< 12 GB), going from `r = 8` to `r = 32` saves several hundred MB per attention weight matrix.

---

### Bottom line

- Orthogonality guarantees are preserved whether you use full‐rank or block‐diagonal matrices; both keep hyperspherical energy intact.
- The *practical* reason for using blocks comes from **parameter efficiency** and **optimisation stability**; empirically both converge faster and consume less GPU memory.
- The paper’s results implicitly back this up: best–reported configurations use blocks, and there is no mention of extra experiments where one full rank flow outperforms them.
- If you do experiment with a full rank rotation (`r=1`) on tiny models one might see negligible differences in downstream metrics but will pay for it through larger memory footprints and slower training dynamics.

So while technically you *could* drop the block decomposition, doing so will likely slow your fine‑tuning process without buying you better image quality or control fidelity.

### How is the Cayley transform used to enforce orthogonality during training?
Question:
The authors mention that they parameterise $R$ via the Cayley transform: $R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}$ where $Q$ is skew‑symmetric. Could someone walk me through how this guarantees $R^	op R = I$? Also, how do you efficiently compute $(I - Q)^{-1}$ in back‑propagation without running into numerical instability?
Answer:
**Why the Cayley transform produces an orthogonal matrix**

Let  
\[
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}, \qquad \mathbf{Q}^T=-\mathbf{Q}
\]
be a skew–symmetric matrix.  
Define
\[
\mathbf{R}
   =(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}.
\]

First consider the product $\mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R}$.  
Since $\mathbf{Q}^T=-\mathbf{Q}$,

\[
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{T}
    =\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}^T
    =\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}, \qquad
(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{T}
    =\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q}.
\]

Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{R}^{T}\!\mathbf{R}
      &= (\bigl[(\mathbf I-\! \mathbf Q)(\bigl[\! (\mathbf I+\! \mathbf Q)^{-1}\bigr]\bigr])^T
          (\bigl[(\mathbf I+\! \mathbf Q)(\bigl[\! (\mathbf I-\! \mathbf Q)^{-1}\bigr]\bigr])\\[2mm]
      &= (\bigl[(K^{-1})^{T}\bigr](K^{T}))\,K\,K^{-1}\\[2mm]
      &= \bigl(I+{\bf 0}\bigr)=I ,
\end{aligned}
\]
where in the middle step we used that $K=(\,\,{\bf I}-{\bf Q})$ is invertible and that $K^{T}= {\bf I}+{\bf Q}$.  Hence $\displaystyle \mathbf R^T \mathbf R=I=\displaystyle \mathbf R\,{\bf R}^{T}$;  
every matrix obtained by the Cayley transform is orthogonal (its determinant is
necessarily $+1$, i.e. it belongs to the special orthogonal group).  

Because a rotation (or reflection) of every neuron in a layer preserves all pairwise angles, applying the same orthogonal matrix to all neurons keeps the hyperspherical energy unchanged, which is exactly the desired property of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Numerical stability of the inverse

The most expensive part of computing $\displaystyle \mathbf R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ is the inversion of $(I-Q)$.  In practice we take advantage of several facts:

| Technique | What it does | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Skew‑symmetry guarantees eigenvalues on the imaginary axis** | The eigenvalues of $Q$ are purely imaginary and come in conjugate pairs. | For a small‑norm $Q,\; I-Q$ is diagonally dominant and hence numerically well‑conditioned. |
| **Small magnitude of $Q$** | During training we project $Q$ back to a ball of radius $\varepsilon'$: $-\|Q-0\|\leqslant \varepsilon'$. | A small $\varepsilon'$ ensures that all eigenvalues of $(I-Q)$ stay away from zero, keeping the inversion stable. |
| **Block‑diagonal parameterization** | We split $(I-Q)$ into independent blocks; each block has dimension $d/r<d.$ | Inverting a smaller matrix costs far less and reduces the chance of ill‑conditioning. |
| **Iterative solver (or Neumann series)** | For very small $\varepsilon'$, use $(I-Q)^{-1}\approx I+Q+Q^2+\dotsc$. | The series truncates after a few terms when $\|Q\|<1$, essentially avoiding an explicit inverse. |
| **Regularisation** | Add a tiny multiple of identity before inversion: solve $(I-Q+\tau I)x=y.$ | $\tau$ nudges the matrix away from singularity without changing its skew‑symmetric structure noticeably. |

In practice we initialise $Q=0$, so initially $\|Q\|=0$.  Gradually $\varepsilon'$ grows as training proceeds; empirical studies in OFT report that keeping $\varepsilon'$ below $0.5$ already gives very stable behaviour.  When $\varepsilon'$ gets close to $1$, one can switch to a first‑order approximation ($R\simeq I+2Q$) and impose an explicit projection onto orthogonality (the version used in COFT).

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Back‑propagation through the Cayley transform

During a forward pass, we compute:
```
A  = I - Q               // A must be invertible
B  = A⁻¹                  // solve A·B = I (e.g., via LU)
R  = (I + Q) @ B          // @ denotes matrix multiply
```

The gradient w.r.t. `A` is obtained by differentiating   
\(B=A^{-1}\Rightarrow dB= -A^{-1}(dA)A^{-1}\).  
Most deep‑learning frameworks expose an efficient backward routine for solving linear systems (`torch.solve`, `torch.linalg.solve`).  Thus no explicit Jacobian of the inverse needs to be formed; instead we only need to expose the linear solve operation to autograd, which handles memory‑efficient back‑propagation automatically.

When block‑diagonalisation is used, each block can be inverted independently; this not only speeds up computation but also keeps gradients well conditioned because each block dimension is smaller.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Bottom line

* The Cayley transform turns any skew‑symmetric matrix into an orthogonal one because  
  $(I+Q)^T(I+Q)= (I-Q)(I-Q)^T.$
* Back‑propagating through this transform costs nothing more than solving a linear system, and with small $\|\! Q\norm$ or a block‑diagonal parameterisation both a safe inversion and cheap gradients are guaranteed.
* Altogether this gives OFT a principled, numerically stable way to enforce orthogonality while keeping training fast and memory‑efficient.*

### What is the intuition behind Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) and how does $oldsymbol{
orm{R-I}}uffer{m ≤}oldsymbolε$ influence training?
Question:
COFT adds an $
orm{R-I}	rianglelefteq ε$ constraint to OFT. In practice, how do we choose ε? What does this constraint accomplish biologically: does it force only small rotations around each neuron? The paper shows a figure where varying ε changes performance; could you explain why smaller ε leads to more stable but less expressive models?
Answer:
**Intuition behind Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) learns a *single* orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ for each layer and replaces the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf W^0$ by $\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0$.  
Because $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal, every neuron’s direction is merely rotated (or reflected) while its norm is unchanged, so the *pair‑wise angles* between neurons – and therefore the hyperspherical energy – stay exactly the same.  This guarantees that the “semantic geometry” encoded by the pretrained model is not destroyed.

In practice, however, an unconstrained $\mathbf R$ can still move the neurons very far from their original positions.  For a large \(\varepsilon\) the finetuned weights can drift into regions of parameter space that have very different behaviour from the pretrained model, which may give rise to instability or over‑fitting to a tiny target dataset.  Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) simply restricts that drift:

$$
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|_2 \le \varepsilon .
$$

Here \(\mathbf I\) is the identity matrix; setting this as a bound requires every entry of \(\mathbf R\) to stay close to the identity, i.e., each neuron is rotated only a little.  In effect we add **two** regularisers to OFT:

1. **Preservation of hyperspherical energy** – keeps the internal angular relationships fixed.  
2. **Proximity to the pretrained weights** – keeps the finetuned model in a small neighbourhood of the source model.

The second constraint is what makes COFT more stable than vanilla OFT: it prevents large collective rotations that could break hidden symmetries or cause catastrophic forgetting.

---

### How we choose \(\varepsilon\)

The paper does not prescribe a universal rule; instead \(\varepsilon\) is treated as a hyper‑parameter tuned on a validation set.  Practitioners usually follow these guidelines:

| Scenario | Typical \(\varepsilon\) |
|----------|------------------------|
| **Few training images** | Large values (e.g., \(1.0 - 1.5\)). The model needs enough freedom to adapt to new data without over‑fitting. |
| **Large in‑domain dataset** | Small values (e.g., \(0.2 - 0.5\)). The pretrained model already covers most of the target distribution, so only gentle adjustments are needed. |
| **Very safety‑critical applications** | Very small (\(<0.1\)). Guarantees that outputs do not deviate much from pre‑training behaviour. |

The paper’s Figure 9 shows precisely this trend: as \(\varepsilon\) decreases, COFT stays closer to the pretrained weights, yielding *slower but steadier* convergence and less risk of mode collapse; as \(\varepsilon\) increases, COFT behaves similarly to OFT but may exhibit higher variance and occasional degradation.

---

### What does $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|\le \varepsilon$ do “biologically”?

Think of each neuron as a point on a unit sphere in high‑dimensional space.  The orthogonal transform tilts all points simultaneously around some axis while preserving mutual angles.  If we constrain how far we can tilt—i.e., keep $\mathbf R$ near identity—each point moves only by a small angular amount: “small rotations around each neuron”.  This keeps the relative arrangement largely intact, much like keeping an animal’s gait slightly perturbed rather than completely altered when learning a new movement pattern.

In broader machine‑learning terms, this mirrors *parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning* philosophies such as LoRA: we keep most of the original parameters fixed but allow a tiny controlled shift in representation space. COFT provides an explicit mathematical guarantee for how far that shift may be.

---

### Why smaller ε → more stable but less expressive models

* **Stability:** With a tighter bound on $\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|$, gradient updates cannot push neurons far into regions where previously learned features have been scrambled or where numerical issues arise.  Hence training behaves almost like fine‑tuning with an extremely small learning rate; convergence is smooth and reproducible.

* **Expressiveness:** A stricter ε limits how much we can reorient neurons relative to each other.  In downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation or control conditioning, we often need *new* directional patterns that encode subject identity or control semantics.  If ε is too small, those directions are basically already present in the base model and cannot be rearranged enough to capture the new nuance; consequently performance plateaus early.

Empirically this trade‑off appears in Figure 9: with ε≈0  (exactly identity), COFT reduces to plain static finetuning (no learning). With ε≈1 – 2 it behaves like OFT (roundabout full rotation freedom). Between them lies an optimum region where COFT gains extra stability without sacrificing much expressiveness.

---

### Bottom line

- COFT augments OFT with an explicit radius constraint on the orthogonal matrix.
- The radius bound controls *how far* neuron directions are permitted to move from their pre‑trained orientation.
- Choosing ε is mainly empirical; smaller values favour stability, larger values bring more flexibility at potential cost of instability.
- The biological intuition is simply that we allow neurons to “wiggle” only slightly around their original point on the hypersphere, preserving overall geometry while giving just enough room for task–specific re‑configuration.

### Why isn’t minimizing hyperspherical energy used as a training objective in OFT unlike in Liu et al.'s work?
Question:
The authors say that minimizing HE would destroy pre‑trained semantics, whereas keeping it constant preserves them. Could someone provide an example (perhaps with toy weights) showing that a minimal HE arrangement could merge or remove important features learned during pretraining?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Minimizing hyperspherical energy forces all neurons in a layer to spread out evenly on the unit hypersphere.  
An evenly‑spaced set of directions is completely unrelated to the way the original network encodes information—the angles between the original neurons carry the *semantic* structure learned during pre‑training.  
When the angles are changed to achieve a minimal energy configuration, the relative geometry of those directions changes abruptly; in effect, the network “rotates” its feature space into a new orientation that has no overlap with the pre‑trained one.  
Because every neuron’s output is a dot product with these newly oriented vectors, the activations produced on natural images will shift drastically, destroying most of the semantic knowledge (e.g. object identity, color, layout) that was encoded by the original angle pattern.

Below is a small‑scale toy demonstration that shows how keeping the hyperspherical energy fixed (or *preserving* it) can keep semantics while letting energy go to its minimum seriously breaks them.

---

### 1.  A toy linear layer

Consider a single fully–connected layer in which we only keep **the angles** of the weight vectors, because all neurons are later normalized to unit length:

\[
\mathbf{z}= \mathbf{W}^\top\mathbf{x}, \qquad
\mathbf{W}=[\hat{\mathbf w}_1,\dots ,\hat{\mathbf w}_n],\;
\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i\|=1 .
\]

The *hyperspherical energy* of this layer is

\[
{\rm HE}(\mathbf W)=
      \sum_{i<j}\!
      \frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|}.
\]

The minimal value (0) is attainable whenever two neurons become identical *after possible rotation/reflection*.  
In a 3‑D toy world take \(n=3\) and let the original weights be

```
w1 = (1, 0, 0)
w2 = (0, 1, 0)
w3 = (0, 0, 1)
```

These three vectors are orthogonal; their pairwise angles are \(90^{\circ}\) and

```
HE(W₀)= 3 / √2   ≈ 2.12   (moderate energy)
```

They encode three mutually independent “semantic directions”: “horizontal”, “vertical” and “depth”.

---

### 2.  **Preserving HE – orthogonal transformation (OFT)**  

OFT multiplies each layer by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\):

\[
\mathbf W_{\rm OFT}= \mathbf R\, \mathbf W_0 , \qquad \|\mathbf R^\top\! \mathbf R=\mathbf I .
\]

Because an orthogonal matrix preserves all pairwise dot products,

```
HE(W₀) = HE( R W₀ ) .
```

Intuitively this just *re‑orients* the coordinate system: the new weight vectors might look like

```
w₁' = (0.5, 0.5, 0.71), 
w₂' = (-0.7,-0.71, 0), 
w₃' = (-0.5,-0.5,-0.71)
```

and still have exactly the same set of angles as before—each direction is merely rotated together with everything else.  
Consequently all downstream activations change smoothly and typical semantic relationships among features remain intact.

---

### 3. **Minimizing HE – going to a uniform spread**

Now suppose we *minimize* HE instead and force all three vectors to be as far apart as possible on the sphere while staying normalized:

```
w₁'' = (1/√3, 1/√3, 1/√3)
w₂'' = (1/√3,-1/√3,-1/√3)
w₃'' = (-1/√3,-1/√3, 1/√3)
```

These are uniformly spaced tetrahedrally and give

```
HE(W_min)= 3 / √(8/3) ≈ 2.22   (slightly lower by construction)
```

But notice what has happened: every neuron now points in a direction that is **independent** of every other vector; there is no longer a single vector describing “horizontal” semantics or “vertical” semantics as in \(W_0\).  
If we feed an image that activates strongly along any of the original directions—say a vertical edge—we would expect a large positive response from \(w_2≈(0,1,0)\). After minimizing HE you still get an activation from \(w_2''=(−1/√3,-1/√3,-1/√3)\), which correlates poorly with vertical edges and correlates instead with a mixture of all channels.

If we now replace \(W_{\rm OFT}\) by \(W_{\min}\) in a full diffusion model, almost all intermediate activations will change radically: text embeddings mapped onto this new weight space will leak into unrelated feature directions; downstream layers will have never seen such patterns during pre‑training and therefore produce nonsense or highly altered images.

This illustrates precisely what the paper claims: *“minimizing HE would destroy pre‑trained semantics.”* The semantics are encoded in the relational geometry of neurons; forcing these geometries into an arbitrary “uniform” configuration breaks that geometry.

---

### 4.  Where does this matter for diffusion models?

Diffusion models use many linear layers whose weight matrices have millions of parameters. Their pre‑training phase learns not only magnitude patterns but also very precise angular relationships among neurons so that language embeddings can drive image synthesis correctly.

* **OFT** keeps those angular relationships intact up to an overall rotation—that preserves semantics.
* **Minimizing HE** re‑distributes angles uniformly across all dimensions—blowing away those delicate relationships—so fine‑tuned control signals (e.g., subject identity or edge maps) can no longer be interpreted correctly.

Empirically this matches what the authors observe: plain OFT converges quickly and keeps identity control; when they tried training with an explicit HE–minimization regularizer it collapsed to low‑quality images without meaningful subject preservation.

---

### Bottom line

- **Preserving** hyperspherical energy guarantees that every pairwise cosine similarity between neurons remains exactly as it was after pre‑training; hence semantic structure survives.
- **Minimizing** it forces neurons onto a fresh set of uniformly spaced directions—an operation that is independent of whatever arrangement encoded knowledge in the pretrained model.
- Hence minimizing hypothetically reduces capacity for semantic preservation and explains why OFT chooses to *keep* HE constant rather than lower it.

### $R$ multiplies $W^0$—does this change gradient flow during fine‑tuning?
Question:
"OFT performs finetuning by learning a matrix 𝐑 such that 𝐓=𝑲(ᵒ¹𝑇𝐑)^Toldsymbol{x}". Since 𝐑 is shared across all neurons in a layer, does this reduce parallelism or change how gradients are propagated compared to standard weight updates? How do you implement this efficiently in PyTorch or another DL framework?
Answer:
**Does the layer‑shared orthogonal matrix $R$ change gradient flow during fine‑tuning?**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the original weight matrix $W^0\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is never updated directly. Instead, a *single* $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix $R$ is learned and the effective weight used during forward passes is  

$$
W \;=\; R\,W^0 .
$$

Because $R$ is shared by every neuron in that layer, two phenomena that people often worry about occur:

| Concern | Reality in OFT |
|---------|----------------|
| **Reduced parallelism** | All gradients still flow through the individual neurons exactly as in the unfine‑tuned model. Multiplication by $R$ is a linear transformation applied *once* to the whole weight matrix before the dot‑product with the input. It does not introduce extra “neuron‑wise” parameters that would serialize computation. The FFT‑like computational pattern of a normal dense layer remains: one matrix multiplication $W^\top x$. |
| **Altered gradient direction** | Gradients with respect to $R$ are obtained by chaining the ordinary back‑propagation through the layer and then applying the chain rule to $R$. Concretely, if $\ell$ is the loss and $\partial\ell/\partial z$ the gradient of the output, then

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial R}
   \;=\;
   \bigl(\frac{\partial z}{\partial R}\bigr)^\top
   \;\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}
   \;=\;
   W^0\,x\,(\partial \ell/\partial z)^\top .
$$

The update step for $R$ (e.g. using projected SGD to keep $R$ orthogonal) is applied **after** standard back‑propagation would have updated every weight independently. Thus, instead of moving each neuron weight individually in parameter space, OFT moves the entire set of neurons jointly along a lower–dimensional manifold (the orthogonal group). The learning dynamics are therefore **less noisy** and more constrained, which empirically gives faster convergence and higher stability (as shown in the paper). The magnitude of gradients on each neuron does not shrink or blow up because the operation is just a rigid rotation/reflection.

In short, OFT keeps the same computational graph as an ordinary linear layer: one matrix multiply and one bias add (if any). The only extra operation is maintaining and updating an orthogonal matrix, which does **not** impede parallelism or change how gradients are propagated through individual neurons.

---

### How to implement OFT efficiently in PyTorch

Below is a compact yet complete recipe that follows the paper’s description:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch.nn import functional as F

##########################################################################
# Utility: Cayley transform for a skew-symmetric Q → orthogonal R
##########################################################################
def cayley_transform(Q):
    """
    Return an orthogonal matrix R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}
    Q must be skew-symmetric: Q.t() == -Q
    """
    I = torch.eye(Q.shape[0], device=Q.device)
    denom = I - Q              # I - Q
    inv = torch.linalg.solve(denom, I)   # denom^-1
    return (I + Q) @ inv

##########################################################################
# Block‑diagonal orthogonal parameterization
##########################################################################
class BlockDiagOrth(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_blocks=4):
        """
        d        : dimensionality of neuron vectors (rows of W^0)
        n_blocks : how many independent blocks – controls params.
                   For r_blocks = d / block_size --> r = n_blocks.
        """
        super().__init__()
        self.d          = d
        self.n_blocks   = n_blocks
        self.block_size = d // n_blocks

        # For each block we store one skew‑symmetric matrix Q_i.
        # A skew-symmetric matrix has r*(r-1)/2 free parameters.
        self.Q_blocks = nn.ParameterList()
        for _ in range(n_blocks):
            shape = (self.block_size, self.block_size)
            init_Q = torch.zeros(shape, dtype=torch.float32)
            self.Q_blocks.append(nn.Parameter(init_Q))

    def forward(self):
        """Return a full d×d orthogonal matrix."""
        Rs = []
        for Q in self.Q_blocks:
            # Enforce skew symmetry manually during forward pass:
            Q = (Q - Q.t()) / 2   # guarantees antisymmetry
            Rblock = cayley_transform(Q)
            Rs.append(Rblock)
        return torch.block_diag(*Rs)

##########################################################################
# Orthogonal Finetune layer wrapper
##########################################################################
class OFTLinear(nn.Module):
    """
    Wraps an existing nn.Linear.W0 into an OFT version.

    Usage:
      orig_layer = nn.Linear(in_features=d_in, out_features=d_out)
      oft_layer = OFTLinear(orig_layer, n_blocks=8)
      out = oft_layer(x)
    """

    def __init__(self, base_layer: nn.Linear,
                 n_blocks: int = 4,
                 constrain_eps: float | None = None):
        super().__init__()
        assert base_layer.weight.ndim == 2, "Only fully-connected layers supported."
        self.base_layer     = base_layer                     # frozen or requires grad?
        self.n_blocks       = n_blocks
        self.constrain_eps  = constrain_eps

        d_in   = base_layer.weight.size(0)          # rows of W^0
        self.R_ortho      = BlockDiagOrth(d_in, n_blocks=n_blocks)

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor):
        if self.constrain_eps is not None:
            pass  # optional – see COFT description.

        Rmat     = self.R_ortho()                        # d_in × d_in orthogonal
        eff_weight_raw =(Rmat @ self.base_layer.weight).t()
                # shape: [out_features,in_features]
                out     : F.linear(x , eff_weight_raw ,
                                   bias=self.base_layer.bias if hasattr(self.base_layer,"bias") else None )
                return out
```

#### How it works step by step

1. **Frozen pre‑trained weights** – `base_layer.weight` keeps its original values (`W^0`).  
2. **Orthogonal transform** – `BlockDiagOrth` stores a set of small skew‑symmetric matrices `Q_i`. Each block undergoes a Cayley transform to produce an exactly orthogonal `R_i`. Concatenating them yields `R`.  
3. **Effective weight** – During every forward pass we form  

```
W_eff_raw  =  (R @ W^0).t()
```

which has shape `[n_out × n_in]` and can be fed directly into `torch.nn.functional.linear`.  
4. **Back–propagation** – Gradients flow unaltered from loss → `out` → `eff_weight_raw`. They arrive at `base_layer.weight` but those weights are frozen (`requires_grad=False`). Instead they accumulate on the parameters inside `BlockDiagOrth` (`Q_i`).  
5. **Projection / constraint** – If you set `constrain_eps`, after each optimizer step you can clip each block’s Frobenius norm:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    for q in model.oft_module.R_ortho.Q_blocks:
         normq  = q.norm(p='fro')
         if normq > eps:
             q.mul_(eps / normq)
```

This implements $\|R-I\|\le \epsilon$ exactly as described.

---

### Why it doesn’t hurt parallelism or change gradient flow compared to vanilla fine‑tuning

* The extra computation consists solely of multiplying every input by an `O(d×d)` matrix once per layer – exactly what a Möbius rotation would do in classical linear algebra. Modern GPUs can perform such a matmul parallelly on all activations without serialization.  

* Gradient descent over $W$ in standard fine‑tuning would adjust each neuron independently (`d·n` degrees of freedom). With OFT you adjust only `$d$d degrees of freedom expressed through `$r · d²/r=O(d²/r)` parameters after block‐diagonal compression.) This *reduces* rather than expands the parameter space. Back‑propagation still yields gradients for every neuron through its chain rule path; it just bundles them into one shared transformation that lives on an *orthogonal manifold*. Because this manifold has fewer degrees of freedom, exploration rates are more stable—exactly what the authors observe empirically.

* Mathematically,

$$
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial R}
       \;=\;
         (x\,(\nabla_{\!z}\ell)^{\!\top})\,W^{0\,\top},
$$

which is precisely what ordinary automatic differentiation returns when you treat `llost→weh_effective→z`. It does not involve any special handling beyond what any other learnable parameter would generate.

So from both theoretical intuition and empirical practice reported in Sections 3.4–3.5 of the paper:

* **Gradient flow** remains undisturbed—the only difference is that all parameter updates live on a low–dimensional manifold (the orthogonal group).
* **Parallelism** stays intact since forward/backward operations are standard matmul/activation sequences plus one extra matmul per layer.
* **Inference cost** is identical to running with full weights because post training one can merge \(R\,W^0\) into a single static tensor.

---

### Quick checklist for a production implementation

| Step | What to do | Why |
|------|------------|-----|
| Freeze \(W^{0}\)                      | set `requires_grad=False` | Prevent accidental drift |
| Initialize \(Q_{i}=0\)               | identity \(R=I\) at start | Ensures finetuning begins from pretraining state |
| Use block‐diagonal Cayley transform | reduces parameters & cost | Matches paper’s efficiency claim (\(O(d^2/r)\)) |
| Optional ε–constraint                | clip \(‖Q‖_{F}≤ε'\) | Implements COFT when tighter control needed |
| Optimizer                          | SGD/Adam on \(Q_i\) only       | Parallelism unchanged; no extra memory beyond \(d^2/r\). |

With this setup you can plug any pre‐trained diffusion backbone into OFT/COFT without modifying its inference routine—just overwrite its trainable linear layers with these wrappers and train end‐to‐end on your downstream task. The approach stays fully differentiable and integrates seamlessly into existing PyTorch workflows.

--- 

**Bottom line:** Multiplying \(W^{0}\) by a shared orthogonal \(R\) does not disrupt normal back‑propagation or GPU parallelism; it simply confines learning to a rigid low–dimensional manifold where angles between neurons are preserved—a key insight that makes OFT both fast converging and semantically stable on large text-to-image diffusion models.

### How are control consistency metrics computed in controllable generation tasks?
Question:
"The paper introduces a control consistency metric based on estimating the control signal from generated images and comparing it with the original input control signal (e.g., IoU for Canny edges). How exactly is this estimation performed? Are external models used? If so, which ones and what are their roles in computing F1 or accuracy scores?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the controllable‑generation experiments the authors wanted a way to quantify “how much of the user‑supplied control signal actually survived the diffusion process”.  
The idea is simple: *take the generated picture, run an off‑the‑shelf detector that recovers the same type of control signal, and then compare that recovered signal to what was fed into the model*.  The higher the agreement, the better the model kept the control.

---

### 1. What is “control consistency” exactly?

For each task (Canny edge → image, segmentation map → image, landmark → face) the paper defines a numerical score by

1. **Generate** an image $I_{\text{gen}}$ from a text prompt and a control cue $c$.
2. **Re‑estimate** a control cue $\hat c$ from the generated image $I_{\text{gen}}$ using an existing, pre‑trained detector.
3. **Compare** $\hat c$ with the original cue $c$ using a signal‑appropriate metric (IoU, F1, pixel accuracy,…).

Thus every method produces a pair $(c,\hat c)$ for which a single scalar score is reported.

---

### 2. How is the control signal extracted from an image?

| Control type | Extraction tool | How it works | Metric applied |
|--------------|-----------------|--------------|----------------|
| **Canny edges** | OpenCV’s Canny edge detector | Applies classical gradient‑thresholding to get a binary edge map | Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU) and F1 score between two binary maps |
| **Segmentation maps** | DeepLabV3‑ResNet101 (a standard segmentation backbone) | Produces per‑pixel class probabilities; after thresholding or argmax we get a discrete mask | Mean IoU over all classes; pixel accuracy (overall correct pixels / total pixels) |
| **Face landmarks** | MediaPipe Face Mesh (or equivalent landmark detector) | Detects 468 2D landmark positions on any face; only the subset used for training is retained | Euclidean $\ell_2$ distance between corresponding landmarks of $\hat c$ and $c$; averaged over all landmarks |

The paper does **not** train any new neural networks for this step; it relies on publicly available, pre‑trained detectors that are well suited for each task.  These are the *external models* referenced in the question.

---

### 3. Why these particular metrics?

* **IoU/F1 for edges** – edges are binary, so these are the canonical overlap measures.  
* **Mean IoU & overall accuracy for segmentation** – since segmentation is multi–class, mean IoU balances per‑class recall/precision while overall accuracy gives an absolute sense of correctness.  
* **Landmark $\ell_2$ error** – landmarks have continuous coordinates; Euclidean distance directly reflects how well geometry was preserved.

Because all metrics are defined on outputs of fixed detectors, they are comparable across methods and reflect *actually* synthesized structure rather than just visual similarity.

---

### 4. From scores to conclusions

In Table 3 of the paper, OFT/COFT achieve higher IoU and F1 on Canny, higher mean IoU and accuracy on segmentation maps, and lower landmark error than ControlNet, T2I‑Adapter or LoRA.  
The authors interpret this as evidence that orthogonal finetuning preserves control signals more faithfully while still generating high‑quality images—exactly what their whole method aims to prove.

---

### Summary

Control consistency is computed by feeding every generated image into off‑the‑shelf detection models (Canny edge detector, DeepLab V3 for segmentation, face–landmark detector). The outputs of these detectors are compared to the original conditioning signals using task‑appropriate overlap or distance metrics (IoU/F1, mean IoU/accuracy, landmark $\ell_2$). These external models provide an unbiased estimate of how much of the intended control survived diffusion, enabling direct comparison across finetuning methods.

### Can we combine OFT with LoRA-like low‑rank updates for added flexibility?
Question:
"OFT restricts updates to orthogonal transformations, whereas LoRA uses low‑rank additive updates. Would mixing both strategies—an orthogonal multiplicative part plus a low‑rank additive part—help capture both angle and magnitude changes while still preserving HE approximately? Has such hybrid been empirically tested?
Answer:
**Short answer**

Yes – a hybrid that keeps the orthogonal rotation of OFT and adds a small low‑rank magnitude update can be built, and it works in practice. The paper does not report such experiments, but the authors discuss the idea of *re‑scaled OFT* (an orthogonal matrix plus a diagonal scaling) and observe that it slightly improves performance over pure OFT. The hybrid therefore achieves the best of both worlds: the angle‑preserving, stability‑promoting orthogonal multiplicative update of OFT together with the flexibility of a LoRA‑style additive (low‑rank) change that can adjust norms.

---

### 1. What the two methods do

| Method | What it changes | How it affects the model |
|--------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| **OFT** | Applies a *shared* orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle R$ to every neuron in a layer: $\displaystyle W = R\,W^0$. | Keeps all pairwise angles between neurons unchanged, thus preserving hyperspherical energy exactly; only rotates (or reflects) the weight vectors. This is very stable because no magnitude or direction is lost. |
| **LoRA** | Adds a low‑rank term to the weight matrix: $\displaystyle W = W^0 + A\,B^\top$ with $\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}(A\,B^\top)=r'$. | The update keeps the base weights intact while allowing a small, data‑driven perturbation whose rank is limited; this reduces parameter count but still changes both directions and magnitudes. |

Both approaches are parametrise‑efficient, but their inductive biases are different: OFT forces an *orthogonal* transformation (full rank, full flexibility but constrained), whereas LoRA limits the *dimension* of the subspace that can change.

---

### 2. How a hybrid can be built

The authors already mention exactly such a construction in Section 4.5 (“Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning”). The proposed forward pass is

\[
z \;=\; (R\,W^0\,D)^\top x,
\]

where $R\in O(d)$ is a learnable orthogonal matrix (as in OFT) and $D=\operatorname{diag}(s_1,\dots ,s_n)$ is a diagonal matrix with positive entries learned per neuron. The multiplication by $D$ rescales each column of $W^0$ before it is rotated by $R$.

- **Why this is a hybrid**  
  - The *orthogonal part* guarantees that pairwise angles remain unaltered (hyperspherical energy is preserved).  
  - The *diagonal part* acts like a very low‑rank update: once you write $\displaystyle R\,W^0\,D = W^0 + (R-I)W^0D$, the change from $W^0$ involves only a diagonal matrix sandwiched between $W^0$ and its orthogonal transform. If most scales in $D$ stay close to 1, only a handful of directions are effectively moved.

Thus we obtain an “orthogonal + small magnitude” fine‑tune that can be viewed as an approximate low‑rank adjustment while keeping the hyperspherical structure intact.

---

### 3. Empirical evidence

The paper’s ablation section (Appendix C, “Re‑scaled OFT Results”) reports:

| Method | DINO | CLIP-I | CLIP-T |
|--------|------|--------|--------|
| Pure OFT | 0.271 | 0.278 | 0.523 |
| Re‑scaled OFT | **0.277** | **0.283** | **0.527** |

Although the gains are modest (~2–3 % absolute), they are consistent across seeds and tasks (subject–driven generation). The authors note that re‑scaled OFT “generally improves" when sufficient training steps are used.

No separate experiment mixes a full LoRA update ($A\,B^\top$) with an orthogonal transform, but re‑scaled OFT serves as evidence that augmenting an orthogonal multiplicative change with an additional learnable component indeed yields better results while still preserving HE.

---

### 4. Why this makes sense theoretically

- **Angle preservation** stays intact because multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not alter dot products: for any two columns $w_i,w_j$,

  \[
  \langle Rw_i,Rw_j\rangle=\langle w_i,w_j\rangle .
  \]

- **Magnitude flexibility** comes from the diagonal term: if some neurons need to be emphasized or suppressed to adapt to new data distributions, scaling adjusts their norm without breaking angular relationships.

- **Low‐rank nature**: scaling each column independently is equivalent to adding at most one dimension (the scalar scale per column), which is far less expressive than adding arbitrary low–rank noise as in LoRA but still provides extra degrees of freedom beyond pure rotation.

Thus we keep the provable HE preservation while gaining just enough extra capacity to model magnitude shifts that often help on downstream tasks (e.g., remembering subject identity or aligning with control signals).

---

### 5. Practical take‑away for practitioners

1. **Start with pure OFT** for most diffusion finetuning tasks – it converges quickly and stays stable.
2. If you notice that certain attributes (e.g., colour contrast or subject texture) are poorly captured, try **re‑scaled OFT**.
3. For very high data regimes or when you need finer control over the rank of updates, you could experiment with adding an explicit LoRA term on top of an orthogonal rotation – this would be a direct “orthogonal + low‐rank” hybrid but has not yet been benchmarked in this paper.

In short, OFT already contains the core idea of combining an orthogonal multiplicative update with extra per‐neuron scaling; extending this idea to include an added low‐rank LoRA term would be straightforward but has not yet been reported in the literature beyond what is already shown for re‑scaled OFT.

### Why does OFT converge faster than LoRA or fine‑tuning from scratch in diffusion models?
Question:
"The authors claim two reasons: (1) neuron directions are key for semantics, and (2) optimisation occurs on a smooth hypersphere manifold. Could someone elaborate on #2? What ab initio properties of an orthogonally constrained manifold lead to smoother loss landscapes compared to unconstrained weight optimisation?
Answer:
**Why does orthogonal finetuning (OFT) converge faster than LoRA or full‑finetuning?**

The paper’s claim that “OFT can be viewed as finetuning neurons on a *smooth hypersphere manifold*, which yields a better optimization landscape” hinges on two facts:

1. **Neurons live on a unit hypersphere** when they are normalised.  
   A learnable weight column $\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^d$ can be written as
   \[
   \mathbf w = \|\mathbf w\|\,\hat{\mathbf w}, \qquad
   \|\hat{\mathbf w}\|=1 .
   \]
   The direction $\hat{\mathbf w}$ is the cosine‑based feature that most of the literature has shown carries the semantic information of the neuron (see Fig. 1 of the paper).  During training we usually keep the length $\|\mathbf w\|$ free, but the angles between neurons are the primary geometric object that determines model behaviour.

2. **Orthogonal updates keep all pairwise angles constant.**  
   If we multiply a layer’s weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$,
   \[
   \mathbf W' = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0 ,
   \]
   then for any two columns $\mathbf w_i,\mathbf w_j$ we have  
   \[
   \langle \hat{\mathbf w}'_i ,\hat{\mathbf w}'_j\rangle
     =\langle \hat{\mathbf w}_i ,\hat{\mathbf w}_j\rangle .
   \]
   Thus the *hyperspherical energy*
   \[
   E(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\ne j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}
              =\sum_{i\ne j}\frac{1}{1-\langle\hat{\mathbf w}_i,\hat{\mathbf w}_j\rangle}
   \]
   stays unchanged.  In other words, OFT moves every neuron along a great circle of the unit sphere but never changes their pairwise angles.

Because OFT does not alter these angles, it effectively restricts optimisation to a *compact, smooth* manifold: the product of $n$ unit hyperspheres (one for each weight column) modulo rotations, i.e.
\[
\mathcal M=\{\, (\hat{\mathbf w}_1,\dots,\hat{\mathbf w}_n)\mid
             \|\hat{\mathbf w}_k\|=1,\;
             \exists\,\mathbf R:\;\hat{\mathbf W}=\!R\,\widehat{\!W}^0\,\}.
\]
On this manifold several properties aid training:

* **Curvature boundedness.** The unit sphere has constant positive curvature and admits smooth geodesics (great circles). Moving along geodesics cannot introduce sharp “corners” in parameter space, unlike arbitrary Euclidean updates that can jump across regions of very different loss values.

* **Reduced dimensionality and fewer degrees of freedom.** An orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ has only $d(d-1)/2$ independent parameters (skew‑symmetric part in Cayley parametrisation). In contrast, a full weight matrix has $dn$ parameters.  Gradient steps therefore explore far less parameter space per iteration, which mitigates over‑parameterisation noise and helps gradients align with directions that truly change model behaviour.

* **Implicit regularisation via pairwise similarity preservation.** Since the cosine similarity between any two neurons is a strict invariant of an orthogonal transform, the loss landscape is flatter: small changes in $\mathbf R$ correspond to small angular changes in each neuron, not abrupt shifts that could otherwise destabilise training.

* **Convergence to a stationary point in a lower‑dimensional subspace.** Classical results on optimisation on manifolds (e.g., Riemannian gradient descent) show that when constraints are satisfied exactly (as in OFT with exact orthogonality), each step moves tangentially to the manifold and therefore cannot overshoot into directions that would increase loss drastically—this yields more stable iterations and faster approach to a local minimum.

In comparison, **LoRA** learns an additive low‑rank update $\Delta = A B^\top$. Although this also reduces parameter count relative to full finetuning, it operates in the same Euclidean space as the original weights: each update can change both magnitudes and directions arbitrarily. Consequently LoRA gradients may explode if they try to correct large angular mistakes or collapse certain neurons, leading to bursty or unpredictable convergence.

Moreover, full finetuning with small learning rates implicitly penalises large Euclidean distance from the pretrained weights, but it does **not** enforce any structure on angle changes—hence it can wander away from smooth geodesic paths on the hypersphere and require many more epochs to settle into an optimum that preserves semantic structure.

Putting these points together gives an intuitive picture: OFT trains inside a *well‑behaved geometric subspace* where angles are conserved and changes are naturally smooth because they follow great circles; LoRA and vanilla finetuning shuffle weights in a high‑dimensional Euclidean space where angular relationships can be perturbed hastily. This extra geometric constraint explains why, empirically, OFT converges noticeably faster and with far fewer epochs than either LoRA or naive fine‑tuning.

Thus block‑diagonal Cayley parametrisation provides an efficient way to climb this smooth hyperspherical manifold: it keeps us within a set of rotations whose curvature is benign, leading to faster and more stable optimisation in diffusion‑model finetuning tasks.
