\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the token‑position non‑uniformity (i.e., keeping the first ∈ n tokens unscaled) improve performance for longer contexts?
Question:
Section 2.3 mentions that for the first ℴ tokens, RoPE should be applied with a smaller interpolation factor. Experiments show performance improves when ℴ = 0, 32, etc. My question is: what is the theoretical intuition behind preserving early positions? How does this affect attention scores or positional entropy for those tokens?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The first few tokens in a Transformer decoding‑entirely‑to‑the‑right stream are the ones that receive the strongest attention heads—they are the “anchors” that anchor the running context.  When RoPE angles for those positions are shrunk too much (as a naïve linear interpolation would do) their relative spacing collapses, so all subsequent positions are forced into a very narrow angular band.  This “crowding” reduces the positional entropy that the attention mechanisms can exploit, causing many heads to become confused about which token is which.  By leaving the early positions untouched (i.e., interpolating them with a factor = 1) we keep the original, well‑structured angular distances for these critical anchors; the following tokens then only have to adjust gradually, preserving more of the original positional signal and keeping the entropy high where it matters most.

---

### Why the first tokens matter

In a causal language model each token \(x_t\) attends to all earlier tokens \(x_{<t}\).  
If we think of the *relative* positional information encoded by RoPE as an angle \(\theta_{t,i}=t\,\beta^{-i}\) for dimension \(i\), then for two nearby positions \(t\) and \(t-1$

$$
\Delta\theta_{t,i}= (t-(t-1))\,\beta^{-i}= \beta^{-i}.
$$

When we extend a model from length \(L\) to \(L'\) (ratio \(s=L/L'\)), standard linear interpolation scales every angle by \(\lambda = s^{-1}\).  The new difference between consecutive tokens becomes

$$
\Delta\theta^{\,\text{new}}_{t,i} = \lambda\,\beta^{-i} = s^{-1}\,\beta^{-i}.
$$

All token pairs therefore get *exactly* the same angular step but smaller in magnitude.  For long contexts this step can become tiny relative to the period of the sine/cosine pair, so a large number of consecutive tokens occupy almost the same point on the unit circle.  

The Transformer’s self‑attention weights are computed from dot‑products of queries and keys; after RoPE the dot product incorporates \(\cos(\theta_{q,i}-\theta_{k,i})\).  When \(\theta_{q,i}-\theta_{k,i}\) is almost zero for many consecutive keys, these dot products become nearly identical, which means that all heads see almost identical “distance” from current query to any previous key in that dimension.  In other words—*positional entropy collapses*.

The first few tokens (usually around one or two hundred) are special because:

1. **They receive strong attention** – empirical studies (e.g., Streaming LLM, LM‑Infinite) show that early tokens contribute most heavily to subsequent queries in a causal setting.
2. **They set up the harmonic reference frame** – RoPE essentially rotates every key and query by an angle proportional to its absolute position.  If we keep early positions at their original angles, we preserve an accurate base‑time reference for all later tokens.

Thus unscaled first tokens act as a *stable root* around which later rotations can be applied more gently.

### How preserving early positions improves scores

Let us look at an attention score between a query at position \(q\) and a key at position \(k<q\):

$$
s_{qk}= Q_q^\top K_k
= \sum_{i}\bigl[\,q_i^{(c)}\,k_i^{(c)} + q_i^{(s)}\,k_i^{(s)}\, \bigr],
$$

where \((c,s)\) denote cosine/sine components formed after RoPE:

$$
q_i^{(c)} = \tilde{q}_i \cos(\theta_{q,i}), 
$q_i^{(s)} = \tilde{q}_i \sin(\theta_{q,i}),
$$
and similarly for \(k_i^{(c)}, k_i^{(s)}\\).

If we leave early keys (\(k< \hat{n}\)) unscaled, their angles remain exactly \(\theta_{k,i}\).  
For any query beyond \(\hat{n}\), its angle is shrunk only for its own position:
$$
\theta'_{q,i} = \lambda(q)\,\theta_{q,i}
   = \lambda(q)\, q\,\beta^{-i},
   \\qquad
   \lambda(q)= 
    \\begin{cases}
    1 & q<\\hat n\\\\
    s^{-1} & q\\ge \\hat n .
    \\end{cases}
$$
Thus

* For **\(k< \\hat n < q\\)**:  
  The relative angle stays close to its original value:
  $$
  |\\theta'_{q,i}-\\theta_{k,i}| 
     = |\\lambda(q)\, q - k|\\,{\\beta}^{-i}
     \\approx |q-k|\\,{\\beta}^{-i},
  $$
  because only one side of the pair has been scaled.
* For **both inside or both outside**:  
  The relative angle shrinks uniformly by \(s^{-1}\), just as with pure linear interpolation.

Because many queries (\(q>\\hat n\\)) attend primarily to early keys (\(k< q-O(1)\))—e.g., when computing next‑token probability—the *cross‑term* 
\(\\cos(\\theta'_{q,i}-\\theta_{k,i}) = \\cos(|q-k|\\,{\\beta}^{-i}) + O(s^{-1})\\)
retains its full oscillatory behaviour instead of being damped towards 0 for short distances.  
Consequently, attention logits keep a larger dynamic range and gradients flow better during fine‑tuning.

### Quantifying called “positional entropy”

A useful quantity is the average angular spread of keys seen by a query:

$$
H_q = -\,E_j [p(q,j)\log p(q,j)], 
   \\quad p(q,j)=
      {exp(s_{qj})}\\,/Z_q.
$$

When angles collapse, many logits become identical → softmax becomes flat → entropy falls.  
Experiments in LongRoPE show that setting an interpolation offset (\(\\hat n>0\\)) keeps roughly **20–30 % higher** angular spread for queries at moderate distances (\(50–200\,{\\rm tokens}\\)).  

This higher entropy lets each head focus on more nuanced structure in longer inputs without paying a price on short inputs: when we revert to normal RoPE (no scaling) during inference on ≤ 8 k sequences, those heads recover their full positional discrimination.

### Summary

- **Uniform scaling** spreads all angles evenly but aggressively compresses early positions when new lengths are introduced; this crowds many token pairs into an almost identical region of rotation space.
- **Keeping initial ∼30–256 tokens unscaled** preserves their original high‑resolution angles, ensuring that these high‑attention anchors retain clear geometric distinctions.
- The resulting self‑attention logits maintain richer positional gradients, yielding lower perplexity on long documents and limiting catastrophic performance drops when re‑encoding short passages.
- Practically this technique requires no architectural change beyond flipping a small binary mask over token indices—a negligible cost compared with full fine‑tuning strategies.

Therefore, theoretically and experimentally LongRoPE’s intuition is: *anchor early tokens, let them carry untouched positional information while still extrapolating later positions; this keeps attention’s positional backbone intact across ultra‑long sequences.*
