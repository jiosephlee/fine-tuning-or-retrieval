\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 3: # 3. Existing Positional Interpolation Strategies

Large language models (LLMs) embed position information through *rotary position‑embedding* (RoPE) in order to encode the sequential order of input tokens. Extending the context window of a pre‑trained LLM requires extending the effective span of its positional encoding without retraining the entire model from scratch. Several interpolation strategies have been proposed to achieve this, each dengan varying degree of success and underlying assumptions. In this chapter we review three representative approaches—Linear Positional Interpolation (PI), Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)‑based interpolation, and YaRN’s frequency‑grouped technique—highlighting both their motivations and limitations.

## Linear Positional Interpolation (PI) and Its Crowding Problem

Linear PI is perhaps the simplest method to extend RoPE when scaling a model from an original context length $L$ to an enlarged length $L'$. It is defined by a single rescaling factor $\lambda$, chosen as the extension ratio $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. The rotary‐angle for each dimension $i$ and token position $n$ is reduced linearly:

\[
\cos \!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda\,\beta^{i}}\right),
\qquad
\sin \!\left(\frac{n}{\lambda\,\beta^{i}}\right),
\]

where $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$ and $\theta = 10\,000$ is the default base used in RoPE implementations. In practice, PI sets $\lambda = s$ across **all** RoPE dimensions, meaning every frequency component is uniformly contracted by the same factor.

The intuition behind PI is straightforward: new token positions beyond $L$ are “compressed” onto the interval that the pre‑trained model has seen during training. However, this blanket contraction causes two intertwined problems:

1. **Crowding of positional information** – Positions that were previously widely spaced become densely clustered once compressed, impeding the model’s ability to distinguish tokens that are close together in space.
2. **Loss of high–frequency details** – Lower RoPE dimensions encode higher frequencies; uniform scaling down disproportionately erases fine‐grained positional cues that are critical for tasks requiring precise alignment.

Empirical evidence shows that while PI allows modest extensions (typically up to $2\times$), perplexity rises sharply beyond this limit because the model cannot adequately recover temporal relationships among newly inserted positions.

## NTK‑Based Interpolation/Extrapolation Theory and Limitations

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework models training dynamics of deep neural networks by linearising them around initial parameters; it has been applied to understand repurposing RoPE frequencies for longer contexts. NTK theory suggests that different RoPE dimensions have varying sensitivities: lower indices ($i$ small) correspond to higher angular frequencies and should be interpolated less aggressively than higher indices ($i$ large). Accordingly, NTK‐based interpolation proposes a dimension‑dependent scaling:

\[
\lambda_{i} = s^{f(i)},
\]

with an exponent $f(i)$ that typically decreases as $i$ grows, thereby applying *extrapolation* to high dimensions while *interpolating* more aggressively on low dimensions. Dynamic NTK variants further adapt $\lambda_i$ per token position so that positions further into a sequence receive larger extensions.

This strategy mitigates some crowding but depends on analytic conjectures about kernel behaviour rather than empirical optimisation of each dimension’s scaling factor. Consequently:

* **Limited extension ratios** – In practice, dynamic NTK methods cap extension ratios at roughly $4\times$. Beyond this, new positions become too distant in representation space, leading again to severe performance degradation.
* **Rigid functional form** – The fixed power‑law relationship between $s$ and $\lambda_{i}$ restricts flexibility; if an LLM’s spectrum behaves differently from theoretical assumptions, performance suffers.

Thus, while NTK improves upon plain PI by acknowledging dimensional heterogeneity, it still imposes strong prior constraints that can hamper optimal adaptation for very long context windows.

## YaRN’s Frequency‑Grouped Approach and Dependency on Hand‑Crafted Thresholds

YaRN (Yet another Rotary Position Embedding) refines the interpolation scheme by explicitly partitioning RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands:

1. **High‑frequency band** – Low indices; these are left *unaltered*, i.e., $\lambda_{i}=1$, preserving sharp positional detail.
2. **Mid‑frequency band** – Middle indices; these receive NTK‐style interpolation ($\lambda_i=s^f$).
3. **Low‑frequency band** – High indices; these are set with *extrapolation* ($\lambda_i=1$ or slightly larger), allowing them to span longer distances without compression.

The grouping thresholds are derived empirically by human designers based on visual inspection or heuristic tuning per LLM architecture. This heuristic grouping allows YaRN to reconcile both crowding mitigation and frequency preservation more flexibly than pure PI or NTK approaches.

However, YaRN brings its own drawbacks:

* **Human‑guided design choices** – The need to manually select group boundaries introduces brittleness: a suboptimal threshold may inhibit learning in certain frequency ranges.
* **Limited extrapolation range** – Even with judicious grouping it still fails to support extensions beyond $\sim 4\times$, as evidenced when evaluating YaRN on simulated 8k passages in LLaMA2: perplexity spikes midway through the extended window.
* **Inconsistency across architectures** – Different base models (LLaMA2 vs Mistral) exhibit distinct spectral properties; a single set of group thresholds may not generalise, forcing re‑tuning per architecture.

In summary, while YaRN demonstrates superior performance over earlier techniques up to moderate extension factors (\(s \le 8\)), its effectiveness diminishes when attempting dramatically longer contexts due to reliance on hand-crafted frequency groups and limited extrapolation capacity.

---

These three strategies illuminate distinct trade‑offs in extending RoPE: simplicity vs precision (PI), theoretically motivated dimension sensitivity (NTK), and pragmatic yet architecture‑specific grouping (YaRN). Their limitations—crowding, stringent extrapolation limits, and hand‑crafted hyperparameters—motivate newer methods such as LongRoPE that seek systematic optimisation over both dimensional and positional non‑uniformities without excessive tuning burden.
