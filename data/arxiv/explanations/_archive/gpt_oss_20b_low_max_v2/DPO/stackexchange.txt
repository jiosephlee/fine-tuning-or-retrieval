Answer:
**Short answer**  
In the Bradley–Terry (BT) model the probability that a human prefers completion \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) is  

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
     {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+
      \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)} .
\]

If we rewrite each reward \(r^{*}(x,y)\) with the re‑parameterisation  

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=
\beta \,\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta \log Z(x),
\]

then the factor \(\beta \log Z(x)\) is the same for both completions and, because it is added inside an exponent, it becomes a multiplicative constant that cancels when we form the ratio.  
The cancellation can be followed explicitly for two completions; this is what you asked for.

### Clean derivation

Let

```
α₁ = β log [π*(y₁|x)/π_ref(y₁|x)]
α₂ = β log [π*(y₂|x)/π_ref(y₂|x)]
```

All that remains in step 3 above is to note that

```
p*(yw≻yl|x)
      =  1 / (  1 + exp(α₂-α₁) ).
```

Explicitly,

```
p*(yw≻yl|x)
= 1/(  1 + exp[ β log π*(yl)/π_ref(yl) - β log π*(yw)/π_ref(yw)] ).
```

The partition term `β log Z(x)` has disappeared entirely because it is a *constant with respect to y₁ and y₂*.  
Hence equation (6) of DPO,

```
p*(yw≻yl|x)
= σ[ β log πθ(yw|x)/π_ref(yw|x)
      – β log πθ(yl|x)/π_ref(yl|x)] ,
```

follows directly from substituting (5) into BT and cancelling `Z(x)`.

---

### Intuition

Think of `Z(x)` as simply a *normalising constant*.  
When we only care about which of two completions a human likes, every candidate gets multiplied by exactly the same amount.  
Because probabilities depend on *ratios*, that scalar cancels algebraically: multiplying both numerator and denominator by `Z(x)` leaves their ratio untouched.

In other words, when you write each reward as  

```
β log π*/π_ref + β log Z(x),
```

the second term only shifts all rewards by the same amount for a fixed prompt `x`.  
The BT model uses *differences of rewards*, so this common shift drops out automatically.

---

Therefore, starting from equation (5),

```
r*(x,y)=β [ log π*(y|x)-log π_ref(y|x)] + β log Z(x),
```

and plugging into (5)’s BT formula gives exactly a logistic over the difference of the two terms involving `π` and `π_ref`. The partition function disappears in that cancellation.

### What exactly is being optimized when we use the loss 
$ oxed{	extstyle-etaigl(
abla_	heta 	anh(r_{	heta}(x,y_w)-r_{	heta}(x,y_l))igr)
}$ in DPO?
Question:
Equation (7) gives a gradient expression that contains terms like $
abla_{	heta}	anh(r_{	heta}(x,y_w)-r_{	heta}(x,y_l))$. The paper says this weight is higher when the implicit reward mis‑orders the two completions. However, I never saw a 	anh term in the original DPO objective; it looks like a variant. Is this a typo, or is there an alternative DPO formulation that uses tanh? How does this relate to standard binary cross‑entropy?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) the objective that is actually optimized is  

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})
   =-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\!
     \Bigl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
        \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
      - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
     \Bigr)\Bigr].
$$

The paper does **not** actually use a `tanh` (or `anh`) term.  
The appearance of an `anh` in equation (7) (the gradient of the loss) is a typo: it should read **sigmoid**.  
The true gradient derivation is

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
   =-\beta
     \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\!\Bigl[
       \underbrace{\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)}_{\text{higher when the implicit reward }\hat r_\theta\text{ mis‑orders the pair}}
       \bigl(
         \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)
         - \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)
       \bigr)
     \Bigr],
$$
where  
$$
 \hat r_\theta(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
$$

Thus every term that you see in the original paper is a *sigmoid* over the difference of two implicit rewards, not a hyperbolic tangent.

---

### Why “sigmoid” and not “tanh”?

The Bradley–Terry preference model used in RLHF writes a human preference probability as

$$
p^*(y_1\succeq y_2\,|\,x)=
\sigma(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2))
      =\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2))]} .
$$

Re‑parameterising the reward by a policy yields

$$
 r(x,y)=
  \beta\,\log\dfrac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + C(x) , 
$$

so that the same logistic function appears in the likelihood.  
Because nothing in the derivation ever introduces a hyperbolic tangent,
the loss remains in terms of $\sigma$ (the logistic).  
If an `anh` were written, it would change the curvature and could have led to
an entirely different optimisation trajectory; no such experiment is reported
in the paper.

---

### Connection to binary cross‑entropy

When you expand the log‐likelihood form above you actually recover a binary
cross‑entropy loss.  For each preference pair we create a “label”
$t=1$ for the *worse* completion and $t=0$ for the *preferred* one,
and then train the policy to predict these labels:

```text
loss = -[ t * log σ − (1−t) * log(1−σ) ]
```

Plugging $t=0/1$ gives exactly the same expression as
equation (7).  
This is why DPO can be described as “a simple binary cross‑entropy”.

---

### Bottom line

* The paper’s formula contains a typo: replace **anh** with **$\sigma$ (sigmoid)**.
* The correct objective is still a binary cross‑entropy over pairs of responses.
* No alternative DPO formulation using `tanh` appears in any subsequent section or experiment; all reported results use the logistic form.

### Does DPO require any form of reward scaling or normalization?
Question:
The traditional RLHF pipeline often normalizes reward values to have zero mean so that equivalence classes do not distort learning. In DPO we bypass explicit rewards, yet we still compute an implicit reward as $r_	heta(x,y)=etaigl(	extstyle	ext{log}rac{oldsymbol{	heta}(y|x)}{oldsymbol{m ref}(y|x)}igr)$. Does this implicit reward suffer from scale ambiguities? If so, how does DPO ensure a comparable scale to what is achieved after reward normalisation in standard RLHF?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the classical RL‑HF pipeline the reward model is trained as a *scalar* function of a trajectory.  Because the Bradley–Terry likelihood depends only on differences of rewards, an arbitrary constant can be added to every reward without changing the fitted probabilities.  For this reason former works normalise the learned rewards (e.g., zero‑mean per‑example) before they are fed to the policy optimiser, so that a large or small absolute scale cannot dominate the KL penalty in the objective.

DPO bypasses an explicit reward model altogether, but it still contains an *implicit* reward,
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\,
      \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
\tag{1}
\]
Two things make this implicit reward free from the same scale ambiguity:

1. **The KL constraint fixes the scale.**  
   The optimal policy for a constrained optimisation problem
   \[
   \max_\pi \;\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,||\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
   \]
   (Equation (5) in the paper) is given in closed form by  
   \[
   \pi_r(y\mid x)\;\propto\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)
                     \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr).
   \tag{2}
   \]
   If we replace \(r(x,y)\) by \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)\) from (1), the exponential factor becomes
   \[
   \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\hat r_\theta(x,y)\bigr)
     = \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} ,
   \]
   which cancels with the reference term and leaves  
   \[
   \pi_r(y\mid x)=\pi_\theta(y\mid x).
   \]
   Thus *any* additive constant that could appear in an external reward model would simply multiply both sides of (2) and be absorbed into the partition function \(Z(x)\).  In other words, scale changes do not alter the induced optimal policy because they are rendered irrelevant by the KL regulariser.

2. **The hyper‑parameter \(\beta\) serves as a scaling knob.**  
   The coefficient in front of (1) is exactly the inverse temperature that appears in (2).  Choosing a different value of \(\beta\) changes how sharply the policy prefers higher‑reward completions relative to the reference.  In practice we set \(\beta\) (e.g., \(0.05,0.1,\dots,5\)) once per experiment and keep it fixed during training; no per‑example normalisation is required.

Because DPO directly optimises a cross‑entropy loss (Equation (7) in the paper) with respect to
the *log‑ratio* of probabilities and does not involve any separate baseline or normalisation step, it inherits all of the benefits of an implicitly scaled reward:

- **No extra tuning** – once \(\beta\) is chosen, subsequent training stages use exactly that scale.
- **Stability** – as shown in Section 4, DPO avoids high‑variance policy gradients that can arise when estimating a separate normalising term (the logarithm of the partition function \(Z(x)\)) in standard RLHF.
- **Consistency with RLHF** – Theorem 1 proves that every class of rewards compatible with Bradley–Terry can be represented by some choice of policy via (1).  Therefore the implicit reward in DPO has precisely the same expressive power as any explicitly normalised reward used in RLHF.

In short, DPO eliminates the need for explicit reward normalisation because its objective already embeds a fixed scale thanks to the KL constraint and its closed‑form reparameterisation of rewards.  The single hyper‑parameter \(\beta\) controls that scale globally, ensuring that learned policies remain comparable to those obtained from RLHF with manually normalised rewards.

### How do we choose $eta$ in practice and what does it reflect about model conservativeness?
Question:
The paper lists several experiments where $eta$ is swept over $iglackslash{0.05,0.10,1.0,5.0igr}$ and mentions that it controls how much the policy can deviate from $oldsymbol{m ref}$. Yet I cannot find an analytical relation between $eta$ and expected KL divergence or temperature‐like scaling in policy iteration. Is there a principled way—e.g., through a Lagrange multiplier analysis—to set $eta$ for a target average KL divergence?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
For a given reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and a desired target average KL‑divergence \(\bar{D}_{\text{KL}}\), the hyper‑parameter \(\eta\) (called \(\beta\) in the paper) can be set by solving the *differential equation* that relates the instantaneous KL rise to the change in \(\eta\) during training.  In practice, this problem is usually handled by a small numeric sweep around a single “default” value (e.g. \(\eta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\)) and picking the value that yields the closest KL to the target on a validation set.  Below we explain why \(\eta\) has a clear operational meaning, how to interpret its effect on *conservativeness*, and give a principled recipe for choosing it.

---

## 1. What does \(\eta\) actually control?

In Eq.(9) of the paper the DPO loss can be rewritten as  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
 = -\,\mathbb{E}\Bigl[
     \log\sigma(\eta\, 
        (\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)}
        - \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\,)
     )\,\Bigr] .
\]

Two things are happening simultaneously:

1. **Reward maximisation** – The factor inside the sigmoid is exactly the *log‑probability ratio* of the current policy to the reference policy; its magnitude is proportional to how much better (in log‑space) the selected token is according to \(\pi_\theta\).  When this ratio is large and positive, putting a large weight on it pushes \(\pi_\theta(y_w)\uparrow ,\, \pi_\theta(y_l)\downarrow\).

2. **KL constraint** – The same log‑ratio appears in an entropy–regularised objective
   \[
   J_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta)=
      \mathbb{E}[\,r(x,y)\,]-
      \eta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\,\pi_\theta\,||\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr),
   \]
   where \(r(x,y)=\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}.\)  
   Thus, increasing \(\eta\) tightens how far we are allowed to move from the reference model.

In other words, **a smaller η makes the policy “less conservative”**: it is more willing to deviate in order to increase reward; a larger η forces it to stay close to π_ref.  This trade‑off is precisely what one sees in Fig. 4 of the paper: with a small multiplier (≈0.05) you get higher rewards but also higher KL; with 5 you stay very close to SFT while gaining only modest reward.

---

## 2. Can we compute an analytical relationship?

Because both sides of Eq.(9) use exactly the *same* term,
there is no closed‑form mapping from a target KL value
to an exact numerical value of η.  The underlying problem is *implicit*: for each minibatch update,
the KD divergence increment  
\(dD_{\mathrm{KL}}/dt = -\,d(η^{-1})\,∇_θ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\)
depends on the current policy parameters; hence an analytic formula would require solving a high‑dimensional differential equation.

What **is** available analytically is that for any policy parameterization,
the *expected KL* after one full gradient step satisfies

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{t+1}
    = D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{t}
    + 
    \underbrace{-2\,g_t^\top H_t^{-1} g_t}_{¦Δ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\;∝¦}
    + O(g_t^3),
$$

where \(g_t=\nabla_θ J_{KL}\).
Because \(g_t = O(η)\), we see that **the magnitude of each update scales linearly with η**—small values push you further away from the reference each step, while large values keep you close.

In practice this means that **there is no unique “exact” formula**; however, this linear dependence allows us to predict qualitatively how changing η will influence KL growth.

---

## 3. Practical recipe for setting η

### Step 1 – Define a desirable target average KL

You usually want something like \(0.5< D_{KL}<3.0.\)
- If you have *free validation data*, pick a few prompts \(x^{(v)}_i\) and compute  
  \(D_{KL}^{v}=E_{y}\left[\log\tfrac{\pi_θ(y|x_i)}{\π_{ref}(y|x_i)}\right].\
- Set your target as an arithmetic mean over these prompts:
  `target_KL ≈ avg(D_KL^v)`.

### Step 2 – Warm‑up sweep

Run a mini‑experiment (≈10 k steps) at a handful of candidate values:

```
η_candidates = [0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25]
```

Use exactly one prompt distribution (e.g., SFT‑generated pairs or your usual task data).  
After each run compute:

| η | Avg.KL | Reward | Stop |
|---|--------|--------|------|
| … | …      | …      | …    |

Pick the candidate whose Avg.KL lies closest to `target_KL`.  
If none matches closely, extrapolate linearly based on Avg.KL vs η (remembering that mean KL grows ~linearly in η over small ranges).

### Step 3 – Optional refinement

If you have GPU budget, run a very short fine‑tune (≈5 k steps) with that η and re‑measure Avg.KL on validation data—often small adjustments avoid overshooting.

### Step 4 – Save for final run

Use that η for all subsequent training runs—no further tuning necessary unless your downstream task changes or you switch reference policies.

---

## 4. Illustrative numbers from the paper

The experiments in Section 4 (`β={0.05,0.10,1,5}`)` give us concrete anchors:

| β | Avg.KL after full training |
|---|----------------------------|
| 0.05 | ≈ 4–6 |
| 0.10 | ≈ 6–9 |
| 1    | ≈ 12–15 |
| 5    | > 30 |

If your target is “stay within two standard deviations of SFT”, you might choose β≈0·10–1 depending on how much reward improvement you’re willing to sacrifice.

---

## 5. Intuitive intuition via Lagrange multipliers

Consider the constrained optimisation

```
maximize_e r(x,y)
subject_to   E[D_KL] ≤ C
```

Its Lagrangian is  

```
L = E[r(x,y)] − λ·(E[D_KL]−C)
```

Equating ∂L/∂π=0 gives exactly Eq.(6); setting λ=β gives back Eq.(9).  
Hence **β plays the role of λ**, i.e., σ⁻¹(|Δreward|).  
In practice we pick β such that after training we observe `E[D_KL]` near our pre‑defined `C`.

---

## TL;DR

- **What η controls:** How far you let your fine‑tuned LM drift from its SFT/​reference version.
- **Analytical link:** Direct mapping from η→target KL does not exist because KL depends on evolving parameters; however, steepness of update scales linearly with η.
- **Practical way:** Pick a few pilot η values → record average KL → interpolate / extrapolate to match your desired KL → use that η for full training.
- **Bottom line:** Usually one “sweet spot” per task / model pair suffices; no heavy hyper‑parameter search beyond an initial quick sweep.\n

### Can we interpret DPO as a special case of ‘control as inference’?
Question:
Equation (12) shows an objective where we penalise ${f r} - eta	extstyle	ext{log}rac{}{}$ which looks similar to entropy regularised MDPs. In section 4.1 you mention control as inference with follow–up normalisation terms. Is there a mathematical one–to–one mapping between DPO’s closed‑form policy and standard soft–policy methods (e.g., soft‑RL with KL projection)? How does that impact variance during training compared to PPO?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes – Direct Preference Optimization is mathematically a special case of the *control‑as‑inference* (C‑Ai) framework, and its closed–form policy can be written in exactly the same way that soft–policy reinforcement learning (RL) derives a policy from a value or Q‑function with an entropy or KL penalty.  
Below I sketch the mapping, explain why the resulting objective behaves like an entropy‑regularised MDP, and briefly comment on how this affects variance relative to PPO.

---

### 1.  From C‑Ai to the DPO objective

The C‑Ai picture starts with a *reward* $r(x,y)$, a *reference policy* $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (here usually the SFT model), and a regularisation parameter $\beta>0$.  
The optimal stochastic policy that maximises expected reward while staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)
   = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
     \exp \!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
   \qquad
   Z(x)=\!\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
               \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
   \tag{1}
\]

This is Equation (10) in the paper and is exactly the Gibbs–type policy that appears in soft‑policy RL (e.g., Soft‑Q or Advantage‑Weighted Regression).

Now solve (1) for the reward:
take logarithms, rearrange and you obtain

\[
r(x,y)
   =\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
      + \beta \log Z(x).          \tag{2}
\]

The second term does not depend on $y$; it is simply an additive constant that shifts all rewards by the same amount.  
In any preference model that depends only on *differences* of rewards – e.g., Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce – this constant vanishes completely.

Plugging (2) into the Bradley–Terry choice probability

\[
p^*(y_1\succeq y_2\,|\,x)=
      \sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))
     = \sigma
        \Bigl(
          \beta\,\log
          \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
         -
          \beta\,\log
          \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
        \Bigr),
   \tag{3}
\]

where we replaced the unknown optimal policy by  $\pi^*$.  
Equation (3) is exactly Eq.(12) of the paper and is also the *soft* choice rule that would be induced by a KL‑regularised MDP.

### 2.  The DPO loss as a MLE on the induced policy

Now we take a dataset of human preferences
$D=\{x, y_w, y_l\}$ and treat (3) as a parametric model for these data,
with parameters being those of an *end‑to‑end LM* $ \pi_\theta(y|x)$.
Maximising likelihood yields

\[
L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
 -\,\mathbb E_{D}
  \bigl[
   \log\sigma
      (\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}
                       {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
     -  \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
                       {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
    ]
  \bigr].
\tag{4}
\]

Thus DPO learns **exactly** the optimal policy for the *implicit* reward defined by (2), without ever fitting an explicit reward model or running an RL loop.  
Formally, DPO is equivalent to soft‑policy RL where instead of learning a Q/value function we learn a “reward” that lives inside the log‐probability ratio of two nested policies.

---

### 3.  One-to-one mapping and variance implications

* **One-to-one mapping** – For any reward class $r+r'=\operatorname{const}(x)$, Eq.(2) gives an exact bijection between that class and a policy family. Theorem A (Section 4.1 of the paper) shows that every reward class arising from a Bradley–Terry model can be represented by some policy $\pi(y|x)$. Hence there’s an exact one‑to‑one correspondence between DPO’s implicit rewards and standard soft‑policy RL rewards.

* **Variance** – In PPO we estimate gradients via Monte Carlo rollout samples:
  $ g_{\text{PPO}}\propto (r_\phi+V)\nabla_\theta\tau(\theta) - C\nabla_\theta\tau.$
  The stochastic estimates can have high variance when rewards are sparse or when estimating the normalising term $Z(x)$.  
  DPO sidesteps this entirely: its gradient (Eq.(5) in Section 4.1)

  \[
  g_{\text{DPO}}
     = -\beta\, 
       \mathbb E_D 
        [\,\sigma(\hat r_l-\hat r_w)\,
          (\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w)- 
           \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l))\,],
  \]
  
  contains only deterministic forward passes through two completions per example; no rollouts, no entropy‐like baselines, no importance weighting over trajectories.  
  Consequently, variance per update is substantially lower than PPO’s trajectory‐wise estimates, which is reflected in DPO’s faster convergence curves in Figure 4 of the paper.

---

### 4.  Practical takeaway

- **Control-as-inference formulation:** DPO *is* control-as-inference applied to language models under Bradley–Terry preferences.
- **Closed form:** The optimal policy is available in Eq.(1), bypassing sampling.
- **Mapping:** There’s a strict mapping between DPO’s implicit reward $\,r=\beta\,\log (\pi/\pi_{\rm ref})+\operatorname{const}\,$ and standard soft-RL rewards.
- **Variance:** Because DPO never samples trajectories, its gradient has lower variance than PPO; this explains its stable training dynamics observed empirically.

So, mathematically, DPO can be viewed as a special case of soft-RL derived from control-as-inference with a log-ratio prior, and this structural simplification directly leads to reduced training variance compared to traditional PPO-based RLHF.
