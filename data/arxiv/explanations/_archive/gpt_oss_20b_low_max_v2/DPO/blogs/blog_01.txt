# What’s in a Preference? Decoding the Simple Math Behind Direct Preference Optimization  

Large–scale language models (LLMs) are great at spitting out fluent text, but steering them to *behave* the way we want is still a tricky business. The state‑of‑the‑art pipeline for “alignment” – called RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) – turns the model into a little reinforcement‑learning problem, trains a separate reward network on human pairwise judgments, and then feeds that reward back into the LM with a policy‑gradient algorithm such as PPO. The result works, but it is procedurally heavy:  

1. Train an unsupervised LM.  
2. Generate two completions for each prompt and ask a human who likes which one better.  
3. Fit a reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) with a Bradley–Terry loss.  
4. Treat the LM as a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) and run reinforcement learning to maximize \(\mathbb{E}[r_{\phi}]\) while keeping a KL penalty to stay close to the original policy.

Reinforcement learning is notoriously unstable on large language models, needs many imports of samples from the current policy, and constantly requires hyper‑parameter tweaking. The paper *Direct Preference Optimization* (DPO) shows that step 3+4 can be avoided altogether by re‑parameterising the reward in terms of the policy itself, ending up with a **plain cross‑entropy loss** that can be optimized with ordinary gradient descent.

Below I’ll unpack the core equations and show how the headline “Your language model is secretly a reward model” becomes true.

---

## 1.  From RL objective to an analytic optimal policy

In RLHF we usually optimise

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim\pi_\theta}\Bigl[
 r_{\phi}(x,y)
 - \beta\, \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\,|x)\bigr)
\Bigr]
\]

where  

* \(x\) is the prompt,  
* \(y\) the generated continuation,  
* \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is our reference policy (often an SFT model),  
* \(\beta>0\) controls how much we are willing to drift away from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

If we ignore the stochasticity of sampling, this regularised optimisation has an **optimum in closed form** (it’s just a softmax over reward):

\[
\boxed{
p^*(y|x)=
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}}
      {\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\,e^{\,r(x,y')/\beta}}
}
\tag{1}
\]

The numerator says: “give us all probabilities that we already have in the reference policy, but boost them according to the exponentiated reward.” The denominator is just a normalising constant that makes total mass 1.

---

## 2.  Switching roads: reward → policy

We would normally fix \(r(x,y)\) first (learn it from data), plug it into (1), and then do RL to move \(\pi_\theta\) towards that optimum. DPO runs this in reverse: we **parameterise** \(r(x,y)\) in terms of *our very own policy* \(\pi_\theta\). 

Take logarithms on both sides of (1):

\[
r(x,y)
=
\beta \Bigl(
   \log p^*(y|x)
 - \log \!\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\,
          e^{\,r(x,y')/\beta}
\bigr)
\]

Now solve for \(p^*(y|x)\):

\[
p^*(y|x)
=
e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}\,\Bigl/
     \underbrace{\sum_{y'} 
        \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\,
        e^{\,r(x,y')/\beta}}
           _{\;\;Z(x)}
     }
     \,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
\]

But we *don’t need* to calculate \(Z(x)\). We pierce through by expressing **reward differences** – what humans actually care about – directly in terms of policies!

### Bradley–Terry as a difference

The Bradley–Terry preference model states that if we have two completions \(y_1,y_2\),

\[
p(y_1\succeq y_2 | x)=
\sigma(
  r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))
= 
\frac{1}{1+\exp[-(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))]}
\]

Only *differences* matter; adding any function of \(x\) to both rewards leaves this unchanged.

Plugging our re‑parameterised rewards into this expression cancels out exactly those unwanted normalising constants:

\[
p(y_1\succeq y_2 | x)
=
\sigma(
  \beta \log
     \frac{\pi_\theta(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
-
  \beta \log
     \frac{\pi_\theta(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
 )
=\sigma(
   \beta\, 
   \log
     
   \!\frac{\pi_\theta(y_1|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
          {\pi_\theta(y_2|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
   )
     
                                                     
                                           
```

So the probability that humans prefer completion \(y_w\) over \(y_l\) can be written *solely in terms of both policies*, as:

$$
p^*\bigl(y_w\succeq y_l\, |\, x\bigr)=
\nonumber
\sigma
        (
          \underbrace{\beta\, 
                \log 
                {\tfrac{\!\pi_\theta(y_w | x)}{\!\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w | x)}}
          }_{=:R_w}
        -
          R_l
         )                                
$$

That’s equation **(3)** in the DPO paper.

---

## 3.  The DPO loss – just binary cross‑entropy

We now have an explicit likelihood for each preference pair:  

Given data $\mathcal D=\{(x_i, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\}$ where $y_w$ humans liked more than $y_l$, simply maximise its probability under (3). In practice you minimise negative log‑likelihood:

$$
L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta;\;\pi_{\text{ref}})
=
-\, 
\E_{(x,w,l)\sim\rho}\!
    [
      \log\sigma(R_w-R_l)
    ]
$$

where $R_w=\beta\,\,ln [\,{\pi_\theta(y_w | x)/ {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w | x)}}]$ and similarly for $R_l$.

This is *exactly* a standard binary cross‑entropy loss where each “positive” example is $(w,l)$ and each “negative” example would be $(l,w)$ if you doubled your dataset.

No RL loop needed – just feed your LM with preferences encoded as pairs, compute $R$ for each answer using your current LM’s log‑probabilities relative to the reference LM, and perform ordinary backpropagation!

---

## 4. Why does this work? A quick geometric intuition

If you look at the gradient of $L_{\text{DPO}}$ you get

$$
\nabla_\theta L = -\, 
\E_{w,l}
[\
\sigma(R_l-R_w)\;
( 
\nabla_\theta (\ln p_\theta(w)+B(w)) -    
\nabla_\theta (\ln p_\theta(l)+B(l))
\
]
$$

The prefactor $\sigma(R_l-R_w)$ weights examples by how poorly your*current* implicit reward mis‑orders them: if you already rank $w$ higher than $l$, there’s little incentive to move; if you mis-order them by far, you get a big push not only **increasing** $\ln p_\theta(w)$ but also strongly **decreasing** $\ln p_\theta(l)$.

In simple terms: *make good choices more likely, make bad choices less likely*, exactly like supervised fine‑tuning but now guided by human relative judgments.

---

## 5. Practical takeaways for building aligned LMs

- **You need only one pass** over your previously collected preference data.
- **No sampler or rollout**: all gradients can be computed via standard engine autograd because they involve only log probabilities from both models.
- The hyperparameter $\beta$ plays the same role as rough KL budget; authors found good defaults between $0.05$ and $5$.
- If you already have an SFT model, use it as $\pi_{\rm ref}$. If not available, fit $\pi_{\rm ref}$ by maximizing likelihood on positive replies – this mitigates mismatch between what data was sampled from vs what policy you’re optimizing.
- DPO consumes roughly **half or less** GPU time compared with PPO while achieving comparable or better win rates on standard benchmarks (sentiment control, summarisation TL;DR, single-turn dialogue).

# lm_theta is same architecture but currently being updated.
# pref_data = [(prompt,x,w,l), ...]   # human-labeled pairs w(like>like)

def reward_log(ratio):
    # r = beta * ln(ratio)
    return beta * torch.log(ratio)

def dpo_loss(lm_theta_logits):
    loss = 0.
    for prompt,w,l in pref_data:
        # Compute log probs wrt theta and ref
        logp_w = lm_theta.log_prob(prompt + w)
        logp_l = lm_theta.log_prob(prompt + l)
        logp_ref_w = lm_ref.log_prob(prompt + w)
        logp_ref_l = lm_ref.log_prob(prompt + l)

        Rw = reward_log(torch.exp(logp_w - logp_ref_w))
        Rl = reward_log(torch.exp(logp_l - logp_ref_l))

        loss += -torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
                     target=torch.tensor([1.0]),
                     input=Rw - Rl,
                     reduction='sum'
                 )
    return loss / len(pref_data)

optimizer.step(lambda: dpo_loss(logits_model))
```

Because everything boils down to computing logits on forward passes through `llama` or `gpt2`, training pipelines can reuse existing training scripts and just swap out the loss function.

## TL;DR

Direct Preference Optimization shows that if we insist on learning *proportional preferences*, we can push those preferences straight onto our language model without ever throwing around “reward networks” or doing reinforcement learning tricks. A simple conditioning on ratios of model probabilities yields an elegant cross‑entropy objective that matches exactly what PPO would try to optimise asymptotically but at a fraction of computational cost and simpler code.

If you’re building next‑generation text generators and want alignment without all that RL baggage – give DPO another look!
