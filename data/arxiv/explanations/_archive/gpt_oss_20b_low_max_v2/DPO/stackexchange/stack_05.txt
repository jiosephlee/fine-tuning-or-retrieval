\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Can we interpret DPO as a special case of ‘control as inference’?
Question:
Equation (12) shows an objective where we penalise ${f r} - eta	extstyle	ext{log}rac{}{}$ which looks similar to entropy regularised MDPs. In section 4.1 you mention control as inference with follow–up normalisation terms. Is there a mathematical one–to–one mapping between DPO’s closed‑form policy and standard soft–policy methods (e.g., soft‑RL with KL projection)? How does that impact variance during training compared to PPO?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes – Direct Preference Optimization is mathematically a special case of the *control‑as‑inference* (C‑Ai) framework, and its closed–form policy can be written in exactly the same way that soft–policy reinforcement learning (RL) derives a policy from a value or Q‑function with an entropy or KL penalty.  
Below I sketch the mapping, explain why the resulting objective behaves like an entropy‑regularised MDP, and briefly comment on how this affects variance relative to PPO.

---

### 1.  From C‑Ai to the DPO objective

The C‑Ai picture starts with a *reward* $r(x,y)$, a *reference policy* $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (here usually the SFT model), and a regularisation parameter $\beta>0$.  
The optimal stochastic policy that maximises expected reward while staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)
   = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
     \exp \!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
   \qquad
   Z(x)=\!\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
               \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
   \tag{1}
\]

This is Equation (10) in the paper and is exactly the Gibbs–type policy that appears in soft‑policy RL (e.g., Soft‑Q or Advantage‑Weighted Regression).

Now solve (1) for the reward:
take logarithms, rearrange and you obtain

\[
r(x,y)
   =\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
      + \beta \log Z(x).          \tag{2}
\]

The second term does not depend on $y$; it is simply an additive constant that shifts all rewards by the same amount.  
In any preference model that depends only on *differences* of rewards – e.g., Bradley–Terry or Plackett–Luce – this constant vanishes completely.

Plugging (2) into the Bradley–Terry choice probability

\[
p^*(y_1\succeq y_2\,|\,x)=
      \sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))
     = \sigma
        \Bigl(
          \beta\,\log
          \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
         -
          \beta\,\log
          \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
        \Bigr),
   \tag{3}
\]

where we replaced the unknown optimal policy by  $\pi^*$.  
Equation (3) is exactly Eq.(12) of the paper and is also the *soft* choice rule that would be induced by a KL‑regularised MDP.

### 2.  The DPO loss as a MLE on the induced policy

Now we take a dataset of human preferences
$D=\{x, y_w, y_l\}$ and treat (3) as a parametric model for these data,
with parameters being those of an *end‑to‑end LM* $ \pi_\theta(y|x)$.
Maximising likelihood yields

\[
L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
 -\,\mathbb E_{D}
  \bigl[
   \log\sigma
      (\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}
                       {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
     -  \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
                       {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
    ]
  \bigr].
\tag{4}
\]

Thus DPO learns **exactly** the optimal policy for the *implicit* reward defined by (2), without ever fitting an explicit reward model or running an RL loop.  
Formally, DPO is equivalent to soft‑policy RL where instead of learning a Q/value function we learn a “reward” that lives inside the log‐probability ratio of two nested policies.

---

### 3.  One-to-one mapping and variance implications

* **One-to-one mapping** – For any reward class $r+r'=\operatorname{const}(x)$, Eq.(2) gives an exact bijection between that class and a policy family. Theorem A (Section 4.1 of the paper) shows that every reward class arising from a Bradley–Terry model can be represented by some policy $\pi(y|x)$. Hence there’s an exact one‑to‑one correspondence between DPO’s implicit rewards and standard soft‑policy RL rewards.

* **Variance** – In PPO we estimate gradients via Monte Carlo rollout samples:
  $ g_{\text{PPO}}\propto (r_\phi+V)\nabla_\theta\tau(\theta) - C\nabla_\theta\tau.$
  The stochastic estimates can have high variance when rewards are sparse or when estimating the normalising term $Z(x)$.  
  DPO sidesteps this entirely: its gradient (Eq.(5) in Section 4.1)

  \[
  g_{\text{DPO}}
     = -\beta\, 
       \mathbb E_D 
        [\,\sigma(\hat r_l-\hat r_w)\,
          (\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w)- 
           \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l))\,],
  \]
  
  contains only deterministic forward passes through two completions per example; no rollouts, no entropy‐like baselines, no importance weighting over trajectories.  
  Consequently, variance per update is substantially lower than PPO’s trajectory‐wise estimates, which is reflected in DPO’s faster convergence curves in Figure 4 of the paper.

---

### 4.  Practical takeaway

- **Control-as-inference formulation:** DPO *is* control-as-inference applied to language models under Bradley–Terry preferences.
- **Closed form:** The optimal policy is available in Eq.(1), bypassing sampling.
- **Mapping:** There’s a strict mapping between DPO’s implicit reward $\,r=\beta\,\log (\pi/\pi_{\rm ref})+\operatorname{const}\,$ and standard soft-RL rewards.
- **Variance:** Because DPO never samples trajectories, its gradient has lower variance than PPO; this explains its stable training dynamics observed empirically.

So, mathematically, DPO can be viewed as a special case of soft-RL derived from control-as-inference with a log-ratio prior, and this structural simplification directly leads to reduced training variance compared to traditional PPO-based RLHF.
