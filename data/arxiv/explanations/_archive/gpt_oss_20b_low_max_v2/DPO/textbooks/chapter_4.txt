\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: # 4. From Reward to Policy: The Key Insight behind DPO  

Large‑scale language models that have been trained on massive corpora do not by default exhibit the specific behaviour a user desires.  Aligning such models therefore becomes a question of *steering* their probability distribution over possible completions.  Traditional approaches fit an explicit reward function to human preference data and then run a reinforcement‑learning (RL) loop that maximizes this reward while keeping the model close to its original distribution.  Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) sidesteps the RL loop entirely by exploiting a simple reparameterization that translates any reward function into a corresponding policy and, crucially, makes the *optimal* policy analytically transparent.

In this chapter we unpack the mathematical heart of DPO.  We begin by showing how the log‑likelihood ratio between the target policy and a chosen reference policy reproduces any reward function up to an additive constant that depends only on the prefix \(x\).  From there we demonstrate how the partition function that normally plagues RL algorithms cancels out when we express human preferences through a Bradley‑Terry model.  The end result is an elegant preference probability that depends solely on the log‑likelihood ratios of two completions given the prompt \(x\), allowing us to replace deep reinforcement learning with a single, stable cross‑entropy objective.

---

## Rewriting Reward via Optimal Policy

The central idea is that under a KL‑constrained objective, the *optimal* policy that maximizes expected reward while staying close to some reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\) has the exponential form

\[
\pi_{r}(y \mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\tag{4.1}
\]
where \(r(x,y)\) is any scalar reward function for generating token sequence \(y\) after prefix \(x\), \(\beta>0\) controls how strictly we enforce proximity to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), and

\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr)
\tag{4.2}
\]
is the partition function needed for normalisation.

Equation (4.1) can be inverted to express \(r(x,y)\) directly in terms of an arbitrary policy \(\pi_{r}\).  
Taking logarithms and rearranging gives

\[
r(x,y)=
\beta\,\log \frac{\pi_{r}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}
+\beta\,\log Z(x).
\tag{4.3}
\]

The first term in (4.3) is simply the log-odds between how often \(\pi_{r}\) would produce \(y\) relative to how often \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) would; it captures precisely how much more we want to generate \(y\) than before.  
The second term, \(\beta\,\log Z(x)\), is a constant with respect to \(y:\) it depends only on the prompt \(x\).  By construction such constants can be absorbed into any *baseline* or *bias* term in a reward model without changing human preferences, as will be shown next.

Thus, **any** reward task can be reparameterised as the log-odds between two distributions—an implicit policy and its reference—plus an arbitrary per‑prefix shift.  This observation is the fulcrum on which DPO pivots: rather than fitting an explicit scalar reward network \(r_\phi(x,y)\), we directly optimise a language‑model policy whose already-trained probability estimates encode the same incentives.

## Cancellation of Partition Function Under Bradley–Terry Probability Expression

Human preference data used in RLHF commonly adopts the Bradley–Terry (BT) model:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
   \frac{\exp[r^{*}(x,y_{1})]}
        {\exp[r^{*}(x,y_{1})]+\exp[r^{*}(x,y_{2})]}.
   \tag{4.4}
   \]

Only differences in rewards matter; adding any constant \(c(x)\) that depends solely on the prompt leaves this expression unchanged because it cancels out in numerator and denominator.

Using (4.3), substitute for each reward:

\[
r^{*}(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log \frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
 +C(x),
   \tag{4.5}
   \]
where \(C(x)=\beta\,\log Z(x)\).

Plugging (4.5) into (4.4):

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
   \frac{
      \exp[\beta\,\log(\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x})) + C(x)]
   }{
      \exp[\beta\,\log(\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x})) + C(x)]
     + 
      \exp[\beta\,\log(\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x})) + C(x)]
   }.
   \tag{4.6}
   \]

Multiplying numerator and denominator by \(e^{-C(x)}=\tfrac1C'\) eliminates every instance of \(C(x)\):

- Numerator becomes
  \( e^{\, -C(x)}e^{\,C(x)}e^{\,...}=e^{\,...}.\)
- Denominator simplifies similarly.

Hence all dependence on partition functions vanishes:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
   \frac{
      \exp[\beta\,(\log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}
                      {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x))]}
   }{
      \exp[\beta\,(\log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}
                      {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x))]]
     +
      \exp[\beta\,(\log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}
                      {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x))]]
   }.
   \tag{4.7}
   \]

Because exponential of a log simplifies to plain ratios, (4.7) can be rewritten exactly as

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
   \frac{
      (\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x}))^\beta
    }{
      (\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x}))^\beta
     + 
      (\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x}))^\beta
    }.
    \tag{4.8}
    \]

Equation (4.8) is remarkable: **the likelihood of observing a human preference between two completions is expressed solely in terms of log‑likelihood ratios** between those completions under our target policy versus under an honest baseline policy—no reference to any separate reward network or partition function!

This cancellation is at the heart of DPO’s simplicity: it enables us to replace learning a reward function altogether by learning an optimal policy directly from comparison data.

## Resulting Preference Probability Expressed Purely in Log‑Likelihood Ratios

Rewriting (4.8) using the logistic sigmoid notation yields an even cleaner expression:

Let

```
q_w = π_θ(y_w | x), q_l = π_θ(y_l | x)
z_w = π_ref(y_w | x), z_l = π_ref(y_l | x)
```

Define an implicit “reward” produced by our current policy:

```
ĥ r_θ(x, y)=β log(q_y / z_y).
```

Then

```
p^*( y_w ≻ y_l | x )
     = σ[ĥ r_θ(x, y_w)-ĥ r_θ(x, y_l)] ,
```

where σ denotes the logistic sigmoid.

Thus **the probability assigned by a human annotator that one completion outranks another can be captured entirely by how much higher our policy’s log-likelihood ratio favors one over the other**.

The direct optimisation objective for our policy follows immediately: minimise negative log-likelihood over all preference pairs:

```
ℒ_DPO(π_θ;π_ref)
    = – E_[𝔇] [ log σ(
                      β log q_w/z_w – β log q_l/z_l )
                ] .
```

This is simply a binary cross‑entropy loss over labelled pairs where labels indicate which completion was preferred; remarkably, no episode sampling or gradient variance reduction tricks are needed—the loss gradients scale exactly like those used in supervised sequence-to-sequence training.

Because this entire derivation hinged on replacing rewards with log-odds ratios between policies and eliminating partition constants via cancelation, DPO rests cleanly on theoretical guarantees inherited from Bradley–Terry modeling while offering computational advantages that match no other method currently available for large-scale language model alignment.
