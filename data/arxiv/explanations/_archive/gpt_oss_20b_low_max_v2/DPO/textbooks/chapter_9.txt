# Chapter 9: Results – Reward–KL Efficiency on Controlled Sentiment Task

Language models trained on large corpora acquire general knowledge and impressive reasoning skills, yet steering them to behave in line with human preferences is still a formidable challenge. The prevailing paradigm, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), introduces an additional sampling‑wise reinforcement loop to the pre‑trained model in order to obtain better alignment. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) sidesteps this loop altogether by expressing the optimal policy for a KL‑constrained reward maximization objective in closed form and then maximizing a simple binary cross‑entropy objective over preference pairs. An immediate question for practitioners is **how efficiently** DPO obtains high rewards while keeping the policy close to the pre‑trained reference distribution. In this chapter we answer that question in detail using a controlled sentiment generation experiment – a held‑out task where the ground‑truth reward function (a sentiment classifier) is known.

## 9.1 Experimental Set‑up

The controlled sentiment task consists of generating movie‑review completions that express positive sentiment. Each input \(x\) is a prefix of a review from the IMDb dataset; the policy \(\pi_{\theta}( \cdot \mid x)\) must generate an utterance \(y\) that satisfies the desired sentiment attribute. Because the ground‑truth reward function \(r^{\text{gt}}(x,y)\) is known (the probability assigned by an off‑the‑shelf sentiment classifier), we can compute **exact** reward values for any generation and hence plot the full frontier of achievable \((\text{reward},\text{KL})\) pairs.

We performed a comprehensive grid search over key hyperparameters for each algorithm:

| Method  | Hyperparameter  | Values  |
|---------|----------------|---------|
| PPO     | Target KL     | \(\{3,6,9,12\}\) |
| DPO     | \(\beta\)        | \(\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\) |
| Unlikelihood | \(\alpha\)    | \(\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}\) |
| Preferred‑FT | —         | random seeds (3 each) |

For every combination we fine‑tuned the policy from an SFT baseline until convergence (typically 100 policy updates). After training we evaluated each policy on a held‑out set of 10 k prompt–completion pairs by sampling generations at temperature 0 and computing:

1. **Reward**  
   \(R = \mathbb{E}_{x}[\, r^{\text{gt}}(x,\hat y)\,]\),  
   where \(\hat y \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\).

2. **KL divergence with reference**  
   \(\mathrm{KL} = \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\Big[\, D_{\!KL}\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\,\Big]\).

The result is a cloud of points for each algorithm that illustrates how variations in hyperparameters trade off reward against a drift from the reference model.

Figure 9.1 displays the frontier plots for all methods together (the plots actually contain each hyperparameter sweep as individual points; lines are only for easier visual comparison). The left panel shows reward versus KL; the right panel shows reward versus temperature for PPO to illustrate temperature sensitivity.

> **Figure 9.1** Reward–KL tradeoffs on controlled sentiment generation (10 k prompts). Each symbol represents one training run with fixed hyperparameters.

Several observations emerge:

1. **DPO dominates PPO across all regimes** – For any given level of allowable KL divergence on the horizontal axis, DPO achieves strictly higher rewards than PPO, regardless of whether PPO uses learned rewards or even oracle ground‑truth rewards (PPO–GT). Even when PPO is allowed a larger KL budget (e.g., target KL = 12), DPO still attains higher rewards at every operating point.

2. **Improved sample efficiency** – Because DPO operates directly on binary cross‑entropy loss over preference pairs without sampling from the model or performing outer-loop RL, its training data usage per gradient update is minimal compared to PPO’s requirement of Monte Carlo rollouts and statistics collection. Consequently, within only 100 deviation steps DPO already reaches utility levels that would require thousands of PPO updates under typical hyperparameter settings.

3. **Stability under \(\beta\)** – The \(\beta\)-parameter directly controls how much advantage is given to maximizing reward versus staying faithful to the reference policy via KL regularisation. The DPO frontier exhibits smooth behaviour across orders of magnitude (\(10^{-2} - 10^{0}\)). In contrast, PPO’s frontier is noisier; often small changes in target KL give significantly different reward outcomes due to variance in rollout estimation and clipping behaviour in its objective.

4. **Effectiveness even with learned rewards** – PPO variants trained with an MLE estimate \(r_{\phi}\) of relative preferences still lag behind DPO despite having access to larger theoretical gaps between ideal and approximated reward functions because they must\numerically optimise a nonconvex objective over sampled trajectories.

The clear dominance of DPO over PPO highlights one of its theoretical virtues: by casting RLHF as clairvoyant supervised learning via an implicit reward reparameterisation, it preserves the same optimum while achieving it without any distribution mismatch caused by RL sampling.

## 9.3 Interpretation of Boundary Behaviour

At extreme points on these frontiers we can interpret what each algorithm trades off:

- **Leftmost edge (low KL)**: All algorithms are highly conservative; they generate almost exactly like the SFT reference model (\(D_{\!KL} < 0.01\)). Here rewards are low because no adjustment has yet been made towards positive sentiment.
- **Rightmost edge (high KL)**: Models explore more aggressively; however DPO remains more sensitive to shot noise in preference data because its objective scales linearly with log‐probability ratios—an “importance weighting” that protects against degenerate climbing.
- **Peaks**: The maximum achievable reward given unlimited KL divergence occurs when the policy collapses onto a single high‐sentiment completion or even to gibberish with perfect labels in seen cases; however such degenerate policies drastically inflate KL and are undesirable from a safety viewpoint.

DPO’s balanced weighting mechanism inherently penalises overly greedy moves: the gradient term contains \(\sigma(r_{\theta}(y_l)-r_{\theta}(y_w))\), which becomes small when the model already strongly prefers \(y_w\). Thus progress stalls when it starts fitting noise rather than structure—a property that explains why DPO’s curves remain convex and well-behaved compared to PPO’s sometimes erratic ridge shapes seen at high target KLs.

## 9.4 Implications for Sample Efficiency

The most practical implication stems from sample complexity:

- **Training Data Usage**    
  Each policy update in DPO consumes two completed responses per prompt: one labelled as preferred and one labelled as dispreferred (\(y_w,y_l\)). Since these completions are pre-sampled from the reference policy once during dataset construction, they can be reused across epochs without extra cost—the entire dataset evolves logarithmically rather than linearly with time.
  
- **No Runtime Sampling**    
  Unlike PPO or other actor–critic methods where every update requires on-the-fly rollouts from current policies (\(O(N\times T)\)), DPO does not invoke sampling at all during training; it directly optimises Jensen–Shannon negativity between two probability distributions explicitly given by model logits.
  
- **Stochastic Gradient Variance**    
  The cross-entropy loss is convex over logits in a rank-based sense and incurs lower variance than high-dimensional rollout returns because each preference pair furnishes a clean binary signal whose variance does not degrade with trajectory length.
  
Experiments confirm these advantages quantitatively: Table 9.1 reports average number of token evaluations per training epoch for PPO vs DPO across identical datasets: *PPO* required roughly 45M tokens per epoch whereas *DPO* needed only ~2M tokens because evaluation was performed solely on pre-recorded generations.

| Method | Tokens / Epoch |
|--------|----------------|
| PPO    | 45M |

Even more critical are *wall-clock* improvements—DPO finished all sweeps within roughly half a day on four GPU nodes vs over two days for PPO owing to fewer forward passes through large transformer blocks during training steps.

## 9.5 Summary

The controlled sentiment experiment demonstrates unequivocally that Direct Preference Optimization achieves higher expected rewards while maintaining tighter adherence to its prior distribution than standard RLHF methods such as PPO—even when those methods employ oracle ground-truth rewards or aggressive clipping strategies designed to temper exploration. Because DPO eliminates costly sampling and harnesses low-variance cross-entropy gradients adjusted by an importance weight tied naturally to supplied preferences, it dramatically reduces both data consumption and computational overhead during fine-tuning while preserving alignment guarantees encoded in human choice data.

These results support adopting DPO wherever pretrained language models need nuanced behavioural control under realistic preference signals—especially beneficial when scaling reinforcement techniques becomes infeasible due to memory constraints or latent hallucination risks inherent in large-scale RL loops.
