RL Objective with KL Constraint and Its Optimum Policy**

---

### 3.1   Formal RL objective

In reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) we desire a language‑model policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that, for every user prompt \(x\),  
1. produces completions \(y\) with high *reward* and
2. does not deviate excessively from the original unsupervised policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) that we used to generate the preference data.

Mathematically this is expressed as

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid x)}
      \bigl[\,r(x,y)\,\bigr]}_{\text{expected reward}}
-
\underbrace{\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
          \,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]}_{\text{regularization}},
\tag{3.1}
\]

where \(\beta>0\) controls how strongly we penalise deviation from the reference policy.  
The KL–divergence is applied at the **conditional** level: for every prefix \(x\) we compare the distributions over generated continuations \(y\).  
We normally obtain the reward function \(r(x,y)\) by training a reward model on a set of human preferences, but at this point we treat it as a black box.

Equation (3.1) captures the intuition behind RLHF: *raise* the probability weight of completions that humans praise while *keeping* the language model close to its pre‑trained behaviour, so that sampling remains diverse and stable.

---

### 3.2   Optimal policy under a KL‑constraint

The objective (3.1) is a **constrained optimisation**: we maximise reward subject to staying close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
It can be solved analytically by using calculus of variations or Lagrangian multipliers.  
Let us denote by \(p(y|x)\) a generic conditional distribution over continuations for a fixed prefix \(x\).  
For that \(x\), maximise

\[
J[p]=
    \sum_{y} p(y|x)\,r(x,y)
    - 
    \beta\,\sum_{y} p(y|x)\,
          \log \frac{p(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\tag{3.2}
\]

The second term is simply \(\beta\,D_{\text{KL}}(p\,||\,\pi_{\text{ref}})\).  
Introducing a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda(x)\) for normalisation (\(\sum_y p(y|x)=1\)) and differentiating with respect to \(p(y|x)\) gives

\[
r(x,y)-\beta
    \bigl[
        1+\log\tfrac{p(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} 
    \bigr]
      + \lambda(x)=0 .
\]

Solving for \(p(y|x)\) yields

\[
p(y|x)
   = 
   Z(x)^{-1}
   \,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
   \exp\tfrac{r(x,y)}{\beta},
   \qquad
   Z(x)=
          \!\!\sum_{y}
          \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
          e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}.
      \tag{3.3}
\]

Thus, for every prompt \(x\), *the optimal policy* is an exponential tilt of the reference distribution by the reward, normalised by a partition function \(Z(x)\).  
If we let  

- \(\tilde{\omega}_{x}=Z(x)\),
- and define “unnormalised” importance weights \(w_y = e^{r(x,y)/{\beta}}\),

then (3.3) becomes

\[
p^{*}(y|x)
= 
   {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\, w_y}\;/{\,Z(x)} .
      \tag{3.4}
\]

In short, **every** completion that scores higher under the reward gets an exponential boost in its probability relative to the reference model.

---

### 3.3   Why computing (or using directly) the optimum is expensive

Equation (3.4) looks clean, but it places a heavy computational burden on us:

1. **Partition function**—the sum over all possible continuations:
   \(Z(x)=
     \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)e^{r(x,y)/{\beta}}\).

   For language models there are astronomically many possible tokens or sequences; even if we limit ourselves to sampling from an autoregressive model, we would need to enumerate or otherwise approximate exponentially many continuations at *every* prompt during training.

2. **Exact optimisation**—any algorithm that wants to use (3.4) would have to normalise each time it evaluates or samples from the policy; this is infeasible at scale.

Because of this, RLHF practitioners turn to *approximate* optimisation schemes:

- They learn a reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\),
- Then they use standard reinforcement‑learning algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- In practice, PPO optimises an auxiliary objective based on Monte Carlo samples *(one or multiple trajectories)*; it never requires computing the full partition function.

To make PPO work efficiently they usually replace (3.4)’s intractable KL term with *sample‑based surrogates*: after each environment step they compute an empirical estimate of an importance ratio
\(w_{t}=e^{[r_t-\hat V_t]/{\beta}}\)
and clip it against a trust region window.
That is why RLHF training loops contain *rewards*, *value functions*, *clipped objectives*, and often many hyperparameters – all designed to approximate (3.4).

---

### 3.4   How surrogate rewards are used in practice

Given an approximate reward model \(r_\phi(x,y)\), practitioners form **surrogate rewards** suited for PPO:

1. **Baseline subtraction** – remove an estimate of expected reward to reduce variance.
2. **KL penalty term** – add (or subtract, depending on convention)

      \( r'(x,y)= r_\phi(x,y)- \beta
               (\log p_\theta(y|x)- 
                \log p_{\rm ref}(y|x)) ,
                \)

     which mirrors the exponentiated ratio in (3.4).

3. **Clipped objective** – PPO adjusts gradients only when importance ratios are within a range like [0.8, 1.2].

Combining those ingredients results in an offline algorithm that iteratively samples trajectories from the current policy, evaluates surrogate rewards and loss functions, then applies gradient descent on parameters θ.

While effective empirically, this procedure involves expensive sampling loops and careful tuning of β (the KL penalty weight), clip thresholds, learning rates etc., all because each step is still trying to approximate the analytic optimum in equation (3.4).

---

### 3.5   Takeaway

The constrained RL objective in RLHF admits a closed‑form optimum: it is simply an exponentiated tilt of the reference policy weighted by reward.
However, implementing this optimum directly is impractical due to:
- The astronomically large partition function,
- The impossibility of exact inference over all possible continuations.

Consequently conventional RLHF relies on approximations—surrogate rewards and on‑policy algorithms such as PPO—to indirectly approach this optimum across millions or billions of parameter updates.
In later chapters we will explore how certain reparameterizations allow us to escape back‑and‑forth sampling entirely while still implicitly targeting that same optimum policy without ever estimating the costly partition function—thus simplifying RLHF into a simple classification problem.*
