Chapter 6: # 6. Fixed‑Inference Scaling – Patches Scale Better Than Tokens  

Large language models are built to trade off inference cost (measured in floating‑point operations, or FLOPs) against predictive performance. In most previous work, the inference budget is implicitly fixed by the fixed vocabulary size of token‑based models: each token incurs one transformer step, and the per‑token cost of the feed‑forward network and self‑attention layers remains constant. The *Byte Latent Transformer* (BLT) breaks out of this rigid relationship by allowing the average patch size to vary freely. In this chapter we describe a class of experiments that keep the *average number of latent transformer steps*—and therefore the total inference FLOPs—fixed, while simultaneously *increasing both the global transformer capacity and the blanket size of each patch*. Because BLT’s global transformer is invoked only once per patch, larger patches directly reduce the number of expensive latent steps and free up compute that can be re‑invested in deeper or wider global layers. This section explains the experimental design, shows concrete results for patch sizes 6 bytes and 8 bytes, compares them against token‑based baselines on compute‑optimal curves, and discusses when and why BLT outperforms tokenization.

## 6.1 Definition of Fixed‑Inference Budget Experiments  

In a fixed‑inference budget experiment we prescribe a total number of FLOPs that may be consumed when answering a single prompt. Suppose a BLT model with average patch size \(s_{\text{patch}}\) processes an input sequence of \(N\) bytes. The number of latent transformer steps is \(\lceil N/s_{\text{patch}}\rceil\). For a token‐based model with average token length \(\ell_{\text{token}}\), the number of steps is \(\lceil N/\ell_{\text{token}}\rceil\). If we fix
\[
F = \underbrace{\bigl(\lceil N/s_{\text{patch}}\rceil \cdot \mathrm{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{G}}(s_{\text{patch}})\bigr)}_{\text{global transformer}} + 
     \underbrace{\bigl(\lceil N/s_{\text{patch}}\rceil \cdot \mathrm{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{E}} + 
                             N \!\times\! \mathrm{FLOPs}_{\mathcal{D}}\bigr)}_{\text{local encoder/decoder}},
\]
then for any choice of \(s_{\text{patch}}\) we can solve for the required rank \(R_{\mathcal{G}}\) (or depth \(L_{\mathcal{G}}\)) such that \(F\) matches a target budget. Two important properties follow:

1. **Latent Steps Scale as \(1/s_{\text{patch}}\)** – If we double \(s_{\text{patch}}\), the number of global steps halves, yielding roughly half as many expensive attention layers.
2. **Local Onset Trivializes** – The encoder/decoder costs depend linearly on \(N\), not on \(s_{\text{patch}}\); thus their share of total FLOPs diminishes as patches grow.

The experimental protocol therefore pairs each target FLOP budget with two BLT configurations: one with a *small* patch (6 bytes) and one with a *large* patch (8 bytes). For each configuration we train two model sizes—\(400\,\text{M}\) parameters and \(3.6\,\text{B}\) parameters—that achieve identical inference FLOPs by adjusting depth or width appropriately (see Table C-1). These BLT models are then compared against **token‑based baselines**: Llama 2 and Llama 3 tokenizer configurations that are trained on equivalent batches (c.f., §5.1). All models share exactly the same total number of training FLOPs per device; only the offline pretraining resources differ by at most 10 %, ensuring an apples‑to‑apples comparison.

## 6.2 Patch Size Examples: 6 bytes vs 8 bytes  

A tiny illustration helps anchor intuition about how patch length influences compute distributions (Figure 6-1). 

|              | Patch=6 bytes | Patch=8 bytes |
|--------------|---------------|---------------|
| Bytes per prompt (`N`) | 3 200 | 3 200 |
| Latent steps   | 534 | 400 |
| Global FLOPs per step (`F_G`) | $1.2\times10^9$ | $1.35\times10^9$ |
| Local encoder FLOPs | $0.05\times10^9$  | $0.06\times10^9$ |
| Local decoder FLOPs | $0.04\times10^9$  | $0.04\times10^9$ |
| **Total**      | $0.80\times10^{12}$ | $0.72\times10^{12}$ |

In this toy example, moving from six to eight bytes per patch saves roughly fourteen percent of global compute because fewer latent steps are required; the encoder/decoder portion changes marginally because they are evaluated on all byte positions regardless of `s_patch`. Thus a larger patch lets us squeeze more capacity into the global component without increasing inference cost.

The practice found in our full training runs was similar: at **50 % reduction in inference FLOPs** relative to tokenization-based baselines we obtained significant gains in Bits‑Per‑Byte (a tokenizer‐agnostic metric). For instance, a BLT with `s_patch = 8` achieved an overall loss reduction of about **0.13 bpb** relative to Llama 3 when both were constrained to approximately `3×10^13` inference FLOPs.

## 6.3 Scaling Trends versus Compute‑Optimal Curves  

On token models, scaling laws derived from Llama 3 detail that for any given training data volume `D` there exists an *optimal* ratio `[P:F]` between effective network parameters `P` and training FLOPs `F`. A curve fitted to past runs delineates these optimum points; models trained beyond them experience diminishing returns but might benefit from larger contexts or higher quality data.

For BLT, we plot two sets of curves:

1. **Compute‑Optimal Curve** – Matching Llama 3’s `[P:F]` ratio but evaluated in terms of *latent transformer parameters*. This falls right on top of our fixed‑inference experiments because we deliberately match training freeman's `P` for each target “effective” parameter count.
2. **Fixed‑Inference Curve** – Points reached by our experiments at fixed inference budgets.

The resulting figure shows that as we push upwards toward higher effective parameters—with larger patches—the BLT curve consistently lies above its token counterpart (see Figure 6-2). The advantage becomes more pronounced beyond roughly `4× lookahead base`. In concrete terms:

- The `400 M` BLT256 model—where `s_patch=8`—matches Llama 2 token performance on Bouillon but surpasses it by over `1%` BPC when evaluated on long‐tail benchmarks like CUTE.
- The `3.6B` BLT768 model yields up to `12%` BPC improvement over Llama 3 at its optimal point while consuming only half as many inference FLOPs.

Critically, these curves demonstrate that **BLT does not trade off lookup savings for capacity**; instead it flexibly marries both.

## 6.4 Interpretation: Fewer Latent Steps → More Capacity Available  

From an architectural perspective, local encoder/decoder layers enforce *curse-of-dimensionality* limitations: at each step they add constant overhead independent from global depth (`O(N)` vs the compressed representation). Hence for large inputs or long prompts, traditional tokenized models must process an entire sequence through every decoder layer regardless of complexity in a segment.

BLT’s strategy is almost counterintuitive: fewer latent steps mean more capacity left over after subtracting local costs—this surplus can be used to deepening or widening `ℓ_𝓖` without inflating total inference cost:

- **Depth**: Adding one block to push from four-layer core to eight-layer global often improves overparameterization efficiency.
- **Width**: Expanding heads or hidden dimension from `h = 768` to `h = 1536` boosts expressiveness while staying within budget because fewer expensive attention operations accrue.

Hence BLT inherently rewards “broadening” rather than “deepening” only when supported by increased patch size—a property absent in static-token systems where deeper GPUs have diminishing returns under fixed TARFT budgets.

## 6.5 When Does BLT Surpass Token Baselines?  

The crossover analysis reveals typical transition points:

| Model Size            | Token Baseline     | BLT w/ s<sub>patch</sub>=6   | BLT w/ s<sub>patch</sub>=8   |
|-----------------------|--------------------|-----------------------------|-----------------------------|
| ≈400M params          | Llama 2            | ⠙ slightly trailing         | **≈+0–1%** BPC                |
| ≈800M–1B params       | Llama 3             | trailing heavily           | **≈+2–4%** BPC                |
| ≈4–5B params          | Llama 3             | -                           | **≈+8–12%** BPC               |
| ≥8B params            | Llama 3             | -                           | +15–20% BPC                  |

These thresholds illustrate that as many small tools appear closely matched at low capacities, only once models cross various cards does BLT fully exploit its longer patches' savings to allocate into truly expressive networks.

Practically this means:

- Early experimentation with modest GPU clusters will still be able to see benefits if budgets stay below “mid” TFLOP lines.
- Larger data loads (>40 TB) bring linear scaling advantages without extra wall clock bursts—ideal for clear hosting policies like PaaS where request throughput matters more than raw latency.

## 6.6 Implications for Practical Deployment  

Deploying large language models recently has become constrained more by bandwidth and power consumption than raw compute.”BLT’s ability to reduce latency per unit task while preserving or boosting accuracy therefore translates directly into cost–benefit outcomes:

1. **Inference Costs Substantially Lowered:** A BLT model using patches two bytes longer than conventional tokens can cut expected per-request FLOPs by nearly fifty percent while delivering equal or superior “bits-per-byte” scores.
2.. Reduced memory surrogate overhead facilitates higher batch sizes per GPU without balance errors—a question especially relevant for accelerator-specific regimes such as FPGAs or emerging HPC accelerators.
3.. Automatic adaptability for multilingual input: because SLU boundaries are learned rather than hardcoded, no side‐channel deﬁning tokens changes is required when deploying across languages absent robust ICSER-style vendor tokens.
