# Scaling Up Without Paying the Price: How Bigger Patches Let BLTs Grow Efficiently

Large language models (LLMs) are usually built on a *fixed vocabulary* of tokens. Every token forces the model to pay the same amount of compute, whether it’s a long word, a single punctuation mark, or a noisy garble of bytes. The newer **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** breaks this convention by letting the model’s *patches* grow in size without needing to rethink the whole token dictionary. This means we can enlarge a model—add layers, more hidden units, or higher hidden dimensionality—while keeping the per‑inference floating‑point operations (flops) identical. In this post we walk through how BLT achieves this rare combination of scale and efficiency, why token‑based models can’t match it, and what it means for future LLM research.

---

## 1. Why Fixing Inference FLOPs Is Hard in Token‑Based Models

In most production environments you care about two things at inference time:

1. **Speed** – How fast can the model respond?  
2. **Cost** – How many GPU cycles does one query consume?

If you upgrade a transformer from 1 B parameters to 8 B, you’re only going to see faster responses *if* you also increase arrow‑heads (layers) or packet‑size (hidden dimension). Those upgrades spike flops linearly because each additional layer still processes *every* token in the input.

Token‑based inferences make this problem sticky because token counts are constrained by the vocabulary:

| Model | Avg tokens | Avg bytes per token |
|-------|------------|---------------------|
| Llama‑2 | 1 k | ~3.7 |
| Llama‑3 | 1 k | ~4.4 |

When you double model size but do not change tokenization, every new step still carries the same cost: one decoder/encoder layer per token. There’s little freedom to redistribute compute on the fly.

---

## 2. BLT’s Dynamic Patching: THE KEY TO FLEXIBLE FLOP ALLOCATION

BLT works on raw bytes but groups them into **patches**—variable length sub‑sequences that act as the raw material for a *latent* transformer.

### What happens when we enlarge patches?

- **Fewer tokens for a fixed byte budget** → The latent transformer sees *fewer* steps.
- **More representational capacity per patch** → The larger patch can encode finer information in its latent vector.
- **Same or even fewer global transformer layers** → Since patching is dynamic, we only forward pass through the heavy global transformer less often.

Because BLT’s local encoder is cheap relative to the global encoder (it uses only shallow transformer blocks), we can let patch size grow aggressively without hurting overall efficiency.

In contrast, a BPE tokenizer has no such knob: every word or sub‑word ends up as a token regardless of its length or complexity. The only way to reduce steps is to *increase* token length by popping more words per token—something that pushes up embedding table sizes and loses fine control over context granularity.

---

## 3. The Fixed‑Inference FLOPs Experiment

The authors ran an elegant experiment to prove that BLT can scale its model size while staying inside an inference flop budget that is identical to a tokenized baseline:

1. Pick two inference flop budgets:
   - **Low**: ~200k flops per query.
   - **High**: ~400k flops per query.
2. For each budget they matched three models:
   - A conventional BPE transformer (Llama‑2),
   - A BPE transformer with an enlarged vocabulary (Llama‑3),
   - BLT variants with patch sizes 6 bytes and 8 bytes.

All three sets had *the same* amount of flops at inference time.

The results were telling:

| Inference Budget | Model            | Scale  | BPB (bits/byte) |
|------------------|------------------|--------|-----------------|
| Low              | Llama‑2          | 400 M  | 1.35            |
| Low              | Llama‑3          | 400 M  | 1.32            |
| Low              | BLT‐PS6          | 400 M  | 1.30            |
| Low              | BLT‐PS8          | >800 M? | 1.29            |
| High             | Llama‑3          | 800 M  | 1.28            |
| High             | BLT‐PS6          | >800 M? | 1.27            |
| High             | BLT‐PS8          | >800 M? | 1.26            |

With identical computation limits, increasing the patch size from six to eight bytes let BLT hold more than double its parameter count while beating—or edge‐winning—the BPE baselines across almost all metrics.

Two key insights emerge:

- **Scaling Up Without Extra Compute:** Because global transformer steps become fewer, we reallocate those saved flops into extra layers or larger hidden dimensions.
- **Robust “Stop at Budget” Strategy:** Even if new research claims better scaling laws for base transformers, exceeding our inference budget would just double cost; with BLT we can keep budget constant while enriching model capacity.

---

## 4. Why Tokenizers Can’t Mirror This Freedom

Tokenizers are baked with a fixed vocabulary; more tokens mean bigger embedding tables and larger softmax classifiers—thats unavoidable specific GPU memory cost beyond flops.

Even if you artificially add longer tokens:

- **Quantization bottleneck:** More byte groups per token increase memory pressure for position encodings.
- **Data sparsity:** Rare long tokens make few‑shot learning harder; more tokens mean hyper­parameter searches explode.
- **Compression mismatch:** Tokenizers rely on heuristics that may mis­estimate locality; they cannot react during generation like BLT does by predicting next‐byte entropy on the fly.

Consequently, you’re stuck between two extremes:

1. Keep your depth shallow and run many small tokens → high per-token cost.
2. Dilute your passages with long subwords → embeddings blow up; we lose orthographic detail and robustness.

BLT sidesteps this tradeoff by turning *predictive complexity* into *compute allocation*, independent of any predefined dictionary.

---

## 5. Practical Takeaways for Model Engineers

### • Policy Design  
If you must stay within an inference budget (e.g., billable API calls), start with **medium patch sizes** (~6–8 bytes). Then simply add more latent layers until you hit your flop ceiling.

### • Training Loops  
During pre-training maintain *constant batch byte count*. For each epoch re‑compute your patch boundaries based on updated entropy thresholds so your effective sequence length stays balanced across scales.

### • Cross‑Attention Channels  
Want improved quality? Sprinkle cross‐attention inside local encoders/decoders: it gives patches richer information while still low‐latency because cross attend only occurs within their constituent bytes.

### • Infrastructure  
BLT’s dynamic masks are natively supported by modern flexible attention kernels such as FlexAttention or FlashAttention-2; test both to see which runs best on your GPU farm.

---

## 6. What Might Come Next

*BLTs open an entirely new “patch dimension” that hasn’t been explored before.* Two promising directions follow:

1. **End-to-end Patch Learner** – Rather than relying on a pre-trained entropy model, train a lightweight boundary predictor jointly with main loss – this could reduce preprocessing overhead and adapt better to domain shift.
2. **Hardware Acceleration** – Custom accelerator firmware could treat patches as variable-size kernel launches—dramatically better cache locality than fixed-size words/resolutions in classic transformers.

If hardware follows software—and with larger data sources coming online—BLTs could become an industry standard where computational budgets are tight but model sophistication must be high.

---

## TL;DR  

- **Problem:** Token‐based LLMs cannot freely adjust compute allocation because each token forces equal expense whether needed or not.
- **Solution:** BLT groups raw bytes into dynamic patches whose size is guided by next-byte entropy; larger patches reduce global transformer steps while preserving expressiveness.
- **Result:** In controlled experiments with fixed inference flop budgets, increasing patch size allowed BLTs to scale almost linearly in parameters—surpassing tokenized baselines in Bits/Byte error without extra cost.
- **Implication:** When licensing fees are tied to flops or latency, investing in BLTs gives you an economic scaling advantage that pure tokenization dictons can't match.
