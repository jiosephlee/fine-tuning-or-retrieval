# Transformer Overview and Self‑Attention Mathematics

The Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al., has become the backbone of modern sequence‑to‑sequence models ranging from language modelling to vision and audio tasks. At its heart lies a single operation – self‑attention – which lets every token inside a sequence query the others, producing a context‑aware representation for each position. The Transformer is typically composed of several alternating layers, each containing a multi‑head self‑attention sub‑layer followed by a position‑wise feed‑forward network, with residual connections and layer normalisation interleaved. Formally, for a single attention head we denote the query, key and value matrices as  

\[
\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},
\]

where \(N\) is the sequence length and \(d\) the head dimension. The attention output \(\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\) is computed via the equations

\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathbf{S} &= \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\\
 \mathbf{P} &= \operatorname{softmax}\bigl(\mathbf{S}\bigr)\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\\
 \mathbf{O} &= \mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{V}\;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} .
\]

The softmax is applied row‑wise, giving each query a probability distribution over all keys. In practice one first subtracts the maximum value of each score row (row‑max) from \(\mathbf S\) before applying the exponential in order to guard against numerical overflow.

Multi‑head attention (MHA) simply stacks several such independent heads in parallel and concatenates their outputs. Each head has its own projection matrices for queries, keys and values, effectively enabling the model to attend along multiple subspaces simultaneously. This increases both representational power and computational workload: we must compute separate Q/K/V embeddings for every head, assemble all head outputs thereafter, and perform independent matrix multiplications per head.

# Quadratic Scaling and Its Impact on Training and Inference

The most significant source of computational burden is the pairwise scoring step \(\mathbf Q\,\mathbf K^{\top}\). Although this operation can be formulated as an \(N\times d \) matrix multiplied by an \(N\times d \) matrix (transposed), its outlier complexity is quadratic in sequence length:

\[
{\rm FLOPs}_{QK} = 2\,N^{2}\,d .
\]

The final aggregation step \(\mathbf P\,\mathbf V\) adds another term \(2\,N^{2}\,d / H\), where \(H\) is the number of heads (the factor \(1/H\) arises because each head processes a subspace of dimension \(d= D/H,\; D=\texttt{hidden dim}\)). In total, the forward pass of one attention layer consumes

\[
{\rm FLOPs}_{k} = 4\,N^{2}\,\texttt{subhead dim}\times H ,
   \tag{1}
\]

which grows as \(O(N^{2}):\)
a modest increase in sequence length from 512 to 2048 multiplies memory traffic and arithmetic operations by sixteen.

This quadratic scaling manifests in two flatlands:

1. **Memory bandwidth bottleneck** – The score matrix \(\mathbf S \in \mathbb R^{N\times N}\) quickly swells beyond on‑chip storage; it is typically streamed from device RAM each iteration.
2. **Compute bottleneck** – The accumulative cost contrasts with other parts of the Transformer that scale linearly with \(N D^2 / H.\)

For large language models (LLMs) moving beyond a few thousand tokens, either they resort to sub‑quadratic approximations or accumulate untenable latencies on conventional GPUs, making long‑context training or inference infeasible.

# Long‑Context and Multimodal Applications

The motivation to push attention to ever longer sequences stems from two broad research directions:

| Category | Typical Use Case | Why Rule–Of–Thumbs Pushing limits? |
|----------|------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Long textual documents** | Legal analysis, scientific literature mining | Models need to reason across multiple paragraphs or full papers; quadratically computing pairwise interactions across all tokens becomes necessary otherwise |
| **Multi‑document reasoning** | Multi‑source summarisation; knowledge graph traversal | Requires contextual linking spanning different documents |
| **Large codebases** | Code completion in repositories with millions of lines | Need to track identifiers across distant source files |
| **High‐resolution images** | 3D point cloud processing or gigapixel vision transformers | Each pixel may attend to any other pixel – a growing problem when spatial resolution exceeds ten thousand pixels |
| **Audio / Video** | Speech synthesis or video captioning over long clips | Temporal sequences grow proportionally to duration |
| **Hybrid modalities** | Visual–language alignment on long documents + images (e.g., scanned PDFs with embedded figures) | Cross modal attention between text tokens and all image patches |

In each scenario reducing the quadratic computational cost directly expands feasible context horizons – e.g., enabling LLMs that can read an entire book in one shot, or vision transformers that can treat an entire geospatial image as one entity.

# Existing Acceleration Strategies

Various methods have emerged over the past few years to address this quadratic wall:

## Approximation Techniques  
These tactics reduce computation by approximating either the softmax or all pairings:
- **Low‐rank factorisation** – exploiting that many self‐attention matrices have low effective rank.
- **Sparse attention patterns** – e.g., linear algebraic locality schemas such as BigBird or Longformer that zero out far entries.
- **Kernelised softmax or locality sensitive hashing** – trading exactness for speed.
- **Probabilistic sampling** – selecting high–impact pairs at random.

While effective at reducing FLOPs drastically, approximation often sacrifices performance guarantees on standard benchmarks (e.g., GLUE), making them riskier for production LLM deployments where accuracy cannot be compromised.

## Software Optimisation  
Engineered kernels that harness GPU architecture more efficiently:
- **FlashAttention** family – fusing matrix multiplication and softmax into a single kernel that avoids writing intermediate matrices (\(\mathbf S,\mathbf P)\) back to global memory.
- **Triton / CUTLASS based custom kernels** - hand tuned code targeting specific GPU instruction sets such as Tensor Cores or update paths.
- **Persisted models for FP32/F16 gelinear mixing**, underlining efficient long-range runtime.

These solutions do not change computational complexity but squeeze close to 10× throughput improvement over naïve PyTorch-attention implementations.

## Alternative Architectures  
Proposals that reframed attention entirely:
- Recurrent memory machines (e.g., RWKV),
- Structured recurrence,
- Hierarchical linear Transformers,
- Mamba/FiLM architectures based on convolutional recurrence instead of self-attention.

Such designs eschew explicit dot product attention altogether but require considerable redesign of pre-existing pretrained models.

## Summary

Self-attention is at the core of transformer capabilities but incurs quadratic costs that limit both model size and input length. The demand from long-context tasks—be it in text, code, vision or audio—drives continuous research into acceleration techniques. While approximation reduces flops at some loss in fidelity, software approaches like FlashAttention aim for full-precision performance gains by harnessing modern GPU hardware more fully. Future chapters discuss how advances in GPU features such as Tensor Core asynchrony and FP8 precision are leveraged in FlashAttention-3 to push these gains yet further while preserving accuracy—even on sequences hundreds of times longer than typical transformer inputs.
