### What role does incoherent processing play in reducing quantization error for FP8 attention?
Question:

Answer:
**What “incoherent processing” does – why it helps FP8 attention, and why the paper uses a random orthogonal matrix**

---

### 1.  The problem: quantization error in FP8

FP8 (the `e4m3` format used on Hopper) has only four exponent bits and three fraction bits.  
Compared to FP16 or BF16, this gives roughly an eight‑fold reduction in the quantisation step size, which in turn inflates the relative error of any number that is far from zero.  
Large transformer models routinely contain out‑lier activations—values that are tens of standard deviations away from the bulk of the distribution (see section 6.3 of the paper).  If a few such outliers end up in a small block, they dominate the dynamic range of that block and cause most other values to be rounded coarsely when the block is quantised with a single scale factor.

A naïve per‑tensor scaling, where one scalar `s_Q`, `s_K`, `s_V` multiplies an entire query, key or value tensor, therefore induces significant loss: every entry is multiplied by a single `s_?`, so large outliers overwhelm the scaling chosen to keep smaller entries representable.  This manifests as a noticeable increase in RMSE when attention is computed entirely in FP8.

---

### 2.  Block‑quantisation: what fixes part of it

Section 5.3.2 of the paper introduces *block quantisation*: each block (e.g., one query‑block or a key/value block in the tiling scheme) gets its own scaling factor.  
Because FlashAttention already works on blocks, this change can be made at virtually no run‑time cost: we just divide each entry by its block's scale when we load it into registers and multiply back at the end of the operation.

Block quantisation significantly reduces error compared to per‑tensor scaling – we won’t see the problem where a single outlier forces an entire head’s precision to collapse – but it does **not** solve one stubborn issue: *the presence of large outliers within a single block*.

If a block still contains even one enormous value, that value will still defeat the other values’ representability because all values in that block share one exponent bias after scaling.

---

### 3.  What incoherent processing does

“**Incoherent processing**” refers to multiplying **both** queries **and** keys by a *random orthogonal matrix* \(\mathbf{M}\) **before** quantising them to FP8:

$$
\mathbf{\tilde Q}= \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M},\qquad
\mathbf{\tilde K}= \mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}.
$$

Since \(\mathbf{M}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top=\mathbf{I}\)), the bilinear form that matters for attention is preserved:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf S} = \tilde{\mathbf Q}\tilde{\mathbf K}^\top
                  = (\mathbf Q\mathbf M)(\mathbf K\mathbf M)^\top
                  = \mathbf Q\underbrace{\mathbf M\mathbf M^\top}_{=\mathbf I}\!\mathbf K^\top
                  = \mathbf S .
$$

Thus attention output is **exactly unchanged** by this transformation, but every entry of \(\tilde{\mathbf Q}\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf K}\) becomes a linear combination of *all* entries in its original row/column.  
Consequences:

1. **Outliers are spread out.**  
   Suppose row \(i\) of \(\mathbf Q\) contains an extremely large entry \(q_{i,j}\). After multiplying by \(\mathbf M\), each component of \(\tilde q_i\) will be roughly an average over many columns weighted by entries of ± 1, so no single coordinate becomes overwhelmingly large. The “spreading” effect turns huge isolated fingerprints into many moderate values.
2. **Dynamic range per block shrinks.**  
   With fewer extreme points inside each block, a single scale factor can now cover most values with relatively small bit‑step without over‑quantising many small numbers.
3. **Perception of “incoherence.”**  
   Because we mix rows/columns differently for queries and keys, their feature maps become decorrelated; this decorrelation is what authors refer to as *incoherent* – signals are no longer aligned with the low‑precision grid.

---

### 4.  Why use *random* orthogonal matrices instead of just any orthogonal

- A random diagonal matrix would simply flip signs; that gives no benefit.
- Using a fixed orthogonal matrix could lead to structure in transformed data (e.g., repeated patterns), which might inadvertently create new outliers.
- A random construction – product of random ±1 diagonals and a Hadamard matrix – ensures:
  - All entries are still ±1 scaled versions; you can perform `ṽ = v ⊙ ±1` and then do an FFT‑like transform `H v` efficiently.
  - Multiplication costs \(O(d\,\log d)\) rather than \(O(d^2)\).
  - It’s easy to incorporate into existing pruning or rotary‑embedding kernels (the same matrix can be reused across all layers if desired).

The paper cites [Chee et al., 2024] and [Tseng et al., 2024] for this exact construction; they show empirically that it brings up to two times lower RMSE compared to plain block quantisation.

---

### 5.  How it fits into FlashAttention‑3

In FlashAttention‑3’s low‑precision kernel:

1. Before quantising any query or key tile into FP8:
   - Multiply by `M`.  
   - Use efficient LDSM/STSM instructions on Hopper to perform an in‑kernel transpose/orthogonal transform without extra global memory traffic.
2. Quantise each resulting tile using its own block scale factor (block quantisation).
3. Proceed with FP8 GEMMs (`WGMMA`) exactly as before – because mathematically nothing changed for scores.

Because all upstream transformations preserve exact dot products, intermediate softmax calculations still give correct probabilities; only rounding errors are alleviated.

---

### 6.  Numerical effect confirmed

Section 6.3 reports that applying both block quantisation **and** incoherent processing reduces overall RMSE by up to **2.6×** compared to standard FP8 per‑tensor scaling while keeping performance high (the extra transform cost is negligible thanks to fast Hadamard + warp‐level operations).

---

### Bottom line

*Incoherent processing* is simply the pre‑quantisation multiplication by a random orthogonal matrix that preserves attention scores but statistically disperses extreme elements across dimensions, thereby shrinking dynamic range per block and dramatically lowering FP8 rounding error without any additional GPU data movement or major algorithmic change.*
