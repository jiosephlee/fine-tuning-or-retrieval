# Automated vs Human Chatbot Evaluation: Why GPT‑4 Ratings Are Cheaper but Not Perfect

Estimating how good a chatbot is usually feels like an art: do you set up a Million‑Dollar contest? Or do you just ask people to read a handful of responses and write their thumbs‑up? In practice, both approaches cost money and time. The QLoRA paper tackles this “chatbot evaluation problem” head‑on by building a **tournament‑style Elo** system in which **GPT‑4** acts as the judge. This blog distills that approach, explains the pitfalls it reveals (order effects, language ambiguity), and shows how statistical agreement metrics (Fleiss κ, Kendall τ) let us gauge when model‑based grading is safe to use.

---

## 1. The Need for a Better Benchmark

* **Human studies** are the gold standard but scale poorly.  
  * Recruiting annotators for every prompt and every model pair can run into thousands of dollars and weeks of work.  
  * Even with multiple annotators per item you get noisy scores because “good” is subjective.

* **Automated scoring** looks tempting: feed all responses to another LLM (e.g., GPT‑4) and let it rate them.  
  * It is “cheap” (just API credits) and fast, but does GPT‑4 truly agree with people?

The QLoRA authors built an **Elo tournament** that lets them compare responses pairwise (Model A vs Model B) across thousands of prompts. They then keep two kinds of judgments:

| Judgement | Source | How it’s gathered |
|-----------|--------|--------------------|
| GPT‑4 scoring | Model | Prompted with two answers, asked to pick the better one |
| Human scoring | Amazon MTurk | Two reviewers per prompt, three reviewers for pairwise picks |

---

## 2. The Tournament Workflow

1. **Collect prompts** – e.g., all 80 Vicuna prompts or 953 Open-AI‑Assistant validation queries.
2. **Generate responses** – each model follows the same nucleus sampling (`p=0.9`, `temperature=0.7`) so all outputs are comparable.
3. **Pairwise comparison** – For every unordered pair `(i,j)` of models we produce two versions of the prompt:
   * `<prompt>\nModel i: <resp> \n Model j: <resp>`
   * `<prompt>\nModel j: <resp> \n Model i: <resp>`
   This removes any bias that might arise from model order.
4. **Score with GPT‑4** – The prompt "Rate these two responses; choose the better one or say 'tie'…" is given to GPT‑4.
5. **Score with humans** – Two crowd–workers read the same prompt and choose their preferred response.
6. **Compute Elo scores** – Treat each choice as a “match” where the winner gains K points from the loser using `K=32` and starting rating `R=1000`.

The Elo score reflects the expected win‐rate against randomly chosen opponents drawn from the same pool.

---

## 3. Where GPT‑4 Shines

- **Speed & Cost** – Running a full tournament on thousands of pairs takes minutes vs weeks for humans.
- **Consistency** – With a single prompt wording GPT‑4 always outputs something like
```
Response A is better because ...
```
while humans scramble between “yes”, “no”, “tie”, etc.
- **Targeted Comparison** – Because GPT‑4 receives only the two candidate answers and no extra context (e.g., model identity), its decisions are mostly content‐driven rather than brand‐driven.

The result reported in the paper was striking: *Guanaco* 65B earned an elo score of ~1100 out of ≈1200 humans may assign around 1030 (still on par with ChatGPT). That’s “cheaper” but not identical.

---

## 4. Order Effects – A Subtle Bug You’ll Never Spot Without Careful Design

When GPT‑4 receives two responses, it often gives slightly higher scores to whichever answer appears first:

```
Model A: ...    ← Ours
Model B: ...    ← Peek
```

This causes an overestimation bias if you never randomize order.

### What did the authors find?

> “We found a significant ordering effect with GPT‑4 assigning higher scores to the response occurring earlier in the prompt.”  
> “…we recommend reporting the mean score over both orders.”

The fix is simple: run each pair twice with swapped orders and average.

---

## 5. Agreement Metrics – Quantifying How Close Model and Human Scores Are

When you have two annotator sources evaluating identical items, you need metrics that are not just binary error rates.

### 5.1 Fleiss κ (Kappa)
* Measures inter‐annotator agreement beyond chance for multi–label classification.
* Values:
    * `<0` : Less than chance
    * `0–0.20` : Slight
    * `0.21–0.40` : Fair
    * `0.41–0.60` : Moderate
    * `0.61–0.80` : Substantial
    * `>0.81` : Almost perfect

The paper reports:
* Human over humans: κ = **0.42** → moderate agreement.
* GPT‑4 over humans at system level (~75 judgments): κ = **~0**, low agreement; but at pairwise level κ = **≥0**, suggesting patterns emerge only in aggregate.

### 5.2 Kendall τ & Spearman Spearman-rank Correlation
* Both capture rank correlation (how well two ranked lists match).
* Kendall τ = correlation of concordant vs discordant pairs.
* Spearman r = Pearson correlation on ranks.

For system‐level ratings:
```
Kendall τ ≈ 0.43
Spearman r ≈ 0.55
```
So about half of human rank order is captured by GPT‑4—useful but not perfect.

### Implications

1. If you plan to replace people by GPT‑4 entirely, expectations must be tempered; reliance on large models alone may overlook subtle human preferences (like tone or humor).
2. You can still use GPT‑4 as a screening tool—identify likely winners/losses—and involve humans only where ratings diverge significantly.

## 7   When Should You Trust GPT‑4?  

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|----------------|
| Quick sanity check on new models | ✅ |
| Prioritizing budget over absolute precision | ✅ |
| Whole product compliance review | ❌ (needs human guardrail review) |
| Benchmarking early research prototypes | ✅ but validate against ground truth later |

### Takeaway Checklist  

- [ ] Randomise response order for every comparison pair.<br>
- [ ] Record both raw scores and final choice (“tie” counts too).<br>
- [ ] Compute Fleiss κ and Kendall τ against a small human sample.<br>
- [ ] Seed Elo start rating / K-factor appropriately per dataset size.<br>
- [ ] Use double-validation trick: if GPT score diverges from human Consensus by >10% flag entry for review.<br>

---

## 8   Final Thoughts  

*(TL;DR)*  
The QLoRA paper’s tournament style Elo uses GPT‑4 as an inexpensive referee that nearly replicates human ranking while saving time & money—provided you guard against order bias and understand that agreement isn’t perfect:

* ~30 % mismatch between human consensus and model judgment at item level (Fleiss κ≈ 0).  
* Substantial match at system level (~kτ≈ 0·43).  
* Around twice as fast as humanoid human scoring once your code is written.

If your goal is to iterate fast on language models without shellding out hundreds of dollars per benchmark run—or if you simply want a first-pass quality gate—an automated tournament like this will fit nicely into your pipeline.

For high stakes applications or regulatory compliance tweaking—think financial advisory bots or healthcare advice—you still want humans on board somewhere in the loop; model judgments should be treated as flagging signals rather than final verdicts.

Happy coding!
