# Chapter Title

**QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized Large Language Models**

---

## Introduction to LLM Finetuning

Large language models (LLMs) have become the backbone of modern natural‑language processing, powering everything from code generation to conversational assistants. However, the “one‑size‑fits‑all” nature of pretrained weights is often insufficient for specialized tasks or for adhering to new safety and ethical constraints. Fine‑tuning—a process where the pretrained model is further optimized on a target dataset—remains the most effective way to imbue LLMs with task‑specific knowledge or behavioral traits.  

Yet fine‑tuning incurs a steep price: the sheer size of state‑of‑the‑art models (e.g., 65 B parameters in LLaMA 65B) turns every training step into an engineering bottleneck. GPU memory usage scales roughly linearly with the number of trainable parameters, and when gradients must be stored for backpropagation, the required memory balloons further. As a result, conventional training workflows often demand dozens of high‑end GPUs or terabytes of consolidated memory—resources that many researchers simply do not possess.

In this chapter we present **QLoRA** (Quantized Low‑Rank Adapter with Retained Optimal Accuracy), a principled finetuning paradigm that reduces memory consumption by more than an order of magnitude while preserving (and sometimes surpassing) the performance of full‑precision training. We detail the theoretical foundations behind its core innovations—4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4), Double Quantization, and Paged Optimizers—and describe how they cooperate to make 65 B parameter finetuning feasible on a single consumer GPU.

---

### Challenges of Conventional Finetuning on Large Models

Conventional finetuning keeps all weights in full precision (typically 16 bits per weight, commonly named FP16 or BF16). Consider a transformer decoder such as LLaMA 65B, whose raw weight tensor occupies roughly \(65\times10^9\times2\ \text{bytes} \approx 260\,\text{GB}\). During training, gradients must be stored simultaneously: a forward pass produces activations that are checkpointed for backward computation; then each weight’s gradient appears as an additional tensor that must reside in GPU memory until the optimiser step updates them. For a mini‑batch covering many long sequences—a common case when finetuning on instruction datasets—the memory consumption spikes far beyond the base weight size.

To mitigate this, two canonical strategies are employed:

1. **Gradient Checkpointing** – only selected intermediary activations are retained; others are recomputed during backpropagation. While effective at cutting activation memory by up to 50 %, checkpointing increases computational cost and introduces sensitivity to sequence length.
2. **Parameter Efficient Fine‑Tuning (PEFT)** – expensive parameters are frozen while introducing many trainable adapters (e.g., LoRA). The idea is that a small set of low‑rank matrices can capture task‑specific perturbations without touching most model weights.

Even with these tricks, training very large models may require 500–800 GB of GPU memory when using mixed precision FLM16 runtimes—clearly beyond what a single consumer GPU offers. Thus, purely architectural modifications alone are insufficient; we must revisit how we store and manipulate model weights back at the arithmetic level.

---

### Memory Overhead of 16‑Bit Training

A straight comparison illustrates where most memory sweat lies:

| Component                     | Size (for a typical layer) | Notes |
|------------------------------|----------------------------|-------|
| Model weights                | ~5 GB (BF16) (for 7B)     | Unsqueezed tensor size |
| Activation gradients         | ~18 MB/checkpoint          | Grows logarithmically w/ batch size |
| LoRA adapters                | ~26 MB                      | Very small relative to weights |
| Optimiser states             | 1–2× parameter size       | Adam/AdamW maintain first/second moments |

When moved up to 65 B parameters, these numbers explode proportionately. Crucially, while LoRA reduces *trainable* parameter count drastically, **most mid‑training memory is still consumed by storing activation gradients**—the backbone tensors produced during forward passes that are needed for error propagation back into the frozen base model’s parameters.

This observation sets the stage for QLoRA: if we can *compress* the base model weights into *four bits* without single‐point loss, we both – a large portion of the static space and – the dynamic space needed to recompute intermediate states from quantised tensors will shrink dramatically.

---

### Overview of Quantization and Its Limitations for Training

Quantization traditionally belongs to **inference acceleration**: reducing computation precision from 32-bit float to an integer representation such asInt8 results in fewer FLOPs and lower power consumption while tolerating minor accuracy loss. Common schemes include *blockwise* quantization (each block gets its own scale factor) and *per-channel* scaling to mitigate outliers.

When applied to training, however, several pitfalls emerge:

- **Gradient Doom:** Backpropagating through coarse quantisation is noisy because low–bit quantisers do not preserve small gradient nuances necessary for fine learning.
- **Dynamic Range Collapse:** Standard Int8 or Float4 representations struggle with highly skewed weight distributions typical in transformers; outliers saturate bins leading to catastrophic clipping.
- **Memory Footprint:** Even after quantizing weights to Int4 or Float4, we still need *scales* per block — usually stored as FP32 — whose overhead can match or exceed the compressed weights’ savings when block sizes are small.

Therefore any attempt at *training with low‐precision weights* must reconcile these issues by designing a “lossless” pathway that simultaneously enables efficient forward pass computations and stable gradient flows through frozen parameters.

---

### Motivation for QLoRA: 4-Bit Finetuning Without Accuracy Loss

QLoRA addresses training gap by combining three complementary ideas:

1. **Zero‐Mean Normalized Distribution Assumption**  
   Pretrained transformer weights empirically follow an almost Gaussian distribution centered at zero with relatively stable standard deviation per layer (see Appendix A). This allows us to construct quantisation schemes rooted in information theory rather than naïve rounding.
   
2. **NF4 Data Type – NormalFloat**  
   NF4 builds on quantile quantisation which guarantees every bin receives the same expected number of samples if data follows its target distribution. Instead of classic Float4 or Int4 formats which place uneven bin widths over wide dynamic ranges, NF4 shapes bins symmetrically around zero so that both tails receive equal counts—a property optimal for normal data.
   
3. **Double Quantisation**  
   The scales required per block dwarf minor savings if left uncompressed. QLoRA first maps raw FP32 scales \(c^{\text{FP32}}\) into FBloat16 coefficients; then applies an *auxiliary* 8-bit floating point quantiser on those scales before returning them during dequantisation. This hierarchical compression yields roughly \(0.37\)-bit savings per weight on top of the primary NF4 bitwidth.
   
These ideas yield a representation where:
- The base model resides entirely in four bits plus compressed constants;  
- All computations during forward/backward passes lift tensors back into BF16 space so that standard cuBLAS kernels run unchanged;  
- Gradient flow only touches LoRA parameters; thus all 65 B base weights remain strictly frozen—even though their values are updated implicitly via adapter adjustments.

Empirical evidence across benchmarks shows QLoRA faithfully reproduces full FP16 performance up through \(65\,\text{B}\), an order of magnitude higher inference accuracy than any prior low-bit finetuning attempt without resorting to reinforcement learning or full retraining.

Using QLoRA across multiple instruction-following datasets—from OASST1’s multi-turn dialogues up through FLAN v2’s curated prompts—the authors built a family of models dubbed *Guanaco*. The standout device among them is Guanaco 65B:

Beyond academic curiosity, this unlocks homogenous research pipelines: teams lacking large compute clusters can safely experiment with privacy-preserving instruction tuning cycles locally, enabling rapid prototyping throughout startups and in academia alike.

# 2. Mathematical Foundations of Quantization

Quantization is a foundational technique in modern machine‑learning systems, especially for large language models (LLMs) where memory and compute budgets dictate the feasible precision of stored parameters. This chapter develops the mathematical ideas underlying block‑wise \(k\)-bit quantization—how high‑precision tensors are compressed into low‑precision representations, how scaling constants (quantization constants) are computed and used, and how the original values are recovered during inference or training. We also examine the ubiquitous problem of outlier values that can ruin quantization efficiency and motivate block‑rescaling as a practical remedy.

## Definition of Block‑wise \(k\)-bit Quantization

Let \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) denote a generic weight or activation tensor that we wish to store in one of the low‑precision data types \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\) with \(k\) bits per element (for example, 8‑bit integers or 4‑bit floating point). Directly mapping all entries of \(\mathbf{X}\) into the narrow range supported by \(\mathbb{Z}_{2^k}\) risks severe loss of detail if the distribution has a large dynamic range. A standard way to mitigate this is **block‑wise quantization**: we partition \(\mathbf{X}\) into \(n\) contiguous blocks, each containing \(B\) elements,
\[
n = \frac{b\times h}{B}\,, \qquad
\mathbf{X}_i = \mathbf{X}\bigl[(i-1)B: iB\bigr]\,,\quad i=1,\dots,n\,,
\]
and quantize each block independently using its own scaling constant \(c_i\). Thus the quantized tensor is a concatenation of block‑wise quantized pieces:
\[
\mathbf{X}^{(k)} = 
\bigl[\mathrm{Quant}(\mathbf{X}_1,c_1),\, \mathrm{Quant}(\mathbf{X}_2,c_2),\, \dots,\, \mathrm{Quant}(\mathbf{X}_n,c_n)\bigr]\,.
\]
The block size \(B\) is tuned to balance two opposing effects: **smaller** blocks yield finer local scaling (reducing quantization error for non‑uniform distributions), but record an extra scaling constant per block, inflating memory overhead; **larger** blocks reduce overhead but degrade precision when the tensor’s values vary dramatically across positions.

## Quantization Constant (Scale) and Its Role

For any block \(\mathbf{X}_i\), we wish to map its continuous values to the discrete set supported by the chosen data type. The most common strategy is **min–max rescaling**, which uses a *scaling constant* computed from a statistic that reflects the magnitude of the block’s entries. Let us denote by
\[
s_i = {\text{absmax}}(\mathbf{X}_i) = \max_{x \in \mathbf{X}_i} |x|
\tag{2}
\]
the maximum absolute value in block \(i\). The corresponding **scaling constant** (also called “quantization factor” or “norm”) is then
\[
c_i = \frac{\alpha}{s_i}\,,
\]
where \(\alpha>0\) is chosen so that after rescaling every value lies in the admissible integer range. For an 8‑bit signed integer with range \([-127,\,127]\), we set
\[
\alpha = 127\,,
\]
yielding
\[
c_i= \frac{127}{s_i}\,.
\tag{3}
\]
Once \(c_i\) is known, we can quantize all entries in block \(i\):
```
Q(x; c_i) = round(c_i · x)\,,  x ∈ ℝ
```
which zips each real number into an integer label \(q = Q(x;c_i)\in[-127,\dots,127]\). In practice this operation can be expressed vectorially as:
\[
Q_{(i)} = \operatorname{round}(c_i\,{\bf X}_i)\,, 
\tag{4}
\]
producing an integer tensor of shape \(B\times h/B.\)

The inverse operation—recovering an approximation of the original tensor from its quantized form—is called **dequantization**, which simply reverses the rescaling:
```matlab
DQ(q; c_i)= q / c_i\,.
```
Formally,
\[
DQ(Q_{(i)}; c_i)= c_i^{-1}\,\operatorname{round}(c_i\,{\bf X}_i)
               \approx {\bf X}_i\,.
   \tag{5}
   \]
Because rounding discards high–frequency bits, round‑trip error cannot be avoided but it can be made tolerable when many parameters are involved and when downstream layers are robust to small perturbations.

Thus, each block introduces two key pieces of data:  
1. The quantized integer tensor \(Q_{(i)}^{(k)}.\)  
2. Its associated scaling constant \(c_i.\)

The balance between granularity (block size), expressiveness (\(k)\), and additional overhead (\(|c|\)) lies at the heart of efficient fixed‑precision storage.  

## Dequantization Process

Dequantization rewinds the forward quantizer so that downstream computations can proceed in floating point precision without ever reconstructing the original high‑precision tensor on disk. In inference engines, this step typically occurs at layer boundaries: whenever a weight matrix must multiply an activation vector *and* it resides as integers on disk, first dequantize it into floating point format (commonly BF16 or FP16), then perform matmul.

Let \(W^{(k)}=[Q_{(1)}, Q_{(2)}, … , Q_{(n)}]\) be our full weight matrix stored in blocks. With dequantized weights
\(W_{\text{BF16}}=\bigl[DQ(Q_{(1)};c_1),\,…, DQ(Q_{(n)};c_n)\bigr]\),
we can compute a linear transform
\(y=W_{\text{BF16}}^T x.\)
During backpropagation we again will require gradients only w.r.t deeper layers or adapter parameters; defining explicit backward passes through low‐precision tensors is more elaborate and is detailed in later chapters.

The key insight here is that *every* downstream computation uses only *one* copy of floating point data—the dequantized representation—while all parameters retain their compact low‑bit storage on disk or GPU memory.

## Challenges with Outliers and Benefits of Block-Rescaling

A principal weakness of naive min–max rescaling is its sensitivity to extreme outliers. Suppose some entry in a large batch has a magnitude many orders larger than most others; its presence forces every element in that block to be rescaled so as to avoid exceeding the 8-bit interval. Concretely:

- If one element equals 10 000 while all others are ≤ 10, say 9 entries vary between –5 and +5; then 
\(s=10\,000,\quad c=127/10\,000 = 0.0127.\)
  
- All 9 “normal” entries are scaled down by factor 0.0127 down to values near ±0.0635 before rounding; after dequant they converge back towards zero with negligible precision because many integer bins are effectively unused.

Thus most entries occupy only a handful of bins near zero while extreme values still occupy one bin near ±127; this wasteful allocation leaves many bins unused and forces loss for normal values. 

The widely adopted solution is **block-wise rescaling** described above: by giving each small slice its own scaling constant we isolate outliers within their own blocks so they do not affect neighboring normal values’ precision. In practice:

- Sliding windows with size often between 32–1024 rows are used.
- For larger matrices one may employ *hierarchical* scaling schemes where a coarse scale covers several fine blocks.

Block-wise approaches also correlate well with typical memory organization: tensors are often stored in contiguous chunks aligning with GPU warp or cache lines, so splitting them correspondingly incurs minimal additional data movement costs.

Another advantage: when training under low precision we typically want gradients backpropagated through these scalars as well but their bit-width‐shifts are usually less frequent because gradients involve fewer operations that require precise knowledge about local magnitude ranges.

## Practical Example: FP32 → Int8 Quantization

We now walk through an end-to-end example using realistic numbers:

1. **Start with FP32 matrix**

   Suppose we have an LLM projection matrix
   {\small
   \[
   W=\begin{bmatrix}
   -0.21 & 0\\
    0 & -0.75\\
   -1 & 2\\
   16 & -12
   \end{bmatrix} ,
   }
   where all four rows will be grouped into one block (\(B=4,\ n=1).\)

2. **Compute scaling constant**

   The maximum absolute entry in this block:
   \(s=\max(|-0.21|,|0|,|{-0.75}|,|-1|,\dots |16|)=16.\)
   
   Using signed Int8 range [-127,…,+127], we choose numerator α=127 so:
   
   \(c=\dfrac {127}{16}=7.9375.\)

3. **Quantize**

   Multiply each entry by c and round:

   - Entry (-0.21): −1.\!66875 → −2  
   - Entry (-0.): 0 → 0  
   - Entry (-0.+): etc.
   
    Resulting integer matrix:
    {\small
    \[
    Q=\begin{bmatrix}
    -2 & 0\\
     0 & -6\\
    -8 & +15\\
      +126 & -95\\
    \end{bmatrix}.
    }
    }

4. **Store**  

    Table Q occupies only 4×2 bytes = 8 bytes at full resolution vs ~64 bytes for full FP32 storage—a reduction by factor 8 as expected for an ideal 8-bit scheme (actual less due to padding).

5. **Dequantize**

   Divide each integer back by c:
   
     e.g., entry –126 /7.\!9375 ≈ –15\.9 ≈ actual value ≈ –16.

6.Note: Block-wise rescaling would have treated any extreme value [say −160] separately: if ±160 appeared it would get its own block where s=160 ⇒ new scale c'=7\.9<… etc., thus normal nearby entries wouldn't be squashed down.

In practice modern LLMs use even smaller `k`, e.g., `4` bits resulting in eight discrete levels per sign side plus zero (symmetrically packed). Though mathematically similar details apply—scale computed as α/s where α equals half‐max representable integer—losses inflate because fewer bins exist overall.

---

Understanding these quantitative mechanics allows practitioners to design judicious blocking strategies window sizes `B`, choose faithful scale estimators (`absmax`, `percentile`, etc.), decide whether fine-grained load balancing or per-tensor calibrations merit extra cost—all key decisions summarized across papers leveraging low-bit inference or training such as QLoRA.xxxx

# 3. Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) Finetuning Theory

Large language models (LLMs) routinely contain billions of parameters, yet only a tiny fraction of them need to be altered in order to adapt a model to a new task or domain.  Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning (PEFT) methods such as Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA) exploit this observation by leaving the pre‑trained weights untouched while inserting a small set of trainable parameters into the network.  This chapter dissects LoRA from first principles, explains its mathematical formulation, contexts in which it is applied, and its memory footprint relative to conventional finetuning.  The discussion is built entirely on the concepts presented in the *QLoRA* paper, without recourse to external sources.

---

## 3.1 LoRA Linear Projection Augmentation

In a transformer layer several linear transformations are performed: attention‐query/key/value projections, and the two matrices in the feed‑forward block.  Each of these operations has the form

\[
\mathbf{Y} \;=\;\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W},
\]

where \(\mathbf{X}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) is the input tensor (batch size \(b\), hidden dimension \(h\)) and \(\mathbf{W}\in \mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\) is a weight matrix whose columns encode output features of dimension \(o\).

LoRA augments this projection by inserting an additional *low‑rank* term inside the forward pass:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{Y}\;=\;\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}
      +s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2\;}
      \qquad(\text{Equation }1)
\]

- \(\mathbf{L}_1\in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) is the first factor matrix.
- \(\mathbf{L}_2\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\) is the second factor matrix.
- \(r\ll h,o\) is called the *rank*.
- \(s>0\) is a scalar that rescales the added term so that its magnitude is commensurate with that of the original projection.

The idea is straightforward: we keep all pre‑trained weights frozen (\(\mathbf{W}\)) and learn only two small matrices \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\).  Because we odd‐out all weight “updates” into a low‑rank contribution, we drastically reduce the number of trainable parameters while preserving most of the expressive power necessary for downstream tasks.

---

## 3.2 Rank‑r Matrix Factorization and Scaling Factor \(s\)

The second term in Equation 1 can be viewed as a rank‑\(r\) approximation of an arbitrary correction matrix \(\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\):

\[
s\,\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2 \;\approx\; \Delta.
\]

By constructing \(\Delta\) as a product of two skinny matrices, we enforce that any adjustment carried out by LoRA lies within an \(r\)-dimensional subspace.  The parameter count for this subspace equals

\[
n_{\text{LoRA}} = r(h+o),
\]

which can be orders of magnitude smaller than \(ho = n_{\text{full}}\), the number of entries in a conventional weight matrix.

The scalar \(s\) plays an essential calibrating role.  In practice, randomly initialised LoRA factors might have magnitudes significantly larger or smaller than those of \(\mathbf{W}\).  An empirically chosen \(s=0.01$–$0.05$ brings the LoRA term to a comparable scale, ensuring that it starts as a subtle perturbation during training and grows only when beneficial.

---

## 3.3 Backpropagation Through Frozen Weights to Adapters

Because all original weights are frozen, gradients never flow into them during training.  Instead, gradients with respect to each parameter are accumulated only for the LoRA factors:

1. **Forward pass**  
   Compute
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{Y}}=\underbrace{\mathbf{X}\,\hat{\mathbf{W}}}_{=\hat{\!D}}\;+\;
   s\,(\underbrace{\mathbf X\,{\bf L}_1}_{A})\,{\bf L}_2,
   \]
   where we denote by \(\hat{\!D}=\mathbf X\,{\bf W}\) the contribution from frozen weights.

2. **Loss computation**  
   Evaluate a loss function \(E(\tilde{\bf Y})\), e.g., cross-entropy on language modeling or instruction following.

3. **Backward pass**  
   Compute gradients w.r.t.\ LoRA factors using standard chain rule:
   - For \({{\bf L}_2}\):
     \[
     \frac{\partial E}{\partial {\bf L}_{2}}
       = s\,{\bf L}_{1}^\top\,({A}^\top\,g),
     \]
     where \(g=\partial E/\partial {\tilde{\bf Y}}\).
   - For `{{bf L}_1}`:
     \[
     \frac{\partial E}{\partial {\bf L}_{1}}
       = s\,({A}^\top g)\, {\bf L}_{2}^\top .
     \]
   The gradients for $\hat W$ do **not** exist because $\hat W$ is frozen; any backpropagation through $\hat D$ stops at $\hat W$.

4. **Optimization update**  
   Apply an optimizer (Adam, SGD, …) only to $\{{\bf L}_1,{ {\bf L }_2},{s}\}$.  Since these tensors are very small compared with $W$, back‑propagating through them incurs far less memory consumption than updating every entry of $W$.

### Splitting Gradients Across Layers

In practice LoRA adapters are inserted in every linear block of each transformer layer:
- Query projection
- Key projection
- Value projection
- Value-to-next-layer feed-forward head
- Feed-forward intermediate layer

For each block we instantiate its own $(r,h,o)$ pair and possibly its own scalar $s$.  The total number of adapters roughly equals twice the number of layers (two per attention head block).  Because each adapter still contains only $O(r(h+o))$ parameters, even on a gigantic model such as LLaMA 65B this overhead remains moderate (typically ~0.1 %–0.5 % of all model parameters).

---

## 3.4 Memory Footprint Analysis: LoRA vs Full Finetuning

Memory consumption during training stems from three sources:

| Source | Full finetuning | LoRA finetuning |
|--------|-----------------|------------------|
| Model weights | Stored once + gradient copies (fp16/float32) | Frozen weights stored once (fp16/float32); adapters stored fp16 |
| Activations | Required for all forward paths | Same amount as full training (save checkpointing effects) |
| Optimizer states | Need per-weight state (moments, etc.) | Need per-adapter state |

Because adaptor tensors are tiny compared with full-weight matrices, their optimizer states occupy negligible space (< 30 MB for a typical 7B model).  

To quantify this effect we can use typical numbers reported in *QLoRA*’s memory analysis:

| Model Size | Full FP16 fine‐tune requirement | LoRA FP16 fine‐tune requirement |
|------------|---------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| 7 B       | ~32 GB                          | ~12 GB                            |
| 33 B      | ~760 GB                         | ~48 GB                            |
| 65 B      | >780 GB                         | <48 GB                            |

Notice how **LoRA reduces estimated GPU memory by an order of magnitude** even when all weights are kept unfrozen but serviced at lower precision inside QLoRA’s quantized base model.

The critical point is that **the majority of the memory consuming component remains activations**—training from scratch or finetuning does not reduce their footprint dramatically unless one uses techniques such as activation checkpointing or reduced precision activations.  However, enabling backpropagation through quantized base weights eliminates large gradient footprints associated with full‐size weight tensors and thus allows training on single GPUs even for huge models.

---

## 3.5 Hyperparameter Choices

### Rank \(r\)

- A too small rank may underfit: the low-rank subspace cannot capture complex task-specific corrections.
- A too large rank increases memory consumption before it becomes wasteful.
- Empirical guidance in *QLoRA* suggests setting \(r=8~10~16~32~64~128~256~,~512,\dotsc.\), with diminishing returns beyond \(r=64.\)

### Adapter Placement

- **All Transformer Blocks:** Empirical studies show that inserting adapters *everywhere* maximises performance equivalence with full fine-tuning; limited placement (e.g., only query/key/value projections or only feed-forward layers) yields noticeable drops at larger scales.
- **Prompt Tuning vs LoRA:** Prompt tuning adds trainable embeddings at input—effective at small scale but often inferior for large models where richer parameterization via adapters becomes necessary.

### Scalar Scaling \(s$

- Typical values: \(s=0.01,\;0.05,\;0.\)
- If too large → unstable training; if too small → slow convergence.
- Some implementations let models learn an adaptive $s$ or set it per-layer based on maximum norm estimation.

### Batch Size & Sequence Length

The tradeoff between batch size and sequence length governs both GPU occupancy and PEFT benefits:
- Larger batch → more parallelism but higher activation memory.
- Longer sequences increase gradient checkpointing spikes – mitigated by *paged optimizers* introduced in *QLoRA*, which spill optimizer states to CPU when GPU runs low on RAM.

By carefully coordinating these hyperparameters one can achieve **full fidelity finetuning performance**—matching standard 16-bit full finetuning—while staying within limited GPU budgets (8–48 GB).

---

## Section Summary

Low-Rank Adapter finetuning reconstitutes what would otherwise be costly full-scale weight updates into inexpensive updates over tiny low-rank matrices and scalars. Its key technical ingredients—the linear augmentation scheme, rank-based factorization, careful scaling, selective backpropagation through frozen weights, and judicious hyperparameter selection—allow researchers to adapt colossal transformer models without overwhelming computational resources. In subsequent sections we explore how these ideas operate when paired with aggressive quantization in QLoRA: revealing that quantized bases plus LoRA adapters can jointly recover full-fidelity performance while preserving extreme memory efficiency across megabyte-to-terabyte model sizes.

# 4. QLoRA Overview and Design Goals

In this chapter we lay out the foundation of **QLoRA** (Quantized Low‑Rank Adapters).  QLoRA is a training recipe that marries two ideas that have independently proved effective in the literature: *block‑wise low‑bit quantization* of a pretrained language model, and *parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning* with Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA).  The combination delivers **dramatic memory savings while preserving full 16‑bit training performance**.  We proceed by dissecting the logical flow of a QLoRA training step, clarifying the data types used for storage versus computation, and showing how gradients are routed only to the adapter parameters.

> **Why QLoRA matters**  
> Regular 16‑bit fine‑tuning of a 65B parameter model needs well over 700 GB of GPU memory—far beyond what is available on commodity hardware.  By freezing the base model’s weights, quantizing them to a highly efficient 4‑bit representation, and iteratively training only a tiny set of adapter weights, QLoRA reduces this requirement to under 48 GB on a single large GPU, while still matching or exceeding the performance obtained by full fine‑tuning.

---

## QLoRA Pipeline Architecture

At its core, QLoRA follows **the same high‑level forward/backward loop** as conventional neural network training, except that two distinct transformations act on the base weights:

1. **Storage‑to‑Compute conversion** – we store each weight matrix in a compact 4‑bit format (the “NormalFloat” data type), but every forward or backward operation requires first converting that compact representation back into a higher‐precision form suitable for linear algebra.

2. **Adapter insertion** – we augment every linear projection in the transformer block with an additional low‑rank term.  This term is trainable; all other parameters remain frozen.

The architectural diagram would look like:

```
Input Token Embeddings          ─────►
                                      │
                                   LayerNorm
                                      │
                                 ┌────▼─────────┐
                                 │ Pretrained   │
                                 │ (quantized)  │
                                 │  Matrix W_Q4 │
                                 └────▲───────▲─┘
                                      │       │
                +----------------——–+dx−−+   │ dequantization
                |                                ▼ 
              LoRA                           Linear Projection
              (trainable)                    (h × h) weight matrix
                                 ┌────►   ▲    ▲
                                 |       |    |
                                 ▼       |    |
                               Residual                  ───►
```

In words: the original transformer projection \( \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} \) is still applied at every layer, but the effective weight matrix is now 

\[
\mathbf{W}_{\text{eff}}=\underbrace{\text{dequant}\bigl(C_1,C_2,\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\bigr)}_{\text{frozen base}}
     \;+\;
s\,\underbrace{\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2}_{\text{adapter}},
\]

where \(C_1,\ C_2\) are the two levels of quantization constants introduced later.

---

## Storage Data Type versus Computation Data Type

*Storage data type* refers to how an individual tensor is serialized into GPU memory; *computation data type* denotes what precision is used during matrix multiplications.

- **Storage:**  
  The base model’s weight tensors are stored in **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** format.  
  NF4 packs each component into a nibble; the bits encode an equal‑frequency mapping onto values derived from the standard normal distribution.  
  This choice gives optimal “information theoretic precision” for typical pretrained weight statistics (zero‑mean Gaussian with moderate variance), yielding better empirical behaviour than plain int4 or float4.

- **Computation:**  
  All arithmetic involving these tensors uses **BFloat16 (BF16)** precision.  
  BF16 offers lower memory usage than FP32 while providing almost identical dynamic range and enough headroom for gradients and activations that can become large during backpropagation.

Because BF16 only fits twice as many bits per element as NF4, swapping between storage and compute incurs minimal overhead: each forward call invokes a *double dequantization* routine that reconstructs \(\mathbf{W}\) from its compressed representation into BF16 buffer on‑the‑fly.

---

## Forward Pass with Double Dequantization

The double dequantization routine performs two consecutive “dequant” operations:

1. **Dequantize first‐level constants:** The first quantization step scaled each block of \(\mathbf{W}\) by a constant \(c_1^{\text{FP32}}\).  
   Since these constants themselves occupy space, they are later re‐quantized itself into an **FP8** format (`c2`), allowing reuse of their own scaling.

2. **Dequantize NF4 weights:** Each block of NF4 weights \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\) was stored with respect to its second constant \(c_2^{\text{k-bit}}\).  

Mathematically:

\[
\begin{aligned}
Z &= \text{dequant}(c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}),\\
\mathbf{\hat W} &= \text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}},\, Z).
\end{aligned}
\]

The routine `doubleDequant(c1,c2,W)` exactly implements this sequence:

```python
def doubleDequant(c1_fp32, c2_kbit, W_nf4):
    # First level: dequant from FP8 constants to real numbers
    Z = dequant(c2_kbit_scaled, W_nf4)          # -> BF16
    # Second level: dequant from FP32 constant to final BF16 weight tensor
    return dequant(c1_fp32, Z)                 # -> BF16
```

With `c2_kbit_scaled` already centered around zero via mean subtraction (since all \(c_2>0\)), `dequant` applies an affine transformation that maps integer codes to floating values belonging to `[-1,+1]`, then rescales them by the remaining constant `c1`.

During evaluation or inference only one dequantization step is needed because gradients are absent; however during training both steps must be executed for every mini‑batch.

---

## Adapter Forward in BF16 Precision

Once the base projection has been computed from its compressed representation:

\[
\mathbf{y}_{Q} = \mathbf{x}\,\rm K\,,
\quad K=\texttt{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\mathbf{W}^{NF4}), 
\]

we add the LoRA term:

\[
\mathbf{y}_{LF} = s\, \bigl( \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf L_1\bigr)\,\mathbf L_2,
\quad s>0.
\]

All three tensors involved—input \(\mathbf{x}\), low-rank factors \(\mathbf L_1,\mathbf L_2\)—are stored and processed in BF16 because their sizes are modest (~\(r\times h + r\times o)\); storing them in lower precision would degrade adapter expressiveness more noticeably than storing them in higher precision.

The *full* effective output is then

\[
Y = \underbrace{\mathbf{x}K}_{Q} + 
    \underbrace{s\,(\mathbf xL_1)L_2}_{LF},
    \quad Y\,:\,\mathbb R^{b\times o}.
\]

No higher precision than BF16 is required for any part of this computation because even the final output will subsequently be used only by other modules which themselves work in BF16/FP32 internally.

---

## Backward Pass Details (Gradients Only for Adapters)

The key memory saving mechanism lies in whom we update during backpropagation:

- The frozen base weights \(K = C^{-1}_1C^{-1}_2\,W^{NF4}\) are never modified; therefore their gradients are always zero.
- The only learnable parameters are the LoRA factors \(L_1,L_{addr}>0}\).

During backpropagation we still need derivatives with respect to *all given inputs*, including \(K^T*\epsilon_x$, where \(\epsilon_x=\partial E/\partial Y$ is the gradient from downstream layers*. However we do **not** store or propagate gradients for \(K$ itself; these derivatives simply pass through when computing gradients w.r.t. input $\mathbf x$ but they never touch $K$’s parameters. 

Formally one can write for any batch:

```python
# Forward:
y_q   = x @ K            # computed using doubleDequant(K)
y_lag = s * ((x @ L1) @ L2)
y     = y_q + y_lag

# Backward:
eps_y     = grad(loss_out)

eps_x_q   = eps_y @ K.T                # derivative wrt input through frozen path
eps_La = ... compute via chain rule using eps_y and current states

update(L1,L2)          # apply optimiser based on eps_La only
```

Because the `eps_La` term does not involve computing gradients through `K`, we avoid both storage of intermediate gradient tensors for `K` and any extra computation outside adapter matrices. Moreover, due to *gradient checkpointing* employed on activation memories within transformer layers, only backpropagated activations through these short paths need to be kept alive until the adapter update step finishes; thus overall memory cost stays bounded at roughly that of executing a regular BF16 forward pass plus a handful of additional parameter updates.

In practice this results in:

- Adapter input gradients averaging ~18 MB per sequence after checkpointing (when mixed with batch size).
- Total LoRA parameters ≪ 100 MB even at 65B scale.
- Base model running as normal but in compressed memory (~5 GB for an uncompressed 65B model vs < 48 GB net).

These figures match those reported in Section 3 of the paper: changing encoder/decoder choice or defaults may slightly alter exact numbers but never upends fundamental principles outlined here.

---

## Recap of Key Design Choices

| Component | Rationale | Practical Impact |
|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| 4-bit NF4 | Optimised for Gaussian distribution; equal–frequency bins | Higher accuracy than plain int/float quants |
| Two–level constants | Reduce memory spent on per–block scaling | ≈ 0.37 bits/param saving |
| Double dequantisation | Guarantees correct mapping from storage → compute | No performance loss despite compression |
| LoRA adapters everywhere | Keeps encoder/decoder geometry unchanged | Enables full accuracy recovery |
| BFloat16 computation | Sufficient dynamic range + less float-overhead vs FP32 | Minor runtime penalty |
| Optimiser Paged Memory | Evicts spike buffers automatically | Avoids OOM without manual tuning |

Together they yield a tight trade: raw parameters live compressed yet remain fully usable during gradient flow thanks to carefully designed dequant layers; local adapters capture fine–grained learning capacity efficiently.

In summary, QLoRA’s core innovation lies not just in compressing weights but coupling that compression with an adaptable low–rank bridge that restores expressive power during training while keeping GPU footprint minimal—exactly what read it needs over next chapters: how we see these design choices playing out across real models and tasks before handing them down to large language model practitioners who hope to run sizable systems on modest hardware.

# 5 – 4‑Bit NormalFloat (NF4) Quantization  

Large‑scale language models contain billions of floating‑point weight values that are, to a very good approximation, drawn from a zero–mean Gaussian distribution with different standard deviations for different layers.  The QLoRA approach exploits this statistical regularity to design a quantisation scheme that is *information‑theoretically optimal* for such data.  The resulting 4‑bit format, termed **NormalFloat 4 (NF4)**, replaces traditional 4‑bit integer or float representations and achieves a sustained performance advantage in practice.  
In what follows we develop the NF4 data type formally, show how it can be derived from first principles, and describe the practical steps required to deploy it in a real large‑scale training pipeline.

---

## Normal distribution assumption for pretrained weights  

Before any quantisation technique can be optimised, we must understand the underlying distribution of the model parameters.  Empirical studies across many transformer architectures—from LLaMA, GPT‑NeoX, OPT, BLOOM to Pythia—reveal that after standard pretraining the weight tensors of most feed‑forward and attention layers resemble samples from a zero‑centred Gaussian distribution \( \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) \), where the standard deviation \(\sigma\) varies by layer but the shape is identical across layers.  This observation follows from multiple sources:

1. **Random weight initialisation**: The prevalent practice of Glorot / He initialisation sets weights as Gaussian random variables centred at zero; during training these distributions preserve their centring even as mean values drift.
2. **Weight decay and regularisation**: These techniques encourage weights to stay close to zero.
3. **Layer normalisation and residual connections**: The internal inductive biases of transformer blocks favour zero‑mean weight distributions.

Because a Gaussian density is fully characterised by its mean (zero) and variance (\(\sigma^2\)), we can *scale* any weight tensor such that its empirical standard deviation equals that of a *canonical* normal distribution \( \mathcal{N}(0,1) \). Once this scaling is performed, all weight tensors share an identical statistical profile; consequently the *entire distribution* over which we need to quantise becomes known.

This key simplification allows us to compute an *information‑theoretically optimal* set of quantisation bins once and reuse them for any tensor—there is no need for per‑tensor calibration or costly look‑ups of empirical cumulative distributions.

---

## Quantile quantisation fundamentals  

Suppose we wish to represent every element \(x\) of a real‐valued tensor using only \(k\) bits.  With \(k\) bits we have \(2^{k}\) distinct codewords.  Let us denote by \(q_i \in \mathbb{R}\) the *quantiser levels* or *codeword values*, where \(i\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}\).  In traditional scalar quantisation the levels are usually chosen as uniformly spaced integers scaled by a fixed range; however this induces heavy errors whenever the true distribution has long tails or concentrated mass at particular regions.

**Quantile quantisation** seeks instead to place each codeword so that an equal fraction of input values fall into each bin:

\[
P(q_{i-1} \le X < q_i) = \frac{1}{2^{k}} .
\]

For a *known* distribution \(p_X(x)\), these thresholds are simply inverses of the cumulative density function (CDF):

\[
q_i = F_X^{-1}\!\Bigl(\frac{i}{2^{k}}\Bigr),
\qquad i=0,\dots,2^{k}-1 ,
\tag{5.1}
\]

where \(F_X^{-1}\) is the quantile function (inverse CDF).  
If every element lands in its own bin with probability around one half‑percent (for k=8), the mean squared error (MSE) induced by rounding becomes theoreticaly minimised under a fixed bit budget: each level carries as much information as possible about its bin’s members.

Quantile quantisation is especially well suited when the source distribution is known analytically—as with Gaussian weights—because we can compute \(F_X^{-1}\) in closed form using error functions or high‑accuracy approximations.

---

## Deriving optimal k‑bit NF data points via standard normal quantiles  

Let us specialise Eq.(5.1) to our canonical normal source \(X\sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).  
The CDF of this distribution is the standard normal cumulative function \(\Phi(z)\), whose inverse is denoted \(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)\). Thus:

\[
q_i
=
\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{i}{2^{k}}\right),
    \quad i=0,\dots ,2^{k}-1 .
\tag{5.2}
\]

Example: for k = 8 bits (\(256\) levels), the first few levels would be approximately:

| i | qᵢ |
|---|------------------------|
| 0 | -3.772|
| 13 | -3.00|
| 64 | -0.40|
| 128| 0 |
| 192| +0.40|
| 255| +3.772|

The mathematical convenience lies in being able to evaluate these numbers with high precision offline and store them in a small lookup table—a cheap operation during runtime.

However, there are two practical complications:

1. **Symmetry & Zero-centering** – We typically desire an exact representation for zero to avoid systematic bias when handling padding tokens or other special symbols.
2. **Bounded Range** – We want all levels mapped into a compact range such that they fit into four bits whilst preserving magnitude resolution.

The first issue leads naturally to an *asymmetric* scheme described next; the second issue dictates mapping our infinite support Gaussian onto a finite interval \([-\,c_{\max}, c_{\max}]\).

---

### Mapping NF quantiles to [- 1, + 1] range

We implicitly define an *absolute maximum value* \(c_{\max}\) for each weight tensor—the largest absolute entry after rescaling by block‐wise standard deviation (Eq.(3) in the background section). In practice we set

\[
c_{\max}
= \max_{\text{block}}
   |x|\quad
   /\,\,\, 
   (\text{block standard deviation})\, .
\]

Because all blocks share the same mean-zero unit-variance profile after rescaling, no block will contain numbers significantly outside \([-\,c_{\max}, c_{\max}]\); outliers are absorbed into the larger block’s scaling factor.

To ensure every level lies within [- 1,+ 1], we *normalize* all codeworts defined in Eq.(5.2):

\[
q_i^{[\!-\,1,+\,1]} 
   = 
   q_i / c_{\max} .
\]

Hence each level now occupies part of the unit interval even as its absolute magnitude may vary between tensors: more spreadout layers receive larger \(c_{\max}\), but because all codewords are proportionally scaled their relative spacing remains preserved.

With this scaling applied uniformly across all tensors, subsequent de‑quantisation simply reappplies the block scale:
\(x = c_{\max} \times q_j^{[\!-\,1,+\,1]}\).

---

## Zero‑point handling and asymmetric construction (k-bit NFₖ)

A naive symmetric mapping gives two representations for zero—one at index \(2^{k-1}-1/2\), one at \(2^{k-1}+0/5/2\)—which violates our desire for an exact zeropoint: many inference engines assume they can look up zero without error (e.g., padding). To enforce discrete zero while keeping all buckets approximately equal in mass, we use an **asymmetric** design:

1. Partition levels into two halves: `negative` and `positive`.
2. For each side compute half as many points as there are total positive indices:
   
   \[
   N^- = N^+ = 2^{k-1},
   \quad
   n^\pm_i = F_X^{-1}\!\bigl(\frac{i+0}{N^\pm}\bigr)-F_X^{-1}\!\bigl(\frac{i+0}{N^\pm}+{\small\tfrac{0}{N^\pm}}\bigr)
   .
   \]

   In practice this means that each side uses exactly half the range but still keeps equal bin probabilities.
3. Concatenate negative and positive lists while removing one duplicate occurrence of zero when both sides produce it.

The final data type now has `NF_k` symbols where `NF_k[0] < … < NF_k[−] < 0 < NF_k[+] …`, all inside `[- c_max,c_max]`.  
With k = 4 bits we obtain eight distinct coding positions exactly matching Neelms’ **NF4** points listed in Appendix A.

This asymmetry guarantees:

- A perfectly reproducible analogue of null tokens with no quantization error.
- Minimal loss-of-information for symmetric places on either side owing to equal bin width in terms of probability mass rather than absolute value.

---

## Comparison to standard FP4/Int4 representations  

### Precision profile  
Standard FP4 packs exponent + mantissa into four bits with typically only one integer bit; probability mass concentrates heavily near zero resulting in large relative errors on extreme values (outliers).  
Int4 uses uniform spacing over some dynamic range; again bins either overshoot tiny valued weights or undershoot bulging tails.

Both schemes ignore knowledge about how many weights actually occupy each interval; they simply spread bins evenly over numeric value space—an intuition consistent with Uniform Quantisers rather than Entropy Optimised ones.

Normalized quantile-based NF4 places more bins where weights genuinely occur—roughly five out entries per bin near tails vs one outperforms uniform spline placement—and uses variable step sizes derived holistically from Gaussian theory rather than arbitrary heuristics like power‐of‐two scales used by float formats.

### Empirical impact  
In experiments reported across several backbone models (OPT, BLOOM, Pythia & LLaMA), NF4 achieves roughly 10–15 % higher perplexity figures on held-out language modelling datasets compared to FP4 or Int4 at identical bit budgets on average per parameter compression factor ~57× relative to FP32 accuracy degradation ~10–12%. Moreover:

- When combined with LoRA adapters (`QLoRA` framework), NF4 fully recovers full-finetuning performance even at low precision—a feat unattainable by FP4 or Int4.
- Double Quantization further compresses per-tensor constants down from 32-bit scalars (~200MB per model block) to less than ~37MB without affecting MSE performance.
- Inference latency improves modestly due to tighter packing (~10–20%) while maintaining minimal memory overflow risk on consumer GPUs today.

These gains derive intrinsically from respecting two statistical facts: (a) the covariance structure stabilises due largely five-bounded variance per token after nuclear cancellation during training; (b) each weight tensor is roughly posteriorly centered around zero.

---

## Implementing NF4 in practice  

Implementing NF4 requires changes only down at two building blocks:

| Step | Detail | Implementation |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **Define level table** | Compute four-bit grid using (*Equation 5.2*) then shift to [- c_max,+ c_max]. | Precompute float array once per bit-size then store as constant memory on GPU; use BF16 conversion on load via custom CUDA kernel (`nf_decode`). |
| **Block-wise scaling `c_max`** | For every contiguous block B (typical B≈64 ops for QC), store maximum absolute value among tensors inside it (`abs(max)`); then keep `scale = max / c_max` stored as FP32 per block.| Plain GPU CPU mem copy + atomic reduction; use TVM fused kernel always on GPUs supporting BF16 arithmetic (`tensorlib`). |
| **Double Quantization** | Compress `scale` itself using an additional eight-bit float injection before applying LoRA update.| Nested kernel: first dequantise scale from FP32→FP8→BF16 during forward pass; revert only when computing gradient w.r.t LoRA params.|

All maths operations respect .bf16 precision only where necessary—so typical forward/backward multiplications run entirely in BF16—while storing large arrays only at low precision – saving forward memory overhead without hurting speed due intense GPU throughput advantage for BF16 multiply-adds.

---

### Practical guidelines

- **Block size trade‐off**: Larger block sizes reduce extra metadata but incur higher MSE because each multiplier covers wider weight variance ranges; B ≈ 64 balances speed & fidelity.
- **Overflow handling**: Add small guard band (\(10^{-3}\)) when computing ordinary maximums so that NaNs do not bleed through blocks.
- **Model loading**: When reading pre-trained checkpoints saved in FP32/FP16 convert them online using built-in decompression functions (`nf_quantize_pretrain`). The runtime cost (~30% extra cpu time before loading session startup) permits one-time conversion offline for persistent deployments.
- **LoRA Adapter updates**: Keep LoRA weights entirely full precision BF16; gradient accumulation done over small batches prevents element-wise accumulation errors common in integer math.

### Putting it together

In QLoRA's flow
the pre-trained base model produces tensors stored on GPU host memory (NVMe+Pageable + Unified Memory).  During forward propagation

```python
W_qf  = nf_quantize(W_fp32)
scale_fb16 = dequant_scale_block(W_qf.blocks)
W_bf16 = dequant(W_qf.scales)
output = matmul(X_bf16 , W_bf16)+ Σ LoRA_terms
```

Only `W_qf` stays low precision; intermediate matrices remain BF16 thanks to fast CUDA kernels that expect BF16 inputs/outputs (`torch.bfloat16`).  
Backpropagation through `W` never updates these tensors—instead gradients flow through LoRA adapters gradually nudging hidden representations until loss decreases while retaining original base cognition intact.

Thus NF4’s design aligns mathematically with information theory and practically with modern deep learning JIT compilers and hardware fp8/bfloat currently deployed across datacentres—allowing researchers who normally require coupled multi–GPU clusters to finetune gigantic LLMs with just one modern consumer card.

# 6. Double Quantization Technique

Finetuning a 4‑bit language model on a single GPU is made possible by a set of memory‑saving tricks that are carefully engineered so that no precision is lost in the forward and backward passes. One of the most elegant of these tricks – and arguably the most surprising – is **double quantization**: we quantize not only the weights themselves but also the very constants used to convert the stored low‑precision weights back into high‑precision values during inference and training. The result is a net reduction of roughly **0.37 bits per parameter** in storage cost on typical block sizes, without any measurable drop in downstream performance.

Below we unpack the idea step by step: first, we recall how low‑bit weight tensors are normally represented, then we motivate why their quantization constants become a significant memory drain, and finally we present the double‑quantization pipeline that shrinks this overhead while preserving the math of forward propagation.

## 6.1 Recap: Block‑wise 4‑bit Weight Quantization

In QLoRA, each tensor \(\mathbf{W}\) (typically a projection matrix in a transformer layer) is sliced into contiguous blocks of fixed size \(B\). For each block \(\mathbf{W}_b\) one obtains a *quantization constant* (or *scale*) \(c_b\) that maps 4‑bit integer codes to floating‐point values:

\[
c_b = \frac{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{W}_b)}{127},
\qquad
\mathbf{W}^{\text{Int4}}_b = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{W}_b}{c_b}\right).
\]

During the forward pass we dequantize as

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}^{\text{BF16}}_b
= c_b \;\odot\; \mathbf{W}_b^{\text{Int4}},
\]

where \(\odot\) denotes element‑wise multiplication and the result is cast to BF16 for use in matrix multiplications.

Because each block requires its own fixed‑point scale, we have to store all these constants. For a commonly used block size of \(B=64\), each constant takes **32 bits** (FP32), meaning that every 64 weights incur an additional 32 bits of storage – i.e., an extra **0.5 bits per weight** on average. On a 65B‑parameter LLaMA model this amounts to more than **3 GB** of extra memory just for scales.

In principle one could avoid this overhead by selecting an extremely small block size so that the constant becomes less significant, but theory says smaller blocks hurt quantization precision because fewer samples are available to estimate a representative scale. In practice we need blocks on the order of 64–128 elements to keep error low while still packing many entries into one 4‑bit type.

Thus we face an unavoidable conflict: *good* quantization requires *many* per‑block constants, which inflates memory footprints; *cheap* quantization requires *fewer* constants but at a cost to accuracy. Double quantization resolves this tug‑of‑war.

## 6.2 The Insight Behind Double Quantization

If each block scale \(c_b\) already contains roughly an order of magnitude more information than the associated block of weights (a full FP32 number vs four bits per weight), it is wasteful to keep it raw in memory. Observe two facts:

1. **All scales are positive and relatively smooth** – because they are ratios of absolute maxima over blocks that come from a well‑behaved distribution (normalized LLaMA weights). Hence they tend to be clustered around a few orders of magnitude.
2. **The growth between consecutive scales has low variance** – neighboring blocks often have similar maximums.

These observations suggest that we can compress the scales themselves *without losing much fidelity*. We do so by treating them as another tensor amenable to block-wise low‑bit quantization: each \(c_b\) will become an entry in another low‑bit array \(\mathbf{s}\), and will be restored at runtime using exactly the same dequantisation routine employed for ordinary weights.

In other words, we **recurse** on the quantisation constant: instead of having raw FP32 scales, we store them as a second set of low‑bit numbers whose values implicitly encode two levels of scaling:

```
   W (originally FP32)
        │
   NF4 + scale1     ← first level (blocks)
        │              (scale1 stored naïvely in FP32)
   scale2             ← second level (blocks)
```

The relationship between these two levels can be written as:

\[
c_b = c^{(1)}_b = c^{(2)}_{\pi(b)} \;\odot \; s^{(2)}_{\pi(b)},
\]

where \(c^{(2)}_{\pi(b)}\) is an FP32 “meta–scale” shared across many blocks (identified by index \(\pi(b)\)) and \(s^{(2)}_{\pi(b)}\) is an 8‑bit floating point encoding that captures fine variations around that meta–scale. By training out or precomputing these two rows, we can reconstruct any individual \(c_b\) with essentially no loss.

Why does this work numerically?  
- Because **8-bit floats** (rather than plain integers) already give us multi–precision range and significant headsway over raw integers: they can represent subnormal numbers and do not suffer from huge shoulder errors.
- Because when we first apply blockwise NF4 on the original weights, our arithmetic remains centered around “1” – thus when we later scan over all resulting scales it squeezes nicely into roughly [0.5 … 2] range, which fits comfortably in an eight‐bit float without extreme tailing.

Using these two levels considerably reduces average bits per weight:
- The first level stores four bits per weight as before.
- Instead of storing one per–block FP32 constant (~0.5 bits/weight for \(B=64\)), we now store
    - one meta–scale per big block (e.g., every 256 entries; negligible overhead),
    - one eight‐bit float per standard block (~\(8/64 = 0.125\,\text{bits}/w.\))

Thus, for typical settings, double quantization reduces former overhead from **0.5 bits/weight** to merely **0.127 bits/weight**, achieving savings close to **0.37 bits/weight**, or about **3 GB** for a 65B model.

### Lemma: Double Quantization Does Not Affect Forward Math  
The forward operation for a single block becomes

1. Reconstruct first-level scale:
   \[
   \hat c_b = c^{(1)}_b
            = c^{(2)}_{\pi(b)} \;\odot\, s^{(2)}_{\pi(b)},
   \]
2. Dequantize weight block:
   \[
   \widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_b = \hat c_b \;\odot\, 
        \bigl(\text{NF4}\text{-dequant}(
                s^{(1)}_b)\bigr).
   \]

Because equality holds exactly up to floating point rounding errors far below machine epsilon for our target precision regime, gradient propagation through all steps yields identical taglines/multiplications as if we had used raw FP32 scales directly – provided aliasing effects are negligible.
Thus double quantization does not perturb the math used during training or inference beyond file‐level rounding artifacts.

## 6.3 Practical Recipe for Building Double Quantized Tensors

Below is an illustrated pseudo–pipeline showing how QLoRA engineers implement double quantized weights within their code base.

| Step | Action | Data Type | Notes |
|------|--------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | Partition weight matrix into standard blocks (\(B=64\)). | FP32 | Raw uncompressed weights |
| 2 | Compute per–block max → basic scale \(s^{(1)}_b = |\max(\mathbf{W}_b)|/127.\) | FP32 | Stores approximately one constant per block |
| 3 | Apply NF4 quantization using scale \(s^{(1)}_b.\) | Int4 stored as BitPackedBool | Actual low‐bit tensor |
| 4 | Gather all virtual “meta” constants over super–blocks (\(S=256.\)) | FP32 | One FP32 per super–block |
| 5 | Decompose each super–block const into its own base + deviation:<br> \(c^{(2)}_{\pi(b)},\, s^{(2)}_{\pi(b)}.\) | FP8 & BF16 integration | Using symmetrized 8-bit float table |
| 6 | Store deviations \(s^{(2)}_{\pi(b)}.\) as Int8 or BF16-CALCable types on GPU static memory.| INT8/Floats | Negligible overhead |
| 7 | During inference / training forward:<br> Compute \(c_b := c^{(2)}_{\pi(b)} \odot s^{(2)}_{\pi(b)}.\)<br> Apply NF4 dequant + multiply by \(c_b.\)| BF16 dequant approx.| Equivalent to standard NF4 dequant but with cheaper constants |

The salient thing here is that once steps 1–6 have been performed offline once when creating `nfq_config` for each layer, all subsequent training epochs reuse only step 7 – computing speed remains practically unchanged relative to naive high-bit dequant because both operations require only a handful of multiply/add instructions per element.

## 6.4 Memory Savings Illustration

Let us quantify savings on typical hardware scenarios:

| Block Size \(B\) | Baseline scale word size (FP32) | Bits/param* |
|------------------|---------------------------------|-------------|
| \(B=64\)         | 0.5 bits                              | 3 GB extra on 65B model    |

Applying double quantization with super Blocks \(S=256,\) mapped via fp8:

- Meta-scale loss ≈ \(32 / S ≈ 0.125\,{\rm bits/param}\).
- Deviation Loss ≈​\(8 / B ≈ 0.125\,{\rm bits/param}\).

Total ≈ \(0.25\,{\rm bits/param}\), i.e., savings ≈ \(0.25\,{\rm bits} × 65\times10^9 × M_{byte}=≈ 3 GB.\)

Unlike typical hyperparameter sweeps where you pay fixed costs like optimizer states or activations that dominate memory budgets (>80% for backprop), these reductions significantly lower overall memory footprint while keeping other contributors minimal; thus you may trade previously prohibitive spacetime gaps for feasible runs on consumer GPUs.

#### Footnote
*Bits/param*: The fraction of bytes saved per parameter averaged across complete tensor including sparsity penalties; when expressed in binary bytes ($8$ bits ≈ $1$~byte).

## 6.5 Empirical Verification

The authors carried out controlled experiments to ensure that double quantizing never hurt performance:

- They trained LLaMA models across sizes from $125$M up to $65$B in QLoRA configuration with NF4 + DQ vs NF4 without DQ.
- Zero‐shot perplexity, GLUE scores and MMLU accuracy measured before / after QLoRA remained statistically indistinguishable within confidence intervals.
- Floating point rounding errors introduced by reconstructing budgets were second-order compared with noise already added by NF4 itself.

These results show that double quantized models produce identically dimples in downstream metrics relative to naive NF4 counterparts while shaving significant hardware headroom off training runs.

## Summary

Double Quantization is more than just “two levels deep.” It is:

- A principle surviving *analysis*: identifying and compressing auxiliary arrays inherent in blockwise low-bit representations.
- A practical optimization enabling state‑of‑the‑art finetuning on GPUs with fewer than $48$ GB memory.
- A proof that ancillary structures can be treated just like primary tensors: once you give them structure you can treat them equally under compression schemes without sacrificing downstream fidelity.

With this technique integrated into QLoRA’s tooling stack – along with NormalFloat weight encodings and paged optimizers – researchers can now assign billions of parameters meaningfully trained on consumer hardware while keeping possible quality loss strictly below any statistical noise present from training hyperparameters alone._END OF CHAPTER_
