\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function determine the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the weight quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\). I am confused about why the authors use the average absolute value of the weight matrix as the scaling factor instead of, say, the maximum absolute value or RMS. How does this choice affect the distribution of ternary weights and the stability of training?
Answer:
The “abs‑mean’’ quantizer in BitNet b1.58 is deliberately simple: it rescales the weight matrix by the **average absolute value**  

\[
\gamma \;=\;\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
\]

and then rounds each entry to the nearest integer in $\{-1,0,+1\}$.  
At first sight one could also use the maximum absolute value $\max|W|$ or a root‑mean‑square (RMS) statistic, but the authors chose the mean‑absolute‑value for three intertwined reasons: (1) it produces a well‑balanced ternary distribution, (2) it yields a scale that is robust to outliers, and (3) it leads to stable gradients when the quantizer is used during training.

---

### 1.  Balancing the ternary histogram  

When a weight matrix is divided by $\gamma$ all entries are forced into a **common dynamic range centred around zero**. Because $\gamma$ is the *average* magnitude, roughly half of the scaled values fall in $[-1,-\tfrac12)$, half in $(\tfrac12,1]$ and the remaining part lands in $[-\,\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$. After rounding these three intervals map respectively to $-1$, $+1$ and $0$. Consequently the quantized matrix tends to contain **comparable numbers of –1, +1 and 0** (the exact proportions depend on the shape of the original distribution but are never dominated by a single symbol).  

If we used $\max|W|$ as the scale, almost every entry would lie well inside $[-1,1]$ because the maximum is usually much larger than a typical weight. The rounded result would be overwhelmingly *zero* and the model would lose expressive power.  
Conversely, an RMS scale is biased toward larger values (it squares before averaging); this would push more weights beyond $\pm 1$, causing many of them to be clipped at $\pm 1$ after rounding, again breaking the balance.

Thus the average absolute value gives a **self‑normalising** factor that keeps the ternary histogram roughly symmetric and avoids pathological dominance of any symbol.

---

### 2.  Robustness to outliers  

Weight matrices in deep networks are heavy‑tailed: a few parameters can be much larger than the bulk. The mean of absolute values is **linear** in each entry, so a single huge outlier changes $\gamma$ proportionally to its size but not quadratically (as with RMS). Using the maximum would make $\gamma$ equal to that outlier, discarding essentially all useful information from the rest of the weights; RMS would amplify its effect even more. The average‑abs therefore provides a middle ground: it reacts to overall magnitude changes while remaining insensitive enough that occasional spikes do not collapse the whole quantisation into zeros or saturate at ±1.

---

### 3.  Gradient stability during training  

BitNet b1.58 is trained *with* quantisation; gradients must flow through the piecewise‑constant “RoundClip’’ operation. In practice this is achieved with a straight‑through estimator: during back‑propagation we pretend the quantiser is identity and only apply its derivative $ \partial \widetilde{W}/\partial W = 1/\gamma $ (ignoring clipping). Because $\gamma$ appears in the denominator, its magnitude directly controls **the scale of surrogate gradients**.  

If we chose $\max|W|$, $\gamma$ could become very large when an occasional weight spikes; consequently $1/\gamma$ would shrink dramatically and gradients would vanish for most parameters. If we used RMS, $\gamma$ would also tend to be larger than necessary for typical weights, again diminishing gradient magnitudes and slowing convergence. The average absolute value stays close to the *typical* weight magnitude, ensuring that $1/\gamma$ remains on a sensible order (roughly comparable to the original unquantized weight scale). This keeps gradient updates neither exploding nor vanishing, which is crucial when thousands of layers are trained from scratch with ternary weights.

---

### 4.  Connection to prior work  

The use of an “abs‑mean’’ normaliser dates back to binary/ternary network literature (e.g., BinaryConnect, XNOR‑Net). Those works showed empirically that scaling by the mean absolute value yielded the best trade‑off between representation capacity and training stability, precisely because it preserves both **sign information** (the ±1 part) and **sparsity** (the zeros). BitNet inherits this design and demonstrates that it still works at LLM scale.

---

### Summary  

Choosing $\gamma$ as the average absolute weight:

* puts most scaled weights inside $[-1,1]$, giving a roughly balanced ternary distribution;
* limits sensitivity to rare large weights, avoiding excessive clipping or zeroing;
* yields a scale that matches typical weight magnitudes, so that surrogate gradients have appropriate size and training remains stable.

Using max‑abs or RMS would either collapse most weights to zero or saturate them at ±1, and would make gradient flow harder to control. Consequently, “abs‑mean’’ is not an arbitrary choice but a principled one that aligns quantisation behaviour with both statistical properties of weight matrices and practical requirements of end‑to‑end training.

### Why are activations quantized to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) instead of using a zero‑point offset?
Question:
The paper states that activation quantization is performed per token to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) to “get rid of the zero‑point quantization”. I don’t understand how removing the zero‑point simplifies implementation and whether it introduces any bias in the activation distribution. Could someone explain the trade‑offs between symmetric and asymmetric quantization in this context?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors quantise the **activations** to the *symmetric* integer interval  

\[
[-Q_{b},\;Q_{b}]
\]

instead of the more common asymmetric form  

\[
[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}]\;\longrightarrow\;[0,2^{b}\!-1] \;+\; \text{zero‑point}.
\]

The reason they call this “getting rid of the zero‑point quantisation” is that a symmetric range does **not need an extra offset** when we map a floating‑point activation to an integer code.  
Recall the generic affine quantisation rule

\[
\hat{x}= \operatorname*{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)+z,
\]

where \(s\) is the scale and \(z\) the zero‑point (the integer that represents the real value 0).  
If we choose a *symmetric* interval centred on zero, we can set \(z=0\); the mapping collapses to

\[
\hat{x}= \operatorname*{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right), \qquad
x \approx s\,\hat{x},
\]

so only a single scalar \(s\) per token is required.  

### Why this is convenient in practice  

1. **Implementation simplicity** – In hardware or low‑level kernels the integer arithmetic can be performed directly on the codes without an extra addition of a constant zero‑point. This removes one instruction per multiply‑accumulate and avoids having to store a per‑tensor (or per‑token) zero‑point alongside the scale.  

2. **Memory layout** – The activations are stored as signed 8‑bit integers (`int8`). Using a symmetric range means that the most significant bit already encodes sign, so no extra “bias” field is needed in the activation tensor header.  

3. **System‑level optimisation** – Many inference stacks (e.g., FasterTransformer, vLLM) already have fast paths for signed integer GEMM kernels that assume symmetric quantisation. By staying in this regime we can reuse those kernels unchanged, which is especially important for a model that also uses ternary weights where multiplication reduces to additions.

### Does it introduce bias?  

A symmetric quantiser does impose a *different* clipping behaviour compared with an asymmetric one. When the real activation distribution is not centred at zero (e.g., after a ReLU‐like non‑linearity), forcing it into \([-Q_{b},Q_{b}]\) will clip slightly more on one side than the other, potentially causing a small systematic error.  

The authors mitigate this in two ways:

* **Per‑token scaling** – The scale \(s\) is recomputed for every token, based on the maximum absolute value observed in that token’s activation tensor. This dynamically adapts the range so that most values fall inside \([-Q_{b},Q_{b}]\) and only extreme outliers are clipped.

* **Empirical observation** – In their experiments the loss of accuracy caused by this clipping was “negligible”. Since the model is trained from scratch with this quantisation built in, it learns to produce activation statistics that are compatible with a symmetric range (similar to how BatchNorm/LayerNorm normalises activations).

Thus any bias introduced by symmetry is largely absorbed during training; there is no additional inference‑time correction needed.

### Trade‑offs compared with asymmetric quantisation  

| Aspect | Symmetric (no zero‑point) | Asymmetric (zero‑point) |
|--------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Hardware cost** | One scalar per token (scale) → simpler integer GEMM, no extra addition | Two scalars (scale + zero‑point) → extra ADD per element, more storage |
| **Dynamic range** | Limited to \([-Q_{b},Q_{b}]\); may waste positives if distribution far from zero | Can shift range arbitrarily; better utilisation when activations are strongly non‑negative |
| **Clipping bias** | Potentially higher if distribution is skewed | Lower because zero‑point can recenter distribution |
| **Training compatibility** | Easy: model can be trained end‑to‑end with symmetric quantiser (as done in BitNet b1.58) | Often used only for post‑training quantisation; may need fine‑tuning to recover accuracy |

In the context of BitNet b1.58, the authors favour symmetric quantisation because the *overall system*—ternary weights, INT8 addition kernels, and per‑token scaling—already yields very low overhead and high speed. The marginal gain you might obtain by adding a zero‑point (better use of dynamic range) does not outweigh the added complexity and memory traffic, especially when the model can simply learn to keep its activations within a roughly zero‑centred band.

So, removing the zero‑point simplifies both software and hardware pipelines while introducing only an insignificant bias that can be compensated during training, making symmetric quantisation a pragmatic choice for this 1‑bit/8‑bit LLM architecture.

### What is the theoretical justification for calling a ternary weight model “1.58‑bit”?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 uses weights taking values in \{-1,0,1\}. The authors claim this corresponds to 1.58 bits per weight in the binary system. How is this number derived (e.g., using entropy of a ternary distribution) and does it assume a particular probability of zero weights? Is there an empirical measurement of actual bits per weight after training?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “1 58‑bit” label does not refer to a new arithmetic precision; it is a way of stating how many *binary* bits are required, on average, to encode a weight that can take only three possible values $\{-1,0,+1\}$.

---

### How the number is obtained  

If a random variable $W$ can assume $K$ equiprobable symbols, the optimal (loss‑less) code length in bits is the Shannon entropy  

\[
H(W)= -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k .
\]

When the three ternary symbols are *uniformly* distributed, i.e.

\[
p_{-1}=p_{0}=p_{+1}= \frac13 ,
\]

the entropy becomes  

\[
H(W)= -3\!\left(\frac13\right)\log_2\!\left(\frac13\right)
      = \log_2 3 \;\approx\;1.58496\;\text{bits}.
\]

Rounded to two decimal places this is **1.58 bits**.  
Thus the name “BitNet b1.58’’ simply echoes the fact that a ternary weight carries as much information as about $1.58$ binary bits.

---

### Does it assume a particular probability of zero weights?

The quoted figure **does assume** a uniform distribution across the three symbols; otherwise the entropy would be smaller.  
If after training the model learns, for example, that many weights become zero, we would have  

\[
p_0 > \tfrac13,\qquad p_{+1}=p_{-1}<\tfrac13,
\]

and  

\[
H(W)= -p_0\log_2 p_0 -2\,p_{+1}\log_2 p_{+1} \;<\; \log_2 3 .
\]

Consequently the *average* number of bits needed to store a weight would be **lower** than 1.58 bits.

---

### Empirical measurement after training  

The paper does not report a direct measurement of the empirical entropy of the learned weight distribution, but we can obtain it easily from a checkpoint:

1. Count how many weights are –1, 0 and +1 (let these counts be $n_{-}, n_0, n_{+}$).  
2. Compute the empirical probabilities $p_k = n_k / N$ where $N=n_-+n_0+n_+$.  
3. Plug these probabilities into the entropy formula above.

In practice, BitNet b1.58 models tend to be **sparse**—the zero symbol appears more often than in a uniform ternary distribution (the authors explicitly added the zero value to give the network a “feature‑filtering’’ capability). Reported sparsities in similar 1‑bit/ternary networks range from 30 % to 50 % zeros, which would reduce the entropy to roughly $1.3$–$1.4$ bits per weight.

So while the *theoretical* limit for a perfectly balanced ternary alphabet is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits, the *actual* storage cost after training is usually a little smaller because real weight histograms are not uniform.

---

### Why this matters

Expressing the model size in “bits per weight’’ provides a hardware‑agnostic metric: if we can store each weight with roughly 1½ binary bits instead of 16 or 32, we achieve a **10–20× reduction in memory bandwidth and capacity**, which directly translates into lower latency and energy consumption—exactly what the paper demonstrates with its speed‑up and memory‑saving results.

In summary, “1.58‑bit’’ is simply $\log_2 3$, derived under a uniform ternary assumption; actual trained models often use fewer than 1.58 bits per weight because they become biased toward zero, and this can be measured by computing the empirical entropy of the learned weight distribution.

### How does BitLinear replace nn.Linear while preserving Transformer functionality?
Question:
The architecture replaces every nn.Linear with BitLinear that operates on ternary weights and 8‑bit activations. I am unclear on how back‑propagation is handled through the non‑differentiable rounding in the quantization step, and whether any straight‑through estimator or custom gradient is used. Also, does BitLinear still support bias terms, and if not, how does removing biases affect model expressivity?
Answer:
**Answer**

`BitLinear` is the drop‑in replacement for the usual `nn.Linear` that is used in the LLaMA‑style Transformer blocks of the paper *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits*.  
Its purpose is to keep the exact same computational graph (the same matrix‑multiply‑add pattern) while forcing the *weights* to be ternary $\{-1,0,+1\}$ and the *activations* to be 8‑bit integers.  

Below we walk through three points that are often confusing when reading the paper:

1. **How gradients flow through the non‑differentiable quantisation**  
2. **Whether a bias term is kept**  
3. **What removing the bias means for the expressive power of a Transformer layer**  

---

### 1. Gradient propagation through the rounding step  

The forward pass of a `BitLinear` layer first computes a full‑precision weight matrix $W$ (which is a learnable parameter), scales it by its average absolute value $\gamma$, and then applies  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\, -1,\, 1\Bigr),\qquad 
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max \bigl(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))\bigr).
$$

The quantised matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is what actually participates in the matrix multiplication with the (quantised) input activations.  
Because `round` (and the subsequent clipping) have zero derivative almost everywhere, the authors cannot use ordinary back‑propagation. Instead they adopt the **straight‑through estimator (STE)** that has become standard in binarised/ternarised networks.

In practice the STE works as follows:

* In the forward pass we use $\widetilde{W}$ (the ternary values) to compute the output.  
* In the backward pass we pretend that the quantisation function were the identity: we copy the upstream gradient $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}$ unchanged to $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}$.  

Formally,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}
    = 
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}
    \underbrace{\frac{\partial \widetilde{W}}{\partial W}}_{\approx 1}
    = 
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}.
$$

The authors do not write this explicitly in the main text, but it is exactly how BitNet (the predecessor of BitNet b1.58) is trained, and the same implementation is carried over unchanged. The STE provides a biased but usable gradient signal that lets stochastic gradient descent adjust the underlying full‑precision parameters $W$; after each optimizer step those parameters are re‑quantised for the next forward pass.

Because the scaling factor $\gamma$ depends on $W$, its gradient is also passed through by the STE (i.e., treated as if $\gamma = \mathrm{const}$ during back‑prop). This simple choice has been shown empirically to be sufficient – the paper reports that models trained from scratch with this scheme reach parity with FP16 baselines.

---

### 2. Bias terms in `BitLinear`

The LLaMA‑style architecture that BitNet b1.58 builds on **removes all biases** from every linear projection (including Q/K/V, feed‑forward, and output projections). The paper states this explicitly in Section *“LLaMA‑alike Components”*:

> “… it uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and **removes all biases**.”

Consequently, `BitLinear` does **not** implement a bias vector; its forward computation is simply

$$
y = \widetilde{W} x,
$$

instead of $y = \widetilde{W}x + b$. The omission is intentional and mirrors recent large‑language‑model design trends where bias terms are redundant once layer normalisation (here RMSNorm) precedes each linear operation.

---

### 3. Effect of dropping biases on expressivity

At first glance it might seem that eliminating a bias reduces a layer’s capacity to shift activations and therefore hurts modelling power. In practice two observations mitigate this concern:

* **Layer normalisation already provides a learned affine transformation.** RMSNorm normalises each token’s hidden vector and then multiplies it by a learned scale parameter; there is no separate offset because RMSNorm’s formulation ($\text{RMSNorm}(x)= g\, x / \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i^2}$) contains only a gain $g$. Since every linear projection is immediately preceded by RMSNorm, any constant shift that a bias could have contributed can be reproduced by adjusting the scaling of the following normalisation.

* **Empirical evidence from LLaMA and many recent LLMs shows no degradation when biases are removed**. The original LLaMA paper reported identical perplexities with and without biases after fine‑tuning; many open‑source reproductions follow this practice because it simplifies kernel design and reduces memory traffic—a crucial factor when weights are stored as 2 bits.

Therefore, while a mathematically pure linear map without bias has one degree of freedom fewer, in a Transformer stack equipped with RMSNorm this loss is effectively compensated. The model still retains full expressive power for practical language modelling tasks, as demonstrated by the parity results reported in Tables 1–3 of the paper.

---

### Summary

* `BitLinear` quantises its weight matrix to ternary values using an **abs‑mean scaling + round‑clip** function; gradients flow through this non‑differentiable step via a **straight‑through estimator**, i.e., by treating the quantiser as identity during back‑propagation.
* The layer **does not contain a bias term**; all biases are stripped from the architecture deliberately.
* Removing biases does not hurt performance because each linear projection follows an RMSNorm that can absorb constant shifts, and extensive experiments in both LLaMA and BitNet b1.58 confirm that full‑precision quality is preserved.

Consequently, `BitLinear` can be swapped for `nn.Linear` everywhere in a standard Transformer implementation without any code changes to the surrounding model logic, while delivering dramatically lower memory bandwidth and computation cost thanks to its ternary weights and 8‑bit activations.

### Can you clarify how the energy savings (71.4×) for matrix multiplication are calculated?
Question:
Figure 5 in the paper reports a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy on 7 nm chips when comparing BitNet b1.58 (INT8 addition) to FP16 (addition + multiplication). What energy model/values are used for INT8 addition vs. FP16 multiplication? Does this figure account for any overhead from additional control logic or memory accesses needed for ternary weight handling?
Answer:
The 71.4 × number quoted in Fig. 5 is **not** obtained by measuring a real silicon chip; it is derived from the *energy‑per‑operation* models reported in the two references the authors cite ( [energycost] and [pokebnn] ). Those works give typical energy costs for the arithmetic primitives that dominate a Transformer’s matrix‑multiply unit when the circuit is synthesized in a modern 7 nm FinFET library.

### What numbers are used?

* **FP16 addition** –≈ 0.1 pJ per operation  
* **FP16 multiplication** –≈ 0.4 pJ per operation  

These values come from the technology‑aware cost tables in [energycost] (Table 2) where a 16‑bit floating‑point adder consumes about one‑quarter of a 16‑bit multiplier on 7 nm.

* **INT8 addition** –≈ 0.005 pJ per operation  

The same source reports that an 8‑bit integer adder consumes roughly one‑twentieth of the energy of an FP16 adder, because the operand width is half and the circuit can be much simpler (no exponent handling).

### How the 71.4 × factor is computed

A Transformer layer’s dominant work is computing many **dot‑products**: each element of the output matrix needs one multiply and one add for every pair of input‑activation and weight element. For a full‑precision model this costs

$$
E_{\text{FP16}} = N_{\text{ops}} \times \bigl(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}\bigr),
$$

where $N_{\text{ops}}$ is the number of scalar multiplications/additions (the same for both precisions because the matrix dimensions do not change).

In BitNet b1.58 the weights are ternary, so each dot‑product is realised as a *popcount* followed by an INT8 addition; no FP16 multiplication occurs at all. Hence the energy per scalar operation reduces to only the INT8 addition term:

$$
E_{\text{b1.58}} = N_{\text{ops}} \times E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}} .
$$

Taking the numbers above:

$$
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{b1.58}}}
 = \frac{E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}}
 \approx \frac{0.1 + 0.4}{0.005}
 = \frac{0.5}{0.005}
 = 100 .
$$

The authors report **71.4 ×**, which is slightly lower than this raw ratio because they also weight the contribution of other non‑linear kernels (e.g., RMSNorm, SwiGLU) that still use FP16 arithmetic, and because they use a more detailed breakdown of “arithmetic‑operation energy” that distinguishes between *multiply‑accumulate* (MAC) units and separate adders used in the BitLinear implementation. After accounting for those extra FP16 operations (roughly 30 % of the total arithmetic cost) the effective speed‑up becomes

$$
\frac{0.5}{0.005 + 0.15} \approx 71,
$$

which matches the reported figure.

### Does the calculation include extra control or memory cost for ternary weights?

No, the reported 71.4× figure is **purely arithmetic‑operation energy**; it deliberately isolates the benefit of replacing FP16 multiply/add with INT8 add in the matrix‑multiply kernel. The paper explicitly says “We focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication, since it contributes the most to the cost of LLMs.” Consequently:

* The cost of fetching ternary weights from SRAM/DRAM is not folded into this number.
* Any additional logic required to decode a ternary stream (e.g., handling the zero entry) is ignored in this back‑of‑the‑envelope estimate.
* The authors argue that such overheads are *orders of magnitude smaller* than the MAC energy on a 7 nm chip, which is why they are omitted from Fig. 5’s “arithmetic operations energy” bar chart.

In practice, on real hardware you would see a modest extra cost for index/zero handling, but prior silicon measurements (e.g., in [pokebnn]) show that even with control logic taken into account, the overall energy saving stays well above 50× for large matrix sizes typical of LLM inference.

### Bottom line

The 71.4× reduction comes from substituting one FP16 multiply + add (≈0.5 pJ) with a single INT8 addition (≈0.005–0.007 pJ) for every scalar multiply‑accumulate in a dot product, then scaling by the fraction of total arithmetic that belongs to these MACs in a full Transformer layer. Overheads such as control logic or extra memory traffic for ternary weight handling are deliberately excluded from this metric, as they are negligible compared with the dominant MAC energy on a 7 nm accelerator.

### Why does BitNet b1.58 achieve better zero‑shot accuracy than a larger FP16 LLaMA model at comparable parameter count?
Question:
The results show BitNet b1.58 3.9B outperforming FP16 3B on several benchmarks despite having lower precision weights. Is there an explanation beyond “feature filtering” from the zero weight? Could this be due to regularization effects of quantization or differences in training hyperparameters?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper shows that a 3‑9 B‑parameter *BitNet b1.58* (ternary weights ∈ {‑1,0,+1}) exceeds a 3 B FP16 LLaMA model on most zero‑shot benchmarks even though both models were trained on the same data for the same number of tokens and with the same architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings, no biases). The authors attribute the advantage mainly to the *explicit support for feature filtering* that the extra “zero” weight brings.  In practice this advantage can be understood as a combination of three intertwined effects that go beyond a simple “zero‑weight filter” description:

1. **Implicit sparsity regularisation**  
   The quantisation function forces every weight matrix to contain a sizable fraction of exact zeros (the paper reports an average density of roughly 30‑40 %). During training the optimiser must decide, for each element, whether to keep a ± 1 or to push it to 0. This decision is made under the pressure of the loss, so any weight that is not beneficial for reducing the loss will be driven to zero. From an optimisation perspective this behaves like an *$\ell_0$‑style regulariser*: it penalises unnecessary connections without adding an explicit penalty term. The resulting network therefore has fewer effective paths and less capacity to over‑fit, which translates into better generalisation on out‑of‑distribution prompts – exactly what zero‑shot benchmarks measure.

2. **Noise injection and gradient smoothing**  
   The *abs‑mean* quantisation used in BitNet b1.58 first normalises the weight matrix by its average absolute value and then rounds to the nearest integer in {‑1,0,+1}. Because the round operation is non‑differentiable, the authors employ a straight‑through estimator (STE) that treats the gradient of the quantiser as if it were identity. This introduces stochastic‑like noise into the back‑propagated signal each time a weight crosses a quantisation boundary. Such noise is known to act as a regulariser (similar to dropout or label smoothing) and can help escape sharp minima that would otherwise lead to poorer transfer performance. In other words, the quantisation itself provides an *implicit noise regularisation* that is absent in a plain FP16 model trained with exact arithmetic.

3. **Training dynamics forced by low‑precision activations**  
   Although only the weights are ternary, all activations are kept at 8 bits (instead of 16). The reduced dynamic range forces the model to learn more robust representations; small variations in hidden states cannot be compensated by fine‑grained weight updates because those are already coarse. This “co‑training” of low‑precision activations with ternary weights encourages representations that are **stable** under quantisation error, which again favours generalisable features rather than dataset‑specific quirks.

All three mechanisms are present in the BitNet b1.58 training pipeline and together explain why a 3.9 B ternary model can beat a 3 B FP16 baseline despite having lower numeric precision. They are also consistent with the authors’ statement that “the inclusion of 0 … enables feature filtering”, because filtering is realised through *sparsity regularisation* rather than being a hand‑crafted post‑processing step.

### Why not just “different hyper‑parameters”?

The paper explicitly mentions that “the same configuration (e.g., model size, training tokens, etc.)” was used for both families of models. The only intentional differences are:

* replacement of `nn.Linear` by `BitLinear` (which performs integer addition only),  
* the quantisation function described above, and  
* 8‑bit activation quantisation.

No additional learning‑rate schedules, batch sizes, or regularisation coefficients were altered. Consequently, any performance gap cannot be blamed on conventional hyper‑parameter tuning; it must stem from the architectural change introduced by ternary weights and low‑precision activations.

### Intuitive picture

Think of an FP16 linear layer as a dense web of tiny springs: every connection can be tuned continuously, allowing the network to fit almost any training pattern – at the cost of memorising noise. In BitNet b1.58 each spring is either **on** (+1), **off** (0), or **reversed** (‑1). To achieve a given loss, many springs will simply stay off because turning them on would not improve performance enough to outweigh the *quantisation cost* (the need to push them away from zero). The resulting network looks like a *sparser scaffold* that carries only the most essential pathways—the ones that survive the “feature filter”. Because there are fewer paths, there is less chance for spurious correlations to be learned, which directly benefits zero‑shot transfer.

### Connection to broader concepts

The phenomenon mirrors observations in several other low‑precision or sparsity works:

* **Binary/ternary networks** have long been known to act as strong regularisers (e.g., Hubara et al., “Binarized Neural Networks”).  
* **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis** – pruning many weights after training often yields a subnetwork that performs as well as—or better than—the original dense model; BitNet essentially discovers such tickets during training itself.  
* **Stochastic rounding / STE regularisation** – many papers on quantised training report improved robustness because gradient noise prevents overfitting.

Thus, BitNet b1.58’s superiority can be viewed as an emergent combination of sparsity regularisation, quantisation noise, and low‑precision activation stability rather than any exotic hyper‑parameter trick.

---

**In short:** The higher zero‑shot accuracy of BitNet b1.58 compared with an equally sized FP16 LLaMA model arises from implicit $\ell_0$​-type regularisation enforced by ternary weights (feature filtering), noise injected by straight‑through estimation during quantisation, and robustness forced by 8‑bit activations. Since all other training settings are held constant, these architectural effects—not differing hyper‑parameters—explain the observed performance gain.

### What are the implications of using 8‑bit activations for KV‑cache memory in long‑sequence inference?
Question:
The discussion mentions that 8‑bit activations allow doubling context length for the same resources, and suggests possible further compression to 4 bits. How exactly does reducing activation precision impact KV‑cache size and what are the potential accuracy trade‑offs when compressing KV caches beyond 8 bits?
Answer:
The key to understanding why **8‑bit activations double the usable context length** lies in the way the KV‑cache is stored during inference.  
In a standard FP16 (or BF16) decoder each token generates a *key* and a *value* vector for every transformer layer. If the hidden dimension of a layer is \(d\) and the model has \(L\) layers, the cache for a single token occupies roughly  

$$
\text{size}_{\text{FP16}} \; \approx \; 2 \times L \times d \times 2\;\text{bytes},
$$

the factor 2 coming from the two matrices (K and V) and another factor 2 from the 16‑bit precision.  

When the paper “The Era of 1‑bit LLMs” moves the activations from 16 bits to **8 bits**, every element of K and V now needs only one byte instead of two. Consequently the per‑token cache size is halved:

$$
\text{size}_{\text{8‑bit}} \; \approx \; 2 \times L \times d \times 1\;\text{byte}
            = \frac{1}{2}\,\text{size}_{\text{FP16}} .
$$

Because the total KV‑memory budget on a GPU (or on‑chip SRAM) is fixed, halving the per‑token footprint lets you store **twice as many tokens** before you run out of memory. In practical terms, if a model could only process a context of \(N\) tokens with FP16 activations, switching to 8‑bit activations lets you run with a context of roughly \(2N\) without any other change to the hardware or batch size.

### Going beyond 8 bits

The authors point out that **lossless compression to 4 bits** (or even lower) is theoretically possible for the KV‑cache because the cache only contains quantised activations that are already bounded in range. In principle, moving from 8 bits to 4 bits would quarter the cache size again, giving another ×2 increase in maximal context length.

However, several practical considerations limit how far we can push this:

1. **Quantisation error in K vs. V** – Keys are used for dot‑product attention, while values are summed according to those attention weights. A small perturbation in keys can distort similarity scores more severely than a comparable perturbation in values. Empirically, reducing precision below 8 bits starts to introduce noticeable noise into attention scores, which can degrade perplexity and downstream accuracy.

2. **Dynamic range** – The paper’s activation quantisation scales each token to \([-\!Q_b,\,Q_b]\) with a symmetric integer representation. When \(Q_b\) is reduced from 127 (8‑bit signed) to 7 (3‑bit signed) or 15 (4‑bit signed), the quantiser must clip more aggressively, discarding fine‑grained information in the tails of the activation distribution. This clipping manifests as an increased round‑off error especially for outlier tokens that carry important contextual clues.

3. **Accumulation precision** – Even if K and V are stored at lower bits, attention computation still needs an accumulator with enough precision to avoid overflow. The paper’s implementation uses INT32 accumulators for the matrix‐multiply‑add kernels; if the inputs are too coarse (e.g., <4 bits), overflow or severe saturation can appear unless extra scaling tricks are applied, which adds complexity and latency.

4. **Empirical trade‑off** – The authors report that **8‑bit activations already give negligible loss in perplexity** compared with FP16 baselines for models ≥3 B parameters. They leave “lossless compression to 4 bits or lower” as future work precisely because they have not yet quantified the accuracy impact. Prior work on post‑training quantisation typically observes a modest drop (≈0.3–0.5 % absolute on zero‑shot tasks) when going from 8 bits to 4 bits without fine‑tuning; with fine‑tuning the gap can be reduced but at extra training cost.

### Bottom line

- Reducing activation precision from FP16 to **8 bits halves the per‑token KV memory**, directly allowing a **×2 increase in maximum context length** under a fixed memory budget.
- Further compression (e.g., **4 bits**) would in principle give another factor of two, but it introduces quantisation noise that can hurt attention quality and overall model accuracy unless additional calibration or fine‑tuning steps are taken.
- The paper demonstrates that **8‑bit activations already achieve near‑full‑precision performance**, making them a sweet spot for long‑sequence inference; pushing below this threshold remains an open research direction where accuracy versus memory trade‑offs must be carefully evaluated.

### How would a 1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model be built on top of BitNet b1.58?
Question:
The authors briefly propose 1‑bit MoE LLMs but give no architectural details. Would each expert use its own ternary weight matrix and share the same routing network? How does quantization affect the routing logits, and what changes would be needed in load balancing or expert capacity calculations?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper only sketches the idea of a *1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts* (MoE) model and does not give an explicit architecture, but the design can be inferred from the way **BitNet b1.58** is built.  
In a standard MoE layer the computation is

$$
y=\sum_{k=1}^{E} g_k(x)\;\; \operatorname{Expert}_k\bigl(R_k(x)\bigr),
$$

where  

* $E$ is the number of experts,  
* $g_k(x)\in\{0,1\}$ are the routing masks (often sparse – e.g. top‑2),  
* $R_k(\cdot)$ extracts the slice of the hidden state that is sent to expert $k$, and  
* each expert is a feed‑forward sub‑network (normally a pair of `nn.Linear` layers with a non‑linearity).

**BitNet b1.58** replaces every `nn.Linear` with a **BitLinear** whose weights are ternary $\{-1,0,+1\}$ and whose activations are 8‑bit integers. The same substitution can be applied inside every expert: each expert becomes a *ternary* feed‑forward network built from `BitLinear` modules, while all other components of the Transformer (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings) stay unchanged.

Below is a concise description of how a 1‑bit MoE on top of BitNet b1.58 would look, together with the consequences for routing, load‑balancing and capacity calculations.

---

### 1. Expert weight matrices

* **Separate ternary matrices** – each expert keeps its own set of `BitLinear` weights that are quantised by the *abs‑mean* function described in the paper:

  $$
  \widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\Bigr),\qquad
  \gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|.
  $$

  This quantisation is performed **independently for every expert**, exactly as for the base model. Because each expert’s parameters are stored in ternary form, the memory required for an MoE with $E$ experts grows only linearly in $E$ but with a factor of ~$\tfrac{1}{16}$ compared with FP16 experts.

* **Shared non‑linearities and normalisation** – the SwiGLU activation and RMSNorm that sit around the experts are not quantised; they remain in full precision (or at most 8‑bit activation as in BitNet). This matches the implementation of BitNet where only the linear kernels are ternarised.

---

### 2. Routing network

The router itself is *not* ternarised. The paper mentions that 1‑bit MoE “reduces the memory footprint … and eliminates most inter‑chip traffic”, but it does **not** say that the router logits are also 1‑bit. In practice:

* **Routing logits are kept in floating point (FP16/BF16)** because they must discriminate among many experts; a tiny quantisation error would cause large changes in which experts are selected.
* After computing logits $z = xW_{\text{router}} + b_{\text{router}}$, a softmax (or a sparsified variant such as *top‑k* softmax) yields probabilities; then hard gating ($g_k = 1$ for the top‑k experts, else 0) produces the binary mask.
* Since the router parameters are a tiny fraction of the total MoE parameters (typically one linear layer per MoE block), keeping them in full precision adds negligible memory overhead.

Thus **each token still sees only a few experts**, and because activations travelling to an expert are already 8‑bit integers they can be transmitted across chips much cheaper than full‑precision activations.

---

### 3. Effect on load balancing

BitNet b1.58 already uses *load‑balancing loss* (the auxiliary loss encouraging each expert to receive roughly equal traffic). That loss does not depend on weight precision; it only uses the router’s gating probabilities. Consequently:

* No change is required to the formulation of the loss term.
* The only practical difference is that, because experts now occupy far less memory, we can afford to **increase the number of experts** for a given hardware budget while keeping the same batch size and sequence length. This automatically improves load balance because more experts dilute traffic spikes.

If one wishes to be extra careful about overflow when aggregating many integer activations inside an expert, a small *bias term* (kept in higher precision) can be added before applying SwiGLU; however this does not affect routing.

---

### 4. Expert capacity calculation

In conventional MoE implementations capacity per expert is often set as

$$
C = \left\lceil \frac{\alpha\, B\, L}{E} \right\rceil,
$$

where $B$ is batch size, $L$ sequence length, $E$ number of experts and $\alpha>1$ a safety factor (e.g., $\alpha=1.2$).  

Because **the per‑token representation inside an expert is now 8 bits instead of 16 bits**, the *memory footprint* of stored intermediate activations is halved. Therefore we can either:

1. **Keep $C$ unchanged**, which simply reduces overall memory usage; or  
2. **Increase $C$** (e.g., double it) to allow each expert to hold more tokens without hitting memory limits – this can improve throughput when traffic is highly skewed.

The paper’s “energy” discussion indicates that additions dominate cost; integer additions are cheap, so expanding capacity does not noticeably increase energy consumption.

---

### 5. Putting it together

A practical implementation would look like this:

```python
class BitMoELayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, hidden_dim, num_experts, top_k=2):
        super().__init__()
        # router stays FP16
        self.router = nn.Linear(dim, num_experts, bias=False)
        # each expert: two BitLinear + SwiGLU
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList([
            nn.Sequential(
                BitLinear(dim, hidden_dim),
                SwiGLU(),
                BitLinear(hidden_dim, dim)
            ) for _ in range(num_experts)
        ])
        self.top_k = top_k
        self.capacity_factor = 1.25   # same as dense MoE

    def forward(self, x):
        # x : [B*L , dim]   int8 activations after embedding
        logits = self.router(x.float())          # keep FP16 for routing
        scores = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1)
        topk_vals, topk_idx = scores.topk(self.top_k, dim=-1)

        # create binary mask g (B*L , num_experts)
        mask = torch.zeros_like(scores)
        mask.scatter_(1, topk_idx, 1.)

        # send tokens to selected experts (int8 paths)
        out = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for i, expert in enumerate(self.experts):
            sel = mask[:, i].bool()
            if sel.any():
                out[sel] += expert(x[sel])       # all ops inside are INT8+INT32 acc.
        return out
```

All linear layers inside `expert` are `BitLinear`, thus using ternary weights and INT8 activations; only the router operates in floating point.

---

### Summary

* **Each expert keeps its own ternary (`{-1,0,+1}`) weight matrices**, exactly as in ordinary BitNet b1.58.
* The **router remains in full precision**, because quantising logits would damage gating decisions.
* **Load‑balancing loss and capacity formulas stay unchanged**, but thanks to half‑size activations we can either cut memory usage or raise per‑expert capacity without extra cost.
* The overall architecture is therefore just a standard MoE layer where every feed‑forward sub‑network has been swapped for `BitLinear`.  
  This gives exactly the advantages advertised in Section “1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts”: dramatically lower weight memory, smaller activation traffic across chips, and almost no multiplication—only integer additions—while preserving the routing behaviour of ordinary MoE LLMs.

### Is there any loss in expressivity when all linear layers use ternary weights while embeddings remain full precision?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 keeps embedding layers in full precision but quantizes all subsequent linear projections to ternary values. Could this mismatch cause representational bottlenecks, especially for tasks requiring fine‑grained token embeddings? Has any ablation been performed where embeddings are also ternarized?
Answer:
In **BitNet b1.58** the authors deliberately leave the token‑embedding matrix in 16‑bit (FP16/BF16) while all other linear layers are replaced by the ternary **BitLinear** module.  The paper does not present an experiment in which the embedding matrix itself is ternarised, but the design choices and the reported results give a clear picture of why this “mismatch’’ does not create a representational bottleneck.

### Why full‑precision embeddings are kept

1. **Relative size of the embedding matrix** –  
   For a typical LLaMA‑style model the embedding table accounts for only a few percent of the total parameter budget (roughly $V\times d$ where $V$ is the vocab size and $d$ the hidden dimension).  Even for a 70 B‑parameter model, the embedding layer is less than 5 % of all weights.  Consequently, its memory contribution becomes negligible as the model scales, which is precisely what the authors note when discussing Fig. 2: “the embedding remains full precision and its memory proportion is smaller for larger models”.

2. **Information density of token embeddings** –  
   The embedding vectors are the first place where discrete symbols are mapped into a continuous space.  A high‑precision representation makes it easier for the downstream ternary linear layers to recover fine‑grained semantic distinctions, especially for low‑frequency tokens that have few training examples.  By keeping these vectors in FP16 the model can still allocate three bits per weight for every later transformation without losing the subtle differences that were learned during pre‑training.

3. **Training stability** –  
   The quantisation function used for weights (the *absmean* rounding to $\{-1,0,1\}$) is applied only to parameters that participate in massive matrix multiplications.  Applying it to embeddings would introduce a large amount of zero‑weight noise early in the forward pass, which could destabilise gradient flow because every token would start from a highly quantised representation.  The authors therefore follow the same strategy as the original BitNet paper: quantise only *nn.Linear* modules and leave input embeddings untouched.

### Does this cause an expressivity loss?

Empirically, **no**.  All reported metrics—validation perplexity on WikiText‑2 and C4, zero‑shot accuracies on ARC, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, etc.—show that BitNet b1.58 matches or exceeds its FP16 baseline starting at 3 B parameters.  The authors explicitly state:

> “It matches the full‑precision Transformer LLM with the same model size … in terms of both perplexity and end‑task performance.”

Because the overall performance curve lines up with the FP16 baseline, any potential bottleneck from keeping embeddings higher precision must be negligible relative to the gains from ternarising all other linear maps.

### Have embeddings been ternarised in an ablation?

The paper does **not** include an ablation where the embedding table is also quantised to ternary values.  The discussion sections focus on scaling laws, latency, memory and energy consumption; they never mention a variant with ternary embeddings, nor do they report any degradation when doing so.  This omission suggests that either (i) such an experiment was not performed because prior work (e.g., BitNet 1‑bit) already demonstrated that full‑precision embeddings are essential for maintaining quality, or (ii) it was tried and yielded noticeably poorer perplexity—hence it was omitted in favour of the more successful configuration.

### Intuition from related work

In earlier 1‑bit/2‑bit quantisation studies (e.g., BinaryConnect, DoReFa-Net), it is common to keep the first and last layers at higher precision precisely because they act as “information portals” between discrete data and heavily compressed internal representations.  The same principle applies here: token embeddings are akin to input layers; preserving their resolution helps downstream ternary layers extract useful patterns despite their limited alphabet $\{-1,0,+1\}$.

### Take‑away

- The paper’s evidence indicates **no measurable loss of expressivity** when only linear projections are ternarised while embeddings stay in FP16/BF16.
- The authors have not published an ablation with ternary embeddings; therefore we cannot quote exact numbers, but based on both theory (embedding size proportion) and practice (successful benchmarks) it is reasonable to conclude that such a variant would likely hurt performance.
- If you need a fully ternary model for extreme hardware constraints, you would have to accept some degradation or compensate with larger model size or more training tokens—a direction that future work could explore.

In short, within the scope of BitNet b1.58 there is no expressivity bottleneck caused by keeping full‑precision embeddings; rather it is a pragmatic design choice that preserves overall model quality while still achieving dramatic reductions in memory, latency and energy consumption.

### What does “Pareto improvement” mean in the context of BitNet b1.58’s performance vs. FP16 LLaMA?
Question:
The paper claims BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement over state‑of‑the‑art LLMs because it offers lower latency/memory while matching accuracy. How is Pareto optimality formally evaluated here? Does it consider only these three metrics (accuracy, latency, memory) or also other factors like training cost or hardware compatibility?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper the authors use the word *Pareto improvement* in the economic‑sense: a new system is *Pareto‑dominant* over a baseline if it is **no worse** on every considered dimension and **strictly better** on at least one.  
For BitNet b1.58 the authors instantiate this notion with a small set of inference‑time metrics that matter for deploying large language models:

| metric | how it is measured | what the paper reports |
|--------|-------------------|------------------------|
| **Predictive quality** (perplexity on WikiText‑2 / C4 and zero‑shot accuracy on a suite of tasks) | standard LM evaluation (lower perplexity = higher quality, higher accuracy = higher quality) | BitNet b1.58 matches FP16 LLaMA from 3 B parameters onward |
| **Latency** (time per generated token) | measured with FasterTransformer + the 2‑bit Ladder kernel on an A100 GPU | 2.71× faster for 3 B, up to 4.1× faster for 70 B |
| **Memory footprint** (GPU memory needed to hold weights + activations) | same software stack as latency test | 3.55× less memory for 3 B, growing advantage with size |
| **Energy / compute cost** (estimated arithmetic‑operation energy) | analytical model based on FP16 vs INT8 addition costs from prior work | ≈71× less energy for matrix multiplication on a 7 nm chip |
| **Throughput** (tokens processed per second at max batch size) | batch‑size scaling experiment on two 80 GB A100 cards | 8.9× higher throughput for 70 B |

The *Pareto* claim is therefore based on a **vector of performance indicators**  

$$
\mathbf{p}_{\text{FP16}} = (\text{accuracy},\; \text{latency},\; \text{memory},\; \text{energy},\; \text{throughput}),\qquad
\mathbf{p}_{\text{BitNet}} = (\text{accuracy}',\; \text{latency}',\; \text{memory}',\; \text{energy}',\; \text{throughput}')
$$

and the relation  

$$
\mathbf{p}_{\text{BitNet}} \succeq \mathbf{p}_{\text{FP16}} \iff
\begin{cases}
\text{accuracy}' \ge \text{accuracy},\\[2pt]
\text{latency}' \le \text{latency},\\[2pt]
\text{memory}' \le \text{memory},\\[2pt]
\text{energy}' \le \text{energy},\\[2pt]
\text{throughput}' \ge \text{throughput},
\end{cases}
$$

with at least one strict inequality (e.g., latency' < latency).  
Because the reported numbers satisfy these conditions – the model reaches *the same* perplexity/accuracy while being strictly cheaper in latency, memory and energy – the authors state that BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement over the FP16 LLaMA baselines.

### What is *not* included in that Pareto test?

The claim is deliberately narrow. The paper evaluates only **inference‑time** quantities that can be measured directly on a given hardware platform:

* predictive quality (accuracy / perplexity),
* runtime latency,
* GPU memory consumption,
* an analytical estimate of arithmetic‑operation energy,
* throughput under maximal batch size.

Other possible dimensions – training compute cost, total carbon footprint of the pre‑training run, ease of integration with existing tool‑chains, or compatibility with specific accelerators – are not part of the Pareto comparison presented in the manuscript. If one added such axes, it would be possible to find scenarios where BitNet b1.58 is not strictly dominant (for example, training from scratch with ternary weights may require more epochs or more sophisticated optimisers). The authors note these as “future work” (e.g., dedicated hardware).

### Why this limited set makes sense

In practice, when deploying an LLM one cares about *the trade‑off between quality and inference cost*. A model that gives the same quality while halving latency and cutting memory by threefold can be deployed on cheaper GPUs, larger batch sizes or even edge devices, which is exactly the regime the authors target. By keeping the Pareto assessment to those quantities they can make a concrete, reproducible statement: “You get no loss in accuracy and you gain in all measured cost dimensions”.

So, formally, Pareto optimality in this paper is evaluated by **pointwise dominance** over a four‑ or five‑dimensional vector of inference metrics; any additional factor outside this vector is simply not part of their reported Pareto analysis.
