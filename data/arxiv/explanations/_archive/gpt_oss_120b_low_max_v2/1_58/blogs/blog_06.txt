# Beyond Quantization: Training Large Language Models Directly in 1.58 Bits  

## Introduction  

Quantizing a pretrained language model to a lower precision (4‑bit, 2‑bit, …) has become the go‑to recipe for squeezing inference cost out of massive Transformers. The workflow is simple: train a full‑precision model, then apply post‑training quantization tricks such as smooth‑quant or GPTQ, and you get a smaller, faster model.  

But recent work on **BitNet b1.58** shows that this two‑step approach may be leaving performance on the table. Instead of “train‑then‑compress,” the authors train the whole model *from scratch* with **ternary weights** \(\{-1,0,1\}\), which corresponds to an average of **1.58 bits per weight**. The result is a model that matches (and sometimes exceeds) its FP16 counterpart on perplexity and downstream tasks while consuming dramatically less memory, latency, and energy.  

In this post we unpack why training directly at 1.58 bits is fundamentally different from post‑training quantization, what technical hurdles it raises, and how the BitNet b1.58 paper overcomes them.

## Post‑Training Quantization vs. From‑Scratch Low‑Bit Training  

| Aspect | Post‑Training Quantization | From‑Scratch 1.58‑bit Training |
|--------|---------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **When the model sees low precision** | After the full‐precision network has already converged | Throughout every forward & backward pass |
| **Typical bitwidth** | 4 bits (sometimes 2 bits) for weights/activations | Ternary weights (−1, 0, +1) + 8‑bit activations |
| **Gradient flow** | Not relevant – gradients are computed in full precision | Must estimate gradients through a discrete quantizer |
| **Performance ceiling** | Limited by quantization error on a fixed representation | Model learns a representation that *is* low‑bit |
| **Hardware friendliness** | Still needs dequantization kernels; mixed‑precision ops | Almost no multiplication – integer addition only |

The key insight is that quantization error is *inherent* when you try to map a high‑capacity FP16 solution onto a low‑bit space after training. In contrast, training with ternary weights forces the optimizer to shape the solution *inside* that low‑bit space from day one, allowing it to discover weight patterns that are inherently robust to the discrete set \(\{-1,0,1\}\).

## Core Challenges of Training at 1.58 Bits  

### 1. Discrete Weight Space  
Standard backpropagation relies on differentiable operations; rounding a weight to \{-1,0,1\} is non‑differentiable. This makes gradient estimation tricky.

### 2. Scale Ambiguity  
Ternary weights have no magnitude information beyond sign and sparsity (the zero). Without proper scaling, activations can either blow up or vanish.

### 3. Optimization Stability  
Training large Transformers is already finicky; adding discrete constraints can cause exploding/vanishing gradients or dead neurons.

### 4. Compatibility with Existing Transformer Building Blocks  
Components such as RMSNorm, SwiGLU and rotary embeddings were designed with floating point arithmetic in mind.

## Solutions Proposed in BitNet b1.58  

### AbsMean Quantization  
The authors introduce an **absmean** quantizer that first normalizes the weight matrix by its average absolute value \(\gamma\):

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
\]

Then each element is scaled by \(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon}\) and rounded/clipped to \{-1,0,+1\}:

\[
\widetilde{W}= \text{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},-1,1\right)
\]

This operation retains a global scaling factor (\(\gamma\)) that restores magnitude information lost by ternarization while keeping each individual weight strictly ternary.

### Straight‑Through Estimator (STE) for Gradients  
During backpropagation the quantizer is treated as an identity function – i.e., gradients are passed unchanged to the underlying full‑precision “shadow” weights before rounding them again for the next forward pass. This classic STE trick sidesteps nondifferentiability while still nudging the real parameters toward better ternary approximations.

### 8‑Bit Activations & Symmetric Quantization  
Activations are kept at 8 bits and quantized symmetrically around zero (range \([-Q_b,Q_b]\)). By avoiding a zero-point offset the implementation becomes simpler and incurs negligible accuracy loss.

### LLaMA‑Style Architecture Tweaks  
To ensure drop‑in compatibility with open-source ecosystems (HuggingFace, vLLM, llama.cpp), BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA’s RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary positional embeddings and removes bias terms altogether. Removing biases reduces unnecessary parameters that would otherwise waste precious bits.

### Efficient Integer Kernels  
Matrix multiplications turn into *integer addition* operations because multiplying by \(-1\), \(0\), or \(+1\) is equivalent to adding/subtracting or skipping terms. The paper plugs in a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder, showing real speedups on NVIDIA GPUs without any custom hardware.

## Empirical Evidence: Why Training Directly Wins  

- **Perplexity Matching:** Starting at 3 B parameters, BitNet b1.58’s validation perplexity on WikiText2 and C4 equals that of an FP16 LLaMA model trained under identical data (100 B tokens).  
- **Zero‑Shot Task Parity:** On benchmarks like ARC-Easy/Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande and BoolQ, the 3.9 B BitNet model matches or surpasses a 3 B FP16 baseline while using ~3× less memory and being ~2½× faster per token.  
- **Scaling Benefits:** At larger scales (70 B), BitNet b1.58 becomes over **4× faster** than FP16 LLaMA with proportionally lower memory usage—thanks to the linear growth of nn.Linear cost with model size being replaced by cheap integer adds.  
- **Energy Savings:** Modeling energy consumption per matrix multiply shows a **71× reduction** on a 7 nm process when using ternary weights versus FP16 multiplication/addition.

These results illustrate that *the representation learned under ternary constraints is not weaker*; rather it adapts to make effective use of sparsity (the zeros) and sign information (±1). Post‑training quantization cannot retroactively introduce such sparsity patterns because they were never part of the original optimization landscape.

## Broader Implications  

### Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Becomes Viable  
MoE models typically suffer from huge memory footprints due to many expert parameters. With 1.58‑bit weights they can fit on far fewer chips and avoid costly inter‑chip traffic—potentially unlocking trillion‑parameter MoEs on commodity hardware.

### Longer Contexts on Fixed Hardware  
Reducing activations from FP16 to INT8 halves KV cache size, effectively doubling achievable context length for the same GPU memory budget.

### Edge & Mobile Deployment  
A ternary weight matrix fits comfortably into SRAM; integer addition pipelines are already present in many low‑power CPUs/NPUs, opening doors for true on‑device LLM inference.

### New Hardware Design Space  
Since multiplication disappears from the main compute path, future ASICs could be built around ultra‑efficient adders and bitwise selectors—think “LPUs” à la Groq but specialized for ternary matrix products.

## Takeaways  

- **Training directly at low bitwidth isn’t just a compression trick; it’s a fundamentally different learning regime** that lets models discover architectures inherently suited for discrete weights.
- The main technical hurdle—non‑differentiable quantization—is solved effectively with absmean scaling plus an STE.
- Empirical results show *parity or superiority* over full precision models at far lower latency, memory and energy costs.
- This paradigm shift could reshape everything from data center inference economics to on-device personal assistants.

If you’re building the next generation of LLMs and care about real-world deployment cost, looking beyond post‑training quantization toward native low‑bit training like BitNet b1.58 may be the most promising path forward.
