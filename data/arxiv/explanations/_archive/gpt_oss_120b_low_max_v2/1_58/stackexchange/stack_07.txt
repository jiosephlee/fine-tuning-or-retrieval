\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What are the implications of using 8‑bit activations for KV‑cache memory in long‑sequence inference?
Question:
The discussion mentions that 8‑bit activations allow doubling context length for the same resources, and suggests possible further compression to 4 bits. How exactly does reducing activation precision impact KV‑cache size and what are the potential accuracy trade‑offs when compressing KV caches beyond 8 bits?
Answer:
The key to understanding why **8‑bit activations double the usable context length** lies in the way the KV‑cache is stored during inference.  
In a standard FP16 (or BF16) decoder each token generates a *key* and a *value* vector for every transformer layer. If the hidden dimension of a layer is \(d\) and the model has \(L\) layers, the cache for a single token occupies roughly  

$$
\text{size}_{\text{FP16}} \; \approx \; 2 \times L \times d \times 2\;\text{bytes},
$$

the factor 2 coming from the two matrices (K and V) and another factor 2 from the 16‑bit precision.  

When the paper “The Era of 1‑bit LLMs” moves the activations from 16 bits to **8 bits**, every element of K and V now needs only one byte instead of two. Consequently the per‑token cache size is halved:

$$
\text{size}_{\text{8‑bit}} \; \approx \; 2 \times L \times d \times 1\;\text{byte}
            = \frac{1}{2}\,\text{size}_{\text{FP16}} .
$$

Because the total KV‑memory budget on a GPU (or on‑chip SRAM) is fixed, halving the per‑token footprint lets you store **twice as many tokens** before you run out of memory. In practical terms, if a model could only process a context of \(N\) tokens with FP16 activations, switching to 8‑bit activations lets you run with a context of roughly \(2N\) without any other change to the hardware or batch size.

### Going beyond 8 bits

The authors point out that **lossless compression to 4 bits** (or even lower) is theoretically possible for the KV‑cache because the cache only contains quantised activations that are already bounded in range. In principle, moving from 8 bits to 4 bits would quarter the cache size again, giving another ×2 increase in maximal context length.

However, several practical considerations limit how far we can push this:

1. **Quantisation error in K vs. V** – Keys are used for dot‑product attention, while values are summed according to those attention weights. A small perturbation in keys can distort similarity scores more severely than a comparable perturbation in values. Empirically, reducing precision below 8 bits starts to introduce noticeable noise into attention scores, which can degrade perplexity and downstream accuracy.

2. **Dynamic range** – The paper’s activation quantisation scales each token to \([-\!Q_b,\,Q_b]\) with a symmetric integer representation. When \(Q_b\) is reduced from 127 (8‑bit signed) to 7 (3‑bit signed) or 15 (4‑bit signed), the quantiser must clip more aggressively, discarding fine‑grained information in the tails of the activation distribution. This clipping manifests as an increased round‑off error especially for outlier tokens that carry important contextual clues.

3. **Accumulation precision** – Even if K and V are stored at lower bits, attention computation still needs an accumulator with enough precision to avoid overflow. The paper’s implementation uses INT32 accumulators for the matrix‐multiply‑add kernels; if the inputs are too coarse (e.g., <4 bits), overflow or severe saturation can appear unless extra scaling tricks are applied, which adds complexity and latency.

4. **Empirical trade‑off** – The authors report that **8‑bit activations already give negligible loss in perplexity** compared with FP16 baselines for models ≥3 B parameters. They leave “lossless compression to 4 bits or lower” as future work precisely because they have not yet quantified the accuracy impact. Prior work on post‑training quantisation typically observes a modest drop (≈0.3–0.5 % absolute on zero‑shot tasks) when going from 8 bits to 4 bits without fine‑tuning; with fine‑tuning the gap can be reduced but at extra training cost.

### Bottom line

- Reducing activation precision from FP16 to **8 bits halves the per‑token KV memory**, directly allowing a **×2 increase in maximum context length** under a fixed memory budget.
- Further compression (e.g., **4 bits**) would in principle give another factor of two, but it introduces quantisation noise that can hurt attention quality and overall model accuracy unless additional calibration or fine‑tuning steps are taken.
- The paper demonstrates that **8‑bit activations already achieve near‑full‑precision performance**, making them a sweet spot for long‑sequence inference; pushing below this threshold remains an open research direction where accuracy versus memory trade‑offs must be carefully evaluated.
