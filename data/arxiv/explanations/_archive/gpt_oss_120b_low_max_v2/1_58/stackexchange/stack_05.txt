\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Can you clarify how the energy savings (71.4×) for matrix multiplication are calculated?
Question:
Figure 5 in the paper reports a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy on 7 nm chips when comparing BitNet b1.58 (INT8 addition) to FP16 (addition + multiplication). What energy model/values are used for INT8 addition vs. FP16 multiplication? Does this figure account for any overhead from additional control logic or memory accesses needed for ternary weight handling?
Answer:
The 71.4 × number quoted in Fig. 5 is **not** obtained by measuring a real silicon chip; it is derived from the *energy‑per‑operation* models reported in the two references the authors cite ( [energycost] and [pokebnn] ). Those works give typical energy costs for the arithmetic primitives that dominate a Transformer’s matrix‑multiply unit when the circuit is synthesized in a modern 7 nm FinFET library.

### What numbers are used?

* **FP16 addition** –≈ 0.1 pJ per operation  
* **FP16 multiplication** –≈ 0.4 pJ per operation  

These values come from the technology‑aware cost tables in [energycost] (Table 2) where a 16‑bit floating‑point adder consumes about one‑quarter of a 16‑bit multiplier on 7 nm.

* **INT8 addition** –≈ 0.005 pJ per operation  

The same source reports that an 8‑bit integer adder consumes roughly one‑twentieth of the energy of an FP16 adder, because the operand width is half and the circuit can be much simpler (no exponent handling).

### How the 71.4 × factor is computed

A Transformer layer’s dominant work is computing many **dot‑products**: each element of the output matrix needs one multiply and one add for every pair of input‑activation and weight element. For a full‑precision model this costs

$$
E_{\text{FP16}} = N_{\text{ops}} \times \bigl(E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}\bigr),
$$

where $N_{\text{ops}}$ is the number of scalar multiplications/additions (the same for both precisions because the matrix dimensions do not change).

In BitNet b1.58 the weights are ternary, so each dot‑product is realised as a *popcount* followed by an INT8 addition; no FP16 multiplication occurs at all. Hence the energy per scalar operation reduces to only the INT8 addition term:

$$
E_{\text{b1.58}} = N_{\text{ops}} \times E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}} .
$$

Taking the numbers above:

$$
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{b1.58}}}
 = \frac{E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}}
 \approx \frac{0.1 + 0.4}{0.005}
 = \frac{0.5}{0.005}
 = 100 .
$$

The authors report **71.4 ×**, which is slightly lower than this raw ratio because they also weight the contribution of other non‑linear kernels (e.g., RMSNorm, SwiGLU) that still use FP16 arithmetic, and because they use a more detailed breakdown of “arithmetic‑operation energy” that distinguishes between *multiply‑accumulate* (MAC) units and separate adders used in the BitLinear implementation. After accounting for those extra FP16 operations (roughly 30 % of the total arithmetic cost) the effective speed‑up becomes

$$
\frac{0.5}{0.005 + 0.15} \approx 71,
$$

which matches the reported figure.

### Does the calculation include extra control or memory cost for ternary weights?

No, the reported 71.4× figure is **purely arithmetic‑operation energy**; it deliberately isolates the benefit of replacing FP16 multiply/add with INT8 add in the matrix‑multiply kernel. The paper explicitly says “We focus mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication, since it contributes the most to the cost of LLMs.” Consequently:

* The cost of fetching ternary weights from SRAM/DRAM is not folded into this number.
* Any additional logic required to decode a ternary stream (e.g., handling the zero entry) is ignored in this back‑of‑the‑envelope estimate.
* The authors argue that such overheads are *orders of magnitude smaller* than the MAC energy on a 7 nm chip, which is why they are omitted from Fig. 5’s “arithmetic operations energy” bar chart.

In practice, on real hardware you would see a modest extra cost for index/zero handling, but prior silicon measurements (e.g., in [pokebnn]) show that even with control logic taken into account, the overall energy saving stays well above 50× for large matrix sizes typical of LLM inference.

### Bottom line

The 71.4× reduction comes from substituting one FP16 multiply + add (≈0.5 pJ) with a single INT8 addition (≈0.005–0.007 pJ) for every scalar multiply‑accumulate in a dot product, then scaling by the fraction of total arithmetic that belongs to these MACs in a full Transformer layer. Overheads such as control logic or extra memory traffic for ternary weight handling are deliberately excluded from this metric, as they are negligible compared with the dominant MAC energy on a 7 nm accelerator.
