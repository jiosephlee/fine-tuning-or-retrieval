\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What are the computational trade‑offs of mLSTM’s matrix memory on GPU hardware?
Question:
The authors acknowledge that mLSTM’s \(d\times d\) updates are computationally expensive yet parallelizable, mentioning a ≈4× slowdown compared to FlashAttention. I would like to understand which operations dominate runtime (e.g., matrix‑matrix multiply vs. outer‑product accumulation), how memory bandwidth affects scaling with dimension \(d\), and whether there are any known tricks (e.g., low‑rank approximations) that could reduce cost without sacrificing the associative recall properties.
Answer:
The mLSTM cell replaces the scalar cell of a classic LSTM by a full **$d\times d$** “fast‑weight’’ matrix $\mathbf C_t$ and updates it with an outer‑product term
$$
\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^{\!\top}.
$$
Because the recurrence has been rewritten in a **fully parallel** form (see Appendix B), every time step can be executed as a collection of dense linear‑algebra kernels that run independently on the GPU.  
From the implementation details given in § 4 (“Memory and Speed Considerations”) and the limitations section we can infer which operations dominate the wall‑clock time and how they scale with the hidden dimension $d$.

### What actually costs time?

1. **Matrix–matrix multiply for the readout**  
   The hidden state is obtained by a matrix‑vector product
   $$
   \tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \frac{\mathbf C_t\mathbf q_t}{\max\{|\mathbf n_t^{\!\top}\mathbf q_t|,1\}},
   $$
   i.e. a dense multiply of an $d\times d$ matrix with a length‑$d$ vector.  
   This kernel is essentially identical to a GEMV (or GEMM when batched) and therefore has a computational cost of $\mathcal O(d^2)$. On modern GPUs it is bandwidth‑limited only for very small $d$; for the typical model sizes used in the paper ($d=1024$–$2048$) it is compute‑bound.

2. **Outer‑product accumulation for the write**  
   The term $i_t\,\mathbf v_t \mathbf k_t^{\!\top}$ is an outer product, also $\mathcal O(d^2)$. In practice this is implemented as a rank‑1 update (a fused “ger” kernel). Although mathematically simple, it still touches $d^2$ memory locations each step because both $\mathbf v_t$ and $\mathbf k_t$ are dense. The authors note that this update “is computationally expensive through its $d\times d$ matrix memory and $d\times d$ update”, confirming that the outer‑product dominates memory traffic.

3. **Normaliser update**  
   The normaliser state $\mathbf n_t = f_t\,\mathbf n_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf k_t$ is only vector‑level ($\mathcal O(d)$) and therefore negligible.

Consequently, the **dominant runtime components are two dense $d\times d$ operations per time step**: one GEMV/GEMM for reading and one rank‑1 update for writing. Because both kernels can be launched in parallel across timesteps (the whole sequence is materialised as a batch), their cost adds linearly with sequence length but quadratically with hidden size.

### How does memory bandwidth affect scaling?

The GPU must move roughly $2d^2$ floats per token (the matrix *and* its rank‑1 increment). If we denote the memory bandwidth of the device by $B_{\text{GB/s}}$, the achievable throughput $T(d)$ obeys roughly

$$
T(d) \approx \frac{B_{\text{GB/s}}}{8\,d^2},
$$

where 8 bytes is the size of a single‑precision float. As $d$ grows, the required bandwidth quickly exceeds what even high‑end GPUs provide, so performance becomes **bandwidth bound** rather than compute bound beyond $d\simeq 1500$. Empirically the paper reports a ≈4 × slowdown compared with FlashAttention (which also performs two dense matrix multiplications per token but benefits from carefully tiled kernels and half‑precision tricks). The slowdown therefore reflects both extra memory traffic from the outer product and a less aggressive kernel implementation.

### Can we reduce the cost without losing associative recall?

The associative recall property of mLSTM hinges on storing full key–value covariances in $\mathbf C_t$. Several avenues have been explored in related work, but only low‑rank approximations preserve the key theoretical guarantee:

* **Rank‑$r$ factorisation** – replace $\mathbf C_t = \mathbf U_t \mathbf V_t^{\!\top}$ with $\mathbf U,\mathbf V \in \mathbb R^{d\times r}$. The update becomes 
  $$
  \mathbf U_t = f_t\,\mathbf U_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf v_t\,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t^{\!\top}, 
  \qquad 
  \boldsymbol{\alpha}_t = \text{solve}(\mathbf V_{t-1}^{\!\top}\mathbf k_t)
  $$
  which reduces both readout ($\mathbf U_t^\top \mathbf q_t$) and write to $\mathcal O(dr)$. When $r \ll d$ this yields an order‑$d/r$ speedup. However, because compression discards part of the covariance information, retrieval quality degrades unless additional regularisation (e.g., orthogonalisation of columns) is applied.

* **Sparse or blockwise updates** – partition the matrix into independent blocks or enforce a sparsity pattern on $\mathbf C_t$. The outer product then touches only a subset of entries, cutting memory traffic roughly by the sparsity factor. This approach works well when keys are known to occupy distinct subspaces (as in block‑structured attention), but it again compromises maximal separability guarantees that underpin classic BAM theory.

* **Mixed precision / kernel fusion** – adopting half‑precision (FP16/BF16) for both storage and arithmetic cuts bandwidth by 2 × while keeping numerical stability thanks to the exponential gate stabilizer (Eq. 12). Fusion of the read/write kernels into a single CUDA kernel—as done for FlashAttention—also removes intermediate writes to global memory, shaving another factor of ~1.5–2.

The authors themselves remark in § 5 (limitations) that *“the CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not optimized, and therefore the current implementation is about 4 times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba.”* This signals that most of the slowdown is engineering rather than algorithmic; an implementation similar to FlashAttention’s tiled shared‑memory approach would likely bring mLSTM within ≈1.5–2 × of FlashAttention even without low‑rank tricks.

### Bottom line

*The runtime per token is dominated by two dense $d\times d$ operations: a matrix–vector multiplication for retrieval and an outer‑product rank‑1 update for storage.* Memory bandwidth becomes the bottleneck as $d$ grows because each step must read/write roughly $2d^2$ floats. Current kernels give about a 4× slowdown versus FlashAttention, largely because they lack heavyweight tiling, fusion and mixed‑precision optimisations.

If one wishes to keep the associative recall guarantees while reducing cost, low‑rank factorisation of the fast weight matrix is the most principled route; it trades exact covariance storage for an asymptotic speedup proportional to $r/d$, at the price of some degradation in recall fidelity unless compensated by additional regularisation. In practice, major gains are expected from better kernel engineering (tiling, fusion, FP16/BF16), which should narrow the gap to FlashAttention without changing the underlying algorithmic properties.
