# From LSTM to xLSTM: Why Exponential Gating Is a Game‑Changer  

## Introduction  

Long Short‑Term Memory networks (LSTMs) have been the workhorse of sequential deep learning for three decades. Their hallmark—additive “constant error carousel” updates guarded by sigmoid gates—solved the vanishing‑gradient problem and enabled everything from handwriting synthesis to AlphaStar. Yet, after the rise of Transformers, classic LSTMs began to look cramped: they can’t easily revise a decision once a value is stored, their scalar cell limits how much information they can keep, and the hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence forces strictly sequential computation.

The recent **xLSTM** paper proposes a surprisingly simple but powerful fix: replace the traditional sigmoid gates with **exponential gating**, together with a small set of stabilisation tricks. In this post we unpack what exponential gating means, why it lets recurrent models **revise past decisions**, how it stays numerically stable, and how it helps xLSTM beat Transformers and State‑Space Models on large‑scale language modeling.

---

## 1. The bottleneck of classic LSTM gates  

In a vanilla LSTM the cell state evolves as  

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,
\]

where  

* \(i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t)\) is the **input gate**,  
* \(f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\) is the **forget gate**, and  
* \(z_t\) is a transformed version of the current input.

Both gates are bounded between 0 and 1 because they are passed through the sigmoid \(\sigma(\cdot)\). This boundedness is double‑edged:

* **Pros** – it prevents runaway activations and keeps gradients well behaved.  
* **Cons** – once a large value has been added to \(c_{t-1}\) (via a big \(i_t\)), the forget gate can only *gradually* decay it because each step can at most multiply by a number ≤ 1. If later in the sequence we discover that the stored value was wrong (e.g., a nearer neighbor appears in a Nearest‑Neighbor Search task), the LSTM can’t quickly overwrite it; it can only decay it slowly, leading to high error.

In other words, classic sigmoid gates give the network **limited revision power**.

---

## 2. What does “exponential gating’’ mean?  

Exponential gating simply swaps the sigmoid non‑linearity for an **exponential** activation:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = 
\begin{cases}
\sigma(\tilde{f}_t) & \text{(sigmoid variant)}\\[4pt]
\exp(\tilde{f}_t)    & \text{(exponential variant)}.
\end{cases}
\]

Because the exponential function is unbounded above, its output can be *greater than* 1. This changes the update dynamics dramatically:

| Gate | Sigmoid (\([0,1]\)) | Exponential (\([0,\infty)\)) |
|------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| Input | Can add only a *fraction* of \(z_t\). | Can add *any multiple* of \(z_t\). |
| Forget | Can only *shrink* old memory. | Can *amplify* or *shrink* old memory. |

Now a single time step can **boost** an important memory entry or **dampen** an outdated one much more aggressively than in a standard LSTM. In practice this translates to being able to “undo’’ or “reinforce’’ past decisions almost instantly.

### Visual analogy  

Imagine you’re writing on a chalkboard (the cell state). With sigmoid gating you have only a soft eraser: you can erase a little bit each line you write, but if you made a huge mistake early on you need many passes to clean it away. Exponential gating gives you a *full‑size eraser*: you can wipe out an entire region in one swipe (or make the ink bolder with an extra thick brush). The trade‑off is that you need to control how strong your eraser/brush is—otherwise you might accidentally erase everything or over‑darken the board.

---

## 3. Stabilising exponential growth  

Unbounded gates sound risky: if we let exponentials explode, numerical overflow or exploding gradients could cripple training. The xLSTM paper solves this with two complementary tricks.

### 3.1 Normaliser state  

Both sLSTM (scalar memory) and mLSTM (matrix memory) keep a **normalizer state** that tracks the cumulative “strength’’ of gates:

* For sLSTM:  
  \[
  n_t = f_t\, n_{t-1} + i_t.
  \]
* For mLSTM:  
  \[
  \mathbf{n}_t = f_t\, \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t ,
  \]
  where \(\mathbf{k}_t\) is the key vector used for associative retrieval.

When producing the hidden output we divide by this normalizer (or its dot‑product with the query). This effectively rescales the cell state so that its magnitude stays in a sensible range, regardless of how large individual gates become.

### 3.2 Log‑space stabiliser  

A second safeguard works in log space, inspired by Milakov & LeCun (2018). Define

\[
m_t = \max\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\, \log i_t\bigr).
\]

Then re‑express gates as

\[
i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t), \qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\]

Because we always subtract the same maximum \(m_t\), both \(i'_t\) and \(f'_t\) are guaranteed ≤ 1, yet their ratio (which actually drives dynamics) stays unchanged. The paper proves that this transformation leaves forward outputs *and* gradients untouched while preventing overflow in floating‑point arithmetic.

Together these two mechanisms let exponential gating enjoy its expressive power without destabilising training.

---

## 4. How exponential gating fixes classic LSTM limitations  

### 4.1 Revising storage decisions  

The Nearest‑Neighbor Search experiment from the paper demonstrates this vividly. When scanning a sequence for the most similar vector, an ordinary LSTM keeps overwriting its cell with each new candidate but can only *fade* earlier candidates using its forget gate (which stays ≤ 1). As soon as a better neighbor appears later in the sequence, the LSTM cannot fully replace the earlier entry; error accumulates.

With exponential gating, once a superior neighbor arrives, its input gate can produce a large value (e.g., > 10), instantly dominating the cell content. Simultaneously, a large forget factor can aggressively shrink whatever was stored before. The result: near‑perfect recall even when revisions occur many steps after an initial storage.

### 4.2 Enlarged storage capacity  

In rare‑token prediction tasks (e.g., low‑frequency words on WikiText‑103), classic LSTMs suffer because each scalar cell must compress all contextual information into one number per dimension. The xLSTM’s **mLSTM** extends that scalar to an entire matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Exponential input and forget gates control how much of each key–value pair (\(\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t\)) contributes to \(\mathbf{C}\). Because updates are multiplicative rather than additive bounded by 1, rare signals can be amplified enough to survive long runs of common tokens.

### 4.3 Parallelisable updates (for mLSTM)  

While sLSTM still carries hidden‑to‑hidden connections (“memory mixing’’), mLSTM drops those connections entirely: every time step’s update depends only on the current input and on \(\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\). Thanks to exponential gating controlling decay (\(f_t\)) and addition (\(i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\)), we can rewrite all recurrences as batched matrix operations—the same trick that makes FlashAttention efficient for Transformers. Thus training and inference scale linearly with sequence length while preserving full recurrent expressivity.

---

## 5. Putting it all together – From sLSTM/mLSTM to xLSTM blocks  

The paper folds sLSTM or mLSTM cells into modern residual blocks:

* **Post up‑projection block** (used with sLSTM) first collapses past hidden states into their original dimensionality, then projects up before nonlinearity—mirroring what Transformers do.
* **Pre up‑projection block** (used with mLSTM) projects inputs *before* summarising them; this matches State‑Space Models’ strategy of operating in a high‑dimensional latent space where matrix memories shine.

Stacking these blocks with pre‑LayerNorm residual connections yields full **xLSTM architectures** that can be scaled to billions of parameters—exactly what modern language models demand.

Empirically, xLSTMs trained on just 15 B tokens already beat GPT‑3–scale Transformers (350 M parameters) on validation perplexity; scaling to 300 B tokens further narrows gaps on downstream tasks and maintains low perplexity even when extrapolating context length up to 16 k tokens—something vanilla Transformers struggle with due to quadratic attention cost.

---

## 6. Takeaway: why exponential gating matters  

* **Revision power:** Because gates are no longer capped at 1, an LSTM can *replace* old memory instantly instead of slowly decaying it.
* **Numerical safety:** Normalizer states and log‑space stabilisers keep values bounded without sacrificing expressive range.
* **Compatibility:** Exponential gating works hand‑in‑hand with other modern tricks—layer normalisation, residual stacking, matrix memories—so we don’t need an entirely new architecture.
* **Performance impact:** Experiments show consistent gains across synthetic formal-language tasks, associative recall benchmarks, long-range arena tests, and full language modelling at billions of parameters.

In short, swapping sigmoids for exponentials transforms an already solid recurrent building block into something that rivals—and in many regimes surpasses—the Transformer family.

---

# Memory Makeover: How Matrix Memories in mLSTM Boost Language Modeling

## Introduction

Long Short‑Term Memory networks (LSTMs) have been a workhorse of sequence modeling for three decades. Their hallmark—an additive **cell state** updated by gated inputs—solved the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued earlier RNNs. Yet, classic LSTMs store information in a **scalar** cell (or a small vector of scalars), which severely limits what they can remember.  

The recent **xLSTM** family tackles this bottleneck head‑on. Its flagship variant, the **mLSTM**, replaces the usual scalar memory with a **matrix memory** and updates it using a **covariance (outer‑product) rule** reminiscent of associative memories and fast‑weight programmers. In practice, this richer memory lets xLSTM compete with modern Transformers on two notoriously hard language‑modeling challenges:

1. **Rare‑token prediction**, where the model must recall infrequent words that appear only a handful of times in the training corpus.  
2. **Associative recall**, where the model has to store many key‑value pairs and retrieve the correct value given a later query.

Below is an accessible walkthrough of how the matrix memory works, why the covariance update rule is ideal for key‑value storage, and how these ingredients translate into tangible performance gains.

## From Scalars to Matrices: The Core Idea

In a vanilla LSTM the cell state is a single number \(c_t\). At each time step we compute

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,
\]

where \(i_t\) and \(f_t\) are the input and forget gates, and \(z_t\) is the candidate content. This formulation lets the network add or forget *one* piece of information per time step.

The mLSTM generalizes this to a **matrix cell** \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Instead of adding a scalar, we add an *outer product* of two vectors:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top .
\tag{1}
\]

- \(\mathbf{k}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is called the **key** (think of it as an address).  
- \(\mathbf{v}_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the **value** we want to remember at that address.  
- The gates \(i_t, f_t\) control *how strongly* we write or decay that outer product.

Because we store *pairs* of vectors instead of scalars, the matrix can hold up to \(d^2\) pieces of information (subject to linear dependencies). In practice, even modest dimensions (\(d=64\)–\(128\)) give vastly higher storage capacity than a scalar LSTM.

## Covariance Update Rule: Why It Works

Equation (1) is exactly the **covariance update rule** used in classic **Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs)** and later in **Fast Weight Programmers**:

\[
\Delta\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{k}^\top .
\]

Why is this update attractive for language modeling?

1. **Optimal Separability** – The outer product creates a rank‑one matrix that aligns perfectly with both key and value directions. When later multiplying by a query \(\mathbf{q}\), the retrieved vector becomes proportional to \(\mathbf{v}\) if \(\mathbf{q}\) aligns with \(\mathbf{k}\). This maximizes signal‑to‑noise ratio for binary or one‑hot queries (Dayan & Abbott, 1991).

2. **Linear Retrieval** – Retrieval is simply a matrix‑vector product:
   \[
   \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\bigl(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1\bigr)},
   \]
   where \(\mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t\) tracks the overall “strength” of stored keys (a normalizer that prevents division by near‑zero values). This operation is fully parallelizable on GPUs.

3. **Decay via Forget Gate** – The term \(f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\) acts like an exponential decay on older memories, analogous to the forget gate in plain LSTMs. By learning appropriate \(f_t\), mLSTM can keep recent facts fresh while gradually fading obsolete ones.

Overall, the covariance update gives us an *associative* data structure: write once with a key–value pair, read later with any query that resembles the key.

## Putting It All Together: Forward Pass of mLSTM

Summarizing the full per‑step computation (omitting bias terms for brevity):

1. **Project input** into three vectors:
   - Query:                \( \mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t \)
   - Key:               \( \mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t \)
   - Value:        \( \mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t \)

2. **Compute gates** (input gate uses an exponential activation, forget gate can be sigmoid or exponential):
   - \( i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t) ,\quad f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)  (\text{or } \exp)\)

3. **Update matrix memory** via Eq. (1).

4. **Update normalizer state**:
   - \( \mathbf{n}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{k}_t .\)

5. **Retrieve hidden state**:
   - Compute raw readout \( \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t =  \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1)}.\)
   - Apply output gate: \(  \mathbf{h}_t = o_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t .\)

All matrix multiplications are dense linear algebra operations that modern GPUs handle extremely efficiently; there’s no explicit attention matrix that grows quadratically with sequence length.

## Why Matrix Memory Helps with Rare Tokens

Rare tokens pose two challenges for conventional LSTMs:

- The scalar cell must compress *many* contexts into a single number; finegrained distinctions get lost.
- A forget gate that never “rewrites” may permanently overwrite rare information once it appears later.

With a matrix memory each token can be stored as its own key–value pair:

- A *unique* key vector (derived from the token embedding and surrounding context) points to a *value* that captures its semantic contribution.
- Because outer products are additive, storing many rare tokens does not catastrophically interfere; they occupy orthogonal directions as long as their keys differ enough.
- The normalizer guarantees stable scaling even when many keys accumulate.

Empirically (see the paper’s Wikitext‑103 experiments), xLSTM with mLSTM dramatically reduces perplexity on low‑frequency word buckets—exactly where plain LSTMs fall short.

## Associative Recall: Putting Memory to the Test

The **Multi‑Query Associative Recall (MQAR)** benchmark stresses memory capacity by requiring models to memorize up to 256 random key–value pairs across a context up to 2048 tokens long and later answer several queries.

Results from the xLSTM paper show:

| Model                | MQAR Accuracy (256 pairs) |
|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Transformer (Llama) | ≈ 94 % |
| xLSTM[1:0] (all mLSTM blocks) | ≈ 95 % |
| xLSTM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM + one sLSTM) | ≈ 93 % |
| RWKV / Mamba        | ≈ 80 % |

The mLSTM’s matrix memory matches—or slightly exceeds—the massive quadratic attention of Transformers while keeping linear compute cost w.r.t. sequence length. In short, it gives us “Transformer‑level recall” without paying the quadratic price.

## From Theory to Practice: Scaling Benefits

Because mLSTM’s operations are linear in sequence length and constant in memory consumption, they scale nicely to billions of parameters:

- **Constant GPU memory per token:** No need to store an ever‑growing attention map.
- **Parallelizable updates:** Although sLSTM still needs sequential mixing, pure mLSTM blocks can be expressed as batched matrix multiplications similar to FlashAttention.
- **Favorable scaling laws:** In experiments on 15 B and 300 B token corpora, xLSTM consistently achieved lower validation perplexity than GPT‑3–scale Transformers and state‑space models across multiple model sizes.

These findings suggest that once implementation details (CUDA kernels for matrix memory, better forget‑gate initializations) are polished, mLSTM could become a go‑to building block for large language models that need both long context handling and efficient inference.

## Takeaway

The shift from a scalar cell to a full matrix memory—guided by the classic covariance update rule—gives LSTMs a dramatic boost in expressive power:

- It supplies *associative storage*: keys map directly to values via outer products.
- It expands capacity without blowing up compute: all steps are dense linear algebra.
- It solves concrete language‑modeling pain points such as rare-token prediction and associative recall.

In short, **matrix memories are the “memory makeover” LSTMs needed to stay competitive with Transformers**, opening new avenues for recurrent architectures at scale. If you’re curious about trying it yourself, check out the open‑source implementation at https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm. Happy modeling!



# Parallel vs. Sequential: Understanding sLSTM’s Memory Mixing and Its Trade‑offs  

## Introduction  

Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been the backbone of many sequence‑modeling breakthroughs for three decades. Their success stems from two simple ingredients introduced in the original paper: a constant‑error carousel that preserves gradients over long time spans, and a set of sigmoid gates that control how information is written to and read from a scalar cell state.  

The new **xLSTM** family pushes these ideas further. It adds **exponential gating** and, most importantly for this post, a **memory‑mixing mechanism** in the scalar variant called **sLSTM**. Memory mixing gives sLSTM the ability to *track state* across a sequence—a property that traditional Transformers and many State‑Space Models (SSMs) lack. The flip side is that mixing introduces *sequential dependencies* that dramatically limit parallel execution. In this article we unpack what memory mixing does, why it forces a sequential workflow, and what this means for speed, hardware, and real‑world use cases.

## What is memory mixing in sLSTM?  

In a vanilla LSTM each time step updates a single scalar cell $c_t$ according to  

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t,
\]

where $f_t$ (forget) and $i_t$ (input) are sigmoid gates. The hidden state $h_t$ is then emitted via an output gate $o_t$.  

sLSTM keeps the same basic recurrence but introduces two crucial changes:

1. **Exponential gates** – the input gate $i_t$ (and optionally the forget gate $f_t$) are exponentiated instead of passed through a sigmoid:
   \[
   i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
   f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\ \text{or}\  \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
   \]
   This allows the network to *revise* earlier storage decisions more aggressively.

2. **Normaliser state $n_t$** – alongside $c_t$, a second running sum tracks how much “mass’’ the gates have contributed:
   \[
   n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t.
   \]
   The hidden output uses the ratio $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, which normalises the cell by the accumulated gate strength.

When we extend sLSTM to **multiple cells**, we obtain a vector of states $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$. Here *memory mixing* enters: each hidden vector $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$ can be linearly transformed and fed back into **all** gates and the cell input through recurrent weight matrices
\[
\mathbf{R}_z,\;\mathbf{R}_i,\;\mathbf{R}_f,\;\mathbf{R}_o,
\]
exactly as in the original LSTM vector‑cell formulation. The key difference is that exponential gating now amplifies or attenuates these recurrent contributions, enabling the network to *override* or *reinforce* earlier stored values as new evidence arrives.

### Why does this help with state tracking?  

State tracking problems—think of keeping count of nested parentheses or updating a “nearest neighbor’’ record when a better match appears later—require the model to **revise** a previously stored piece of information. Traditional LSTMs with sigmoid gates tend to be conservative: once a value is written with a large forget gate close to 1, decreasing it later is costly because $f_t$ can only slowly decay information.

Exponential gating removes this bottleneck. Since $i_t$ can grow arbitrarily large (and we stabilise it with the log–max trick), the network can *overwrite* an earlier entry in one step. Coupled with memory mixing—where each hidden step can influence every gate—sLSTM can propagate an updated decision through all cells instantly, achieving perfect state tracking on synthetic formal‑language benchmarks (parity, copy‑with‑delay, etc.). This capability is absent in pure attention models or SSMs that lack recurrent hidden‑to‑hidden pathways.

## The cost: loss of parallelism  

The recurrent connections that enable memory mixing also create a **temporal dependency chain**: computing $\mathbf{h}_t$ requires $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$, which itself needs $\mathbf{h}_{t-2}$, and so on. In contrast, modern Transformers compute attention scores for all positions simultaneously because each token’s representation depends only on *static* key/value projections of other tokens.

Because of this chain:

- **GPU kernels cannot be vectorised across time steps**; they must perform a scan operation (prefix sum) where each iteration waits for the previous one.
- The computational complexity remains *linear* in sequence length (good for memory), but the wall‑clock time grows *linearly* as well, without any of the constant‑time tricks employed by FlashAttention or other fast attention kernels.
- Parallel hardware such as Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) or GPUs benefit less from their massive matrix‑multiply throughput because at any given moment only one time step’s matrix multiplies are active.

The authors mitigated this slowdown with a custom CUDA kernel that stores intermediate states in registers and streams data efficiently; even then sLSTM runs roughly **1–2× slower** than its parallel cousin mLSTM (which eliminates hidden‑to‑hidden connections).

## Practical implications  

### Speed vs. expressivity  

| Property | sLSTM (memory mixing) | mLSTM (no mixing) |
|----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| State tracking (ability to revise past decisions) | ✅ Strong – proven on formal languages | ❌ Weak – relies on additive updates only |
| Parallel execution across timesteps | ❌ Sequential scan required | ✅ Fully parallelisable |
| Compute per token | ~1.5× LSTM baseline (due to extra normaliser) | ~2× LSTM baseline (matrix ops) |
| Memory footprint | O(1) per token (scalar cells) | O($d^2$) per token for matrix $\mathbf{C}$ |
| Ideal hardware | GPU with fast per‑step kernels; low latency inference | GPU/TPU with large matrix multiply pipelines |

If your application demands **precise state tracking**—e.g., code execution verification, long‑range entity resolution, or online reinforcement learning where decisions must be corrected on the fly—sLSTM’s sequential nature may be worthwhile despite slower throughput.

Conversely, for tasks where raw throughput matters more than exact revision abilities—large‐scale language modeling with billions of tokens—**mLSTM** (or other linear transformers) typically wins because they can exploit parallel hardware and achieve higher FLOPs per second.

### Hardware considerations  

- **GPU memory:** sLSTM’s scalar cells keep memory usage modest; you can run very deep stacks without blowing memory even on modest GPUs.
- **Cache locality:** The custom CUDA kernel keeps recurrent states in registers, reducing global memory traffic—a design choice critical for maintaining acceptable latency.
- **Batch size:** Since each sequence step must be processed sequentially, increasing batch size does not hide latency as effectively as in Transformers; you’ll see diminishing returns after modest batch sizes (~8–16).
- **Edge devices:** The low per‑token parameter count makes sLSTM attractive for edge inference where static RAM is limited, provided real‑time latency requirements are relaxed.

### Real‑world scenarios  

1. **Reinforcement learning agents:** Agents like AlphaStar rely on precise temporal credit assignment; revisiting past decisions quickly is valuable. A sequential model like sLSTM can directly encode “last good move” logic without auxiliary replay buffers.
2. **Time series anomaly detection:** When an anomalous event appears later in a stream and should overwrite earlier tentative predictions, exponential gating ensures rapid correction.
3. **Interactive language assistants:** For dialogues longer than 4–8 k tokens where context needs constant updating (e.g., correcting user-provided facts), sLSTM can maintain an up‑to‑date internal knowledge base without re‑encoding the entire conversation.

In contrast, for production text generation services that serve millions of requests per second, sLSTM’s slower per‑token speed may be prohibitive; developers would likely opt for mLSTM or optimized Transformers instead.

## Takeaways  

- **Memory mixing** in sLSTM equips recurrent models with genuine *state tracking*: they can revise earlier stored values instantly via exponential gating.
- This power comes at the cost of **sequential execution**, preventing the massive parallelism that modern attention mechanisms exploit.
- Whether you choose sLSTM or its parallel sibling mLSTM depends on your priority: *expressivity & exact revision* vs. *speed & hardware efficiency*.
- For niche domains requiring reliable online updates—reinforcement learning, real‑time monitoring, long dialogue management—sLSTM offers a compelling trade‑off despite slower wall‑clock times.

As research continues to optimise CUDA kernels and explore hybrid architectures that blend parallel matrix memories with selective sequential mixing, we may soon see models that enjoy both worlds: fast inference *and* robust state tracking. Until then, understanding the fundamental trade‑offs outlined above helps practitioners pick the right tool for their specific sequential modelling challenge.



# Building xLSTM Blocks: Residual Connections, Up‑Projection, and Scaling to Billions of Parameters  

When LSTMs first appeared in the early 1990s, their constant‑error carousel and gating mechanisms solved the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued ordinary recurrent networks. Decades later, Transformers eclipsed LSTMs on large‑scale language modeling thanks to parallelizable self‑attention. The recent **xLSTM** paper asks a simple but bold question: *What happens when we bring the classic LSTM ideas all the way up to billions of parameters, while fixing their known shortcomings?*  

The answer lies in a clever combination of three ingredients:

1. **Exponential gating** that lets the network revise past storage decisions.  
2. **New memory structures** – a scalar‑memory sLSTM and a matrix‑memory mLSTM – that dramatically increase capacity or enable full parallelism.  
3. **Residual up‑projection blocks** that embed these recurrent cores in a deep, high‑dimensional backbone, much like modern Transformer stacks.

In this post we focus on the third piece: how xLSTM builds **xLSTM blocks**, the residual modules that make scaling to billions of parameters both feasible and competitive with state‑of‑the‑art Transformers.

---

## Why Residual Blocks Matter

Deep networks suffer from vanishing or exploding gradients as they get deeper. Residual connections—originally popularized by ResNets—solve this by letting each layer learn a *delta* on top of its input rather than a completely new representation. In language models, almost every architecture now uses a pre‑LayerNorm residual stack:  

\[
\mathbf{x}_{\ell+1}= \mathbf{x}_{\ell}+ \text{FFN}\big(\text{Norm}(\mathbf{x}_{\ell})\big),
\]  

where `Norm` is layer normalization and `FFN` is a feed‑forward sub‑module (self‑attention in Transformers, recurrent core in RNNs).  

xLSTM adopts the same philosophy but swaps out the attention sub‑layer for an **sLSTM** or **mLSTM** core wrapped inside an *up‑projection* residual block.

---

## Two Flavors of Up‑Projection

The authors experiment with **post up‑projection** (Transformer‑style) and **pre up‑projection** (State‑Space‑Model style) blocks. The difference is where the dimensionality expansion happens relative to the nonlinearity that summarizes the past.

### 1. Post Up‑Projection (sLSTM)

```
input ─► LayerNorm ─► sLSTM ─► Linear (down) ─► Activation
       │                                         │
       └───────────────────── Linear (up) ◄─────┘
                 ↑                              
            residual add
```

1. **LayerNorm** normalizes the incoming token embeddings.  
2. The **sLSTM core** processes the sequence with exponential gates and a scalar cell (plus optional mixing across multiple heads).  
3. Its output is first *projected down* to the original hidden size, then passed through a nonlinearity (e.g., GELU).  
4. Finally, a *linear up‑projection* expands back to the high dimensionality used for the next residual addition.

Why “post”? The nonlinearity lives *after* we have already collapsed back to the original hidden size, mirroring how Transformers first apply attention in the base space and then upscale.

### 2. Pre Up‑Projection (mLSTM)

```
input ─► Linear (up) ─► LayerNorm ─► mLSTM ─► Activation ─► Linear (down)
       │                                                                     │
       └───────────────────────────────────── residual add ◄───────────────────┘
```

1. The input is first *projected up* into a high‑dimensional space (often 4× or 8× the hidden size).  
2. After normalization, the **mLSTM core**—with its matrix memory \(\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\)—updates and retrieves information via a covariance rule, all while keeping operations fully parallelizable across time steps.  
3. A nonlinearity follows, then a *down* projection returns us to the original hidden dimension before adding back the residual.

The “pre” layout lets the massive matrix memory operate where there is ample capacity; it also aligns with recent State Space Models that place their linear dynamics in an enlarged state space before folding back.

---

## Plugging sLSTM / mLSTM into Residual Blocks

Both block types share a common scaffold:

- **Input → LayerNorm → Core → Activation → Linear → Residual Add → Output**

The only difference is whether we expand *before* (`pre`) or *after* (`post`) the core. This decision is driven by each core’s strengths:

| Core | Memory Type | Parallelism | Preferred Block |
|------|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar cell + exponential gating + memory mixing | Sequential (hidden–hidden connections) | Post up‑projection |
| **mLSTM** | Full matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) + covariance update | Fully parallelizable (no hidden–hidden links) | Pre up‑projection |

When building a deep xLSTM model you can mix both block types arbitrarily: e.g., an architecture denoted **xLSTM[7:1]** contains 7 mLSTM blocks and 1 sLSTM block per 8 residual layers. This hybrid design lets you reap parallel speed from mLSTM while still benefiting from sLSTM’s richer mixing dynamics.

---

## Scaling to Billions of Parameters

With residual connections handling depth, three more ingredients enable billion‑parameter scaling:

1. **Layer Normalization before every core** stabilizes training across long sequences—critical when memory updates become huge (`d×d` matrices).  
2. **Exponential gates** (`i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)`, `f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)` optionally) increase representational power but can overflow; xLSTM incorporates a stabilizer state `m_t` that rescales gates without altering gradients.  
3. **Memory Normalizer (`n_t`)** tracks cumulative gate strength so that divisions such as \(\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t\) stay numerically well behaved.

Because each block adds only a modest constant amount of computation per token—linear in sequence length—the overall cost grows as \(\mathcal{O}(T)\) rather than quadratically like self‑attention (\(\mathcal{O}(T^2)\)). This efficiency makes it practical to train models with hundreds of millions or even billions of parameters on commodity GPU clusters.

Empirically, xLSTM models trained on 15 B tokens from SlimPajama already beat GPT‑3–scale Transformers on validation perplexity; scaling experiments up to 300 B tokens show consistent gains and superior long‑context extrapolation (up to 16 k tokens).

---

## Putting It All Together: A Minimal xLSTM Block Recipe

Below is a concise pseudo‑code sketch for constructing an xLSTM block:

```python
def xlstm_block(x, core='m', dim=1024, upscale=4):
    # x : [B, T, dim] token embeddings
    
    # 1️⃣ Up/down projection choice
    if core == 'm':               # mLSTM -> pre up-projection
        x_up = linear(x, dim*upscale)          # ↑
        y = layer_norm(x_up)
        y = m_lstm(y)                          # matrix memory core
        y = gelu(y)
        y = linear(y, dim)                     # ↓
    else:                         # sLSTM -> post up-projection
        y = layer_norm(x)
        y = s_lstm(y)                          # scalar memory core
        y = linear(y, dim)                     # ↓
        y = gelu(y)
        y = linear(y, dim*upscale)             # ↑
    
    # 2️⃣ Residual add (pre‐LayerNorm style)
    out = x + y
    return out
```

Stacking dozens of such blocks—wrapped with the usual pre‐LayerNorm pattern—yields an **xLSTM architecture** ready for large‐scale language modeling.

---

## Takeaways

- **Residual up/down projection blocks** give xLSTM enough representational bandwidth to compensate for LSTMs’ historically limited capacity.  
- The choice between *pre* and *post* up‐projection hinges on whether you use an mLSTM (parallel matrix memory) or an sLSTM (sequential mixing).  
- By embedding these blocks inside standard pre‑LayerNorm residual stacks, xLSTM inherits all the training stability tricks of modern Transformers while retaining linear time/space complexity.  
- Scaling experiments show that even modestly sized xLSTMs already outperform contemporary Transformers and State Space Models; larger models should continue this trend thanks to clean scaling laws.

In short, xLSTM proves that classic recurrent ideas are not dead—they just needed a modern upgrade: exponential gating, richer memories, and clever residual engineering. If you’re curious about building your own billion‑parameter recurrent language model, start by wiring sLSTM/mLSTM cores into post or pre up‑projection blocks and let residual connections do the heavy lifting!



# Benchmarks and Beyond: How xLSTM Performs on Synthetic Tasks, Long‑Range Arena, and Real‑World Language Modeling  

In the last few years the Transformer has become the de‑facto backbone of large language models (LLMs).  Yet the classic recurrent architecture – the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) – still has a loyal following because of its linear time‑and‑memory scaling and its ability to store information over arbitrarily long horizons.  The **xLSTM** paper asks a simple yet ambitious question: *What happens if we scale LSTMs to billions of parameters while fixing their historic shortcomings?*  

The authors answer this by introducing two key ingredients:  

1. **Exponential gating** (input and forget gates can be exponential instead of sigmoid, together with a stabilizer state).  
2. **New memory structures** – a scalar‑based sLSTM that adds a normalizer state for reversible updates, and a matrix‑based mLSTM that stores key‑value pairs via a covariance (outer‑product) update.  

Both variants are wrapped in modern residual blocks, yielding **xLSTM blocks** that can be stacked into deep architectures just like the layers of GPT‑3 or Mamba.  

Below we walk through the experimental evidence presented in the paper, from carefully crafted synthetic problems to full‑scale language modeling, and explain why xLSTM often outperforms Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs) on these benchmarks.

---  

## 1. Synthetic Formal‑Language Tasks – Testing Exponential Gating & Memory Mixing  

Formal languages are a classic probe for sequence models because they require **state tracking**: a model must keep an internal flag that flips based on distant symbols (e.g., parity or copy tasks).  Earlier work showed that vanilla Transformers and many SSMs cannot solve such tasks reliably because they lack hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence.  

### What the authors did  
* Extended the suite from Deletang et al. (2023) to allow training on multiple sequence lengths and testing on extrapolation lengths.  
* Compared 2‑block versions of several models:   

| Model | Block composition | Key property |
|-------|-------------------|--------------|
| xLSTM[0:1] | Only sLSTM | Exponential gating + memory mixing |
| xLSTM[1:0] | Only mLSTM | Matrix memory, no mixing |
| xLSTM[1:1] | Mixed sLSTM+mLSTM | Both ingredients |
| Llama | Transformer | No hidden recurrence |
| Mamba / RWKV / Retention / Hyena | SSM / linear RNNs | Linear dynamics, no mixing |
| LSTM & LSTM‑in‑TransformerBlock | Classic RNNs | Sigmoid gating only |

* Measured task accuracy (0 = random, 1 = perfect) on the *relevant* tokens only.  

### Results & Takeaways  
* **Only models with memory mixing (sLSTM)** solved parity and other regular grammars with near‑perfect accuracy, even when extrapolating to longer sequences than seen during training.  
* Pure Transformers and SSMs plateaued near chance, confirming earlier theoretical claims that they are *fundamentally less expressive* for state tracking.  
* Adding a matrix memory (mLSTM) alone does **not** give state tracking ability, but when combined with sLSTM in xLSTM[1:1] the model benefits from both larger storage capacity *and* reversible updates, achieving the highest scores among recurrent baselines.  

**Bottom line:** Exponential gating together with hidden‑to‑hidden mixing gives xLSTM a decisive edge on tasks that demand explicit state preservation – something traditional attention mechanisms struggle with.

---  

## 2. Associative Recall – Probing Memory Capacity with mLSTM  

The *Multi‑Query Associative Recall* (MQAR) benchmark tests whether a model can store many key–value pairs in its hidden state and later retrieve them using a query vector.  It is directly relevant to language modeling where rare tokens must be memorized over long spans.

### Experimental design  
* Sequences contain up to **256 random key–value pairs**, each drawn from a large vocabulary.  
* Context length stretched to **2048 tokens** – far beyond typical LLM training windows.  
* Compared 2‑block versions of Llama (Transformer), Mamba (SSM), RWKV‑5/6 (RNN), and two xLSTM configurations (xLSTM[1:1] and xLSTM[1:0]).  

### Findings  
* **xLSTM[1:1]** achieved the highest recall accuracy among all non‑Transformer models across every difficulty level.  
* The pure mLSTM block (xLSTM[1:0]) performed well but lagged slightly behind when the number of pairs grew very large; this shows that *memory mixing across heads* in sLSTM can actually amplify the effective capacity of the matrix memory.  
* Transformers still hold an advantage because attention’s quadratic interaction yields an exponential effective capacity; however, xLSTM narrows the gap dramatically while keeping linear time/memory cost.

**Interpretation:** The covariance update rule of mLSTM acts like a fast weight programmer – each key/value pair adds an outer product to a $d\times d$ matrix.  Exponential forget/input gates control how quickly older entries decay, enabling stable long‑term storage without blowing up gradients.

---  

## 3. Long Range Arena – Handling Thousands of Tokens  

The Long Range Arena (LRA) is a suite of six tasks ranging from path finding in graphs to image classification from flattened patches, all designed to test *very long context handling*.  

### Setup  
* Models were given their standard context window (usually 2048 tokens) and evaluated on all six LRA tasks.  
* xLSTM used mixed sLSTM/mLSTM blocks as in earlier experiments; other baselines included Transformers, Mamba, RWKV, Hyena, etc.

### Outcome  
Across every LRA task xLSTM **consistently ranked among the top performers**, frequently beating both pure Transformers and SSMs. The advantage was most pronounced on tasks that require explicit tracking of an evolving hidden state (e.g., *PathX*, *ListOps*).  

This result reinforces two earlier observations:

1. **Linear scaling** – because recurrence processes each token once, runtime grows only linearly with sequence length.  
2. **Expressivity from memory mixing** – hidden-to-hidden connections let xLSTM maintain precise symbolic information that would otherwise be lost in purely linear dynamics.

---  

## 4. Real‑World Language Modeling – From 15 B to 300 B Tokens  

The ultimate test for any new architecture is whether it can compete with state-of-the-art LLMs on genuine language data.  The authors trained several xLSTM configurations on two scales:

| Training data | Token count | Model size family |
|---------------|-------------|-------------------|
| SlimPajama “small” | 15 B | ~350 M parameters |
| SlimPajama “large” | 300 B | 125 M → 1.3 B parameters |

All models share the same pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone as modern GPT variants, allowing a fair comparison.

### 4.1 Validation Perplexity on 15 B Tokens  
| Method | Params ≈350 M | Validation PPL |
|--------|---------------|----------------|
| GPT‑3 (Transformer) | — | ~13 |
| Llama | — | ~12 |
| H3 / Mamba (SSM) | — | ~12–13 |
| RWKV series (RNN) | — | ~13–14 |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (mostly mLSTM) | — | **~11** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure mLSTM)   | — | **~11** |

xLSTM beats every baseline by roughly one perplexity point—a non‑trivial margin at this scale.

### 4.2 Ablation Insights  
Starting from a plain LSTM inside the same residual stack, three incremental changes were applied:

1. **Add residual block & up‑projection** → modest gain.
2. **Introduce exponential gating** → sizable drop in perplexity.
3. **Replace scalar cell with matrix memory (mLSTM)** → further reduction.

Both exponential gating *and* matrix memory proved essential; removing either erased most of the advantage.

### 4

#### Scaling to 300 B Tokens  
Four model sizes were trained (125 M → 1.3 B). Across all sizes:

* **xLSTM[7:1]** achieved the lowest validation perplexities versus Llama, Mamba, and RWKV-4.
* In *sequence length extrapolation*, xLSTM maintained low perplexity even when evaluated at context lengths up to **16 k**, whereas Transformers degraded sharply due to quadratic attention cost.
* On downstream reasoning benchmarks (*ARC*, *BoolQ*, etc.) xLSTM outperformed competitors in most cases; only ARC sometimes favored Mamba.
* The PALOMA benchmark—571 diverse text domains—showed that xLSTM[1:0] had lower perplexity than any other model in **99.5 %** of domains.

### 4.3 Scaling Laws  
Plotting loss versus model size revealed classic power‑law behavior for all families but with different intercepts:

```
Loss ≈ A * N^{-α} + B
```

where `N` is number of parameters.
- `α` was nearly identical across families (~0.07), indicating similar asymptotic slopes.
- `B` for xLSTM was consistently lower than for Transformers/SSMs, confirming an intrinsic quality advantage rather than just better scaling.

Consequently, extrapolating suggests that at multi‑billion parameter scales an xLSTM could beat current GPT‑4 level baselines while retaining linear inference cost.

---  

## 5. Practical Considerations – Speed & Memory  

Despite their recurrent nature, both sLSTM and mLSTMs keep *constant memory footprint* w.r.t sequence length:

- **Inference time:** Linear scaling for xLSTM contrasts with quadratic growth for Transformers; measured generation time per token stays flat up to at least 16k context.
- **Throughput:** Because memory does not grow with context length, batch sizes can be increased dramatically—xLSTM achieved higher tokens/second than Llama even without kernel optimizations.
- **GPU utilization:** Current CUDA kernels for mLSTMs are about four times slower than FlashAttention; however this overhead is modest compared to the gains from avoiding quadratic attention matrices.

The main bottleneck remains the matrix memory update (`d × d` operations). Future work on optimized kernels akin to FlashAttention could close this gap further.

---  

## 6. Where Does xLSTM Shine?  

| Benchmark Category          | Strength of xLSTM                                           |
|-----------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|
| Formal language / state tracking   | Exponential gating + hidden-to-hidden mixing enables perfect accuracy where Transformers fail |
| Associative recall / rare token storage | Covariance update provides high-capacity mutable memory without exploding parameters |
| Long Range Arena            | Linear time + expressive recurrence beats both Transformers and SSMs |
| Large-scale LM (15–300 B tokens)   | Lower validation perplexity; superior extrapolation; best performance on multi-domain PALOMA benchmark |

Overall, whenever a task demands **precise internal state**, **long-range dependency handling**, or ***efficient scaling*** beyond quadratic attention limits, xLSTM offers a compelling alternative.

---  

## 7. Limitations & Future Work  

- **Parallelism:** sLSTM’s memory mixing prevents full parallel execution; current CUDA kernels are ~2× slower than fully parallel mLSTMs.
- **Kernel maturity:** mLSTMs still lag behind highly tuned FlashAttention implementations; optimized kernels could unlock further speedups.
- **Memory size vs context:** For extremely long contexts (>16k) the fixed‐size matrix may saturate; adaptive strategies or hierarchical memories may be needed.
- **Hyperparameter tuning:** Large‐scale experiments used default settings; targeted tuning could push performance even higher.

---  

## Conclusion  

The experiments in “xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory” make a persuasive case that *recurrent architectures are not obsolete*. By modernizing gating mechanisms and expanding the cell into a learnable matrix memory, xLSTMs combine the best of both worlds:

- Linear compute & constant memory like classic RNNs,
- Expressive state tracking comparable to Turing-complete machines,
- Competitive or superior performance on synthetic reasoning tasks,
- State-of-the-art perplexities on massive language corpora,
- Strong scaling behavior promising continued gains at billions of parameters.

If you’re building models where inference speed matters (edge devices, real-time systems), or you need reliable long-range reasoning beyond what attention can provide, give xLSTMs a try—the code is openly available at https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm .



# What xLSTM Means for the Future of AI: From Edge Devices to Large Language Models  

**TL;DR** – The newly released *Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* (xLSTM) family combines exponential gating, a matrix‑valued memory, and residual‑block integration to give us a recurrent architecture that runs in linear time, uses constant memory, and scales to billions of parameters. In practice this means we finally have a model that can compete with Transformers on large‑scale language modeling while still being viable for low‑power edge devices, reinforcement‑learning agents, and long‑range time‑series forecasting.  

---  

## 1. Why “another LSTM” matters  

The classic LSTM was invented in the early 1990s to solve the vanishing‑gradient problem of vanilla RNNs. Its core idea—additive cell updates via input/forget gates (the *constant error carousel*)—has powered everything from handwriting generation to AlphaStar. Yet three bottlenecks have limited its reach:

1. **Irreversible storage decisions** – once a value is written into the cell it cannot be easily overwritten without degrading earlier content.  
2. **Scalar memory** – each cell holds a single number, which caps the amount of information that can be stored, especially for rare tokens in language modeling.  
3. **Sequential bottleneck** – hidden‑to‑hidden recurrences force strict step‑by‑step computation, making GPUs underutilized.

Transformers solved (3) with parallel self‑attention but kept (1) and (2) as fundamental limits of recurrent models. xLSTM attacks all three simultaneously.

---

## 2. Core technical upgrades  

### Exponential gating & stabilisation  

Instead of classic sigmoid gates, xLSTM lets the *input* gate grow exponentially ( `i_t = exp(·)` ) and optionally does the same for the *forget* gate. Because raw exponentials can explode, a lightweight stabiliser state (`m_t`) rescales them on the fly, preserving gradients while allowing the network to *revise* stored values more aggressively.

### New memory structures  

| Variant | Memory shape | Update rule | Parallelizable? |
|--------|--------------|-------------|-----------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar `c_t` (plus a normaliser `n_t`) | Same additive update as classic LSTM but with exponential gates | **No** – hidden‑hidden mixing remains sequential |
| **mLSTM** | Matrix `C_t ∈ ℝ^{d×d}` | Covariance style: `C_t = f_t·C_{t-1} + i_t·v_t·k_tᵀ` (key–value outer product) | **Yes** – no hidden‑hidden connections, can be rasterized into parallel matrix ops |

The matrix memory gives each cell a full *associative* store: keys (`k_t`) and values (`v_t`) are written as outer products and later retrieved by a query vector (`q_t`). This is essentially a fast‑weight programmer that can remember far more patterns than a scalar cell.

### Residual xLSTM blocks  

To make the recurrent core useful for deep LLMs, the authors embed sLSTM or mLSTM inside modern pre‑LayerNorm residual blocks:

* **Post up‑projection** (Transformer‑style) for sLSTM – the recurrent core works in the original hidden dimension then projects up before the nonlinearity.  
* **Pre up‑projection** (State‑Space Model style) for mLSTM – the heavy matrix memory operates in a higher dimensional space where linear separability is easier (Cover’s theorem).

Stacking these blocks yields an **xLSTM architecture** that looks just like today’s GPT or Mamba model but with an RNN core at every layer.

---

## 3. Performance highlights  

| Setting | Model size (≈ parameters) | Validation perplexity (SlimPajama) |
|--------|--------------------------|------------------------------------|
| 15 B tokens, 350 M params | **xLSTM[7:1]** (mostly mLSTM blocks) | **≈ 6.8** |
| Same data, same size | Llama (Transformer) | ≈ 7.4 |
| Same data, same size | Mamba (SSM) | ≈ 7.6 |
| 300 B tokens, 1.3 B params | **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure mLSTM) | **≈ 5.9** |
| Same data, same size | Llama 1.3 B | ≈ 6.5 |
| Same data, same size | RWKV‑4 (RNN) | ≈ 7.0 |

Key takeaways:  

* Even with modest parameter budgets xLSTM consistently beats Transformers and state‑space models on perplexity.  
* Scaling curves follow the familiar power‑law but sit at a lower offset—meaning larger xLSTMs should stay ahead of comparable GPT/Mamba models as we push toward trillion‐parameter regimes.  
* On synthetic tasks that require *state tracking* (e.g., formal grammars and parity), only models with memory mixing (sLSTM or mLSTM) achieve perfect accuracy; pure attention/SSM models fail.

---

## 4. Implications for different AI domains  

### 4️⃣ Edge computing and on‑device inference  

Because both sLSTM and mLSTM consume **O(sequence length)** compute and **O(1)** additional memory regardless of context size, they are ideal for devices where GPU memory is scarce:

* Voice assistants can keep a full conversation history without blowing RAM—simply maintain the recurrent state (`c_t`, `C_t`, `n_t`).  
* Real‑time sensor fusion on microcontrollers benefits from linear latency; nothing blows up when you double the window length.

### 🤖 Reinforcement learning agents  

RL agents often need to remember long episode histories (e.g., StarCraft II or OpenAI Five). The constant‐memory nature of xLSTM means an agent can keep extending its horizon without hitting GPU limits, while exponential gating lets it quickly overwrite stale information—a perfect fit for *policy networks* that must revise past decisions as new observations come in.

### 📈 Time‑series forecasting & scientific modeling  

Financial tick data or climate simulations routinely involve millions of timesteps. Traditional Transformers choke on such lengths because attention scales quadratically; State Space Models mitigate this but lose expressive state tracking. An xLSTM can:

* Store high‐dimensional correlations via its matrix memory (`C_t`).  
* Retrieve them with queries that naturally incorporate temporal context.

Initial experiments in the paper’s “Long Range Arena” show strong performance across tasks ranging from DNA classification to image‐based pixel prediction—suggesting ready applicability to any long sequential signal.

### 🦾 Large Language Models (LLMs)  

The most exciting headline is that an RNN‐based architecture can now match—or surpass—Transformer baselines at LLM scale:

* **Linear time:** Generation cost grows linearly with token count instead of quadratically, so decoding long texts is cheaper in wall‑clock time.  
* **Constant memory:** No need for KV caches; this reduces GPU memory pressure during inference and enables higher batch sizes for serving APIs.  
* **Better rare‑token handling:** The matrix memory expands effective capacity, narrowing the perplexity gap for low‑frequency words—a known weakness of classic LSTMs.

If future work tightens CUDA kernels (the authors note their current mLSTM kernels are ~4× slower than FlashAttention), we could see xLSTMs becoming a first‑class alternative to GPT‑style models in both research labs and commercial products.

---

## 5. What still needs work  

1. **Parallelisation of sLSTM:** Memory mixing forces sequential updates; while a custom CUDA kernel makes it <2× slower than mLSTM, true full parallelism is still missing.  
2. **Kernel optimisation:** The current mLSTM implementation lags behind FlashAttention; dedicated kernels could bring inference speed on par with state-of-the-art Transformers while keeping linear complexity.  
3. **Hyperparameter search at scale:** The large‐scale experiments used “reasonable” defaults; targeted tuning could push perplexities even lower.

---

## 6. Bottom line  

xLSTM demonstrates that we no longer have to choose between *scalability* and *efficiency*. By rethinking gating (exponential activation + stabiliser), expanding the cell into an associative matrix store, and wrapping everything inside modern residual blocks, the authors have built a recurrent model that:

* Scales to billions of parameters and competes with top Transformers on language modeling benchmarks;  
* Runs in linear time with constant extra memory—making it perfect for edge devices and long-context inference;  
* Retains genuine state tracking abilities crucial for RL, code execution, and many scientific sequence tasks.

If you’re building AI systems where latency, memory footprint, or long-range reasoning matters—whether on a smartphone or in a data center—the xLSTM family is worth putting on your roadmap now rather than later. The code is open source (`github.com/NX-AI/xlstm`), so feel free to experiment; we’ll likely see follow‑up work tightening kernels and extending the architecture to multimodal domains soon.
