\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 3: **Limitations of Traditional LSTMs for Large‑Scale Language Modelling**

The Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) network was introduced in the early 1990s to overcome the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued ordinary recurrent neural networks. Its core mechanism—an additive “constant‑error carousel’’ together with three multiplicative gates (input, forget, and output)—remains a powerful tool for sequence modelling. Nevertheless, when one attempts to scale LSTMs to the billions of parameters that modern large language models (LLMs) employ, three fundamental shortcomings become evident. These limitations not only degrade performance on standard language‑modelling benchmarks but also impede LSTMs from exploiting architectural advances that have made transformers and state‑space models (SSMs) dominant at scale.

Below we dissect each shortcoming in depth, illustrating the failure modes with concrete tasks introduced in the xLSTM paper, and we contrast them with the capabilities of contemporary transformer‑based and SSM‑based language models.

---

### 1. Inability to Revise Stored Information  
#### The Nearest‑Neighbor Search Illustration  

A recurrent network processes a sequence one token at a time, updating its hidden state and cell state at each step. In the classic LSTM formulation the cell update is

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t\,c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t\,z_t ,
\qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t\,\psi(c_t),
\]

where \(i_t\), \(f_t\), and \(o_t\) are sigmoid‑activated gates and \(z_t\) is the candidate cell input. The forget gate \(f_t\) can only *decay* past information; it cannot actively *overwrite* a previously stored value when a more relevant piece of evidence arrives later in the sequence.

The paper highlights this rigidity with a **nearest‑neighbor search** task. A reference vector is provided, then a long sequence of candidate vectors is streamed in. The goal is to output, at the final time step, the value attached to the vector that is **most similar** to the reference. An optimal recurrent system would be able to **revise** its provisional answer whenever it encounters a nearer neighbor later in the stream.

Empirical results (Figure 1 of the paper) show that a vanilla LSTM’s mean‑squared error remains high even after many timesteps: once an initial candidate has been stored, subsequent more similar vectors cannot replace it because the forget gate decays past information only multiplicatively, and the input gate cannot compensate for a previously stored large magnitude entry without destabilising training. Consequently, the LSTM “locks in’’ an early decision and fails to correct it—a critical flaw for any task requiring dynamic memory revision.

**Why this matters for language modelling:**  
In natural language, entities may be reintroduced or clarified later (e.g., pronoun resolution, coreference chains). A model that cannot reliably revise earlier representations will struggle to maintain consistent context over long passages, leading to poorer next‑token predictions.

---

### 2. Restricted Storage Capacity – Scalar Cell States vs. Rich Representations  
#### Rare‑Token Prediction as a Stress Test  

The canonical LSTM stores a **single scalar** per memory cell (or—after vectorisation—a fixed‑size vector of scalars). Each scalar must compress all information relevant to its position in the sequence into a one‑dimensional quantity before being passed forward through additive updates. This compression imposes a hard bound on how much distinct information can be retained simultaneously.

The xLSTM paper demonstrates this bottleneck by measuring perplexity on **rare tokens** within the Wikitext‑103 benchmark. Tokens are grouped by their frequency; while common tokens are predicted well by all models, **rare tokens**—those that appear only a handful of times across the entire corpus—exhibit dramatically higher perplexities for standard LSTMs than for transformers or SSMs.

The intuition behind this phenomenon is twofold:

1. **Limited representational bandwidth:** A scalar cell cannot encode subtle nuances such as morphological features, long‑range dependencies, or co‑occurrence statistics needed to disambiguate infrequent words.
2. **Interference between updates:** Because each update adds a scaled version of \(z_t\) onto an existing scalar, repeated contributions from many frequent words can drown out the signal from rare words, especially when the forget gate is close to one (the default setting for preserving long‑range information).

In contrast, transformer layers maintain **high‑dimensional token embeddings** throughout all layers; each token’s representation remains a vector of size 1024 or larger, allowing fine‑grained distinctions even for low‑frequency items. State‑space models also propagate high‑dimensional hidden states linearly across timesteps, preserving capacity.

Thus, purely scalar memory places an intrinsic ceiling on how well an LSTM can learn to predict rare events—a crucial capability for any LLM intended to handle diverse vocabularies.

---

### 3. Sequential Bottleneck from Hidden‑Hidden Connections  
#### The Hidden‑Hidden Recurrence Constraint  

Beyond gating and storage size, classic LSTMs feature **hidden‑hidden recurrent connections**: the hidden state \(h_{t-1}\) feeds directly into every gate and into the candidate cell input \(z_t\). Formally,

\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + r_i\,h_{t-1} + b_i,
\]
and similarly for \(\tilde{f}_t,\tilde{o}_t,\tilde{z}_t\).

These dependencies create an explicit **sequential dependency graph**: computing any quantity at time \(t\) requires knowledge of \(h_{t-1}\), which itself depends on \(h_{t-2}\), and so forth back to \(t=0\). Consequently:

* **No parallelisation across timesteps:** Unlike self‑attention where all token pairs can be processed simultaneously (once key/value projections are computed), an LSTM must be unrolled step‑by‑step during both training and inference.
* **Quadratic wall‑clock cost when scaling depth:** Even though each timestep has linear computational complexity in \(d\), processing long sequences (e.g., 16 k tokens) yields wall time proportional to sequence length—a severe bottleneck when billions of tokens are required for pretraining.
* **Limited throughput on modern hardware:** GPUs excel at large matrix multiplications performed in parallel; recurrent loops restrict utilisation of these cores because intermediate results cannot be overlapped across positions.

Transformers bypass this limitation entirely by employing self‑attention that is *parallelizable* across positions after an initial projection stage. Recent linearised attention variants (e.g., Performer, FlashAttention) further reduce memory footprints while preserving parallelism. State–space models such as S4 or Mamba achieve linear × constant memory overhead because they formulate recurrence as a convolution with precomputed kernels that can be evaluated using fast Fourier transforms or other parallel operators.

Therefore, traditional hidden–hidden recurrence makes classic LSTMs unsuitable for large‑scale language modelling where both sequence length and model width must be maximised simultaneously.

---

### 4. Comparative Perspective: Transformers and State‑Space Models  

| Aspect | Classic LSTM | Transformer (self‑attention) | State‑Space Model (e.g., S4/Mamba) |
|--------|--------------|-----------------------------|------------------------------------|
| **Memory update** | Scalar additive update (`c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t`) | Full token embedding participates in pairwise attention → effectively high‑dimensional associative memory | Linear dynamical system with discretised convolution kernel; hidden state evolves linearly |
| **Revision ability** | Forget gate only decays past values → limited revision | Attention weights can be recomputed at each layer allowing arbitrary reweighting of past tokens | Linear dynamics permit exact recombination of past contributions |
| **Storage capacity** | One scalar per cell (or fixed small vector) | High dimensional embeddings preserved throughout depth | Hidden dimension often hundreds → richer storage than scalar |
| **Parallelism over time** | Strict sequential dependence via `h_{t-1}` | Fully parallel after projection; attention computed across all positions simultaneously | Convolutional formulation enables parallel evaluation using FFT or other kernels |
| **Scalability** | Wall-clock grows linearly with sequence length; GPU utilisation low for long contexts | Initially quadratic memory/compute but mitigated by linearised/efficient attention variants; highly parallelisable | Linear compute & constant memory w.r.t sequence length; strong hardware utilisation |

These contrasts make clear why transformers have become dominant: they combine high capacity with full parallelism. However, they also introduce new issues—quadratic attention cost (addressed by linearised variants) and lack of explicit “persistent’’ cell state that some reasoning tasks benefit from. The xLSTM paper therefore proposes *exponential gating* and *matrix memories* precisely to bridge these gaps while retaining recurrence’s strengths.

---

### Summary  

Traditional LSTMs falter at scale for three intertwined reasons:

1. **Irreversible storage decisions:** The forget gate’s multiplicative decay cannot replace previously stored information without destabilising training; this hinders tasks like nearest–neighbor search where later evidence should supersede earlier guesses.
2. **Scalar compression bottleneck:** Storing information as scalars forces severe dimensionality reduction, dramatically hurting rare-token prediction where fine-grained distinctions matter.
3. **Sequential hidden–hidden recurrence:** The dependence on `h_{t-1}` forces strict stepwise computation, preventing parallel processing across timesteps and limiting throughput on modern GPUs.

When contrasted with transformers and state‐space models—both of which provide richer representations and full temporal parallelism—the limitations become starkly apparent. Overcoming them motivates the design choices explored later in the xLSTM framework: exponential gating that permits aggressive revision without overflow, matrix‐valued memories that expand storage capacity beyond scalars, and elimination of hidden–hidden connections (in mLSTM) to enable parallel evaluation. These innovations aim to bring recurrent architectures back into contention with today’s leading large language models.
