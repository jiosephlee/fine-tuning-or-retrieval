\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

# Motivation and Challenges of Long Context in Large Language Models

Large language models (LLMs) have transformed natural‑language processing by achieving remarkable performance across a wide spectrum of tasks, from open‑ended generation to structured reasoning.  Modern LLMs such as LLaMA‑2, GPT‑4, and Mistral are typically trained with a **context window** of only a few thousand tokens (e.g., 4 K–8 K).  This limitation is not a design choice but rather an artifact of the way positional information is encoded and the practical constraints of training.  In this chapter we explore why extending that window is essential, what kinds of applications would benefit from **millions** of tokens, how previous work has attempted to push the boundary, and finally what fundamental obstacles have kept earlier approaches capped at roughly 128 K tokens.  Understanding these motivations and challenges provides the necessary backdrop for the subsequent discussion of LongRoPE, a method that finally reaches a 2 048 K (2 M) token context.

## Success of Modern LLMs and the 4 K–4096 Token Bottleneck

The tremendous success of contemporary LLMs stems from three intertwined ingredients: massive model capacities (billions of parameters), pre‑training on vast text corpora, and the **Transformer** architecture that enables efficient parallel computation.  A critical component of a Transformer is the **positional embedding**, which injects information about the order of tokens because the self‑attention mechanism itself is permutation‑invariant.  Most recent models employ **Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)**, where each token’s hidden vector is multiplied by a rotation matrix parameterised by sinusoidal frequencies.  Formally, for a token at position \(n\) the RoPE vector (simplified) is

\[
\bigl[\cos (n\theta_0),\sin (n\theta_0),\cos (n\theta_1),\sin (n\theta_1),\dots,\cos (n\theta_{d/2-1}),\sin (n\theta_{d/2-1}) \bigr],
\]

where \(d\) is the hidden dimension and \(\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}\) with a default base \(\theta=10{,}000\).  
During pre‑training, models only ever see positions up to a fixed maximum length \(L\) (e.g., \(L=4096\)).  Consequently, the rotation angles for indices beyond \(L\) are never learned; when such *new* positions appear at inference time, they are **catastrophic**—the model receives signals it has never been exposed to, leading to severe degradation in predictive quality.

Because RoPE’s frequencies are fixed once training concludes, simply feeding longer inputs results in **out‑of‑distribution** positional values.  The model’s attention scores become unreliable, and the final language‑modeling loss spikes dramatically. This phenomenon explains why many state‑of‑the‑art systems still report a hard ceiling at roughly four thousand tokens despite having billions of parameters.

## Applications Requiring Millions of Tokens

### In‑Context Learning with Massive Exemplars

In‑context learning allows an LLM to adapt its behaviour at inference time by conditioning on demonstrations within the prompt.  When the number of examples grows beyond a few hundred, their total token count easily exceeds tens or hundreds of thousands.  Tasks such as multi‑document question answering, large‑scale reasoning over codebases, or chain‑of‑thought prompting with many intermediate steps all benefit from being able to *simultaneously* embed all exemplars.  A context window limited to four thousand tokens forces practitioners to truncate or selectively sample demonstrations, often discarding useful information.

### Autoregressive Agents with Long Histories

LLM agents that interact with environments—whether for planning, tool use, or autonomous dialogue—must retain an unbounded history of observations, actions, and thoughts.  As an episode proceeds, the accumulated transcript can easily surpass one hundred thousand tokens.  Without a sufficiently large context window, agents must rely on external summarisation or state resetting mechanisms that risk losing essential context or introducing latency.

### Retrieval‑Augmented Generation and Document‐Scale Summarisation

Retrieval‑augmented generation pipelines commonly retrieve dozens of long documents (each many thousands of tokens) before feeding them to an LLM for synthesis or summarisation.  When dealing with legal contracts, scientific articles, or books, it is not uncommon for the total retrieved text to amount to **millions** of tokens.  A model restricted to a few thousand tokens cannot process all relevant material at once; it must iteratively chunk and stitch outputs together—a process that can propagate errors and increase inference time.

These scenarios highlight a concrete demand: **LLMs need to read and reason over sequences that are orders of magnitude longer than what current positional embeddings support**.

## Survey of Prior Extensions

Researchers have pursued three broad families of techniques to enlarge the effective context window:

1. **Fine‑Tuning on Longer Sequences**  
   By continuing training on texts longer than the original \(L\), models can learn representations for new positions. Works such as LongLoRA, PI (Positional Interpolation), and recent scaling efforts have demonstrated extensions up to ≈128 K tokens when fine‑tuned on appropriately long corpora. However, this approach suffers from two practical impediments: obtaining sufficiently long training data (most public corpora contain few samples exceeding even 100 K tokens) and the massive compute required for billions of parameters on sequences hundreds of times longer than during pre‑training.

2. **Positional Interpolation / Extrapolation**  
   Instead of retraining from scratch, several methods modify RoPE directly at inference time:
   - **Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)** rescales every rotation angle by the extension ratio \(s = L'/L\). While simple, PI compresses all positions uniformly into a smaller angular space (“crowded” positions), leading to rapid perplexity degradation beyond modest extensions.
   - **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)–based adjustments** distribute interpolation pressure unevenly across dimensions: low‑frequency dimensions receive more scaling while high‑frequency dimensions receive less. This mitigates crowding but historically caps extensions at about \(4\times\).
   - **YaRN** partitions RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands and applies either extrapolation (\(\lambda=1\)), linear interpolation (\(\lambda=s\)), or NTK scaling depending on band membership. YaRN improves over PI but still requires human‑designed grouping thresholds; its performance varies across model families and does not reliably reach beyond ≈8–16 K without fine‑tuning.

3. **Retrieval‑Based or External Memory Augmentation**  
   Methods like LongLLaMA or retrieval-augmented generation attach an external datastore that stores prior context and fetches relevant fragments during inference. While powerful for certain retrieval tasks, these approaches alter model architecture or require additional indexing infrastructure; they also cannot directly address tasks demanding *continuous* attention over an entire document (e.g., long narrative generation).

Collectively these strategies have pushed context windows from the original few thousand up to roughly one hundred thousand tokens but have not broken through to the million‑token regime.

## Three Fundamental Challenges Limiting Extension Beyond ≈128 K Tokens

Despite impressive engineering advances, extending RoPE consistently beyond roughly \(128\,\text{k}\) tokens encounters three intertwined obstacles identified in recent analyses:

### 1. Catastrophic Values from Untrained New Position Indices

When extending from a base length \(L=4\,\text{k}\) to any length \(L'\gg L\), more than ninety percent of position indices become *novel*—they have never been seen during pre‑training.  The resulting rotary angles lie far outside the distribution that shaped the model's attention matrices and feedforward layers. Consequently,
- Gradient updates during fine‑tuning become unstable because backpropagation operates on embeddings that are effectively random.
- In non-fine‑tuned scenarios, attention scores become overly diffuse or highly peaked in undesirable ways, causing rapid loss spikes.

The problem is fundamentally about **information entropy**: RoPE’s sinusoidal encoding carries most discriminative power in its higher frequencies (lower dimensions). Blindly interpolating all dimensions destroys this structure.

### 2. Scarcity of Extremely Long Texts and Prohibitive Compute

Fine‑tuning a model on sequences longer than \(256\,\text{k}\) requires source documents that themselves exceed those lengths. Public datasets rarely contain such examples; most books or articles fall well below one hundred thousand tokens after tokenisation.  
Even if such data were available,
- The quadratic memory footprint of self‑attention scales as \(\mathcal{O}(L'^2)\); at \(L'=1\,\text{M}\) this becomes untenable on contemporary GPUs without sophisticated memory‐reduction tricks.
- Training for even a few hundred steps would demand thousands of GPU hours—a cost prohibitive for most research labs.

Thus “just train longer” is not viable beyond moderate extensions.

### 3. Degradation on Short Contexts After Extreme Extension

A naïve extension strategy forces high‐frequency RoPE dimensions within the original short window (e.g., first 4 K positions) into an artificially compressed angular region because those dimensions now have to cover an exponentially larger range up to \(L'\).  
The consequences are:
- Diminished ability to distinguish adjacent tokens in short windows → higher perplexity even when evaluating on tasks designed for ≤4 K contexts.
- Attention patterns become noisier: query/key pairs that previously aligned well now interfere because their relative positional encodings lose granularity.

Prior work observed this “crowding” effect especially with large extension ratios (\(>8\times\)), limiting practical usefulness: users care about *both* very long inputs *and* retaining strong performance on conventional benchmarks.

---

Together these three challenges form a triad: (**catastrophic new positions**, **data/computation scarcity**, **short-window degradation**).  Any method aspiring to reach multi‑million token windows must address each simultaneously—preserving informative high‐frequency RoPE components while providing sensible initialisations for fine‐tuning; limiting reliance on ultra‐long training data; and rebalancing embeddings so short contexts remain expressive.

The remainder of this textbook will describe how **LongRoPE** systematically tackles these obstacles by exploiting two previously overlooked non‑uniformities—variation across RoPE dimensions *and* across token positions—and by employing an efficient evolutionary search coupled with a progressive fine‑tuning pipeline that only needs modest computational resources yet yields a robust 2 048 K token context window.

# Background: Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)

Large language models (LLMs) such as LLaMA‑2 and Mistral rely on self‑attention to capture dependencies among tokens.  Since the attention mechanism itself is permutation‑invariant, the model must be supplied with an explicit encoding of each token’s position in the input sequence.  The prevailing choice for many recent LLMs is **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)**, a formulation that intertwines positional information directly with the query–key vectors used in the attention computation.  This chapter presents a complete derivation of RoPE, interprets it as a Fourier‑style encoding of token positions, explains how it influences attention scores, and discusses why naïve extrapolation beyond the lengths observed during pre‑training leads to severe degradation.

---

## 1. Derivation of RoPE Angles and the Frequency Base  \(\theta\)

RoPE originates from the observation that rotating a vector in a two‑dimensional subspace can be expressed by a simple sinusoidal matrix.  Consider an embedding dimension \(d\) that is even; we can view each pair of consecutive dimensions \((2i,2i+1)\) as forming a planar subspace.  For a token located at absolute position index \(n\) (counted from the beginning of the sequence), we define a rotation angle for the \(i^{\text{th}}\) subspace:

\[
\alpha_i(n) \;=\; n \,\theta_i ,
\qquad
\theta_i \;=\;\theta^{-2i/d},
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\theta\) is a fixed scalar base (the default value in most open‑source LLMs is \(\theta=10{,}000\)).  Substituting \(\theta_i\) into \(\alpha_i(n)\) gives

\[
\alpha_i(n)= n\,\theta^{-2i/d}.
\]

Because each subspace rotates independently, the complete RoPE vector for token \(n\) can be written as a concatenation of cosine–sine pairs:

\[
\underbrace{
\bigl[\,\cos (\alpha_0(n)),\, \sin (\alpha_0(n)),\, 
      \cos (\alpha_1(n)),\, \sin (\alpha_1(n)),\, 
      \dots,\,
      \cos (\alpha_{d/2-1}(n)),\, \sin (\alpha_{d/2-1}(n))
\bigr]
}_{d \text{ entries}} .
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) is precisely the expression shown in the paper’s Eq. (1).  The factor \(\theta^{-2i/d}\) creates **geometrically decreasing frequencies** across dimensions: low‑indexed dimensions correspond to high frequencies (fast oscillation), while high‑indexed dimensions correspond to low frequencies (slow oscillation).  This hierarchy of frequencies is essential for representing both fine‑grained relative offsets (captured by high frequencies) and coarse‑grained absolute positions (captured by low frequencies).

---

## 2. RoPE as a Fourier Encoding of Token Positions

The sinusoidal pattern in Eq. (2) can be interpreted as a **Fourier basis** over integer positions.  Each dimension \(i\) stores the real and imaginary parts of a complex exponential:

\[
e^{j\, n\, \theta_i} 
   = \cos (n\theta_i) + j\,\sin (n\theta_i).
\]

Thus the RoPE embedding for position \(n\) is equivalent to sampling a set of complex exponentials at frequencies \(\{\theta_i\}\).  In signal‑processing terminology, this provides a *band‑limited* representation: higher‑frequency components resolve small relative distances between nearby tokens, while lower‑frequency components encode larger spans.

When two tokens at positions \(n_a\) and \(n_b\) interact through attention, their RoPE vectors are multiplied element‑wise with the query and key vectors.  Because multiplication by a complex exponential corresponds to **phase shift**, the dot product between query and key acquires an extra phase term that depends only on the relative offset:

\[
q_{n_a}^{\!\top}k_{n_b}
   = (q^{(0)}_{n_a})^{\!\top}k^{(0)}_{n_b}
     \;\times
     \underbrace{\exp\!\bigl(j\, (n_a-n_b)\,\theta_i\bigr)}_{\text{rotary factor per dimension }i}.
\]

Consequently, RoPE injects *relative* position information directly into the attention score without requiring an extra bias term.  The model can learn to use these phases to modulate attention based on distance, while still retaining absolute cues via the underlying frequency hierarchy.

---

## 3. Effect of RoPE on Attention Scores

Recall that in scaled dot‑product attention we compute

\[
A_{ab}
   = \frac{ q_{n_a}^{\!\top}k_{n_b}}{\sqrt{d_k}},
   \qquad
   w_{ab}= {\rm softmax}_b(A_{ab}),
\tag{3}
\]

where \(w_{ab}\) is the weight assigned to token \(b\) when producing token \(a\).  Substituting the rotary-modified query–key product yields

| Component | Role |
|-----------|------|
| \(\cos((n_a-n_b)\theta_i),\, \sin((n_a-n_b)\theta_i)\) | Encodes *relative* offset in each subspace |
| High‑frequency subspaces (\(i$ small$) | Sensitive to small offsets; enable precise discrimination of neighboring tokens |
| Low‑frequency subspaces (\(i$ large$) | Vary slowly with distance; provide a smooth bias favoring nearer tokens but also allowing long‑range influence |

Because each subspace contributes additively in the dot product, attention scores become a weighted sum over sinusoidal functions of distance.  Empirically this yields a **soft locality bias**: nearby tokens receive higher scores due to constructive interference in high‑frequency components, while distant tokens still retain non‑negligible contribution through low-frequency components.

The paper emphasizes that this balance is delicate: if we modify the underlying frequencies arbitrarily—e.g., when extending to unseen positions—some dimensions may become overly compressed (high frequencies crowd together) or overly stretched (low frequencies lose resolution), breaking the learned locality pattern and causing loss of performance.

---

## 4. Limitations When Extrapolating Beyond the Trained Length

During pre‑training, an LLM observes only positions up to its original context window \(L\) (e.g., \(L=4096\) for LLaMA‑2).  The rotary angles for positions greater than \(L-1\) are never seen; thus their corresponding embeddings constitute **out‑of‑distribution inputs** for all downstream linear layers that consume queries and keys.

Two concrete problems arise when we naïvely evaluate an LLM at length \(L'>L\):

1. **Catastrophic values from untrained angles**  
   The sinusoidal terms continue to oscillate indefinitely, but because no gradient updates ever shaped the downstream weights for those angles, they may lead to extreme activation magnitudes or destructive interference patterns.  The paper reports that more than 90 % of positions become “new” when extending from 4 k to over 1 M tokens; this sudden influx of unseen angles makes fine‑tuning extremely unstable.

2. **Crowding of positional information**  
   Linear interpolation methods (the original Position Interpolation or PI approach) shrink all rotation angles by a uniform factor equal to the extension ratio \(s = L'/L\).  In effect each angle becomes \(\alpha_i'(n)= n/(s\, \beta^i)\), where \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\).  As \(s\) grows large, high‑frequency dimensions collapse: many neighbouring positions map to almost identical cosine–sine pairs, eliminating discriminative power for short distances.  Consequently attention cannot reliably distinguish tokens that are close together, leading to rapid degradation of perplexity when evaluating long contexts.

The paper further notes that more sophisticated schemes such as NTK‑based interpolation or YaRN attempt to redistribute interpolation pressure across dimensions—scaling lower dimensions less and higher dimensions more—to alleviate crowding.  Nonetheless, these methods still treat each dimension group uniformly and ignore finer-grained *non‑uniformities* both across dimensions and across token indices themselves.

Empirical analysis in Section 3 of the paper uncovers two crucial observations:

- **Dimension‐wise non‑uniformity:** Optimal rescaling factors \(\lambda_i\) differ substantially across RoPE dimensions; simply applying one global scaling factor wastes information.
- **Position‐wise non‐uniformity:** Early tokens (the first \(\hat n\) positions) benefit from *less* interpolation because they receive disproportionately large attention scores during inference; preserving their original angles improves performance.

When these two forms of non‑uniformity are incorporated into a learned interpolation schedule—via an evolutionary search over per‑dimension factors and an early‐token threshold—the resulting *LongRoPE* initialization dramatically reduces information loss, enabling eightfold extensions without any fine‑tuning and serving as a robust starting point for further progressive extensions up to two million tokens.

--- 

In summary, Rotary Positional Embedding encodes token order through frequency‐scaled sinusoidal rotations that intertwine directly with self‑attention. Its success hinges on a carefully balanced spectrum of high‐ and low‐frequency components learned under a finite context length. Extending beyond that length naïvely creates catastrophic unseen angles and overwhelms the model’s locality bias. Recognizing and exploiting hidden non‑uniformities across both dimensions and token indices—precisely what LongRoPE does—provides an effective remedy and opens the door to multi‑million token contexts while preserving performance on short windows.

# Existing Positional Interpolation Techniques  

In order to make a pre‑trained large language model (LLM) capable of processing sequences that are longer than the context length it was originally trained on, most recent works modify the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) rather than redesigning the whole architecture.  RoPE encodes a token’s absolute position \(n\) as a set of sinusoidal angles that are applied as rotations to the query and key vectors in every attention head.  The canonical RoPE formulation (see Eq. \ref{eq:rope}) uses a base frequency \(\theta\) (default \(10\,000\)) and a dimension‑wise decay \(\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}\), where \(d\) is the model’s hidden size.  When an LLM that was trained on a maximum length \(L\) must attend to positions beyond \(L\), the extra indices have never been seen during pre‑training, which produces “catastrophic” values in the attention scores and makes fine‑tuning difficult.

The most common remedy is to **interpolate** the RoPE angles so that positions beyond \(L\) are mapped back into the trained range.  Below we review three families of interpolation strategies that have appeared in the literature, explain their underlying assumptions, and discuss why they tend to stall once the extension ratio exceeds roughly eight‑fold.

---

## Linear Position Interpolation (PI)

Linear Position Interpolation, abbreviated **PI**, is the simplest possible scheme.  Suppose we wish to expand an original context window of length \(L\) to a target length \(L' = sL\).  PI uniformly rescales every rotary angle by the factor \( \lambda = s \).  Formally, each component of Eq. \ref{eq:rope} is replaced by  

\[
\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda}\Bigr), \qquad 
\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda}\Bigr), \qquad 
\text{with }\lambda = s,
\]

so that a token at position \(n\) in the extended sequence uses the same effective angle that a token at position \(n/s\) would have used in the original model.  

The intuition behind PI is straightforward: by compressing the entire position axis, every token still receives a distinct rotation, preserving the injectivity of the positional map.  However, this compression makes nearby positions **crowded**—the angular separation between consecutive tokens shrinks by a factor of \(s\).  In an attention computation that relies on inner products of rotated query–key pairs, such crowding reduces discriminability, especially for high‑frequency dimensions where small angular differences already encode fine‑grained order information.  

Empirically, PI can be applied without any further fine‑tuning because it does not change any learned parameters; it merely reinterprets the existing RoPE matrix.  Nonetheless, experiments reported in the LongRoPE paper show that PI’s perplexity begins to deteriorate sharply once \(s > 2\).  At an eight‑fold extension (e.g., from 4 k to 32 k tokens), PI already incurs a large loss of information, and performance collapses for larger ratios.

---

## Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)–Based Scaling

The **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)** perspective treats RoPE as a Fourier feature map and studies how interpolation affects the kernel induced by attention.  Building on NTK theory \cite{jacot2018neural,tancik2020fourier}, researchers observed that uniformly scaling all dimensions (as PI does) is sub‑optimal because each RoPE dimension corresponds to a different frequency band.  Lower‑indexed dimensions encode **high‑frequency** (rapidly varying) components, while higher‑indexed dimensions encode **low‑frequency** (slowly varying) components.

Consequently, NTK‑based methods propose **dimension‑dependent** rescaling factors  

\[
\lambda_i = s^{\,i},
\]

where index \(i = 0,\dots ,d/2-1\) enumerates the RoPE dimensions in order of decreasing frequency.  The idea is that high‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) small) should be interpolated **less aggressively** (smaller \(\lambda_i\)), preserving their ability to distinguish close positions; conversely, low‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) large) can tolerate more interpolation because they already vary slowly with position.

Mathematically, Eq.\,\ref{eq:unified_rope} becomes  

\[
\bigl[\cos(n/\lambda_0),\, \sin(n/\lambda_0),\, 
      \cos(n/\lambda_1),\, \sin(n/\lambda_1),\, \dots\bigr],
\]
with \(\lambda_i = s^{\,i}\).  

When applied without fine‑tuning, NTK scaling can extend context windows up to roughly four times longer than PI before perplexity diverges.  The improvement stems from reduced crowding in high‑frequency subspaces; however, for extension ratios beyond \(4\times\), even low‑frequency dimensions become overly compressed and information loss becomes severe.  Moreover, NTK scaling assumes a **monotonic** relationship between frequency and required interpolation strength, which may not hold for all transformer architectures or training regimes.

---

## YaRN – Three‑Group Piecewise Scaling

The **YaRN** method (\textbf{Y}et another \textbf{R}otary \textbf{N}onlinear scaling) refines NTK’s dimensional treatment by dividing RoPE dimensions into three empirically chosen groups:

1. **High‑frequency group** – receives **extrapolation**, i.e., \(\lambda=1\).  
   No scaling is applied; angles are left unchanged so that early tokens retain maximal discriminability.

2. **Mid‑frequency group** – follows an NTK‑style scaling where \(\lambda_i = s^{\,i}\).

3. **Low‑frequency group** – undergoes **linear interpolation**, i.e., \(\lambda = s\), exactly as PI does.

The grouping is typically based on fixed frequency thresholds derived from prior experiments on specific models (e.g., LLaMA2).  By assigning each dimension to the most suitable interpolation regime, YaRN aims to balance two opposing forces: avoiding overcrowding for high frequencies while still allowing enough compression for low frequencies so that long sequences can be represented within the same bounded angular space.

YaRN demonstrates solid performance up to about eight times longer context windows when applied without any further training.  Nevertheless, it still suffers from three practical limitations:

* The group boundaries are hand‑crafted; they may be suboptimal for newer or differently trained LLMs.
* Even with piecewise scaling, extending beyond roughly eightfold leads to noticeable perplexity spikes because all three groups eventually encounter severe crowding.
* When a target length vastly exceeds the original one (e.g., moving from 4 k to several hundred thousand tokens), YaRN’s fixed rescaling ratios cannot compensate for the sheer number of newly introduced positions.

---

## Empirical Performance Limits

All three families share a common empirical ceiling: they work well only when the extension ratio \(s\) stays modest (roughly \(s \leq 8\)).  The LongRoPE paper quantifies these limits by evaluating perplexity on long‑document benchmarks such as PG19 and Proof‑pile:

| Method | Extension Ratio | Perplexity Trend |
|--------|----------------|------------------|
| PI     | ≤ 2×           | Stable           |
| PI     | > 2×           | Rapid increase   |
| NTK    | ≤ 4×           | Moderate growth |
| NTK    | > 4×           | Sharp degradation|
| YaRN   | ≤ 8×           | Competitive      |
| YaRN   | > 8×           | Spike after ~7 k tokens |

The degradation manifests as “crowded” rotary angles: as more positions are forced into a fixed angular interval, distinct tokens become indistinguishable in attention scores, leading to higher next‑token prediction loss (perplexity).  Moreover, when extending beyond eightfold, more than ninety percent of positional indices become completely novel relative to pretraining data; this out‑of‑distribution shift hampers any fine‑tuning process unless special initialization strategies are employed.

These observations motivate the central contribution of LongRoPE: rather than using uniform or hand‑crafted piecewise scalings, it discovers **non‑uniform**, dimension‑and token‑position–aware rescaling factors via an evolutionary search.  By allowing each RoPE dimension its own optimal factor and optionally leaving early tokens untouched (\(\hat n>0\)), LongRoPE achieves an eightfold gain *without* fine‑tuning and provides a robust initialization for subsequent short fine‑tuning phases—ultimately enabling extensions up to two million tokens.

In summary, linear PI offers simplicity but fails quickly under strong compression; NTK introduces frequency awareness but remains limited to moderate extensions; YaRN adds a third regime but still relies on manually chosen groups and cannot scale past ≈8× without substantial loss.  Recognizing these shortcomings sets the stage for more adaptive approaches such as LongRoPE that explicitly model and exploit the inherent non‑uniformities of RoPE across both dimensions and token positions.

# Empirical Discovery of Positional Non‑Uniformities  

In this chapter we detail the systematic investigations that uncovered two previously hidden sources of non‑uniformity in rotary positional embeddings (RoPE). By probing how different RoPE dimensions and different token positions react to interpolation, we arrived at a more faithful way to stretch an LLM’s context window. The experiments described here form the empirical backbone of the LongRoPE method: they show that naïve, uniform scaling of rotation angles is sub‑optimal, that preserving the original encoding for the earliest tokens is beneficial, and that the discovered non‑uniform scaling improves both fine‑tuned and non‑fine‑tuned models. Throughout, we focus on intuition and the logical steps that led from raw perplexity measurements to concrete design choices for extending context windows to millions of tokens.

## Experimental Setup: Evolutionary Search Guided by Perplexity  

The central hypothesis driving our analysis was that the optimal way to interpolate RoPE should be **data‑driven**, not fixed by hand‑crafted formulas. To test this hypothesis we framed the search for suitable interpolation factors as an **evolutionary optimization** problem, where each candidate solution specifies a set of per‑dimension rescaling factors \(\lambda_i\) (one for each RoPE dimension) and a token‑position threshold \(\hat n\).  

### Datasets and Evaluation Metric  

Two large, heterogenous corpora were used as test beds:

1. **PG19** – a collection of long literary works (~19 GB). Because these texts are naturally organized into books with coherent narrative structure, they stress a model’s ability to retain distant information over many thousands of tokens.  
2. **Proof‑Pile** – a subset of the Pile focused on scientific proofs and technical documentation. This dataset evaluates how well a model maintains precise factual consistency across long spans.

For each candidate interpolation, we measured **perplexity** on five randomly selected validation samples from PG19 and on a comparable number of samples from Proof‑Pile. Perplexity, denoted \(\mathcal{L}\), quantifies the model’s next‑token prediction loss; lower values indicate that the positional embedding better preserves the information learned during pre‑training.

### Evolutionary Search Procedure  

Each individual in the population encoded a vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = (\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1})\) together with a scalar \(\hat n\). The search space was deliberately large:

- \(\lambda_i\) could take any value in \([1.0,\; s\times 1.25]\) with step size 0.01, where \(s = L'/L\) is the desired extension ratio (e.g., \(s=8\) for an 8× stretch).  
- \(\hat n\) was drawn from a small discrete set \(\{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}\), representing how many initial token positions would be left untouched.

Because evaluating perplexity for every possible combination is infeasible (the combinatorial space quickly exceeds \(10^{160}\) configurations for modest extensions), we employed an **evolutionary algorithm** with two efficiency tricks:

1. **Seeded Initialization** – The initial population contained three well‑known baselines (linear Position Interpolation (PI), NTK‑based scaling, and YaRN grouping). The remaining individuals were random perturbations of these seeds.
2. **Monotonicity Constraint** – We required \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) for all dimensions \(i\). This leverages insights from Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory: higher‑frequency dimensions (low \(i\)) should be scaled less aggressively than lower‑frequency ones. Imposing monotonicity pruned away many implausible candidates and reduced the number of perplexity evaluations dramatically.

After each generation we kept the top‑\(k\) individuals (lowest perplexities), applied mutation (random adjustments respecting monotonicity) and crossover (swapping sub‑vectors between parents), and repeated for a fixed number of iterations (typically 40). The final output of this process was a **searched RoPE scaling schedule**—a concrete set of \(\lambda_i\) values together with an optimal \(\hat n\).

## Finding 1 – Dimension‑wise Optimal Scaling Factors \(\lambda_i\)

The first surprise emerged when examining the best‐performing individuals: **the optimal rescale factors varied considerably across RoPE dimensions**. Recall that each dimension encodes a sinusoid with frequency \(\beta^{-i}\); lower indices correspond to high frequencies (rapid oscillations), while higher indices correspond to low frequencies (slow oscillations). If we denote the original rotary angle at position \(n\) as \(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\), then after interpolation it becomes

\[
\cos\Bigl( \tfrac{1}{\lambda_i} \tfrac{n}{\beta^i} \Bigr), 
\qquad 
\sin\Bigl( \tfrac{1}{\lambda_i} \tfrac{n}{\beta^i} \Bigr).
\]

A uniform choice such as \(\lambda_i = s\) (the linear PI rule) forces *all* dimensions to stretch or compress by the same factor. Our evolutionary search consistently discovered schedules where:

- **High‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) small)** received **small rescaling** (\(\lambda_i \approx 1.0 - 1.3\)). This preserves fine‑grained relative positions among nearby tokens.
- **Mid‑frequency dimensions** adopted intermediate values (\(\lambda_i \approx 2 - 4\)).
- **Low‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) large)** were allowed **larger rescaling** (\(\lambda_i\) approaching the global extension ratio \(s\times 1.25\)).

Intuitively, high frequencies encode “local” ordering; compressing them too much would cause distinct adjacent tokens to map onto nearly identical sinusoidal phases—a phenomenon we refer to as *positional crowding*. Low frequencies capture “global” ordering over long spans; they can tolerate more compression because their phase changes slowly anyway.

When evaluated on PG19 and Proof‑Pile **without any fine‑tuning**, this non‑uniform schedule reduced perplexity by up to **30 % relative** compared with PI and by about **15 % relative** compared with NTK or YaRN. The improvement was most pronounced at context lengths beyond 8 k tokens, exactly where uniform scaling begins to break down. Hence we concluded that *dimensionwise non‑uniformity is a fundamental property of RoPE that must be respected when interpolating*.

## Finding 2 – Reduced Interpolation for the First \(\hat n\) Tokens  

While unraveling dimensionwise behavior we also examined whether all token positions should be treated identically. Prior work on streaming LLMs and infinite‑length language models suggests that **early tokens receive disproportionately large attention weights**, because they appear in every subsequent attention matrix row/column pair. If these early positions are severely distorted by interpolation, downstream attentions may suffer dramatically.

To test this intuition we conducted a controlled experiment: using linear PI or NTK scaling we varied the number of *unmodified* initial tokens \(\hat n\). For each choice (\(\hat n = 0, 2, 8, 16,\dots ,256\)) we kept the first \(\hat n\) token embeddings exactly as originally trained (no rescaling), while applying the chosen uniform scaling to all later positions.

The results showed a clear pattern:

| Extension Length | Best \(\hat n\) (tokens) | Perplexity Improvement vs. \(\hat n=0\) |
|------------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 8 k              | 16                       | ~8 % lower |
| 16 k             | 32–64                    | ~12 % lower |
| 32 k             | 128                      | ~15 % lower |

Two insights follow:

1. **Preserving early RoPE clearly helps** irrespective of whether we use linear or NTK scaling.
2. **The optimal amount of preserved tokens grows with the target extension length**; longer stretches benefit from protecting more initial positions because their influence propagates through more attention steps.

Consequently our final formulation introduces an explicit binary mask:

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)=
   \begin{cases}
     1 & n < \hat n \\[4pt]
     {\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i}} & n \ge \hat n,
   {\end{cases}
}
\]

which leaves early angles untouched while applying per-dimension scaling thereafter.

## Finding 3 – Non‑Uniform Scaling Benefits Both Fine‑Tuned and Non‑Fine‑Tuned Regimes  

Having identified superior scaling rules under pure inference conditions, we next asked whether these rules would also serve as better *initializations* for subsequent fine‑tuning on longer contexts—a crucial practical question because extending beyond roughly \(4\times L\) typically requires some amount of gradient update.

We performed two complementary experiments:

1. **Pure Inference Extension** – Using only searched non‑uniform factors (\(\lambda_i,\hat n\)) we evaluated perplexity at context lengths up to 64 k without any additional training.
2. **Fine–Tuning after Initialization** – Starting from the same searched configuration we fine‑tuned LLaMA2‑7B for a modest 400 steps on documents segmented at either 64 k or 128 k length (the latter being more aggressive). After fine‑tuning we re-evaluated perplexity across a range of lengths.

The outcomes were striking:

- In pure inference mode, non‑uniform scaling already outperformed PI/YaRN at every evaluated length beyond 8 k tokens.
- After fine‐tuning, models initialized with our searched schedule achieved *lower final perplexities* than those initialized with PI or YaRN even when both received identical training budgets. For instance, at a target length of 64 k token windows:

| Initialization | Post–FT Perplexity (PG19) |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| Linear PI      | 12.8 |
| YaRN           | 12.5 |
| **Non‑Uniform Search** | **11.3** |

The gap widened for longer targets (128 k and beyond), confirming that respecting both dimensionwise and positional non‑uniformities yields an embedding space that is already close to what gradient descent would otherwise have to discover from scratch.

In summary, *non-uniform positional interpolation is universally advantageous*: it reduces information loss when acting alone and provides a superior starting point when fine-tuning is necessary.

## Visualizing Learned \(\lambda_i\) Curves (Illustrative Insight)

Although not required for downstream usage, visualizing the discovered scaling curves helps build intuition about how RoPE reshapes itself across dimensions. A typical curve for an \(s=8\times\) extension appears as follows:

- The curve starts near unity for dimensions \(i=0,\dots,15\), gently rising.
- Around mid–range dimensions (\(i≈30–40\)) it accelerates sharply.
- Near the highest dimensions (\(i≈63–95\)) it plateaus close to \(s\times1.2 ≈9.6\).

Superimposing curves from different target extensions shows that larger extensions simply push the high‐frequency tail upward while preserving the low‐frequency baseline—exactly mirroring our earlier theoretical reasoning about local versus global positional information.

## Concluding Remarks on Empirical Discoveries  

The systematic evolution‐driven analysis led us to two concrete “hidden” non‑uniformities in rotary embeddings:

1. **Dimensionwise frequency variation:** higher frequencies require milder interpolation; lower frequencies can absorb stronger scaling.
2. **Token position variation:** early tokens should retain their original encoding because they dominate attention dynamics.

By explicitly modeling both aspects—through per–dimension rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) and an early‐token threshold \(\hat n\)—we obtain an interpolation scheme that dramatically lowers perplexity across diverse long-text corpora both before and after fine‐tuning. These findings directly motivate the design of LongRoPE: an evolutionary search algorithm discovers optimal non‑uniform schedules; progressive fine‐tuning leverages them; and a final re‐adjustment restores performance on short contexts.

The remainder of this textbook will build upon these empirical foundations: Chapters on algorithmic implementation will describe how LongRoPE searches efficiently despite an astronomically large solution space, while later sections will demonstrate how this machinery enables context windows up to two million tokens without sacrificing accuracy on standard benchmarks or downstream tasks such as passkey retrieval.

# Formulating the Search Problem

In order to extend a pre‑trained large language model (LLM) to an extremely long context window, we must determine how to modify the Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) so that the model can continue to generate accurate next‑token predictions.  This section formalizes that objective as a concrete search problem.  We first recapitulate the notation used throughout the paper, then present the full optimization formulation, describe the design of the search space, and finally explain why a monotonicity constraint on the per‑dimension rescaling factors is imposed, drawing on insights from Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory.

## Notation Recap

Let \(L\) denote the original context length for which a given LLM was trained (e.g., \(L=4096\) tokens).  The target extended length is \(L'\), and we define the **extension ratio** as  

\[
s = \frac{L'}{L}.
\]

RoPE encodes a token at position index \(n\) by rotating each half of the embedding dimension with a sinusoid whose frequency depends on a base value \(\theta\) (default \(\theta=10{,}000\)).  For compactness we introduce  

\[
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\]

where \(d\) is the full hidden dimension of the model.  In the original RoPE formulation, the rotary angle for dimension \(i\) (with \(i = 0,\dots,\frac{d}{2}-1\)) at position \(n\) is simply  

\[
\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}.
\]

To adapt these angles for an extended window we apply a **rescaling function**  

\[
\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)=
\begin{cases}
1, & n < \hat n,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{1}{\lambda_i}, & n \ge \hat n,
\end{cases}
\]

where:

* \(\hat{\lambda}_i \ge 1\) is a per‑dimension rescale factor (the inverse of the factor actually multiplied into the angle),
* \(\hat n\) is a **token‑position threshold**; for the first \(\hat n-1\) tokens we keep the original RoPE angles, while for later tokens we apply the rescaling.

Thus, after applying \(\mathbb I(\cdot)\), the RoPE vector at position \(n\) becomes  

\[
\bigl[\; \cos\bigl(\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\bigr),~
        \sin\bigl(\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\bigr)\;\bigr]_{i=0}^{d/2-1}.
\]

When every \(\lambda_i = s\) and \(\hat n = 0\), this reduces to **linear positional interpolation** (PI); other choices recover NTK‑based or YaRN‑style interpolations.

## Optimization Problem Statement

Given a collection of very long documents \(\mathbf{X}\) such that each document \(\mathbf{x}\in \mathbf{X}\) satisfies \(|\mathbf{x}| \ge L'\), our goal is to select a set of per‑dimension rescale factors \(\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\) together with a token‑position threshold \(\hat n\).  The selection should minimize the expected next‑token loss (equivalently, perplexity) of the LLM when evaluated on these long sequences.  Formally, we write

\[
%
\begin{aligned}
& \underset{\{\lambda_i\},\, \hat n}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\;
   \mathcal L_{\text{LLM}}\bigl(\text{RoPE}(\{\lambda_i,\hat n\}),\, \mathbf{X}\bigr)\\[4pt]
& \text{s.t.}\quad
   \text{RoPE}_{(n)} =
   \bigl[\cos(\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}),
         \sin(\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}})\bigr]_{i=0}^{d/2-1}.
%
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
%
\]

Here \(\mathcal L_{\text{LLM}}\) denotes the standard cross‑entropy loss used during language modeling; minimizing it directly minimizes perplexity.  Equation (1) captures precisely what LongRoPE seeks: an interpolation scheme that preserves as much of the information encoded in the original RoPE as possible while allowing the model to attend to far‑away positions.

## Definition of the Search Space

The optimization in Eq. (1) is highly non‑convex and involves discrete choices (the integer threshold \(\hat n\)) as well as continuous variables (\(\lambda_i\)).  Rather than attempting analytic gradients through this objective, LongRoPE treats it as a **black‑box search problem** where each candidate set of parameters is evaluated by measuring perplexity on a small validation subset.

The search space is defined as follows:

| Variable | Domain | Description |
|----------|--------|-------------|
| \(\lambda_i\) | \( [\,1.0,\; s\times1.25\,] \) with step size \(0.01\) | Per‑dimension rescale factor. The lower bound corresponds to *pure extrapolation* (no scaling), while the upper bound permits up to \(25\,\%\) more interpolation than naive linear scaling would dictate. |
| \(\hat n\) | \( \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\} \) | Number of initial token positions that retain their original RoPE angles. The set includes powers of two and intermediate values typically observed in practice. |

For a target extension ratio \(s=32\times\), for example, each dimension can independently choose any value from roughly \(1.00,\;1.01,\dots ,40.00.\)  The combinatorial explosion is immense: even with just two dimensions this would yield more than \(10^4\) possibilities; with \(d/2=64\) dimensions it amounts to roughly \(400^{64}\times14>10^{167}\).

Because enumerating all possibilities is infeasible, LongRoPE uses an **evolutionary search algorithm** that iteratively refines a population of candidates toward lower perplexity.

## Monotonicity Constraint Derived from NTK Theory

Although the search space permits arbitrary assignments of \(\lambda_i,\;\forall i,\), empirical evidence and theoretical analysis suggest that not every combination leads to sensible embeddings.  NTK theory tells us that higher‑frequency components (lower RoPE dimensions) are more sensitive to positional distortion: small changes in their angles cause large changes in attention scores.  Conversely, lower‑frequency components (higher dimensions) can tolerate stronger interpolation because their sinusoidal oscillations vary slowly across positions.

To encode this intuition we impose a **monotonic non‑decreasing constraint** on the rescale factors:

```math
    \lambda_0 \leq \lambda_1 \leq \dotsc \leq \lambda_{d/2-1}.
```

In words, as we move from low‑frequency dimensions (\(i=0\)) to high‑frequency ones (\(i=\tfrac{d}{2}-1\)), the amount of interpolation may only increase or stay constant.  This constraint has several practical benefits:

1. **Search Efficiency** – By pruning candidates that violate monotonicity early, we dramatically reduce the number of perplexity evaluations required in each evolutionary iteration.
2. **Theoretical Plausibility** – It aligns with NTK‑based interpolation schemes where scaling follows a power law across dimensions.
3. **Stability During Fine‑Tuning** – Models fine‑tuned on extended windows tend to converge faster when their positional embeddings respect this ordering.

During evolutionary mutation and crossover operations we therefore only generate offspring that satisfy this monotonicity condition; any offspring violating it are discarded before evaluation.

---

With these definitions in place—clear notation for extension ratio and base frequency, an explicit optimization objective (Eq. 1), a well‑structured yet expansive search space for per‑dimension scaling and token‐position thresholds, and a principled monotonicity constraint—we have fully formulated the problem that LongRoPE solves.  The remainder of this text will describe how an efficient evolutionary algorithm navigates this space to discover high‑quality RoPE configurations capable of supporting context windows beyond two million tokens while preserving performance on shorter sequences.
