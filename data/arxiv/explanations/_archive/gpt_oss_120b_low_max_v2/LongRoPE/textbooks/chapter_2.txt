\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 2: # Background: Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)

Large language models (LLMs) such as LLaMA‑2 and Mistral rely on self‑attention to capture dependencies among tokens.  Since the attention mechanism itself is permutation‑invariant, the model must be supplied with an explicit encoding of each token’s position in the input sequence.  The prevailing choice for many recent LLMs is **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)**, a formulation that intertwines positional information directly with the query–key vectors used in the attention computation.  This chapter presents a complete derivation of RoPE, interprets it as a Fourier‑style encoding of token positions, explains how it influences attention scores, and discusses why naïve extrapolation beyond the lengths observed during pre‑training leads to severe degradation.

---

## 1. Derivation of RoPE Angles and the Frequency Base  \(\theta\)

RoPE originates from the observation that rotating a vector in a two‑dimensional subspace can be expressed by a simple sinusoidal matrix.  Consider an embedding dimension \(d\) that is even; we can view each pair of consecutive dimensions \((2i,2i+1)\) as forming a planar subspace.  For a token located at absolute position index \(n\) (counted from the beginning of the sequence), we define a rotation angle for the \(i^{\text{th}}\) subspace:

\[
\alpha_i(n) \;=\; n \,\theta_i ,
\qquad
\theta_i \;=\;\theta^{-2i/d},
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\theta\) is a fixed scalar base (the default value in most open‑source LLMs is \(\theta=10{,}000\)).  Substituting \(\theta_i\) into \(\alpha_i(n)\) gives

\[
\alpha_i(n)= n\,\theta^{-2i/d}.
\]

Because each subspace rotates independently, the complete RoPE vector for token \(n\) can be written as a concatenation of cosine–sine pairs:

\[
\underbrace{
\bigl[\,\cos (\alpha_0(n)),\, \sin (\alpha_0(n)),\, 
      \cos (\alpha_1(n)),\, \sin (\alpha_1(n)),\, 
      \dots,\,
      \cos (\alpha_{d/2-1}(n)),\, \sin (\alpha_{d/2-1}(n))
\bigr]
}_{d \text{ entries}} .
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) is precisely the expression shown in the paper’s Eq. (1).  The factor \(\theta^{-2i/d}\) creates **geometrically decreasing frequencies** across dimensions: low‑indexed dimensions correspond to high frequencies (fast oscillation), while high‑indexed dimensions correspond to low frequencies (slow oscillation).  This hierarchy of frequencies is essential for representing both fine‑grained relative offsets (captured by high frequencies) and coarse‑grained absolute positions (captured by low frequencies).

---

## 2. RoPE as a Fourier Encoding of Token Positions

The sinusoidal pattern in Eq. (2) can be interpreted as a **Fourier basis** over integer positions.  Each dimension \(i\) stores the real and imaginary parts of a complex exponential:

\[
e^{j\, n\, \theta_i} 
   = \cos (n\theta_i) + j\,\sin (n\theta_i).
\]

Thus the RoPE embedding for position \(n\) is equivalent to sampling a set of complex exponentials at frequencies \(\{\theta_i\}\).  In signal‑processing terminology, this provides a *band‑limited* representation: higher‑frequency components resolve small relative distances between nearby tokens, while lower‑frequency components encode larger spans.

When two tokens at positions \(n_a\) and \(n_b\) interact through attention, their RoPE vectors are multiplied element‑wise with the query and key vectors.  Because multiplication by a complex exponential corresponds to **phase shift**, the dot product between query and key acquires an extra phase term that depends only on the relative offset:

\[
q_{n_a}^{\!\top}k_{n_b}
   = (q^{(0)}_{n_a})^{\!\top}k^{(0)}_{n_b}
     \;\times
     \underbrace{\exp\!\bigl(j\, (n_a-n_b)\,\theta_i\bigr)}_{\text{rotary factor per dimension }i}.
\]

Consequently, RoPE injects *relative* position information directly into the attention score without requiring an extra bias term.  The model can learn to use these phases to modulate attention based on distance, while still retaining absolute cues via the underlying frequency hierarchy.

---

## 3. Effect of RoPE on Attention Scores

Recall that in scaled dot‑product attention we compute

\[
A_{ab}
   = \frac{ q_{n_a}^{\!\top}k_{n_b}}{\sqrt{d_k}},
   \qquad
   w_{ab}= {\rm softmax}_b(A_{ab}),
\tag{3}
\]

where \(w_{ab}\) is the weight assigned to token \(b\) when producing token \(a\).  Substituting the rotary-modified query–key product yields

| Component | Role |
|-----------|------|
| \(\cos((n_a-n_b)\theta_i),\, \sin((n_a-n_b)\theta_i)\) | Encodes *relative* offset in each subspace |
| High‑frequency subspaces (\(i$ small$) | Sensitive to small offsets; enable precise discrimination of neighboring tokens |
| Low‑frequency subspaces (\(i$ large$) | Vary slowly with distance; provide a smooth bias favoring nearer tokens but also allowing long‑range influence |

Because each subspace contributes additively in the dot product, attention scores become a weighted sum over sinusoidal functions of distance.  Empirically this yields a **soft locality bias**: nearby tokens receive higher scores due to constructive interference in high‑frequency components, while distant tokens still retain non‑negligible contribution through low-frequency components.

The paper emphasizes that this balance is delicate: if we modify the underlying frequencies arbitrarily—e.g., when extending to unseen positions—some dimensions may become overly compressed (high frequencies crowd together) or overly stretched (low frequencies lose resolution), breaking the learned locality pattern and causing loss of performance.

---

## 4. Limitations When Extrapolating Beyond the Trained Length

During pre‑training, an LLM observes only positions up to its original context window \(L\) (e.g., \(L=4096\) for LLaMA‑2).  The rotary angles for positions greater than \(L-1\) are never seen; thus their corresponding embeddings constitute **out‑of‑distribution inputs** for all downstream linear layers that consume queries and keys.

Two concrete problems arise when we naïvely evaluate an LLM at length \(L'>L\):

1. **Catastrophic values from untrained angles**  
   The sinusoidal terms continue to oscillate indefinitely, but because no gradient updates ever shaped the downstream weights for those angles, they may lead to extreme activation magnitudes or destructive interference patterns.  The paper reports that more than 90 % of positions become “new” when extending from 4 k to over 1 M tokens; this sudden influx of unseen angles makes fine‑tuning extremely unstable.

2. **Crowding of positional information**  
   Linear interpolation methods (the original Position Interpolation or PI approach) shrink all rotation angles by a uniform factor equal to the extension ratio \(s = L'/L\).  In effect each angle becomes \(\alpha_i'(n)= n/(s\, \beta^i)\), where \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\).  As \(s\) grows large, high‑frequency dimensions collapse: many neighbouring positions map to almost identical cosine–sine pairs, eliminating discriminative power for short distances.  Consequently attention cannot reliably distinguish tokens that are close together, leading to rapid degradation of perplexity when evaluating long contexts.

The paper further notes that more sophisticated schemes such as NTK‑based interpolation or YaRN attempt to redistribute interpolation pressure across dimensions—scaling lower dimensions less and higher dimensions more—to alleviate crowding.  Nonetheless, these methods still treat each dimension group uniformly and ignore finer-grained *non‑uniformities* both across dimensions and across token indices themselves.

Empirical analysis in Section 3 of the paper uncovers two crucial observations:

- **Dimension‐wise non‑uniformity:** Optimal rescaling factors \(\lambda_i\) differ substantially across RoPE dimensions; simply applying one global scaling factor wastes information.
- **Position‐wise non‐uniformity:** Early tokens (the first \(\hat n\) positions) benefit from *less* interpolation because they receive disproportionately large attention scores during inference; preserving their original angles improves performance.

When these two forms of non‑uniformity are incorporated into a learned interpolation schedule—via an evolutionary search over per‑dimension factors and an early‐token threshold—the resulting *LongRoPE* initialization dramatically reduces information loss, enabling eightfold extensions without any fine‑tuning and serving as a robust starting point for further progressive extensions up to two million tokens.

--- 

In summary, Rotary Positional Embedding encodes token order through frequency‐scaled sinusoidal rotations that intertwine directly with self‑attention. Its success hinges on a carefully balanced spectrum of high‐ and low‐frequency components learned under a finite context length. Extending beyond that length naïvely creates catastrophic unseen angles and overwhelms the model’s locality bias. Recognizing and exploiting hidden non‑uniformities across both dimensions and token indices—precisely what LongRoPE does—provides an effective remedy and opens the door to multi‑million token contexts while preserving performance on short windows.
