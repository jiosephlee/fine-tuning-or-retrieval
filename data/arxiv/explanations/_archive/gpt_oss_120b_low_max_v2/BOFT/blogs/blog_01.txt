# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters: Preserving Knowledge in Large Models  

## Introduction  

Large foundation models—think GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or modern vision transformers—are incredibly powerful, but training them from scratch is often out of reach for most practitioners. The practical solution is *finetuning*: we start from a pretrained checkpoint and adapt it to a downstream task (sentiment analysis, image classification, style‑controlled generation, etc.).  

Most finetuning recipes fall into three families:  

* **Model finetuning** – directly update a subset of the original weights.  
* **Adapters** – add small trainable modules that sit alongside the frozen backbone.  
* **Prompt tuning** – prepend learnable tokens to the input.  

All three are popular, but they differ in how aggressively they disturb the pretrained knowledge. In this post we focus on **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a principled approach that keeps the *geometric relationships* between neurons intact while still giving the model enough flexibility to learn new tasks. We'll unpack why preserving angles matters, contrast OFT with the widely used LoRA and adapter methods, and give an intuitive feel for the underlying math—all without assuming a PhD in linear algebra.

## The Geometry Behind a Neural Layer  

Consider a single linear layer with weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\). Each column of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) is a *neuron* (or feature direction) that maps an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) to an output scalar. Two neurons \(\mathbf{w}_i\) and \(\mathbf{w}_j\) have a **pairwise angle**  

\[
\theta_{ij}= \arccos\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^{\top}\mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\;\|\mathbf{w}_j\|} \right),
\]

which encodes how similar their representations are. During pretraining, the model discovers a whole constellation of such angles that captures linguistic or visual regularities.

If we naïvely update \(\mathbf{W}^0\) with gradient descent, those angles can drift arbitrarily. This *drift* may erase the useful structure learned from billions of tokens or images, leading to poorer generalization on the new task.

## Orthogonal Transformations: Rotating Without Stretching  

An **orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\). Geometrically, multiplying by \(\mathbf{R}\) rotates (or reflects) vectors but never changes their lengths or the angles between them:

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{b} \rangle = \langle \mathbf{a},\,\mathbf{b} \rangle.
\]

OFT leverages this property by reparameterizing the finetuned weight matrix as  

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top},
\]

where we keep the original pretrained matrix frozen and only learn the orthogonal “rotator” \(\mathbf{R}\). In other words, every neuron of the layer is transformed by exactly the same rotation. Because angles are preserved, the *relative geometry* of all neurons stays unchanged—pretrained knowledge is retained even as the whole constellation swings to better align with the downstream data.

### Zero‑Initialization for Safety  

Training starts from \(\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\) (the identity matrix), so initially nothing changes. As learning progresses, \(\mathbf{R}\) gradually rotates the weight space; however, because orthogonality forces a pure rotation, the model never “blows up” or forgets its original structure.

## How OFT Differs from LoRA and Adapters  

| Method | Parameter budget | How it updates weights | What it preserves |
|--------|------------------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| **LoRA** (Low‑Rank Adaptation) | Low (adds two low‑rank matrices) | Adds a low‑rank correction: \(\Delta\!\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\) | No explicit geometric constraint; can change singular values arbitrarily |
| **Adapters** | Low–moderate (small bottleneck modules) | Inserts tiny feed‑forward blocks; original weights stay frozen | Indirectly preserves knowledge but adds new nonlinear pathways |
| **OFT** | Moderate (full orthogonal matrix → many parameters; later reduced by structure) | Multiplies original weights by a shared orthogonal matrix: \(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\) | Exactly preserves pairwise angles and spectral norm of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) |

- **LoRA** introduces new directions in weight space but may alter the *spectral norm* (largest singular value), sometimes hurting training stability.  
- **Adapters** keep the backbone untouched but add extra compute at inference time.  
- **OFT** keeps computation identical at inference (no extra modules) and guarantees that any rotation leaves the singular values unchanged—this often translates to smoother training and better generalization.

## Making OFT Practical: From Full Orthogonal Matrices to Butterflies  

A naïve orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) needs \(O(d^{2})\) parameters—prohibitive for large models. The original OFT paper mitigates this by forcing a block‑diagonal structure (splitting dimensions into independent groups). While this cuts parameters, it also limits expressivity because each block can only rotate its own subspace.

The recent follow‑up work introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**. The key idea: factorize a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of *sparse* orthogonal matrices arranged in a **butterfly pattern**, inspired by the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. 

- Each butterfly factor has only \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries.
- Stacking \(\log d\) such factors yields an overall dense rotation using only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.
- Despite being sparse internally, the composition can transmit information between any input and output coordinate—so angles are still preserved globally.

The butterfly factorization not only restores full expressivity but also injects an inductive bias reminiscent of classic transforms (Fourier, Hadamard), which empirically helps generalization.

## Why Preserving Angles Helps Retain Pretrained Knowledge  

1. **Spectral stability** – Orthogonal updates leave singular values untouched, keeping gradients well‑behaved.
2. **Metric consistency** – Many downstream tasks rely on similarity metrics built on inner products; rotating all neurons together maintains these metrics.
3. **Regularization effect** – The orthogonality constraint restricts the hypothesis space to rotations rather than arbitrary deformations, reducing overfitting risk.

Empirical studies reported in BOFT’s paper show consistent gains over LoRA and adapters across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models—especially when parameter budgets are tight.

## Takeaways  

- **Orthogonal Finetuning** treats adaptation as a *global rotation* of each layer’s weight space, guaranteeing that neuron‑to‑neuron angles remain unchanged.  
- This geometric preservation translates into stable training dynamics and better retention of pretrained knowledge compared with additive low‑rank or adapter approaches.  
- By factorizing rotations with butterfly structures (BOFT), we achieve parameter efficiency comparable to LoRA while keeping OFT’s theoretical benefits.  

If you’re fine‑tuning massive models and care about both performance and memory footprint, give orthogonal finetuning—or its butterfly‑enhanced variant—a try. It offers a clean mathematical guarantee that your model’s learned representations stay geometrically coherent while still learning what your new task demands.
