\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is the relationship between BOFT’s weight interpolation (Figure 2) and linear interpolation in the orthogonal group?
Question:
The authors claim that BOFT enables an "intriguing weight interpolation" because the orthogonal matrix is a product of several sparse orthogonal factors. Does this mean that interpolating between two sets of BOFT parameters corresponds to moving along a geodesic on the Stiefel manifold, or is it simply a linear interpolation of each factor? How does this affect training stability compared to directly interpolating a full orthogonal matrix?
Answer:
In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a frozen pretrained layer is not stored as one dense $d\times d$ matrix but as a product of several *sparse* orthogonal factors  

$$
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{(d,m)}\;\mathbf{B}_{(d,m-1)}\;\cdots\;\mathbf{B}_{(d,1)},
\qquad
\mathbf{B}_{(d,i)}^\top\mathbf{B}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf{I}_d .
$$

Each factor $\mathbf{B}_{(d,i)}$ has a butterfly‑type sparsity pattern (or a block‑diagonal version of it) and its non‑zero blocks are themselves low‑dimensional orthogonal matrices (e.g. $2\times 2$ rotations, or $b\times b$ rotations when a block size $b>2$ is used).  

During training the parameters of **every** factor are updated independently with a standard optimiser (e.g. Adam) while the orthogonality constraint is enforced by the Cayley or exponential map on each factor. Consequently, when we interpolate between two sets of BOFT parameters $\{\mathbf{B}^{(a)}_{i}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{B}^{(b)}_{i}\}$ we simply form the interpolated factors

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{i} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathbf{B}^{(a)}_{i}+ \lambda\,\mathbf{B}^{(b)}_{i},
\qquad 0\le \lambda \le 1,
$$

and then multiply them in the same order to obtain an interpolated orthogonal matrix  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(\lambda)= \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{m}\;\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{m-1}\;\cdots\;\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{1}.
$$

Because each $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ lives in a *linear* space of matrices (the set of all real matrices with the given sparsity pattern), the interpolation above is **linear in every factor**. The product of the linearly interpolated factors is **not** a geodesic on the Stiefel manifold; it does not follow the shortest‑path curve that would be obtained by interpolating directly on the manifold of full orthogonal matrices (for example via the matrix exponential of a skew‑symmetric direction). In other words, BOFT’s “intriguing weight interpolation’’ shown in Figure 2 is simply *linear interpolation of each sparse factor followed by reconstruction*, not a Riemannian geodesic on $\mathcal{O}(d)$.

Why does this matter for training stability?  

* **Preservation of orthogonality at each step** – Since every factor is re‑orthogonalised after the linear blend (e.g. by applying the Cayley transform again), each intermediate $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{i}$ remains exactly orthogonal. The final product is therefore also orthogonal, guaranteeing that the spectral norm of the transformed weight $\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{W}^{0}$ stays equal to that of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$. This property is what gives OFT and BOFT their superior stability compared with additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA.

* **Controlled deviation from the identity** – The linear blend between two orthogonal factors yields matrices that are close to identity when both endpoints are close to identity (the usual zero‑initialisation in BOFT). Hence the interpolated full matrix does not jump abruptly across the manifold; it changes smoothly in Euclidean space, which empirically translates into smoother loss landscapes and more stable optimisation.

* **Parameter efficiency vs. curvature** – A true geodesic interpolation on the full orthogonal group would require moving on a curved manifold whose dimension is $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$. Computing such a geodesic for a dense matrix would need to store and exponentiate a full skew‑symmetric matrix—prohibitively expensive for large $d$. By staying linear at the level of sparse factors we avoid this overhead while still benefiting from exact orthogonality after re‑projection.

In summary, BOFT’s interpolation corresponds to **linear interpolation of each butterfly (or block‑butterfly) factor** followed by multiplication, not to moving along a geodesic on the Stiefel/orthogonal manifold. This simple scheme preserves orthogonality after each step, keeps the computational cost low, and yields a more stable training dynamics than directly interpolating a dense orthogonal matrix, whose geodesic interpolation would be far more expensive and would not enjoy the same sparsity‑induced regularisation.
