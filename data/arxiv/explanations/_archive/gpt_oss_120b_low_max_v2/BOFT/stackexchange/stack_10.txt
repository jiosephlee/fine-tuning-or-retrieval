\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Could alternative network topologies outperform butterfly graphs for dense orthogonal factorization?
Question:
The paper mentions that it is unknown whether butterfly networks are optimal for information transmission under orthogonality constraints. From a graph‑theoretic perspective, what properties should an alternative topology have to reduce edge count while still enabling dense connectivity and respecting orthogonality? Are there known constructions (e.g., Benes networks, expander graphs) that could serve as better factorizations for BOFT?
Answer:
The paper itself frames the problem of “dense‑but‑parameter‑efficient’’ orthogonal finetuning as an **information‑transmission** task on a layered graph: each factor \(B_i\) of the factorisation \(\;R = B_m\cdots B_1\) defines a set of directed edges from level \(i\) to level \(i+1\).  To obtain a dense orthogonal matrix we must guarantee that **every source node in the first level can reach every sink node in the last level** while keeping the total number of *free* edges (the trainable non‑zero entries) as small as possible.  Orthogonality imposes an additional constraint: each bipartite sub‑graph between two consecutive levels must be a *bijection* (e.g. each node has exactly one outgoing and one incoming edge), otherwise the factor cannot be orthogonal.

### What a competing topology has to satisfy

1. **Dense connectivity with $O(d)$ edges per layer.**  
   Because each $B_i$ must be orthogonal, a minimum of $d$ edges is required between any two adjacent levels (the edges that correspond to a permutation matrix).  Any topology that respects orthogonality therefore starts with exactly $d$ “free’’ edges per layer; the challenge is to reuse those edges across layers so that the total number of distinct trainable parameters grows only like $d\,\text{polylog}(d)$.

2. **Logarithmic depth.**  
   If we have $m$ layers, the total number of distinct edges is at most $m d$.  To keep this sub‑quadratic we need $m = O(\log d)$.  This matches the butterfly construction, where each layer mixes pairs of indices that are spaced by a power‑of‑two stride.

3. **Permutation‑like structure with limited redundancy.**  
   Orthogonal matrices are essentially signed permutations composed with rotations in low‑dimensional subspaces (e.g. $2\times2$ Givens rotations).  A good topology should allow every pair of source–sink indices to appear together in *some* rotation block after at most $\log d$ steps.  In graph‑theoretic language, the edge set must be a **routing scheme** that connects any source–sink pair within logarithmic hops.

These three ingredients are precisely what makes the classic butterfly graph optimal among well‑studied interconnection networks: it yields an $(d,\,O(\log d))$-routing with exactly $d\log d$ trainable entries, and each intermediate bipartite graph is a perfect matching (hence orthogonal).

### Could other known networks beat it?

**Benes networks.**  
A Benes network is a rearrangeable non‑blocking switching fabric that also achieves full connectivity with depth $2\log_2 d -1$.  Its edge count is roughly $2d\log_2 d$, only a constant factor larger than the butterfly’s $d\log_2 d$.  Because every stage of a Benes network can be realised as a block‑diagonal matrix of $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks (just like the butterfly), it satisfies the orthogonality requirement.  However, its larger depth means more matrix multiplications during training and therefore higher runtime overhead; there is no asymptotic improvement in parameter count.

**Expander graphs.**  
Expanders provide excellent spectral properties and guarantee that short random walks rapidly mix across all nodes.  One could imagine using an expander as the bipartite connectivity between two consecutive levels, then stacking $\Theta(\log d)$ such layers to achieve full reachability.  The difficulty lies in enforcing **exact bijections** required for orthogonality: an expander typically has degree $>1$, so each node would have multiple outgoing edges, breaking the one‑to‑one mapping needed for a unitary factor unless additional constraints (e.g., orthogonalisation of each row/column) are imposed, which would dramatically increase the number of free parameters.  Thus, while expanders are attractive from a theoretical mixing standpoint, they do not readily lead to an orthogonal factorisation with fewer than $d\log d$ trainable entries.

**Other routing topologies (e.g., hypercubes, shuffle-exchange).**  
A hypercube of dimension $\log_2 d$ also permits any source–sink pair to connect via at most $\log_2 d$ hops, and each stage corresponds to swapping halves of the vector—exactly what a butterfly does but without the interleaved “twiddle’’ factors.  In practice the hypercube’s adjacency matrices are just permutations; without additional rotation blocks they cannot increase expressivity beyond signed permutations, so they would not improve over the block‑diagonal OFT.

### Why butterfly remains appealing

* **Minimal edge budget under orthogonality.**  
  Each layer contributes exactly $d$ trainable scalars (the non‑zero entries of its $2\times2$ Givens blocks).  With $\log_2 d$ layers this yields the lower bound $\Theta(d \log d)$ that follows from counting degrees of freedom needed to span a dense orthogonal group modulo the permutation baseline.

* **Closed‑form recursive construction.**  
  The butterfly factorisation can be written recursively (Equation 4 in the paper), which makes implementation straightforward and allows us to exploit existing fast‑FFT libraries for efficient forward passes when needed.

* **Known expressive power.**  
  Theorem 1 shows that products of butterfly matrices already generate a richer subset of $\mathcal{O}(d)$ than block‑diagonal OFT; adding more alternating products ($B\,B^\top\,B\,B^\top\,\dots$) even yields universal approximation of any orthogonal matrix (the kaleidoscope hierarchy).  This indicates that we are not sacrificing expressivity by staying within butterfly topologies.

### Practical take‑away

From a graph‑theoretic perspective any alternative topology must respect three constraints: (i) each bipartite slice is a perfect matching (or signed permutation), (ii) depth is logarithmic in dimension, and (iii) total distinct edges are on the order of $d\log d$.  Benes networks meet these constraints but give no asymptotic gain; expander or hypercube style graphs either violate (i) or require extra parameters to enforce orthogonality.  Consequently, **the butterfly network is essentially optimal among known routings for constructing dense orthogonal matrices with $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ free parameters**, which explains why the authors conjecture but do not prove its optimality.

If future work discovers a routing scheme that reduces either the constant factor in front of $d\log d$ or eliminates one layer altogether while still guaranteeing bijective bipartite connections, it could replace butterflies in BOFT.  Until such constructions appear, butterflies remain the most natural and theoretically justified choice for parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning.
