\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How does producer‑consumer warp‑specialization remove the first $s$ synchronization stalls in Algorithm 1?
Question:
In Algorithm 1 (the CTA view of FlashAttention‑3 without intra‑consumer overlapping), the producer warpgroup fills a circular SMEM buffer of size $s$ before any waits are issued. I am confused about why the first $s$ iterations do not need to wait for the consumer, and how the warp‑specialization (deallocating registers for producers) guarantees that the consumer will not read stale data. Could someone walk through a concrete example with, say, $s=2$ and $B_c$ blocks, showing the order of TMA loads, notifications, and consumer GEMM execution?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the “producer‑consumer” view of FlashAttention‑3 the CTA is split into two *warp‑groups*: a **producer** that only issues Tensor‑Memory‑Accelerator (TMA) loads of the key/value blocks and a **consumer** that only runs the WGMMAs (the two GEMMs) and the soft‑max work.  
The two warp‑groups communicate through a circular shared‑memory (SMEM) buffer that has **$s$** stages.  
The paper (Alg. 1, §3.1) states that *the producer never waits for the consumer during the first $s$ iterations*.  The reason can be understood by looking at what the buffer does and how warp‑specialisation reshapes the register file.

---

### 1.  What the $s$‑stage buffer buys us

Consider a buffer with stages indexed `0 … s‑1`.  
When the producer writes a block $(\mathbf K_j,\mathbf V_j)$ it stores it in stage `j % s`.  
A *stage* is considered **occupied** as long as the consumer has not finished using its contents.  
Conversely, a stage is **free** as soon as the consumer has committed the WGMMAs that depend on that block.

Because the producer and consumer are different warp‑groups, they can execute *independently*: issuing a TMA load does not stall any other warp; it merely places a request in the TMA engine and returns immediately.  
Thus, as long as there is at least one free stage, the producer can keep issuing loads without ever having to inspect a consumer flag.

When we start a new CTA, all stages are initially free.  Consequently, for the first `s` values of `j` we have

```
j = 0 … s-1
    → write (Kj,Vj) into stage j% s,
    → stage j% s is still free because consumer has not read it yet.
```

Only when `j = s` does the producer want to write into stage `0` again; at that point stage `0$` is *still occupied* (the consumer has not yet consumed the block written at `j = 0`).  The producer therefore executes a **wait‑for‑stage‑0** before issuing another TMA.  This wait is precisely the *first synchronization stall* described in the paper.  

Hence, **the first $s$ iterations are stall‑free because the buffer starts empty**; after those iterations every further iteration must wait for the stage it wants to reuse.

---

### 2.  Why deallocating registers for producers guarantees safety

Warp‑specialisation on Hopper lets us change the register budget of each warp‑group with `setmaxnreg`.  
The consumer warp‑group needs many registers to hold:

* the current soft‑max row maxima $\mathbf m_i$ and row sums $\boldsymbol\ell_i$,
* two tiles of $\mathbf S_i^{(j)}$ (`S_cur`, `S_next`) for pipelining,
* partial results $\widetilde{\mathbf P}_i^{(j)}$.

The producer warp‑group, on the other hand, never touches these values; its only state is the *pointer* to where in SMEM it will store the next $(\mathbf K_j,\mathbf V_j)$.  By calling `setmaxnreg` we explicitly give **all of those registers to the consumer** and **free them for the producer**.  

Because registers are *private* to each thread, freeing them for the producer means those registers no longer exist in that warp group’s register file – they cannot hold stale data that could be read later.  The only way a consumer could ever see stale data would be if it read from SMEM before the corresponding TMA had finished writing.  That cannot happen because:

1. The producer issues a TMA and **immediately commits** a notification (`notify_consumer`) that tells which stage of the buffer now contains valid data.
2. The consumer’s main loop begins each iteration with `wait_for_stage(j% s)`; this instruction blocks until it receives that notification.
3. The TMA engine guarantees *completion ordering*: every load finishes before its notification is raised.

Thus, even though registers have been reallocated, there is no window where both warps could race on SMEM: every read by the consumer is gated by an explicit barrier that can only be satisfied after the TMA write has completed.

---

### 3.  Concrete walk‑through with $s = 2$

Assume we have one query block $\mathbf Q_i$ (size $B_r \times d$) and three key/value blocks
$\{(\mathbf K_0,\mathbf V_0), (\mathbf K_1,\mathbf V_1), (\mathbf K_2,\mathbf V_2)\}$
so that $T_c = 3$.  
The circular buffer has two stages, labelled **stage 0** and **stage 1**.

| step | warpgroup | action | SMEM state | barrier |
|------|-----------|--------|------------|---------|
| **0** | producer | `TMA_load(Q_i)` → stage – (Q lives in its own slot) | Q ready | notify consumer |
| **1** | consumer | wait for Q → start | Q in SMEM | none |
| **2** | producer | `TMA_load(K_0,V_0)` → store in **stage 0**, then `notify(stage 0)` | stage 0 now holds (K₀,V₀) | – |
| **3** | consumer | wait for stage 0 (`wait(stage 0)`) → WGMMAs: <br> • SS‑GEMM: $\mathbf S^{(0)} = \mathbf Q_i \mathbf K_0^\top$<br> • softmax on $\mathbf S^{(0)}$<br> • RS‑GEMM: $\mathbf O_i += \widetilde{\mathbf P}^{(0)} \mathbf V_0$ | consumes stage 0 | after RS‑GEMM → `release(stage 0)` |
| **4** | producer | `TMA_load(K_1,V_1)` → store in **stage 1**, then `notify(stage 1)` | stage 1 now holds (K₁,V₁) | – |
| **5** | consumer | wait for stage 1 → WGMMAs using $(K_1,V_1)$. After RS‑GEMM release stage 1. |
| **6** | producer | wants to write next block (`j = 2`). It would reuse **stage 0**, but *stage 0 is now free* because step 3 already called `release(stage 0)`. Thus it can immediately issue `TMA_load(K_2,V_2)` into stage 0 and notify. No stall occurs – this is exactly why we needed only two stages: after step 5 both stages are free again, so from now on each new load simply overwrites a freed slot. |
| **7** | consumer | wait for stage 0 (`j = 2`) → final WGMMAs with $(K_2,V_2)$. After finishing it releases stage 0 (no further loads). |

Notice that **no explicit stall appears before step 6**, even though we are reusing stage 0. The reason is that by step 6 the consumer has already signalled `release(stage 0)`; therefore from the producer’s point of view stage 0 is free again.  

If we had set $s = 1$, step 6 would need to wait because there would be only one slot, still occupied by $(K_2,V_2)$ when we tried to write it; this would manifest as a stall on every iteration after the first one.

---

### 4.  Connecting back to Algorithm 1

Algorithm 1 in the paper mirrors exactly what we just described:

* Lines inside “producer warpgroup” (lines 5–13) execute “fill buffer” operations without any “wait” until they reach iteration `$j = s$`. The comment “no waits will be issued for the first $s$ iterations as the buffer gets filled” follows directly from our reasoning: all stages start empty.
* The “consumer” path (lines 15–30) begins each iteration with  
``` 
wait for K_j   // which implicitly waits for notify from producer
```  
guaranteeing that no stale data can be read.
* The deallocation/reallocation of registers (`setmaxnreg`) happens right before entering these two paths; it does not affect correctness because all communication happens through SMEM + explicit notifications rather than through registers.

---

### Summary

* The circular SMEM buffer with $s$ stages starts out completely empty, so during the first $s$ productions there is always at least one free slot; thus no stalls are needed.
* After those iterations each new production must reuse a slot that was just freed by an earlier consumption; this reuse creates exactly one synchronization point per iteration—the *first stall* discussed in the paper.
* Warp‑specialisation moves all registers needed by soft‑max/GEMM into the consumer warps and frees them from producers, eliminating any possibility of a producer reading or writing stale values via registers.
* The correctness hinges on two hardware guarantees: (i) TMA loads complete before their notification may be raised, and (ii) each consumer iteration begins with an explicit wait on that notification. Consequently, even though producers issue loads asynchronously, consumers never see unfinished or stale data.

With $s=2$ and three key/value blocks this results in an execution pattern where producers fill stages 0 and 1 back‑to‑back, consumers consume them in order, and from iteration 2 onward each new load reuses a stage that has already been released—exactly what Algorithm 1 claims.

### What is the exact data layout required for FP8 WGMMA operands and how is the in‑kernel transpose implemented?
Question:
The paper states that FP8 WGMMA only accepts k‑major operand layout, unlike FP16 which accepts both mn‑major and k‑major. It also mentions using LDSM/STSM instructions to transpose $V$ tiles after loading them into shared memory. I would like a detailed description of:
1) the memory stride/order of $V$ before and after the transpose;
2) how LDSM/STSM pack two 8‑bit entries to load 128‑byte tiles;
3) any additional register shuffles needed after the transpose to match the accumulator layout described in Figures 5 and 6.
A concrete code sketch or pseudo‑PTX would be very helpful.
Answer:
**Answer**

The FP8 version of FlashAttention‑3 has to obey two constraints that do not exist for FP16 WGMMA:

1. **Only k‑major layout is accepted for the *B* operand of the second GEMM** (the `V` matrix).  
2. **The accumulator layout produced by the first FP8 WGMMA does not match the layout expected by the second WGMMA**; a small register‑permute has to be inserted.

Below I describe how the kernel satisfies those constraints, how the in‑kernel transpose of `V` is performed with the `ldmatrix`/`stmatrix` (LDSM/STSM) instructions, and what register shuffles are needed before the second WGMMA.  The description is tied to the tile sizes used in the paper (`$B_r = 64$`, `$B_c = 64$`, `$d = 128$`) and to the $8\times8\times8$ FP8 tensor‑core tile that Hopper’s WGMMA implements.

---

### 1. Memory stride / order of **V** before and after the transpose  

In global memory (GMEM) the value matrix is stored **row‑major in the head‑dimension** (`$d$`).  
For a head dimension of 128 this means that a contiguous tile of shape  

```
V_tile_gmem[ B_c ][ d ]   // B_c rows, d columns
```

has stride `$stride\_row = d$` (128) and stride `$stride\_col = 1$`.  
The GEMM we need to compute is

\[
O_i \;+=\; (Q_i K_j^{\top})\; V_j ,
\]

i.e. we multiply an **mn‑major** `$Q_i K_j^{\top}` (rows = query block, columns = key block)` with a **k‑major** `$V_j$`.  
Therefore each tile of `V` that lives in shared memory must be laid out as

```
V_tile_smem[ d ][ B_c ]   // d rows, B_c columns   <-- k‑major
```

with stride `$stride\_row = B_c$` (64) and stride `$stride\_col = 1$`.  

Consequently we have to **transpose** a `$B_c \times d$` tile after it is copied from GMEM into shared memory.  The transpose changes the effective strides from `$(d, 1)$` to `$(B_c, 1)$` while leaving the data order in registers unchanged.

---

### 2. How LDSM / STSM pack two 8‑bit entries

Hopper’s `ldmatrix` (`LDSM`) instruction moves an **$8 \times 8$ matrix of *half‑words*** (16 bits) from shared memory to registers in a single warp instruction.  For FP8 we can treat two adjacent FP8 values as one half‑word and therefore load *two* FP8 elements per half‑word.

A tile that we want to transpose consists of **$64 \times 128$** FP8 values, i.e. **4 × $(8 \times 8)$** sub‑tiles when we view them as half‑words:

```
          ┌───────┐ ┌───────┐ ┌───────┐ ┌───────┐
          │ h0–h1 │ │ h2–h3 │ │ h4–h5 │ │ h6–h7 │
          └───────┘ └───────┘ └───────┘ └───────┘
           ... repeats for each 8‑row block ...
```

* **Loading (`ldmatrix`)** – each warp issues one `ldmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.shared.b64` (the “b64” suffix tells the hardware that each element is a *byte* pair).  
  The instruction reads a contiguous **128‑byte** region from shared memory (8 rows × 8 columns × 2 bytes per packed element) and expands it into eight registers per thread (`%r0 … %r7`).  
* **Storing (`stmatrix`)** – after we have rearranged those registers (see §3), we issue one `stmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.shared.b64`.  It writes exactly the same 128‑byte region back to shared memory, but because our registers now contain the transposed ordering, the destination region holds a transposed sub‑tile.

Because each instruction processes an entire *packed* $8 \times 8$ block, four consecutive invocations cover a full `$64 \times 128$` tile:

```ptx
// Assume: %shared_V points to beginning of V_tile in SMEM
//         %reg[0..31] are per‑thread registers

// Loop over four sub‑tiles (each 8 rows × 32 columns)
.for i in [0:3] {
    .reg .b32   r0, r1, r2, r3, r4, r5, r6, r7;   // will hold packed FP8

    // Load packed block (rows i*8 … i*8+7 , cols 0 …31)
    ldmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.shared.b64
        {r0, r1, r2, r3, r4, r5, r6, r7}, 
        [%shared_V + i*128];                     // 128 B stride between blocks

    // ---- register permutation for transpose ----
    // See §3 for exact shuffle pattern.
    // After this step each thread owns elements that belong
    // to a different column in the original matrix.

    // Store transposed block back to SMEM at transposed location
    stmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.shared.b64
        [%shared_V_trans + i*128],
        {r0, r1, r2, r3, r4, r5, r6, r7};
}
```

Each iteration moves exactly **128 bytes**, which matches the bandwidth of a single Tensor Core lane and therefore incurs essentially no extra latency beyond the normal TMA load that placed the original tile into SMEM.

---

### 3. Register shuffles needed after the transpose  

#### (a) Register layout produced by the first FP8 WGMMA  

For an FP8 WGMMA with tile shape `$M=64$, $N=64$, $K=64$`, each thread owns an **2 × 4** fragment of the C accumulator.  
In PTX this fragment is laid out in registers as

```
c00 c01 c02 c03   // row 0 columns 0..3
c10 c11 c12 c13   // row 1 columns 0..3
c20 c21 c22 c23   // row 2 …
c30 c31 c32 c33
c40 …            // … up to row 7 (each thread owns two rows)
c50 …
c60 …
c70 …
```

If we write those eight registers sequentially (`%c0 … %c7`) they appear in *row‑major* order.

#### (b) Layout required for the second WGMMA  

The second GEMM multiplies `(Q K^{\top})` – now stored as accumulator C – with transposed `V`.  
WGMMA expects its **A operand** (the left matrix) to be *k‑major*: consecutive elements must correspond to successive values of K inside a single warp‐group column.  
Because our accumulator is currently row‑major we have to apply a *column‑shuffle*:

```
new_order = { d0 d1 d4 d5 d2 d3 d6 d7 }
```

where each `d?` denotes one of the original eight registers (`%c0 … %c7`).  
In practice this permutation can be expressed with a single warp‑wide byte‑permute (`prmt.b32`) followed by an intra‑warp shuffle (`shfl_xor`). The code fragment below shows one possible PTX implementation:

```ptx
// %c0…%c7 contain row–major accumulator fragment
// pack them into two 32‑bit values so that prmt can work on bytes

.reg .b32 temp0, temp1;
mov.u32 temp0, {%c0,%c1,%c2,%c3};   // low half of fragment
mov.u32 temp1, {%c4,%c5,%c6,%c7};   // high half

// byte–permute according to mask {0,1,4,5,2,3,6,7}
prmt.b32 %c0, temp0, temp1, 0x50403020;
prmt.b32 %c1, temp0, temp1, 0x70605040;
prmt.b32 %c2, temp0, temp1, 0x92817060;
prmt.b32 %c3, temp0, temp1, 0xb4a39080;

// Now %c0…%c3 hold columns {d0,d1,d4,d5}.  
// A second set of shuffles (shfl_xor) swaps the remaining pairs 
// so that we finally have {d2,d3,d6,d7} in %c4…%c7.
shfl_sync.b32 %c4,%c4,%tid.x ^ 0x01;
shfl_sync.b32 %c5,%c5,%tid.x ^ 0x01;
shfl_sync.b32 %c6,%c6,%tid.x ^ 0x01;
shfl_sync.b32 %c7,%c7,%tid.x ^ 0x01;
```

After this permutation each thread’s registers are exactly in k‑major order and can be fed directly into the second WGMMA:

```ptx
wmma.mma_async_fp8_f16_f16_fp32 
    .dest_accumulator (%acc), 
    .src_a (%acc),                 // now k‑major accumulator from first GEMM
    .src_b (%v_transposed_regs),   // V tile already transposed in SMEM → loaded with ldmatrix
    .src_c (%zero);                // accumulate into fresh C buffer
```

#### (c) Matching permutation on **V**

The same column permutation performed on the accumulator must be mirrored on `V`.  
Because we transpose `V` *in shared memory* using LDSM/STSM (section 2), we can write each sub‑tile into a location whose logical column index already incorporates that permutation. In other words,

```
store_address = base_smem + permuted_column_offset(i)
```

where `permuted_column_offset(i)` follows exactly `{0→0 , 1→1 , 2→4 , 3→5 , …}`. The PTX loop shown earlier stores each packed block at an address computed with that offset; no additional shuffle is required after loading because each thread already sees its elements in k‑major order.

---

### Putting it all together – pseudo kernel

```ptx
.visible .entry flashattention_fp8_forward(
    .param .u64 Q_ptr,
    .param .u64 K_ptr,
    .param .u64 V_ptr,
    .param .u64 O_ptr,
    .param .u32 seq_len,
    .param .u32 head_dim,
    .param .u32 block_q,
    .param .u32 block_k)
{
    // -------------------------------------------------------
    //   Producer warp: TMA loads Q_i , K_j , V_j into SMEM
    // -------------------------------------------------------
    tma.load.async.shared [smem_Q], [Q_ptr + i*block_q*head_dim];
    tma.load.async.shared [smem_K], [K_ptr + j*block_k*head_dim];
    tma.load.async.shared [smem_V_raw], [V_ptr + j*block_k*head_dim];

    // -------------------------------------------------------
    //   Consumer warp – first GEMM: Q_i * K_j^T -> Acc_fp32
    // -------------------------------------------------------
    wgmma.mma_async_fp8_fp16_fp16_fp32 
        .dest_accumulator (%acc), .src_a(%Q_reg), .src_b(%K_reg);

    // -------------------------------------------------------
    //   In‑kernel transpose of V_j while waiting for next K
    // -------------------------------------------------------
    @producer_done {
        // four packed sub-tiles per V_j tile
        for (int s=0; s<4; ++s) {
            ldmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.shared.b64 
                {r0..r7}, [smem_V_raw + s*128];
            // permute registers so that they become k-major after store
            permute_for_k_major(r0..r7);
            stmatrix.sync.aligned.m8n8.shared.b64 
                [smem_V_tiled + s*128], {r0..r7};
        }
        @producer_done_barrier;            // signal that V_j is ready
    }

    // -------------------------------------------------------
    //   Register shuffle of accumulator to k-major order
    // -------------------------------------------------------
    permute_accumulator_to_k_major(%acc);

    // -------------------------------------------------------
    //   Second GEMM: (QK^T)_k-major * V_j_transposed -> O_i partials
    // -------------------------------------------------------
    wgmma.mma_async_fp8_fp16_fp16_fp32 
        .dest_accumulator (%O_partial), 
        .src_a(%acc),                // now k-major accumulator from first GEMM
        .src_b(%V_tiled_reg);        // loaded from smem_V_tiled via ldmatrix

    ...      // softmax reduction & accumulation as described in Alg.5

}
```

The kernel above follows exactly what is described in Sections §4.3–§4.4 of the paper:

* The producer warp uses TMA to bring tiles of `Q`, `K`, and raw `V` into shared memory.
* Immediately after a `V` tile lands in shared memory we launch an LDSM/STSM‐based transpose; because LDSM works on packed FP8 pairs it moves a full **128‑byte** sub‑tile per instruction.
* While the next `K` tile is being fetched we already start the second WGMMA with the transposed `V`; thus no extra synchronisation point is needed between GEMMs.
* The only extra register work beyond what a plain FP16 kernel would do is:
  * a small byte‑permute (`prmt.b32`) plus an intra‑warp shuffle (`shfl_xor`) to reorder the accumulator,
  * and an identical permutation applied during the LDSM/STSM transpose of `V`.

With these steps satisfied every iteration complies with Hopper’s k‑major requirement for both operands of the second FP8 WGMMA and produces exactly the accumulator layout shown in Figures 5 and 6 of the paper. The result is an end‑to‑end FP8 FlashAttention kernel that attains close to **$1.2\,\text{PFLOP/s}$** on H100 while keeping numerical error low thanks to block quantisation and incoherent processing.

### Why does overlapping softmax with GEMM improve utilization despite softmax’s data dependencies?
Question:
Section 3.2 describes a 2‑stage pipeline where softmax for iteration $j+1$ overlaps with the second WGMMA of iteration $j$. I understand that GEMM throughput is much higher than exponential throughput, but I’m unclear how the dependencies (softmax needs $S_{cur}$ from the first GEMM, and the second GEMM needs $	ilde P_{cur}$ from softmax) are broken. How does keeping $S_{next}$ and $	ilde P_{cur}$ in registers allow these operations to proceed concurrently? A step‑by‑step timeline of kernel instructions would clarify this.
Answer:
The key to the 2‑stage pipeline in FlashAttention‑3 is that the two operations that look sequential on paper – the first GEMM ( $S_{\text{cur}} = Q\,K_j^{\top}$ ) and the soft‑max that follows it – are *physically* executed on different warpgroups and on different hardware units.  
Because the Hopper Tensor Cores (exposed as the `WGMMA` instruction) are **asynchronous** and can pull their operands directly from shared memory, a warp that is waiting for a soft‑max to finish does not have to idle while another warp is issuing a new GEMM. The only thing that has to be preserved across iterations is the data that later stages need: the current score tile $S_{\text{cur}}$ and the already‑computed soft‑max tile $\widetilde P_{\text{cur}}$. Both of these can be kept in registers, which are private to each thread and therefore do not interfere with the shared‑memory loads performed by the producer warpgroup.

Below is a walk‑through of one iteration of the inner loop in **Algorithm 2** (the consumer warpgroup).  
For clarity I will label each step with the hardware resource that actually executes it:

| Cycle (conceptual) | What happens | Where the data lives | How asynchrony breaks the dependency |
|--------------------|--------------|----------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **0** | The warpgroup has already finished loading `K_j` from global memory into shared memory. | `K_j` sits in SMEM, `Q_i` is already in SMEM. | – |
| **1** | **Issue** `WGMMA` to compute `S_next = Q_i · K_jᵀ`. The instruction is launched but **not waited for** (`commit‑but‑don’t‑wait`). | The operand tiles stay in SMEM; the partial result `S_next` is streamed into a set of registers (`S_next_reg`). | Because `WGMMA` works asynchronously, the warp can continue issuing other instructions while the Tensor Core works on this multiplication. |
| **2** | Meanwhile, the previous iteration’s value `V_{j‑1}` has already been loaded into SMEM by the producer warpgroup. The consumer now **issues** a second `WGMMA`: `$O_i \leftarrow O_i + \widetilde P_{\text{cur}} \cdot V_{j‑1}$`. Again it is committed without waiting. | $\widetilde P_{\text{cur}}$ was produced in the *previous* iteration and resides in registers; $V_{j‑1}$ lives in SMEM. The product is accumulated directly into the output register file (`O_i_reg`). | The two GEMMs are independent: they use disjoint operand tiles (`K_j` vs. $V_{j‑1}$) and distinct output registers, so they can run concurrently on separate Tensor Core pipelines (or sequentially but overlapped with non‑Tensor‑Core work). |
| **3** | Now we **wait for** the first GEMM (`S_next`) to finish. When it does, its register tile contains the raw scores for block $j$. | `S_next_reg` now holds valid numbers. | The wait point is placed *after* we have already started the second GEMM, so no idle time remains. |
| **4** | **Softmax stage**: using `S_next_reg` we compute  
  $m_i = \max(m_i^{\text{old}}, \text{rowmax}(S_{\text{next}}))$  
  $\widetilde P_{\text{next}} = \exp(S_{\text{next}} - m_i)$  
  $\ell_i = \exp(m_i^{\text{old}}-m_i)\,\ell_i + \text{rowsum}(\widetilde P_{\text{next}})$.  
All of these operations are simple elementwise arithmetic and an exponential, which run on the CUDA cores (the “special‑function unit”). They operate entirely on data that lives in registers (`S_next_reg`, $m_i$, $\ell_i$). | Registers only. | Because this work is happening on a completely different functional unit than the Tensor Cores, it can occupy cycles that would otherwise be wasted while waiting for matrix multiplication results. |
| **5** | **Wait for** the second GEMM (`O_i += \widetilde P_{\text{cur}}\cdot V_{j‑1}`) to finish, then **rescale** `$O_i$` by the factor $\exp(m_i^{\text{old}}-m_i)^{-1}$. This rescaling also uses only registers. | `O_i_reg`, $\widetilde P_{\text{cur}}$ (still in registers), scaling factor from step 4. | Again there is no stall: as soon as both productions are ready (the softmax result from step 4 and the accumulation from step 2), they are combined immediately. |
| **6** | Finally we *rotate* buffers: copy `S_next_reg → S_cur_reg`, move `\widetilde P_next_reg → \widetilde P_cur_reg`, and release the shared‑memory slots used for `K_j` and `V_{j‑1}` so that the producer warpgroup can load `K_{j+1}` / `V_{j}`. | All data needed for the next iteration is now in registers; shared memory is free for new loads. | The next loop iteration can start at step 1 without any dependency on what happened earlier, because all cross‑iteration state lives in registers. |

In short, **the dependency chain looks like this when drawn naïvely**

```
S_cur  →  softmax  →  \tilde P_cur  →  second GEMM  →  O_i
   ↑                                            ↓
   └─────── first GEMM (needs S_cur) ────────────┘
```

but after introducing two *register buffers* (`S_next_reg` and `\tilde P_cur_reg`) we obtain three *pipelines* that run in lock‑step:

```
pipeline A (first GEMM):    S_cur → S_next
pipeline B (softmax):       S_next → \tilde P_next
pipeline C (second GEMM):   \tilde P_cur → O_i
```

Because each pipeline uses a different hardware engine (Tensor Core vs. CUDA cores) and because all data that must be handed from one pipeline to another stays inside each thread’s register file, no thread ever has to wait for a global memory operation or for another warpgroup’s instruction stream. The only synchronisation points are lightweight *barriers* that tell a warpgroup “the tile you need has been produced”, which on Hopper are essentially a few nanoseconds.

### Why registers make this possible

*Registers are private* – they do not need any coherence protocol between warps; once a warp writes a value into its own register file, another warp cannot see it until it is explicitly copied into shared memory or global memory. By keeping both $S_{\text{next}}$ and $\widetilde P_{\text{cur}}$ in registers we avoid any such copy, eliminating both latency and bandwidth pressure.

*Register capacity is limited*, which is why FlashAttention‑3 carefully chooses block sizes ($B_r,B_c$) so that a tile of scores ($B_r\times B_c$) fits comfortably in per‑thread registers after being split among the 32 threads of a warp (each thread holds roughly $B_r B_c/32$ elements). If a tile were larger than what can be kept in registers, we would have to spill to shared memory and reintroduce a real dependency.

### Connection to broader concepts

What FlashAttention‑3 does here is essentially a classic *software pipeline*: each stage produces data needed by the next stage but does so early enough that while stage i works on iteration k, stage i+1 can already start work on iteration k+1 using its own private buffer. In CPU literature this pattern appears as “loop‐carried dependence breaking” with double buffering; on GPUs it becomes even more powerful because different functional units can truly operate concurrently.

The technique also mirrors what compilers do for *producer–consumer* pipelines on heterogeneous architectures: separate queues for compute vs. special function units, with explicit synchronization only at queue boundaries. By exposing those queues explicitly through warp specialization (`producer` warps issuing TMA loads, `consumer` warps issuing WGMMAs), FlashAttention‑3 lets us write exactly those synchronization points instead of relying on an opaque compiler schedule.

---

**Bottom line:**  
The apparent serial dependency between softmax and GEMM disappears once we store each iteration’s intermediate score tile (`S_next`) and its softmaxed version (`\tilde P_cur`) in per‑thread registers. This enables two asynchronous WGMMAs (the first producing scores for iteration $j+1$, the second consuming softmaxed scores from iteration $j$) to overlap with each other and with elementwise/exponential work performed by CUDA cores. The result is much higher Tensor‑Core utilization—up to 75 % on H100—without sacrificing correctness because all cross‑iteration data movement stays inside fast register space.

### How is block quantization applied to Q, K, V matrices without extra runtime cost?
Question:
The paper claims that block quantization "can be fused with an operation right before attention (e.g., rotary embedding) with no additional slowdown". I am trying to understand:
- What is the exact scaling factor stored per block?
- At which point in the kernel are these scalars applied (during TMA load, before WGMMA, or after?)
- Does this require storing additional per‑block metadata in global memory?
Any implementation details or code references would help.
Answer:
In **FlashAttention‑3** the only place where the extra work of block‑wise quantisation appears is in the *pre‑processing* of the $Q$, $K$ and $V$ tensors, i.e. **before any data are moved from global memory**.  
Because the algorithm already works on a tiled basis (each CTA consumes a $B_r\times d$ tile of $Q$ and a $B_c\times d$ tile of $K,V$), the per‑block scale can be folded into the very first load that brings the tile into shared memory. Consequently there is **no additional runtime overhead**: the load, the scaling, and the TMA‑to‑SMEM copy are issued as one fused instruction sequence.

### What is stored for each block?

For a given block we keep a **single 32‑bit floating‑point scalar** $s_{\text{blk}}$.  
The block is quantised to FP8 by

$$
\widehat{X}_{\text{blk}} = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{X_{\text{blk}}}{s_{\text{blk}}}\Bigr)_{\!\! \text{FP8}},
$$

where $X_{\text{blk}}$ denotes the original FP16 (or FP32) values of the block and “round …$_{\text{FP8}}$” means rounding to the nearest representable FP8 e4m3 value.  
During inference we simply multiply back by the same scalar after the FP8 GEMM, i.e.

$$
Y_{\text{blk}} = s_{\text{blk}}^{(Q)}\, s_{\text{blk}}^{(K)} \; ( \widehat{Q}_{\text{blk}} \widehat{K}_{\text{blk}}^{\top} )
$$

for the first GEMM and analogously for the second one with $s_{\text{blk}}^{(V)}$.

Thus each block carries **one 4‑byte scale**; no per‑element or per‑tensor extra data are required.

### When and where is the scale applied?

The scaling is performed **immediately after the TMA load of a tile** and **before** any WGMMA instruction consumes the data.

1. **Producer warpgroup** issues a TMA that copies a tile of $Q$ (or $K,V$) from global memory into shared memory.  
2. As soon as the TMA completes (the hardware notifies via a lightweight “done” flag), a few scalar instructions multiply every element of that tile by its pre‑computed block scale $s_{\text{blk}}$. The multiplication is done in registers or shared memory using a single broadcast of the scalar; because all threads of the warpgroup see the same value, only one register needs to hold it.  
3. The now‑scaled FP8 values are left in shared memory exactly in the layout expected by FP8 WGMMA (k‑major for both operands). The subsequent WGMMA instruction reads those bytes directly; no extra conversion or “de‑quantisation” step appears in the kernel’s main loop.

Because TMA and WGMMA are asynchronous, the producer warp can start loading the *next* block while the consumer warpgroup is already executing a WGMMA on the previously loaded—and already scaled—block. The scaling therefore **happens while data are still in shared memory**, overlapping with computation on other warps and incurring zero extra latency.

### Do we need extra metadata in global memory?

No. The per‑block scales are **not stored alongside the original tensors in global memory**. They are produced on‑the‑fly in a tiny buffer that lives in registers (or, if one prefers, in a small piece of shared memory allocated for metadata). The workflow is:

* Before launching FlashAttention‑3 we run a tiny “pre‑quantisation” pass that scans each logical block (size $B_r\times d$ or $B_c\times d$) of $Q,K,V$, computes its max‑absolute value, derives an appropriate FP8 scale (often using a simple power‑of‑2 rounding so that multiplication can be performed as an integer shift), and stores that scalar in a **per‑CTA register array**.  

* Because each CTA processes contiguous blocks, these scalars are generated once per CTA launch and remain resident for its whole lifetime. No additional global reads/writes are required.

In practice this means that **the only extra global memory traffic compared with pure FP16 attention is the original loading of Q/K/V themselves**; everything else lives on‑chip.

### Putting it together

When you look at the pseudo code in §4.3 (the “block quantisation” paragraph) you will see essentially this pattern:

```cpp
// producer warpgroup
tma_load(Q_tile);                     // async copy Q_{i} -> SMEM
float s_q = compute_block_scale(Q_tile); // cheap reduction over tile
scale_tile_inplace(Q_tile, s_q);      // broadcast multiply
notify_consumer();                    // smem ready

// consumer warpgroup
wait_for_Q();                         // barrier
wgmma(Q_tile_fp8, K_tile_fp8);        // uses already scaled fp8 values
```

The same applies to K and V tiles; for K we also need to apply its block scale *before* feeding it to the first GEMM, while for V we apply its scale before the second GEMM (or equivalently multiply out both scales after both GEMMs have completed – both choices are equivalent because they are just scalar factors).

Thus:

* **Exact scaling factor:** one 32‑bit floating point number per logical block (e.g. per $B_r\times d$ slice of $Q$, per $B_c\times d$ slice of $K$, per $B_c\times d$ slice of $V$).  
* **Application point:** immediately after each TMA load, before any WGMMA instruction; concretely inside the producer warpgroup while data sit in shared memory.  
* **Metadata storage:** no additional global memory structure; scales reside in registers or a tiny shared‐memory buffer local to each CTA.

Because all three steps—load, scale, and write back to shared memory—are fused into a single asynchronous pipeline, they do not introduce any new latency or bandwidth consumption beyond what would already be required for loading Q/K/V. This is why the authors can claim that block quantisation “can be fused … with no additional slowdown”.

### What is incoherent processing and why does multiplying Q and K by a random orthogonal matrix not affect attention?
Question:
The authors introduce "incoherent processing" where Q and K are multiplied by a random orthogonal matrix M before FP8 quantization. They claim $(Q M)(K M)^\top = Q K^\top$ because $M M^\top = I$. I am confused about:
1) How is M generated efficiently (product of diagonal \(\pm1\) and Hadamard matrices)?
2) Does this multiplication happen on‑the‑fly during TMA load or as a separate kernel?
3) Does applying M change the statistical distribution of Q and K in a way that reduces FP8 quantization error? Please explain both mathematically and practically.
Answer:
**Incoherent processing** is the trick that the authors use to make FP‑8 quantisation of the \(Q\) and \(K\) matrices less harmful.  
The idea is simply to premultiply both matrices by the same random orthogonal matrix  

$$
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\qquad M M^{\top}=I .
$$

Because the attention score matrix is a *bilinear* form,
$$
S = QK^{\top} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; (QM)(KM)^{\top}=QM\,M^{\top}K^{\top}=QK^{\top}=S,
$$
the scores – and therefore the final output – are unchanged.  What changes, however, is the *distribution* of the entries of \(Q\) and \(K\) before they are quantised to FP‑8.

---

### 1.  How is \(M\) generated efficiently?

The paper follows the construction used in QUIP [Chee et al., 2024] and Tseng et al. (2024).  
An orthogonal matrix can be built as  

$$
M = D_1 H D_2 H \cdots D_{\ell} H ,
$$

where each \(D_i\) is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are independent Rademacher variables
(\(\pm 1\) with equal probability) and \(H\) is the (unnormalised) Hadamard matrix of size \(d\times d\).  

* **Hadamard transform**: Multiplication by \(H\) can be performed in‑place with the fast Walsh‑Hadamard transform (FWHT), which costs \(O(d\log d)\) arithmetic operations instead of the naïve \(O(d^{2})\).  The FWHT is just a sequence of butterfly additions/subtractions and fits naturally on GPU warps – each warp can process a block of the vector with only shared‑memory shuffles.

* **Diagonal signs**: Applying a diagonal \(D_i\) amounts to flipping the sign of selected elements, which is a single XOR with a bit‑mask per thread; this costs virtually nothing.

Because we need only a *few* such factors (the authors use \(\ell=2\) or 3 in practice), constructing \(M\) on‑the‑fly is cheap: for each block of \(Q\) or \(K\) we run the FWHT, interleaved with sign flips.  No explicit dense matrix ever materialises, so memory bandwidth is untouched.

---

### 2.  When is the multiplication applied – on‑the‑fly or as a separate kernel?

In FlashAttention‑3 the whole attention kernel already works block‑wise: a CTA loads a tile of \(Q\), then iterates over tiles of \(K,V\).  
The authors embed the incoherent processing *inside* that same kernel:

1. **Load** a tile of \(Q\) (or \(K\)) from global memory into shared memory via TMA.
2. **Immediately** apply the sequence “sign flip → Hadamard → sign flip → …” while the data sit in registers/shared memory.  
   Because the operations are element‑wise (sign flips) and involve only butterfly adds/subtracts (Hadamard), they can be fused with the existing arithmetic instructions that already occupy the warp.
3. **Quantise** the transformed tile to FP‑8 and feed it to the FP‑8 WGMMA GEMM.

Thus there is **no extra launch**; the extra work occupies only a few extra GPU cycles per element and does not require an additional memory round‑trip.  In practice it is implemented as a small inline routine that runs on each warp before issuing the GEMM instruction.

---

### 3.  Why does multiplying by \(M\) reduce quantisation error?

#### Intuition  

Quantising to FP‑8 uses a shared scaling factor (per tensor or per block).  If an input tensor contains *outliers* – entries that are many standard deviations larger than typical values – a single scale will have to be chosen large enough to accommodate those outliers. Consequently most “regular” entries fall into a much coarser region of the FP‑8 mantissa and suffer larger rounding error.

Multiplying by a random orthogonal matrix spreads each outlier over all coordinates of the transformed vector:

*Each entry of \(QM\) (or \(KM\)) is a signed sum of many original entries.*  
If one original component was an outlier, its contribution gets diluted by adding with many non‑outlier components whose signs are random. By the Central Limit Theorem, after a few Hadamard layers each coordinate looks roughly Gaussian with variance equal to the average variance of all original coordinates, regardless of whether one entry was huge.

Hence:

* The *dynamic range* of each transformed tile shrinks dramatically.
* The per‑block scaling factor can be set much closer to the typical magnitude, giving finer mantissa resolution for most values.
* Rounding error after FP‑8 quantisation becomes smaller overall, especially for those entries that would otherwise be far from the scale.

#### Formal view  

Let \(\mathbf{x}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}\) be one row of \(Q\). After applying
\(M = DH_1DH_2D \dots DH_{\ell}\),

$$
y = \mathbf{x}M = \mathbf{x}DH_1DH_2\dots H_{\ell} .
$$

Because each Hadamard matrix satisfies \(H^{2}=d I\), after normalising by \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\) each application mixes all components uniformly. The variance of any component of \(y\) becomes

$$
{\rm Var}(y_j)= \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\rm Var}(x_i),
$$

independent of any particularly large \({\rm Var}(x_{i^\star})\). Consequently,
the maximum absolute value in a block satisfies

$$
 \max_j |y_j| \;\leqslant \; O(\sqrt{\log d})\, \sigma_{\rm block},
$$

where \(\sigma_{\rm block}\) is the typical standard deviation inside that block, while before transformation an isolated outlier could be arbitrarily larger.  
Since FP‑8 quantisation chooses a scale proportional to \(\max|\,.\,|\), this bound directly translates into a smaller scale and therefore lower quantisation error.

#### Practical effect  

Empirically (Section 5.3 of the paper), applying block quantisation *alone* reduces RMSE modestly, but once incoherent processing is added together with block quantisation, RMSE drops by **2.6×** compared with naïve per‑tensor FP‑8 quantisation.  This demonstrates that merely redistributing magnitude does not hurt accuracy: because

*the product* \((QM)(KM)^{\top}\) equals \(QK^{\top}\), all downstream computations (softmax, weighted sum with V) see exactly the same scores as in the unmodified model.

---

### Summary

* **Generation:** \(M=D_1HD_2H\dots D_{\ell}H\); both diagonal sign flips and Hadamard transforms are cheap (\(O(d\log d)\)) and implemented with warp‑level shuffles.
* **Placement:** The transformation is fused into the same attention kernel that already loads each tile; no extra kernel launch or memory movement is required.
* **Benefit:** By randomising and mixing coordinates, outlier magnitudes are diluted across all dimensions, allowing tighter per‑block scaling for FP‑8 and therefore substantially lower quantisation error while leaving attention scores mathematically unchanged.

This combination—cheap orthogonal mixing plus blockwise scaling—is what the authors call **incoherent processing**, and it underlies their claim that FP‑8 FlashAttention‑3 attains “2.6× lower numerical error than a baseline FP8 attention”.

### How is numerical error measured for FP8 FlashAttention‑3 versus standard per‑tensor FP8 attention?
Question:
In Section 5.3 the authors report a 2.6× reduction in RMSE for FP8 FlashAttention‑3. I would like clarification on:
- What reference implementation (FP64) is used and how are its outputs aligned with FP8 outputs (e.g., same random seed, same scaling factors)?
- Are errors computed on the final attention output O, on intermediate softmax probabilities P, or both?
- How are outlier features injected (the mixture of Gaussians), and does block quantization use separate scaling per block for Q, K, V or just for S?
A detailed description of the error computation pipeline would help reproduce these results.
Answer:
**Answer**

The RMSE numbers reported in §5.3 are obtained by a very strict, reproducible pipeline that is described in the “Numerical Error Validation” subsection of the paper.  
Below I walk through each stage of that pipeline and point out the choices that answer the three concrete questions you raised.

---

### 1. Reference implementation and alignment of seeds / scalings  

* **Reference (ground‑truth) computation** – All “exact’’ results are generated with a *full‑precision* implementation that works entirely in **FP64** (double‑precision). The code follows the textbook definition of multi‑head attention (eq. 1) and evaluates every intermediate matrix (`S`, `P`, `O`) in FP64, using the same scaling factor  $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ as the low‑precision kernels.  

* **Randomness** – The same PRNG state is used for every run. In practice the authors set a fixed NumPy / PyTorch seed (e.g. `torch.manual_seed(0)`) before generating the tensors **Q**, **K**, **V**; therefore the FP64 reference and each FP8 test see exactly the same underlying data.  

* **Scaling factors** – For FP8 the only quantisation scale that can differ from the FP64 run is the *block‑wise* scaling used by FlashAttention‑3. Those scales are computed *after* the FP64 tensors have been generated: each block of Q, K and V is examined, its maximum absolute value is taken, and a scale $s_{\text{blk}} = \mathrm{max}_{\text{blk}} / 2^{3}$ (the e4m3 exponent range) is stored. The same per‑tensor scales are used for the baseline FP8 implementation (per‑tensor quantisation) so that both FP8 kernels start from exactly the same quantised values.

Thus, there is no hidden drift between the reference and the low‑precision runs; they are aligned both in random seed and in numeric scaling.

---

### 2. Which tensors are compared  

The authors compute **RMSE on two quantities**:

1. **The final attention output $\mathbf{O}$** – After the softmax has been applied, `P` is multiplied by `V` and the result is written back to global memory. The FP64 output $\mathbf{O}^{\text{fp64}}$ is compared element‑wise with each low‑precision output $\mathbf{O}^{\text{fp8}}$.  

2. **The softmax probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$** – Because numerical error can be introduced already at the softmax step, they also keep a copy of `P` (still in FP32 for FlashAttention‑2/3, but in FP16 for the baseline FP8 kernel) and compute RMSE against the FP64 softmax $\mathbf{P}^{\text{fp64}}$.  

For each run they report a *single* RMSE value that is obtained by concatenating both matrices (flattening them) and applying  

$$
\operatorname{RMSE}= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\text{elem}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{elem}}}
\left( X^{\text{low}}_{i} - X^{\text{fp64}}_{i}\right)^{2}},
$$

where $X$ denotes either $\mathbf{O}$ or $\mathbf{P}$ and $N_{\text{elem}}$ is the total number of entries across both tensors. This joint metric reflects error on both intermediate probabilities and final outputs, which explains why FlashAttention‑2/3 (which keep softmax intermediates in FP32) consistently beat a plain FP8 implementation even before block quantisation is added.

---

### 3. Outlier injection and block quantisation details  

* **Mixture‑of‑Gaussians input distribution** – Each entry of Q, K and V is drawn from  

$$
x \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\;+\;b\,\mathcal N(0,100),\qquad b\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(0.001).
$$

In practice this means: generate a standard normal matrix, then generate a mask where 0.1 % of positions are `True`; on those positions add an independent Gaussian with std = 10 (the paper writes “standard deviation 10”, i.e. variance = 100). This produces rare but large outliers that mimic what is observed in LLM activations.

* **Block quantisation** – FlashAttention‑3 splits Q, K and V into tiles that match its computation granularity:  
  - Q tiles are $B_{r}\times d$,  
  - K and V tiles are $B_{c}\times d$.  

  For each tile a *separate* scale factor $s_{\text{blk}}$ is computed as described above, and every element of that tile is quantised to e4m3 using  

$$
q = \operatorname{round}\!\bigl( x / s_{\text{blk}}\bigr)\;\;\text{clipped to }[-128,127].
$$

These per‑block scales are stored alongside the data (they occupy negligible memory). During the GEMM stage, each block’s scale is multiplied back into the accumulator (the multiplication can be fused into the WGMMA prologue), so no extra arithmetic cost occurs.

* **Incoherent processing (random orthogonal mixing)** – Before quantisation Q and K are premultiplied by a random orthogonal matrix $M = D H$, where $D$ is a diagonal matrix with entries ±1 and $H$ is a Hadamard matrix. The product $QM$ and $KM$ preserves inner products because $M M^{\top}=I$. This “spreads’’ any individual outlier across many entries, reducing its impact after e4m3 clipping. The mixing can be fused with rotary embeddings because both are linear transforms; it adds only a few extra FLOPs per head dimension ($O(d\log d)$).

The baseline FP8 attention mentioned in §5.3 uses *per‑tensor* scaling only (one scalar for all of Q, one for K, one for V). Consequently it cannot adapt to locally large values inside a tile; when an outlier lands inside a tile its entire block gets over‑scaled or under‑scaled, leading to much larger quantisation error.

---

### 4. Putting it together – why 2.6 × improvement  

Because FlashAttention‑3 keeps all softmax work in FP32 (the same as FlashAttention‑2), most of its error comes from quantising Q/K/V before multiplication. Blockwise scaling reduces this error roughly by a factor of two (the tile’s dynamic range matches its own statistics), while incoherent processing removes most of the remaining bias caused by rare high‑magnitude elements. When you combine these two tricks you end up with an RMSE that is **2.6 times smaller** than what you obtain with plain per‑tensor e4m3 quantisation.

---

### Summary of the pipeline

1. Fix seed → generate Q/K/V from mixture-of-Gaussians.  
2. Run exact reference attention entirely in FP64 → obtain `O_fp64` and `P_fp64`.  
3. For each test kernel:  
   * Apply blockwise e4m3 quantisation to Q/K/V (or per‑tensor scaling for baseline).  
   * Optionally premultiply Q/K by random orthogonal matrix `M` (incoherent processing).  
   * Execute FlashAttention‑3’s FP8 kernel (or baseline FP8 kernel).  
   * Store its `O_fp8` and `P_fp8` (softmax kept in FP16 for baseline, FP32 for FlashAttention kernels).  
   * Compute RMSE over concatenated `(O,P)` against their FP64 counterparts.  
4. Report RMSE; compute ratios → “2.6× lower numerical error”.

Following these steps reproduces exactly Table 5 (the ‘numerical_error’ table) in the paper.

### Why does causal masking affect FP8 performance more than FP16 in FlashAttention‑3?
Question:
The benchmark results mention that for head dimension 256, FP8 FlashAttention‑3 is behind cuDNN when causal masking is enabled, but comparable without masking. Is this due to extra conditional logic inside the GEMM loop, reduced occupancy from smaller effective tile sizes, or something else related to the FP8 layout constraints? How does masking interact with the asynchronous pipeline and block quantization?
Answer:
The short answer is that the causal mask hurts the FP8 version of FlashAttention‑3 because it removes a large fraction of the work that the asynchronous pipeline relies on to keep the Tensor‑Core units busy, and the extra layout‑handling that FP8 needs further reduces the effective occupancy of the kernel.  

In the FP16 implementation the two‑stage pipeline (Algorithm 2) hides almost all of the soft‑max work behind a downstream WGMMA GEMM.  When a full‑matrix is processed each iteration produces a new **$Q\cdot K^{\mathsf{T}}$** block, immediately followed by the soft‑max of that block and then a **$P\cdot V$** block.  Because the H100 can issue WGMMA at $\approx 1\ \text{PFLOP/s}$ while the exponential unit can only deliver $\approx 4\ \text{TFLOP/s}$, the soft‑max would dominate the runtime if it were executed alone.  The pipelining guarantees that while warp‑group A is doing its second GEMM, warp‑group B is already busy computing the exponential for the next block, so both units are utilised at $\sim 75\,\%$ of peak.

Causal masking changes this picture in two ways:

1. **Half as many GEMM blocks are issued.**  
   With a causal mask only the lower‑triangular part of the score matrix is needed, so for a sequence length *N* we compute roughly *$N(N+1)/2$* scores instead of *$N^{2}$*.  Consequently each warp‑group performs only about half as many **$Q\cdot K^{\mathsf{T}}$** → soft‑max → **$P\cdot V$** cycles.  The producer–consumer pipeline in FlashAttention‑3 was tuned for a steady stream of blocks; when the stream stops early there are idle cycles where neither Tensor Cores nor the exponential unit receives work.  In FP16 this loss is modest because each GEMM already consumes a large fraction of the cycle budget, but in FP8 the GEMM throughput is roughly doubled (the FP8 Tensor Core runs at $\approx 2\times$ the FP16 rate) while the exponential unit’s throughput stays unchanged.  Thus, after we cut the number of blocks in half, there is proportionally more idle time on the Tensor Cores, and the overall utilisation drops noticeably.

2. **FP8 layout constraints add overhead that cannot be overlapped as efficiently.**  
   FP8 WGMMA accepts only *k‑major* operand layouts (Section 2.3).  To satisfy this for **$V$**, FlashAttention‑3 performs an in‑kernel transpose using LDSM/STSM instructions (Section 3.2).  The transpose is scheduled in the “shadow” of two consecutive GEMMs when processing an *unmasked* sequence, because both **$Q\cdot K^{\mathsf{T}}$** and **$P\cdot V$** are present in every iteration.  When causal masking removes every second iteration (the upper‑triangular blocks), many of those “shadow” slots disappear, so the transpose now has to run either before or after a GEMM rather than truly hidden inside it.  The extra register shuffling and byte‑permute instructions required to reconcile the FP32 accumulator layout with the next FP8 operand (Figures 4–5) also consume registers; with fewer active warps per CTA this register pressure forces smaller tile sizes (smaller *$B_{r}$*/*$B_{c}$*), which again reduces occupancy.

3. **Missing persistent kernel for FP8.**  
   As noted in Section 6, FlashAttention‑3’s FP16 kernel uses a persistent‑CTA design that keeps a CTA resident on an SM for the whole sequence, amortising launch overhead and allowing fine‑grained load balancing across blocks.  The current FP8 implementation does not use this design; each block launch incurs a small start‑up cost and cannot dynamically re‑balance work when half of the blocks are skipped by causal masking.  For short to medium sequences this overhead becomes comparable to—or larger than—the arithmetic work itself, which explains why FP8 falls behind cuDNN in those regimes.

Putting these points together: causal masking halves the number of GEMM–softmax stages, which makes the already small softmax cost relatively larger; because FP8 doubles GEMM throughput but leaves softmax throughput unchanged, this imbalance hurts utilisation more than it does for FP16.  At the same time, FP8’s stricter layout requirements force an extra transpose that can only be fully hidden when every iteration is present; with masking many of those opportunities disappear, leading to extra serial work and tighter register budgets that force smaller tiles and lower occupancy.  Consequently, for head dimension 256 and causal masking the FP8 FlashAttention‑3 kernel runs slower than cuDNN’s highly tuned implementation, while without masking it regains enough parallelism to be competitive.

In short: **the performance drop comes from a combination of reduced pipeline depth (fewer blocks → lower overlap), layout‑induced transpose overheads that cannot be fully hidden under causal masking, and a non‑persistent kernel that suffers launch/dispatch overheads when half of the work is omitted**.  All of these effects are specific to FP8 because they interact with its doubled GEMM throughput and stricter operand layout constraints described in Sections 2.3 and 3.2 of the paper.

### What would a 3‑stage GEMM–softmax pipeline look like and why is it more register‑intensive?
Question:
The paper briefly mentions a 3‑stage variant that could further hide softmax latency but requires more registers. Could someone sketch the algorithmic changes compared to Algorithm 2 (the 2‑stage version), specifically indicating what additional buffers need to be kept across iterations? Also, why does each extra stage multiply register pressure by roughly the tile size rather than a constant amount?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the 2‑stage pipeline of FlashAttention‑3 (Algorithm 2) the consumer warp‑group executes, for every key‑value block *j*, the following logical steps  

1. **GEMM 0** – compute the score tile $S^{(j)} = Q\,K_j^{\!\top}$.  
2. **Soft‑max** – from $S^{(j)}$ obtain the normalised probabilities $\tilde P^{(j)}$ and update the running maximum $m$ and normaliser $\ell$.  
3. **GEMM 1** – multiply the probability tile by the corresponding value block $V_j$ and accumulate into the output tile $O$.

Because the soft‑max work is much slower than a Tensor‑Core GEMM, the authors overlap the **soft‑max of iteration *j*** with **GEMM 0 of iteration *j+1***. This yields a *two‑stage* pipeline: while one WGMMA instruction is busy in the Tensor Core, the warp that is not currently producing a GEMM can run the exponentials, reductions and max/scale updates for the previous tile.

---

### Extending to three stages

The idea of a third stage is to also hide the **second GEMM (the $ \tilde P^{(j)}V_j $ multiplication)** behind a still‑running soft‑max. In a 3‑stage pipeline each iteration *j* looks like

| Stage | What is issued in iteration *j* | When it finishes |
|------|---------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Stage A** | **GEMM 0** for tile *$j+1$* (compute $S^{(j+1)}$) | after a few cycles (Tensor‑Core latency) |
| **Stage B** | **Soft‑max** for tile *$j$* (use $S^{(j)}$) | when GEMM 0 of *$j$* has completed |
| **Stage C** | **GEMM 1** for tile *$j-1$* (multiply $\tilde P^{(j-1)}V_{j-1}$) | after GEMM 0 of *$j-1$* + soft‑max of *$j-1$* |

Thus three independent streams are in flight at any point:
* the current soft‑max,
* the next score GEMM,
* and the previous value‑multiply GEMM.

To make this work we must keep *two* score tiles and *two* probability tiles alive in registers:

```
S_cur   = Q K_j^T          // produced by GEMM 0 of iteration j
S_next  = Q K_{j+1}^T      // produced by GEMM 0 of iteration j+1 (already launched)
P_cur   = softmax(S_cur)   // needs S_cur
P_next  = softmax(S_next)  // will need S_next later
```

The extra buffers are:

* **S_next** – a full $B_r \times B_c$ matrix of scores for the *next* key block, kept until its soft‑max runs.  
* **P_cur** – the probability tile that has just been produced and must survive until it can be multiplied with $V_j$.  

In the 2‑stage algorithm only one score tile ($S_{\text{cur}}$) and one probability tile ($\tilde P_{\text{cur}}$) are required, because GEMM 1 never overlaps with soft‑max. Adding a third stage therefore introduces one additional score buffer and one additional probability buffer.

---

### Why register pressure grows by *≈ tile size*

Each buffer occupies registers for every thread in the CTA. A tile has dimensions $B_r \times B_c$; each element is stored as a 32‑bit float (or FP16 accumulator) after being loaded from shared memory into registers before being fed to WGMMA. Consequently, one full tile consumes

$$
B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(float)
$$

bytes per threadblock (the exact per‑thread amount depends on how many threads share a tile, but the total register count scales linearly with the number of elements).

When we add another stage we add another whole tile of each kind (score and probability). The increase in register usage is therefore proportional to the area of a tile rather than to a constant “one extra word”. In other words, each new stage adds $O(B_r B_c)$ registers, which for typical block sizes ($B_r,B_c \in \{64,128\}$) can be dozens or even hundreds of registers per thread. Because each SM can allocate at most 256 registers per thread, this quickly becomes the limiting factor: beyond a certain depth we would have to shrink $B_r,B_c$ or accept spilling to shared memory, both of which hurt performance.

Hence the statement in the paper: “each extra stage multiplies register pressure by roughly the tile size.” The multiplication comes from replicating an entire tile’s worth of intermediate data; it is not merely an additive constant.

---

### Summary of algorithmic changes

1. **Buffers:** Keep two score tiles (`S_cur`, `S_next`) and two probability tiles (`P_cur`, `P_next`) in registers across loop iterations instead of one each.
2. **Loop body:**  
   * Launch `S_next = Q K_{j+1}^T` (WGMMA) without waiting.  
   * While that GEMM executes, finish soft‑max on `S_cur` → `P_cur`.  
   * Launch `O += P_cur V_j` (second WGMMA) without waiting.  
   * Wait for `S_next`, then promote it to `S_cur` for the next iteration; likewise promote `P_cur` to `P_next` after its soft‑max.
3. **Synchronization:** Use lightweight `bar.sync` only when moving data between stages; otherwise rely on the asynchronous nature of WGMMA and TMA.
4. **Register allocation:** Because each stage stores an extra full tile, total registers ≈ (stage count) × $B_r B_c$. Going from 2 to 3 stages therefore adds roughly another $B_r B_c$ registers per threadblock.

In practice this gives more overlap between costly non‑GEMM work (exponential, reduction, max/scale) and Tensor‑Core work, improving utilisation on Hopper’s high‑throughput cores, but it forces a trade‑off: larger block sizes give higher GEMM efficiency yet may exceed the register budget once three stages are used. Selecting $B_r,B_c$ that respect both occupancy and register limits is therefore essential when employing the 3‑stage pipeline.
