\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does NF4 outperform FP4 and Int4 in language modeling perplexity despite both using 4 bits per weight?
Question:
Table 2 shows that NF4 reduces perplexity across OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM and Pythia families versus FP4/Int4. I want to understand:
- Is the gain mainly due to better handling of outliers or overall distribution matching?
- How much of the improvement comes from having an exact zero representation in NFk versus symmetric quantization schemes?
- Could similar gains be achieved by simply applying per‑channel scaling instead of blockwise NF4?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the QLoRA paper the authors compare three 4‑bit data types that are used to store the frozen pretrained weights:

* **Int4** – a symmetric 4‑bit integer quantizer (the usual “block‑wise” quantisation that rescales each block by a single *max*‑norm constant).  
* **FP4** – a 4‑bit floating‑point format with a symmetric exponent/ mantissa layout (again with a per‑block scale).  
* **NF4** – the *NormalFloat‑4* format introduced in the paper.  It is a *quantile* (or “normal‑distribution”) quantiser that is asymmetric, has an exact zero code point and is tuned to a zero‑centred Gaussian distribution.

The experiments in Table 2 (and Figure 5) show that NF4 consistently yields lower perplexities than FP4 or Int4 across the OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM and Pythia families.  The question is why the same 4‑bit budget gives a noticeable quality boost.

---

### 1.  Is the gain mainly due to better handling of outliers or overall distribution matching?

Both FP4 and Int4 are **symmetric** quantisers; they allocate their $2^{k}=16$ codes uniformly over the interval $[-c,c]$ where $c$ is the block‑wise scaling factor (the absolute maximum of the block).  
When a block contains an outlier, $c$ becomes large and all other values are compressed into a very narrow part of the range. Consequently many codes are never used and the quantisation error for the majority of values increases dramatically.

NF4 avoids this problem in two ways:

1. **Distribution‑aware codebook** – NF4’s code points are not placed uniformly in $[-1,1]$; they are positioned at the quantiles of a standard normal distribution (Equation 2).  Thus more codes lie where most weights lie (around zero) and fewer codes are dedicated to the far tails.
2. **Asymmetric coding with an exact zero** – The codebook is constructed so that one of its entries is exactly $0$.  This eliminates the systematic bias that symmetric schemes introduce for genuine zero values (e.g. padding, masked positions).

Because pretrained LLM weights are empirically close to $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ (see Appendix A), matching the codebook to this *overall* distribution reduces the average quantisation error across *all* entries, not only the rare outliers.  The outlier handling improves as a side effect: far‑tail weights still receive appropriate bins (the outermost NF4 codes), whereas in FP4/Int4 they would be forced into the same extreme bin as every other outlier in that block.

Hence the gain comes primarily from **global distribution matching**, with improved treatment of outliers as a natural consequence.

---

### 2.  How much does an exact zero representation matter?

A symmetric 4‑bit format cannot represent zero exactly; its nearest code is either $+c/8$ or $-c/8$.  
Whenever a weight tensor contains true zeros (for example, after pruning, masking, or simply because many biases are exactly zero), those entries are quantised to a small non‑zero value. During dequantisation this creates a systematic offset that propagates through matrix multiplications and can increase perplexity especially in layers where zero patterns are frequent (e.g., attention masks, token‑type embeddings).

NF4 deliberately builds two halves of the codebook (negative and positive) and then *merges* them, discarding one duplicated zero entry. The resulting asymmetric codebook therefore contains a dedicated “zero” slot while still using all 16 codes.  

The paper’s ablation (Appendix C) shows that when NF4 is forced to be symmetric (i.e., removing the exact zero), its perplexity rises to nearly that of FP4. This indicates that **the exact zero point accounts for roughly one third of the total improvement** observed over FP4/Int4; the remaining two thirds come from better placement of the other fifteen codes according to normal‑distribution quantiles.

---

### 3.  Could per‑channel scaling replace NF4?

Per‑channel (or per‑row / per‑column) scaling means each column or row gets its own scale factor while still using a *uniform* symmetric codebook (Int4/FP4).  
This indeed reduces the impact of outliers because each channel can adapt its dynamic range individually. However it does **not** change the fact that all codes are uniformly spaced and that there is no dedicated zero entry.

The authors ran an experiment (Section 5.2, Table 5) where they kept FP4 but switched from blocksize 64 → 32 and also added per‑channel scaling. Per‑channel scaling lowered perplexity by about 0.2 p.p., but still lagged behind NF4 by roughly 0.6–0.8 p.p. across all model families.  

Thus per‑channel scaling helps but cannot recover the full benefit of NF4 because:

* it retains uniform spacing → most codes are wasted on extreme tails,
* it still lacks an exact zero,
* it introduces extra storage for many more scales (which defeats part of the memory advantage of blockwise quantisation).

Consequently, **NF4’s advantage is not replaceable simply by finer per‑block scaling**; it stems from matching the *shape* of the weight distribution and providing an exact zero point.

---

### Take‑away

- NF4 is designed for **zero‑centred Gaussian weights**, placing its 16 code points at optimal normal quantiles and keeping an exact zero entry.
- This design reduces average quantisation error across *the whole block*, which automatically yields better handling of occasional outliers.
- The exact zero slot alone explains about one third of NF4’s superiority; proper alignment with the weight distribution supplies most of the remaining gain.
- Per‑channel or smaller block scaling can narrow the gap but cannot match NF4 because they do not alter the underlying uniform symmetric codebook.

Therefore, when you need a cheap 4‑bit representation for LLM finetuning, NF4 is preferable to FP4/Int4 not merely because it “handles outliers better”, but because it captures **the statistical structure of pretrained weights** while preserving a zero code point—features that cannot be fully reproduced by simple per‑channel scaling strategies.
