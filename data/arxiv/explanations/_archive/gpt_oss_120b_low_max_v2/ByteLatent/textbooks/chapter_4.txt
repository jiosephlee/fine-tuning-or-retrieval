\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 4: # Computational Cost Analysis  

In this chapter we develop a detailed accounting of the floating‑point operations (FLOPs) required by the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)**.  We begin by recalling the standard FLOP formulas for Transformer building blocks—feed‑forward networks and the QKV attention sub‑layers—because they constitute the bulk of the computational budget in any large language model.  We then explain why the byte‑embedding lookup in BLT contributes essentially zero FLOPs, before moving on to a complete breakdown of the per‑patch cost incurred by each component of the BLT architecture: the lightweight **local encoder**, the **global latent transformer**, the **local decoder**, and the two cross‑attention modules that bridge bytes and patches.  With these ingredients in hand we can derive an explicit relationship between the *average patch size* (i.e. the mean number of bytes that are grouped together) and the FLOPs incurred per processed byte, both during training and inference.  Finally we discuss how this relationship enables **inference‑flop‑controlled scaling**: keeping a fixed inference budget while increasing either model capacity or average patch size, a capability that is fundamentally unavailable to conventional token‑based models.

---

## 1. Standard Transformer FLOP Formulas  

A Transformer layer consists of two main computational stages: (i) a multi‑head self‑attention (MHSA) block, and (ii) a position‑wise feed‑forward network (FFN).  Assuming hidden dimension \(h\), sequence length \(m\), number of attention heads \(H\), and feed‑forward expansion factor \(f\) (typically \(f=4\)), the FLOP counts are:

### 1.1 QKV Projection and Attention  
Each token (or patch) produces queries, keys, and values via three linear projections from dimension \(h\) to \(h\).  The projection FLOPs are

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{proj}} = 3 \times (m \cdot h \cdot h).
\]

The scaled dot‑product attention requires forming the matrix product \(QK^{\top}\), applying softmax, and multiplying by \(V\).  The dominant matrix multiplication costs are

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{QK^{\top}} = m^{2} \cdot \frac{h}{H},
\qquad
\text{FLOPs}_{QV}   = m^{2} \cdot \frac{h}{H},
\]

where we have split the hidden dimension evenly across heads; each head handles a sub‑dimension \(h/H\).  The subsequent output projection adds another

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{out}} = m \cdot h \cdot h .
\]

Summing these terms yields the total attention FLOPs per layer:

\[
\boxed{\text{FLOPs}_{\text{attn}}(h,m)=
4mh^{2}+2m^{2}\frac{h}{H}} .
\]

In practice, for modest context windows (e.g., fixed‑width windows of size 512 used in BLT’s local models) the quadratic term is much smaller than the linear term; for the global latent transformer, which operates on *patch* sequences rather than raw bytes, the quadratic term also remains modest because patches are far fewer than bytes.

### 1.2 Feed‑Forward Network  
The FFN applies two linear layers separated by an activation (SwiGLU in BLT).  Its FLOP count per layer is

\[
\boxed{\text{FLOPs}_{\text{FFN}}(h,m)=
2\, m\, h\, f\, h = 8\,m\,h^{2}} ,
\]

since \(f=4\).  This term dominates overall Transformer cost because it is executed for *every* token/patch regardless of attention pattern.

### 1.3 Total Layer Cost  

A full Transformer layer therefore costs

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{layer}}(h,m)=
8mh^{2}+4mh^{2}+2m^{2}\frac{h}{H}
   =12mh^{2}+2m^{2}\frac{h}{H}.
\]

When counting training FLOPs we double this quantity to account for the backward pass, as customary in scaling‑law analyses.

## 2. Zero‑FLOP Byte Embedding Lookup  

BLT reads raw bytes \(\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\) from a corpus, where each byte belongs to a fixed vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\) of size 256.  Instead of projecting each byte through a dense matrix, BLT stores an **embedding table** \(\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{256\times h_{\mathcal{E}}}\) and retrieves rows by index:

```python
x_i = E[byte_i]   # constant‑time table lookup
```

Because a table fetch consists solely of memory reads and no arithmetic on floating point numbers, it contributes **zero FLOPs** under the Chinchilla counting convention used throughout this work.  Consequently, all subsequent FLOP expenditure originates from the three Transformer‐style modules (encoder, global latent transformer, decoder) and their cross‑attention bridges.

---

## 3. Patch‑Level FLOP Breakdown  

Let us denote:

- \(n_{\text{ctx}}\): number of *bytes* presented to BLT in a training or inference window;
- \(n_{p}\): number of *patches* produced by the patching function \(f_{p}\);
- \(\bar{s}= n_{\text{ctx}}/n_{p}\): **average patch size** (bytes per patch);
- \(h_{\mathcal{G}},l_{\mathcal{G}}\): hidden size and depth of the global latent transformer;
- \(h_{\mathcal{E}},l_{\mathcal{E}}\): hidden size and depth of the local encoder;
- \(h_{\mathcal{D}},l_{\mathcal{D}}\): hidden size and depth of the local decoder;
- \(w_{\mathcal{E}}, w_{\mathcal{D}}\): sliding window sizes used for local self‑attention (fixed to 512 bytes in all experiments);
- \(V=256\): output vocabulary size for the byte decoder head.

Below we express each component’s contribution using the generic layer formula from Section 1, substituting appropriate sequence lengths.

### 3.1 Local Encoder  

The encoder processes *bytes* with a short causal window; its sequence length is therefore \(m = w_{\mathcal{E}}\).  
Its total FLOPs per **byte** are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\mathcal{E}} &=
l_{\mathcal{E}}\,
          \bigl[12\,w_{\mathcal{E}}\, h_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}
                +2\,w_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}\frac{h_{\mathcal{E}}}{H_{\mathcal{E}}}
          \bigr] .
\end{aligned}
\]

Because every byte participates in exactly one encoder step regardless of how patches are formed, this term does not depend on average patch size.

### 3.2 Global Latent Transformer  

The global model operates on *patches*. Its effective sequence length is \(m=n_{p}=n_{\text{ctx}}/\bar{s}\). The global FLOPs per **byte** become

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{FL}_{\mathcal{G}}
&=
 \frac{
   l_{\mathcal{G}}\,
   \bigl[12\, n_{p}\, h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
         +2\, n_{p}^{2} \frac{h_{\mathcal{G}}}{H_{\mathcal{G}}}
   \bigr]
 } { n_{\text{ctx}} }                                    \\[4pt]
&=
 l_{\mathcal{G}}\,
 \biggl[
    \frac{
        12\, h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
    }{\bar{s}}
   + 
    \frac{
        2\, h_{\mathcal{G}}
    } { H_{\mathcal{G}} } 
    \frac{
        n_{p} } { n_{\text{ctx}} }
    n_{p}
 \biggr] \\[4pt]
&=
 l_{\mathcal{G}}\,
 \biggl[
    \frac{
        12\, h_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}
    }{\bar{s}}
   + 
    O(\bar{s}^{-2})
 \biggr] .
\end​aligned}
\]

Since BLT chooses *moderate* patch sizes (e.g., 4–8 bytes), the quadratic term is negligible compared with the dominant linear term proportional to \(1/\bar{s}\). Hence **the global model’s per‑byte cost scales inversely with average patch size**.

### 3.3 Local Decoder  

The decoder also works on bytes, but it must predict from a softmax over all possible byte values; therefore its output projection contributes an extra factor that scales with vocabulary size \(V=256\). Its per‑byte FLOP count reads

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{
FL}_{%
   {\scriptstyle
   {\rm decoder}}
&=
l_{\mathcal D}
   \bigl[12\, w_{\mathcal D}\, h_{\mathcal D}^{2}
        +2\, w_{\mathcal D}^{2} 
           \tfrac {h_{\mathcal D}}{{H}_{%
                                               {\scriptstyle 
                                                {\rm D}}
                                           }}
   \bigr]    
\\
&\
+\;\underbrace{
      n_{\text {ctx}}\, V\, h_{{%
                {\rm D}}
             }
      }_{%
         {\rm output~projection~flops}}
\\[4pt]
&=
l_\cD   
      [12 w_\cD h_\cD^2 + O(w_\cD^2)] 
      + V h_\cD .
% note: divided by n_ctx later gives per-byte
%
\\[4pt]
&=
      \frac{
         l_\cD [12 w_\cD h_\cD^2 ] + V h_\cD
       } { n_\mathrm{{ctx}} }
      .
%
\end​aligned}
\]

Again this term does not depend on patch granularity because every byte must be emitted.

### 3.4 Cross‑Attention Between Bytes and Patches  

Both encoder–to–latent and latent–to–decoder cross‐attention modules aggregate information across variable‐size groups. Their FLOP formulas follow Eqn (1) but with asymmetric query/key/value counts. For a given module we let:

- \(r = n_{q}/n_{k}=k/n_{p}\), where \(k>1$ is typically set to 2 in BLT,
- “queries’’ have dimension \(h_{q}=h_{\star}\) (global or decoder size),
- “keys/values’’ have dimension \(h_{kv}=h_{{{\rm local}}}\).

The cross‑attention cost per **byte** can be written compactly as

| Module | Query length | Key/Value length | Per‑byte FLOP |
|--------|--------------|------------------|---------------|
| Encoder Cross‑Attention | \(n_{p}\) | \(k=n_{p}r=n_{p}(k/n_{p})=k)\) | \(\displaystyle 
      l_\cE
      \Bigl[12\, n_{p} h_\cG^2 +
            2\, n_{p} k      \tfrac {h_\cG}{H_\cE} 
      \Bigr]\! / n_\mathrm{{ctx}}\) |
| Decoder Cross‑Attention | \(k=n_{p}r=k)\) | \(n_p]\) | same form with $(h_\cD,h_\cG)$ swapped |

Because each cross-attention step processes at most one patch’s worth of bytes, its contribution scales linearly with **the number of patches**, i.e., inversely with average patch size:

\[
\null
    \text{
FL}_{%
   {\scriptstyle 
    {\rm cross}}
     = O(1/\bar{s}) .
    }
    
Thus both cross-attention modules follow exactly the same scaling pattern as the global latent transformer.

### 3.5 Full Per‑Byte FLOP Expression  

Collecting all pieces from Sections 3.1–3.4 yields BLT’s total FLOPs per processed byte:

\[
\boxed{
%
    {\rm FL}_{ {\rm BLT} } =
     {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm encoder}}}
   + {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm decoder }}}
   + {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm global }}}
   + {\rm FL}_{{}^{ }\!\!\!{\scriptstyle{\rm cross }}}
 } .
%
\]

Explicitly,

\[
{\rm FL}_{ {\rm BLT} }=
 l_{\cE}[12 w_{\cE} h^2_\cE ]
+ l_{\cD}[12 w_\cD h^2_\cD]+\tfrac {V h_\cD}{n_\mathrm{{ctx}}}
+ 
        O(1/\bar{s}) .
        
In words: *the only terms that depend on how we group bytes are those belonging to components that see patches rather than raw bytes*. All other computations—local encoding/decoding—are invariant to average patch size because they always run once per byte.

## 4. Relationship Between Average Patch Size and Inference Cost  

During inference we typically drop gradients, halving all training costs above. The dominant variable portion becomes

> **Per‑byte inference FLOPs**  
> 
> \( {\displaystyle
>     {\rm FL}^{(\text {inf})}_{\<{\rm BLT}>}
>     = C_0 
>       + C_1/\bar{s},
>     }
>   
where  
\(C_0 = l_{\cE}[6 w_{\cE} h^2_\cE ]+ l_\cD[6 w_\cD h^2_\cD]+\tfrac {V h_\cD}{n_\mathrm{{ctx}}}\) is independent of patching,
and   
\(C_1 = l_{\cG}[6 n_p h^2_\cG] + (\text {cross attn terms}) = O(l_G h_G^2)\).

Thus **doubling average patch size roughly halves** those portions of computation that involve patches—the global latent transformer and both cross-attention blocks. This inverse relationship explains why BLT can achieve up to a 50 % reduction in inference flops when moving from an average patch size of about 4 bytes (similar to Llama 3’s token length of ≈ 4 bytes) to ≈ 8 bytes: 

| Avg Patch Size | Global‐Transformer Ratio |
|----------------|--------------------------|
| ~4 bytes       | baseline                 |
| ~6 bytes       | ≈ 0.66×                  |
| ~8 bytes       | ≈ 0.50×                  |

Because **the encoder/decoder cost remains constant**, once patches become sufficiently large most of their contribution drops out relative to these fixed costs; at very large patches (> 10 bytes) additional gains diminish because $C_0$ dominates.

## 5. Inference‑Flop Controlled Scaling  

The inverse dependence on $\bar{s}$ affords a novel scaling knob: **maintain a strict inference flop budget while simultaneously increasing model capacity**.

### 5.1 Fixed Inference Budget  

Suppose we allocate $F$ inference FLOPs per generated byte (e.g., $F=10^9$ flops for an online service). From Eqn (5),

```
F = C_0 + C_1 / s_bar .
```

If we enlarge any hidden dimension—say increase $h_{\cG}$ or depth $l_{\cG}$—we inevitably increase $C_1$. To keep $F$ constant we must raise $\bar{s}$ accordingly:

```
s_bar_new = C_1_new / (F - C_0).
```

Thus larger models can be paired with larger patches without exceeding our budget.

### 55️⃣ Practical Example  

Consider two BLT configurations trained with identical numbers of training flops:

| Config | $l_G$ | $h_G$ | Avg Patch Size $\bar{s}$ | Inference Flops/Byte |
|--------|------|------|-----------------------------|----------------------|
| A – “small” | 12 | 1024 | 4.5 bytes | $9.8\times10^8$ |
| B – “large” | 24 | 2048 | 8 bytes   | $9.7\times10^8$ |

Even though Config B has four times more parameters in its global transformer, raising $\bar{s}$ from 4.5 to 8 reduces $C_1$ enough that total inference flops stay essentially unchanged—this is precisely what Figure 5 (“fixed inference scaling”) demonstrates empirically for BLT versus token-based baselines.

### 5.3 Why Token Models Cannot Replicate This Tradeoff  

In conventional BPE systems:
- The *vocabulary* fixes both average token length (\(\approx4 .\!7`\) bytes for Llama 3) **and** output dimension (\(|V|\)).  
- Enlarging model width/depth inevitably raises *all* components’ FLOPs because there is no separate “patch” mechanism; each token still incurs full attention+FFN cost.
- To reduce flops one must reduce token count, which can only be done by *increasing* token length via a larger vocabulary—a move that inflates embedding tables and final projection layers dramatically.

Therefore token-based models lack an orthogonal “patch size” degree of freedom: any change in model capacity unavoidably alters inference cost.

---

## 6. Summary  

We derived explicit formulas for every computational constituent of BLT—the local encoder/decoder, global latent transformer, and bidirectional cross-attention—and highlighted how only those components that process *patches* depend on average patch size $\bar{s}$. Because $\bar{s}$ appears in denominator form ($C_1/\bar{s}$), enlarging patches yields linear reductions in per‑byte inference flops without sacrificing any part of the fixed cost $C_0$. This relationship underpins **inference-flop controlled scaling:** with a fixed flop budget one may grow model depth or hidden dimension while compensating by using larger patches; such simultaneous scaling is impossible for standard tokenizer-based models whose token granularity is static and whose vocabularies impose an unavoidable tradeoff between sequence length and embedding matrix size.

The analysis confirms why BLT can match or exceed token-based performance at up to eight billion parameters while spending up to half as many flops during generation—a key empirical observation reported throughout this work—and it provides a principled framework for future research seeking even larger models under strict latency or energy constraints.
