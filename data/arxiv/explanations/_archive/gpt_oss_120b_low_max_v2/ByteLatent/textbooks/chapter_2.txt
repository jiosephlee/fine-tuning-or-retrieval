\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 2: # From Bytes to Patches: Dynamic Segmentation Strategies  

In modern large language models (LLMs) the raw text is almost always first transformed by a tokenizer, which groups the underlying bytes into a fixed‑size vocabulary of *tokens*.  Tokens are static: once a vocabulary has been constructed, every occurrence of a particular byte sequence is always mapped to the same token, regardless of its surrounding context.  While this design has enabled impressive performance at scale, it also imposes a rigid allocation of computation—each token receives the same amount of model processing, even though some tokens are trivial to predict (e.g., punctuation) while others are highly ambiguous (e.g., the first character of a new word).  

The **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** eliminates this pre‑processing step and instead works directly on raw bytes.  Crucially, BLT does not operate on each byte individually; instead it *dynamically* groups consecutive bytes into **patches**.  A patch is a variable‑length chunk of the byte stream that is fed as a single unit to the global latent transformer.  By allowing the size of a patch to adapt to the difficulty of predicting the next byte, BLT can devote more compute where it matters most and skip unnecessary work where predictions are easy.  This chapter provides a complete, self‑contained exposition of the four patching schemes considered in BLT, how they are formalized, and why they improve efficiency relative to static tokenization.

---

## Formal definition of a patching function  

Let  

\[
\mathbf{x}= \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n}, \qquad x_i \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{byte}} = \{0,\dots ,255\}
\]

be a sequence of $n$ raw bytes.  
A **patching function** $f_{p}$ maps this byte sequence into a shorter sequence of *patches*

\[
\mathbf{p}= \{p_j\}_{j=1}^{m}, \qquad m < n,
\]

by indicating for each position $i$ whether it marks the beginning of a new patch.  Formally we define an indicator vector  

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } x_i \text{ starts a new patch},\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\quad i=1,\dots ,n,
\]

with $b_1=1$ by convention.  The set of patch boundaries $\{t_j\}$ follows from the indices where $b_i=1$, and each patch $p_j$ consists of the contiguous bytes  

\[
p_j = \bigl(x_{t_j},\, x_{t_j+1},\,\dots ,\, x_{t_{j+1}-1}\bigr).
\]

The **average patch size**  

\[
\bar{s} = \frac{n}{m}
\]

directly determines the number of steps executed by BLT’s global transformer; larger patches mean fewer transformer steps and consequently lower FLOP consumption per byte (see Section § “FLOPs Estimation” in the paper).  Any viable patching scheme must therefore balance two competing desiderata:

1. **Predictive adequacy** – patches should be short enough that their internal bytes are not too heterogeneous in information density; otherwise a single transformer step would be forced to model highly uncertain predictions.
2. **Computational efficiency** – patches should be as long as possible in low‑entropy regions, so that unnecessary transformer steps are avoided.

Below we describe three concrete families of $f_p$ that satisfy these constraints to varying degrees.

---

## Static (strided) patching and its drawbacks  

The simplest $f_p$ partitions the byte stream into *fixed‑size* chunks; this is called **strided** or **static** patching.  Given an integer stride $k$, we set

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } i \equiv 1 \pmod{k},\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus every patch contains exactly $k$ bytes (except possibly the final one).  This approach mirrors the “MegaByte” architecture [Yu et al., 2023] and has three appealing practical properties:

* **Determinism:** No preprocessing beyond slicing is required.
* **Explicit flop control:** Because $\bar{s}=k$, we can directly tune inference cost by choosing $k$.
* **Implementation simplicity:** Both training and inference pipelines can treat patches as regular tokens.

However, static patching suffers from fundamental inefficiencies:

| Issue | Explanation |
|------|--------------|
| **No dynamic compute allocation** | All patches receive an identical amount of transformer work regardless of how predictable their contents are. Predicting whitespace or repeated characters therefore wastes computation, while predicting high‑entropy tokens (e.g., the first character after a space) may not receive enough capacity. |
| **Inconsistent segmentation of identical substrings** | The same word may be split at different positions depending on its location in the stream, breaking any potential reuse of learned sub‑word patterns. |
| **Inflexible trade‑off between context length and flop budget** | Increasing $k$ reduces FLOPs but also shortens effective context for the global transformer, which can degrade performance when modeling long‑range dependencies. |

Empirically, static strided patching lags behind tokenization‑based baselines when scaling to billions of parameters (see Figure 5 left in the paper).  These shortcomings motivate more context‑aware strategies.

## Space (whitespace) patching  

A modest refinement over strided patching is **space patching**, introduced by [Slagle et al., 2024].  The intuition is that whitespace (or any “space‑like” byte) frequently demarcates linguistic units such as words or tokens in many scripts.  Hence we define a *space‑like* set $\mathcal{S}\subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\text{byte}}$ that includes any byte that is *not* a Latin letter, digit, or UTF‑8 continuation byte; each patch must contain at least one non‑space‑like byte.  

The indicator becomes

\[
b_i = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } x_i \in \mathcal{S},\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad 
b_1 = 1.
\]

Effectively we start a new patch after every whitespace character.  This yields patches that correspond roughly to words (or their equivalent in languages using spaces as delimiters).  The advantages are:

* **Contextual coherence:** All occurrences of the same word are consistently grouped together across different sequences.
* **Targeted compute:** Hard predictions—typically those occurring immediately after a space because they narrow down possible continuations—receive a dedicated transformer step.

Nevertheless, space patching still has limitations:

* **Language bias:** Not all writing systems use space as a delimiter (e.g., Chinese, Japanese), so the heuristic breaks down for many multilingual corpora.
* **Fixed granularity:** Once a space occurs, we must begin a new patch; we cannot further coalesce low‑entropy subsequences within long words (e.g., common suffixes).

Consequently, while space patching improves over static striding, it does not fully exploit entropy variations inside words or across domains.

## Entropy‑based patching using a small byte LM  

The most data‑driven approach introduced in BLT is **entropy patching**, which leverages an auxiliary *byte‑level* language model (“entropy model”) to estimate how uncertain each next‑byte prediction is.  Let $p_e(\cdot\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i})$ denote the distribution over the next byte output by this small LM.  The *Shannon entropy* for position $i$ is

\[
H(x_i) = -\,\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{byte}}} p_e(x_i=v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i}) \,\log p_e(x_i=v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i}).
\tag{1}
\]

High entropy indicates that many possible continuations are plausible; low entropy signals that the next byte is nearly determined by its context.

### Deriving boundaries from entropies  

Two complementary criteria are used to decide whether a byte should start a new patch:

1. **Global threshold constraint**  
   A fixed scalar $\theta_g$ is chosen; if $H(x_t) > \theta_g$, we mark $x_t$ as a boundary:
   \[
   H(x_t) > \theta_g .
   \tag{2}
   \]
2. **Approximate monotonicity constraint**  
   Within any given patch we expect entropy to decline as more bytes become available (the model becomes more certain).  If this monotonic decay is violated beyond a tolerance $\theta_r$, we cut:
   \[
   H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r .
   \tag{3}
   \]

During data loading we compute entropies for every position using the pretrained entropy model and then apply either (2), (3), or both simultaneously to produce binary boundary indicators $b_i$.  The result is *incremental*: boundaries depend only on past context $\mathbf{x}_{<i}$ and thus can be decided during generation without looking ahead.

### Practical considerations  

* **Threshold selection** – Prior to training BLT, we sweep over candidate $\theta_g$ or $\theta_r$ values on a validation subset and select those yielding a desired average patch size $\bar{s}$ (e.g., $\approx4.5$ bytes).  
* **Context reset** – For very long documents entropy can drift because the auxiliary LM’s hidden state accumulates information unrelated to local uncertainty.  In large‐scale runs we periodically reset its context at line breaks and rely primarily on the monotonicity constraint; this mitigates “entropy drift” while preserving dynamic adaptivity.
* **Efficiency** – Computing entropies once during dataloading incurs negligible overhead compared with full attention over all bytes; it replaces an expensive per‑byte attention cost with cheap lookups from the pretrained entropy model.

Empirically, entropy‐based patches outperform both static striding and space‐only heuristics across all scaling regimes (Figures 5–7 in the paper).  They allocate longer patches to highly predictable regions (e.g., repeated code fragments) and short patches around unpredictable junctures such as the first character after punctuation or in mathematical expressions.

## Threshold selection: global vs. monotonic constraints  

Choosing an appropriate threshold directly influences both model efficiency and accuracy:

| Criterion | Effect on Patch Size | Typical Use Cases |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Global entropy threshold $\theta_g$** | Larger $\theta_g$ ⇒ fewer boundaries ⇒ longer patches; smaller $\theta_g$ ⇒ many short patches | Works well when overall data distribution has relatively stable entropy levels (e.g., homogeneous prose). |
| **Monotonicity gap $\theta_r$** | Controls sensitivity to local spikes in entropy; useful when overall entropy slowly decays within words but occasional spikes indicate concept changes | Preferred for heterogeneous domains where entropy fluctuates sharply within short spans (e.g., code mixed with natural language). |

In practice BLT often combines both: first enforce monotonicity to avoid pathological growth within low‑entropy stretches, then apply a global ceiling to ensure overall flop budget remains bounded.  The combined rule can be expressed as

\[
b_t = 
\begin{cases}
1 & H(x_t) > \theta_g
      \;\;\textbf{or}\;\;
      H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r ,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\tag{4}
\]

During hyperparameter search we monitor average $\bar{s}$ and adjust $(\theta_g,\theta_r)$ until target FLOPs per token align with our inference budget.

## Incremental patching vs. BPE tokenization  

A key theoretical distinction between *patches* and conventional *subword tokens* lies in **incrementality**:

A patching function $f_p$ is *incremental* if it satisfies

\[
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i},
\qquad
\forall i,
\tag{5}
\]

meaning that when processing only the prefix up to position $i$, one obtains exactly the same set of patches as one would obtain by first segmenting the whole sequence and then discarding later patches.  Entropy‐based and space‐based schemes both satisfy (5) because their decisions rely solely on past bytes.

Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), by contrast, does not enjoy this property: tokenizing a prefix may yield different tokens depending on future characters because BPE merges frequent byte pairs greedily based on statistics computed over whole strings. Consequently BPE fails incremental consistency:

* A word “reading” may be tokenized as `read` + `##ing` when seen alone,
* but as `readi` + `ng` when followed by “ness”.

Thus BPE models cannot determine at generation time whether they have reached a token boundary without peeking ahead—a requirement incompatible with left‑to‑right autoregressive decoding.

BLT’s incremental nature enables *online* boundary decisions during inference: after generating each byte we simply consult its precomputed entropy or whitespace flag to decide whether to invoke the costly global latent transformer for a new patch or remain within the lightweight local encoder/decoder path.  This leads directly to substantial inference‐flop savings: longer low‑entropy stretches bypass expensive computation altogether.

---

### Concluding remarks  

Dynamic segmentation via patches represents a paradigm shift from fixed vocabularies toward *compute‑aware* processing of raw text.  By formalizing patches as context‑dependent groupings determined either by simple heuristics (whitespace) or by information‑theoretic measures (entropy), BLT achieves two complementary goals:

1. **Efficiency:** Average FLOPs per processed byte drop dramatically because many predictable regions are collapsed into single transformer steps.
2. **Expressiveness:** High‑entropy regions still receive fine‐grained modeling power via short patches, preserving—or even improving—downstream performance relative to tokenized models.

The remainder of this textbook will build upon these foundations: Chapter X will detail how BLT’s encoder–decoder architecture consumes these patches, while later chapters explore scaling laws, robustness gains, and practical implementation tricks required for training billion‑parameter models on raw bytes.
