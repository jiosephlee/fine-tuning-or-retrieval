\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: **Building a Large‑Scale Math Corpus from the Web**

---

### Seed Corpus Selection (OpenWebMath) and fastText Classifier Basics  

The first step in constructing a high‑quality mathematical pre‑training dataset is to obtain a reliable seed of math‑rich documents.  The authors of *DeepSeekMath* chose **OpenWebMath** as this seed because it is a curated collection of web pages that already exhibit strong mathematical content.  Using this seed, they trained a lightweight text‑classification model based on **fastText** \citep{joulin2016fasttext}.  

FastText is particularly suited for this task for three reasons.  First, it represents each document as an average of word‑level n‑gram embeddings, allowing rapid inference over billions of pages.  Second, its training procedure is extremely efficient: the authors sampled **500 k** positive examples (randomly drawn from OpenWebMath) and an equal number of negative examples (randomly drawn from the broader Common Crawl).  The model was trained with a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of 0.1, 3‑gram word features, a minimum word count of three, and three epochs of stochastic gradient descent.  After training, the classifier outputs a real‑valued score indicating how “math‑like’’ a page is; higher scores correspond to stronger mathematical relevance.

With this classifier in hand, the authors performed an initial recall over a deduplicated subset of Common Crawl amounting to **40 billion** HTML pages.  By ranking pages according to their fastText scores and keeping only the top‑ranked pages, they obtained a first batch of candidate mathematical documents.  The size of this batch was later calibrated by pre‑training experiments that measured downstream performance when using the top 40 B, 80 B, 120 B and 160 B tokens; for the initial iteration they settled on the top **40 B tokens**, which already yielded noticeable gains on downstream mathematical benchmarks.

---

### Iterative Retrieval, Ranking, and Domain‑Level Expansion  

A single pass of classification is insufficient because the initial positive set (OpenWebMath) does not encompass the full diversity of web‑hosted mathematics.  After the first recall many mathematically relevant pages remained undiscovered.  To address this, the authors introduced an **iterative expansion loop** that gradually enriches the seed and refines the classifier.

1. **Domain grouping** – The entire Common Crawl corpus was partitioned into disjoint domains defined by their base URL (e.g., all pages under `mathoverflow.net`).  
2. **Domain‑level math prevalence** – For each domain the fraction of pages already captured in the previous iteration was computed.  Domains where **more than 10 %** of their pages were retrieved were deemed “math‑related.”  
3. **Manual URL annotation** – Human annotators inspected these math‑related domains and explicitly marked sub‑paths that host mathematical content (for instance `mathoverflow.net/questions`).  
4. **Seed augmentation** – Pages reachable through these newly annotated URLs but not yet collected were added to the positive training set.  

The augmented seed now contains a broader variety of styles—ranging from question–answer forums to lecture notes—so a new fastText model can be trained with improved recall.  This process was repeated **four times**, each time expanding the pool of math pages and retraining the classifier on a richer positive set.

After the fourth iteration, the authors had collected **35.5 million** web pages amounting to roughly **120 billion tokens** of mathematical text.  They observed that the marginal gain between the third and fourth iterations fell below 2 %, indicating that further iterations would be unlikely to bring substantial new material; consequently they stopped data collection at this point.

---

### Deduplication, Token Budgeting, and Stopping Criteria  

Handling billions of web pages requires aggressive deduplication to avoid wasteful training on redundant content.  The authors applied two complementary techniques:

* **URL‑based deduplication** – Pages sharing identical URLs or exact URL prefixes were collapsed, eliminating exact copies produced by different crawl snapshots.
* **Near‑deduplication** – Using similarity thresholds on tokenized text (e.g., shingling or MinHash), near‑identical pages differing only by small boilerplate changes were also merged.

After deduplication the corpus shrank to **40 B HTML pages**, which served as the pool for guided retrieval.  

The overall **token budget**—the amount of text ultimately fed into pre‑training—was managed by progressively increasing the token ceiling in successive experiments (40 B → 80 B → 120 B → 160 B).  The authors monitored downstream performance on a suite of mathematical benchmarks after each pre‑training run; performance improvements plateaued once roughly **120 B tokens** had been consumed, providing an empirical stopping signal for corpus growth.

---

### Decontamination: Preventing Benchmark Leakage  

A critical concern when building a pre‑training corpus from openly available web data is inadvertent inclusion of benchmark test items, which would artificially inflate evaluation scores.  The authors therefore instituted a rigorous **decontamination pipeline** modeled after earlier work on code corpora \citep{deepseek-coder}.

* They compiled all evaluation benchmark texts across English (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese (CMATH, AGIEval) sources.
* For any web page in their candidate pool they scanned for exact n‑gram overlaps with benchmark texts:
  * If a page contained any **10‑gram** that matched verbatim any substring from any benchmark, that page was discarded.
  * For shorter benchmark excerpts (3–9 grams), an exact match also triggered removal.
* This rule ensured that even partial leakage—such as an entire problem statement appearing in source material—could not survive into training data.

Through this thorough filtering step, the final DeepSeekMath Corpus is guaranteed not to contain test items from any of the benchmarks used later for evaluation.

### Comparative Analysis with MathPile, Proof‑Pile‑2, and OpenWebMath  

To assess whether their newly built corpus offers advantages over existing public math corpora, the authors conducted controlled pre‑training experiments using a **1.3 B parameter language model** (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B).  Each model was trained for an equal number of tokens (**150 B**) on one of four corpora:

| Corpus | Origin & Composition | Approx. Size |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|
| **DeepSeekMath Corpus** | FastText filtered Common Crawl + multilingual expansion | **120 B tokens** |
| MathPile | Multi‑source (textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CC, StackExchange, arXiv); >85 % arXiv | 8.9 B tokens |
| OpenWebMath | Directly filtered CC for math | 13.6 B tokens |
| Proof‑Pile‑2 | Mix of OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack (code), arXiv with ratio 2:4:1 | ~42 B tokens |

All models employed identical training hyperparameters: AdamW optimizer with \(\beta_1{=}0.9\), \(\beta_2{=}0.95\), weight decay \(0.1\), peak learning rate \(5.3\times10^{-4}\), batch size \(4\) M tokens and context length \(4\) K.

**Results across eight downstream benchmarks (both English and Chinese)** demonstrated three salient points:

1. **Higher Quality:** Models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperformed those trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath or Proof‑Pile‑2 when evaluated via few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting.  Even at relatively low token exposure (≈50 B tokens), DeepSeekMath’s learning curve was steeper than Proof‑Pile‑2’s full‐epoch curve.
   
2. **Multilingual Benefit:** Because the retrieval pipeline did not limit itself to English domains—domains such as Chinese educational portals were also captured—the DeepSeekMath Corpus improved performance on Chinese benchmarks (e.g., CMATH) whereas predominantly English corpora sometimes degraded Chinese results.

3. **Scale Advantage:** The sheer volume (120 B tokens) allowed continued gains beyond what smaller corpora could deliver; baseline corpora quickly saturated after multiple passes over their limited data, producing diminishing returns while DeepSeekMath maintained upward progress throughout its token budget.

These comparative experiments validated that publicly available Common Crawl material harbors abundant high‐quality mathematical text when coupled with a carefully engineered selection pipeline; further, multilingual inclusion yields measurable benefits for non‑English tasks—a property lacking in many prior math corpora.
