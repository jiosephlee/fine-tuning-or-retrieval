\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# How DeepSeekMath Turned 7 B Parameters into a Math Powerhouse  

Open‑source language models have long lagged behind the commercial giants when it comes to solving math problems. DeepSeekMath 7B flips that narrative: a model with only 7 billion parameters reaches **51.7 %** on the competition‑level **MATH** benchmark—roughly the level of GPT‑4 and Gemini‑Ultra—without any external toolkits or voting tricks. The secret isn’t a larger model; it’s a data‑centric recipe built on three main ideas:

1. **A fast, high‑precision web‑crawling pipeline that harvests 120 B math tokens from Common Crawl.**  
2. **A multilingual corpus that lifts both English and Chinese reasoning.**  
3. **A lightweight reinforcement‑learning step (GRPO) that fine‑tunes the already math‑savvy model.**  

Below we break down each component, explain why it works, and point out the lessons that other open‑source projects can steal.

---

## 1. Mining Math from the Open Web – The FastText Pipeline  

### Why Common Crawl matters  
Common Crawl (CC) contains petabytes of raw HTML pages, many of which host high‑quality mathematics content (forum discussions, lecture notes, problem sets). Prior work (e.g., Minerva) used only a few billion tokens from CC; DeepSeekMath scales this up **sevenfold**.

### Step‑by‑step data selection  

| Phase | What happens | Why it matters |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Seed creation** | Start with **OpenWebMath** (≈13 M pages). This gives us a clean, high‑precision set of math pages to train on. | Provides positive examples for the classifier. |
| **FastText training** | Train a fastText text classifier on 500 k positive (seed) vs 500 k random CC pages (negative). Hyper‑parameters: dim = 256, lr = 0.1, n‑gram = 3, epochs = 3. | FastText is lightweight yet captures token n‑gram patterns typical of math notation (“∑”, “∫”, “proof”). |
| **First recall** | Run the classifier over ~40 B deduplicated CC pages and keep the top‑scoring ones; this yields roughly 40 B tokens of math‐like text. | Gives an initial large pool while controlling noise. |
| **Domain expansion** | Group pages by domain (same base URL). If >10 % of a domain’s pages were selected in the first pass, label the domain as “math”. Manually annotate URLs within those domains (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/*`) and add the newly discovered pages back into the seed set. | Captures whole sites that were under‑represented in the seed corpus (e.g., university lecture portals). |
| **Iterative refinement** | Retrain fastText with the enriched seed and repeat recall. After four iterations we end up with **35.5 M pages ≈ 120 B tokens**. The fourth round adds <2 % new data, so we stop. | Each iteration expands coverage while keeping precision high—almost all new pages are genuinely mathematical. |

### Decontamination – no benchmark leakage  
Before feeding the data into training, any sentence containing an exact 10‑gram match to test sets (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval) is stripped out. Shorter n‑grams (3–9) are also checked for exact matches when they appear in benchmark examples shorter than ten tokens. This removes any chance that the model has simply memorized test questions.

### What we learned  

- **Scale + quality beats “more papers”**: Even though arXiv papers are abundant, they contributed little to downstream performance (see Section 4 of the paper). The web pages harvested by FastText contain richer explanatory text and step‑by‑step solutions than dense LaTeX abstracts.
- **FastText is enough**: A simple linear classifier with n‑gram features reliably separates math from non‑math web text; there was no need for heavyweight transformers at this stage.
- **Iterative human validation matters**: Adding a small manual annotation step after each iteration dramatically improved recall without sacrificing precision.

## 2. Multilingual Boost – Chinese Data Helps English Math Too  

Most existing math corpora are English‑only; DeepSeekMath deliberately kept every language that appeared in CC after filtering. The final corpus broke down roughly as:

- **≈70 % English**
- **≈25 % Chinese**
- **≈5 % other languages (mostly code comments or mixed snippets)**

### Impact on benchmarks  

| Benchmark | Language | Base model score (few‑shot CoT) | DeepSeekMath‑Base improvement |
|-----------|----------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| GSM8K (EN) | English | ~64 % (baseline) | +18 % → 82.9 % |
| MATH (EN) | English | ~36 % (baseline) | +15 % → 46.8 % |
| CMATH (ZH) | Chinese | ~30 % (baseline) | +30 % → 60+ % |
| Gaokao‑MathCloze (ZH) | Chinese | ~45 % (baseline) | +20 % → 65+ % |

The multilingual mix not only lifted Chinese tests but also gave a modest bump on English ones—likely because the model learns universal mathematical phrasing (“prove that …”, “show that …”) across languages, making its internal reasoning patterns more robust.

### Why it works  

- **Cross‑lingual pattern transfer**: Even when the problem statement is in English, many LaTeX fragments (`\int`, `\sum`, `\frac`) remain identical across languages; exposure to varied surrounding text helps the model disambiguate symbols.
- **Balancing data bias**: Purely English pretraining tends to overfit to Western curricula; adding Chinese K‑12 problems diversifies problem styles (different wording, diagram descriptions), which surprisingly improves performance on standard U.S./EU benchmarks.

## 3. From Pretraining to Fine–Tuning – The Instruction & RL Steps  

### Instruction tuning with chain/ program of thought  

- **Dataset size:** 776 K examples spanning GSM8K, MATH, MathInstruct, Lila-OOD and a massive Chinese K‑12 collection.
- **Formats:** chain‑of‑thought (CoT), program‑of‑thought (PoT) for Python usage, and tool‐integrated reasoning.
- **Result:** DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B surpasses all open‐source 7B models and competes with closed‐source 70B instruction‐tuned models; on MATH it scores **46.8 %**, already a record for its size class.

### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) – a leaner PPO  

Standard PPO requires a separate critic network to estimate *value* at every token—a heavy memory burden for LLMs. GRPO sidesteps this by:

1. Sampling a *group* of \(G\) completions for each question from the current policy.
2. Computing rewards for each completion with a learned reward model.
3. Using the *group average* as a baseline; each sample’s advantage is its reward minus this baseline.
4. Adding an explicit KL term between policy and reference model rather than embedding it in per-token rewards.

Because no extra value network is needed, GRPO reduces GPU memory usage by roughly half while still delivering strong variance reduction.

#### Training details  

- **Group size:** \(G = 64\) samples per question  
- **Reward model:** trained on pairwise comparisons derived from correctness judgment on GSM8K/MATH; updated iteratively via replay buffer  
- **Learning rate:** \(1\times10^{-6}\) for policy; KL weight \( \beta = 0.04\)

#### Gains  

| Metric | Before GRPO (Instruct) | After GRPO (RL) |
|--------|-----------------------|-----------------|
| GSM8K CoT accuracy | 82.9 % | **88.2 %** |
| MATH CoT accuracy   | 46.8 % | **51.7 %** |
| CMATH CoT accuracy   | ~60 %  | **~68–70 %** |

The improvements are not just due to more epochs—the same data run through plain supervised fine‐tuning stalls at ~48 % on MATH, while GRPO pushes it past 51 %.

## 4. Takeaways for Building Your Own Math LLM  

1️⃣ **Start with a clean seed and let a cheap classifier expand it iteratively.**  
   - FastText + manual URL curation = high recall with minimal compute.

2️⃣ **Don’t overvalue arXiv papers for math pretraining.**  
   - Experiments showed no measurable benefit on GSM8K/MATH/miniF2F even after hundreds of billions of arXiv tokens.

3️⃣ **Multilingual data is cheap and powerful.**  
   - A modest fraction of Chinese problems lifted both Chinese and English benchmarks.

4️⃣ **Code pretraining helps later math reasoning**, especially for program‐of‐thought tasks where Python libraries (`sympy`, `numpy`) are used.

5️⃣ **GRPO offers PPO-level gains without training an extra critic**, making RL feasible even on single RTX 4090 cards.

6️⃣ **Reinforcement learning primarily reshapes *output distribution* rather than pure competence.*  
   - Pass@K stays flat while Maj@K improves: RL nudges more correct answers into the top few samples—exactly what you want when you can sample multiple candidates and pick the best.

---

## 5. Roadmap – What Still Needs Work  

- **Geometry & theorem proving** remain weaker than GPT‑4; targeted geometry data or synthetic diagram generation could close that gap.
- **Few-shot robustness:** DeepSeekMath’s zero/few-shot gap is smaller than GPT‑4’s; adding diverse prompt styles during instruction tuning may help.
- **Reward model quality:** Future work should explore uncertainty estimation in rewards or weak–to–strong alignment to further stabilize GRPO.

---

### Bottom line

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that *data beats size*. By carefully mining massive yet high-quality math text from Common Crawl, embracing multilingual sources, and applying an efficient RL algorithm, a modest 7B parameter model can stand shoulder to shoulder with multi-billion parameter closed models on hard quantitative reasoning tasks—all while staying fully open source.

If you’re building an open LLM for any specialized domain—be it chemistry, law, or finance—borrow this recipe: start with a crisp seed set, let a light classifier iteratively harvest web data, enrich with non‑English content, and finish with a lightweight RL finetune like GRPO. With those ingredients you’ll likely see disproportionate gains without having to spend billions on compute or parameters.

Happy modeling!
