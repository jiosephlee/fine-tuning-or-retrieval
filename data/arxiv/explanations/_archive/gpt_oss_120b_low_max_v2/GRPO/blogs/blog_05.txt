\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# What Makes a Math Benchmark Hard? Inside the MATH Competition Dataset and DeepSeekMath’s 60 % Self‑Consistency Leap

Mathematical reasoning is one of the toughest tests we can give a language model.  Unlike simple fact‑recall, solving a math problem requires the model to understand a statement, plan a chain of logical steps, perform exact calculations (often with symbolic manipulation), and finally produce a correct answer in a very specific format.  The **MATH** benchmark captures this difficulty at a competition‑level: it contains college‑level problems taken from real math contests, each demanding multi‑step reasoning and precise computation.

In the recent DeepSeekMath paper, the authors report a **51.7 %** accuracy on MATH using standard chain‑of‑thought prompting—already a milestone for any open‑source 7‑billion‑parameter model.  Even more striking, when they apply *self‑consistency* (sampling 64 independent solutions per question and taking the majority vote), the score jumps to **60.9 %**.  Below we unpack why MATH is so hard, why breaking the 50 % barrier matters, how self‑consistency works, and what the gap to closed‑source powerhouses still looks like.

---

## 1. The Anatomy of the MATH Benchmark

| Aspect | What it means for a model |
|--------|---------------------------|
| **Source** | Problems from past university‑level contests (e.g., Putnam, IMO short answer). |
| **Length & Format** | Questions are often several sentences long and require interpreting diagrams or multi‑part statements. |
| **Reasoning Depth** | Typical solutions need *5–10* reasoning steps (algebraic manipulation, case analysis, induction, etc.). |
| **Answer Precision** | The final answer is a single numeric expression or short symbolic form; any deviation leads to an outright failure. |
| **No Tool Use** | In the standard evaluation the model must solve everything internally—no external calculator or Python interpreter is allowed. |

Because each problem mixes natural‑language understanding with rigorous logical flow, a model can’t rely on memorizing patterns; it must truly “reason”.  This explains why even massive closed models (GPT‑4, Gemini‑Ultra) achieve only around 75–80 % on MATH.

---

## 2. Why > 50 % Is a Breakthrough for Open Models

Open‑source LLMs have historically lagged behind proprietary giants:

| Model (parameter count) | MATH Accuracy (no tool) |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Mistral 7B                | ~20 % |
| Llama‑2 7B                | ~22 % |
| Llemma 34B (math‑pretrained) | ~38 % |
| **DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B** | **51.7 %** |
| GPT‑4 (closed)            | ~78 % |

A few observations emerge:

1. **Scale alone isn’t enough** – DeepSeekMath has only 7 B parameters but reaches higher accuracy than many 30–70 B models that lack targeted math data.
2. **Data matters** – The authors built a *120 B token* math corpus from Common Crawl using an iterative classifier pipeline. This high‑quality pretraining data gives the model exposure to diverse problem statements and solution styles.
3. **Instruction tuning + RL** – After the base model is trained, additional instruction fine‑tuning (chain‑of‑thought + program‑of‑thought) plus reinforcement learning (GRPO) squeeze out another few percentage points.

Crossing the 50 % line tells us that a model can now solve **more than half** of competition-level problems *without external assistance*, which was previously considered out of reach for open models.

---

## 3. Self‑Consistency: Sampling Many Answers, Voting for the Best

### The core idea

Self‑consistency is conceptually simple: instead of generating one answer per question, generate *multiple* answers with different stochastic seeds (different sampling trajectories). Because each generation follows its own chain of reasoning, correct solutions tend to converge on the same final answer, while incorrect attempts scatter across many values.

The steps are:

1. **Sample N responses** – DeepSeekMath draws *N = 64* distinct completions per question using temperature sampling.
2. **Extract final answers** – Each response ends with an answer token (e.g., “42”, “\( \frac{3}{4} \)”).
3. **Majority vote** – The answer that appears most often among the N samples becomes the model’s final prediction.

### Why does it help?

* **Noise reduction** – Random mistakes in intermediate reasoning get “averaged out”. If a particular reasoning path makes an algebraic slip, its final answer will likely be unique and thus ignored by majority voting.
* **Implicit ensemble** – Each sampled run can be seen as an independent sub‑model; voting approximates an ensemble without retraining.
* **Capturing diverse solution strategies** – Some problems admit multiple valid approaches; self-consistency lets the model explore them and pick the most common result.

### Results in numbers

| Setup | Accuracy on MATH |
|-------|-------------------|
| Standard chain‑of‑thought (single sample) | 51.7 % |
| Self‑consistency with 64 samples | **60.9 %** |

That ~9 % jump is huge: it moves DeepSeekMath from “just above half” to “well over half”, narrowing the gap to closed models by roughly one third.

---

## 4. Open vs. Closed Models: Where Do We Stand?

| Metric | Open (DeepSeekMath‑RL) | Closed (GPT‑4) |
|--------|------------------------|----------------|
| MATH (no tool) | 60.9 % (self‑consistent) | ~78 % |
| GSM8K (elementary) | 88.2 % | >94 % |
| Multi‑step reasoning (BBH) | Competitive with many closed models at similar scale | State‑of‑the‑art |

Key takeaways:

- **Open models excel at lower‐grade quantitative reasoning**, but still trail closed models on harder contest problems.
- **Closed models benefit from massive proprietary data and longer instruction tuning pipelines**, which likely include more diverse geometry and proof data.
- The *trend* suggests that continued scaling of high-quality math data and smarter RL tricks could further shrink this gap without requiring billions more parameters.

---

## 5. What Remains Challenging?

Even with self-consistency, certain problem families stay stubborn:

1. **Geometry & Diagram Interpretation** – Many MATH items involve implicit figures; without visual grounding the model struggles.
2. **Formal Proof Steps** – Problems that require rigorous justification beyond numeric answers still see lower scores.
3. **Rare Symbolic Forms** – Uncommon notations or domain‐specific conventions sometimes confuse tokenizers trained mostly on code or plain text.
4. **Out‐of‐distribution prompts** – When confronted with problem styles not seen during pretraining (e.g., novel competition formats), performance drops noticeably.

These gaps hint at two research directions:

- **Multi‐modal pretraining** that incorporates diagrams or LaTeX renderings.
- **Enhanced process supervision**, where reward models evaluate each intermediate reasoning step rather than just the final answer—a capability already explored in DeepSeekMath’s GRPO framework.

---

## 6. Bottom Line

The MATH benchmark is deliberately unforgiving: it demands precise multi-step reasoning, exact arithmetic, and clean final answers—qualities that are hard for any language model to master without external tools. DeepSeekMath’s achievement—> 50 % accuracy purely from language modeling, and > 60 % when augmented by self-consistency—shows that careful data selection (the massive math web corpus), targeted instruction tuning, and efficient reinforcement learning can dramatically boost an open LLM’s mathematical abilities.

Self-consistency alone adds almost nine points by turning stochastic generation into an implicit ensemble, illustrating that *how* we query a model can be just as important as *what* we train it on.

The road ahead still has hurdles—geometry reasoning, formal proofs, and truly out-of-distribution challenges—but DeepSeekMath demonstrates that open models are now in striking distance of their closed counterparts on competition-level math tasks. With more multilingual math data, better reward models, and perhaps visual grounding, we may soon see open LLMs regularly scoring above 70 % on MATH—turning “hard” into “handlable.”
