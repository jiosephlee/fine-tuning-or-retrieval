\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What does "iterative RL with GRPO" entail and how does replaying 10% of historic data affect training?
Question:
Algorithm 1 mentions updating the reward model after each iteration using a replay buffer that contains 10% historical samples. I am confused about: (i) how new training pairs for the reward model are generated from the current policy, (ii) why only 10% of old data is kept instead of full history, and (iii) what impact this has on stability or preventing reward drift across iterations.
Answer:
**Answer**

Algorithm 1 in the DeepSeekMath paper describes an *iterative* reinforcement‑learning loop built on the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) objective.  
The loop consists of two tightly coupled moving parts:

1. **the policy (the LLM) that generates new answers**, and  
2. **the reward model that judges those answers**.

At the end of each GRPO iteration the reward model is refreshed with a small replay buffer that contains *10 %* of the samples that were used to train it in previous iterations. Below we unpack how the new training pairs are obtained, why only a fraction of the old data is retained, and what effect this design has on stability and reward drift.

---

### (i) How are new training pairs for the reward model generated?

Each GRPO iteration proceeds as follows (see Algorithm 1):

1. **Sample a batch of questions** \(q\) from the instruction‑tuning set \(\mathcal D\) (the same GSM8K/MATH prompts used for SFT).  
2. **Generate a *group* of outputs** \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\) for every question from the *current* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). In the experiments \(G=64\); the generation uses nucleus sampling with a maximum length of 1024 tokens.  
3. **Score each output** with the *current* reward model \(r_{\varphi}\), obtaining scalar rewards \(r_i\). In outcome‑supervision these scores are the final‑step rewards; in process‑supervision each reasoning step is also scored, but the pairwise data needed for training the reward model are still whole answer‑reward pairs.  
4. **Form training pairs** \((q,o_i,r_i)\). For each question we now have up to 64 new (question, answer, reward) triples that reflect how the latest policy behaves on that prompt.

These triples are then fed to the reward‑model update step (the “continuous training” line in Algorithm 1). The loss is a standard pairwise preference loss: for two answers \(o_a, o_b\) to the same question we encourage the model to assign a higher score to the one with larger empirical reward (e.g., higher correctness or higher process‑reward). Thus each iteration creates fresh supervision data directly from the policy’s own behaviour.

---

### (ii) Why keep only 10 % of historic samples instead of the whole history?

The replay buffer is deliberately *small* for two practical reasons that are discussed implicitly in Section 5.2 (“Iterative RL with GRPO”) and reflected in the experimental design:

| Reason | What happens if we keep everything? |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| **Memory / compute budget** – The reward model is trained repeatedly after every iteration. Storing millions of past (question, answer, reward) triples quickly overwhelms GPU memory and would make each update orders of magnitude slower. |
| **Staleness of old data** – As training progresses, newer policy outputs are *systematically better* (or at least different) than early‑stage outputs. Old pairs become less informative for refining a model that now operates in a different region of answer space; they can even bias the reward model toward rewarding obsolete behaviours. |
| **Diversity vs. redundancy** – A random 10 % sample preserves a representative slice of earlier behaviours while discarding duplicated or near‑identical examples that add little signal. Empirically this small slice is enough to keep a smooth trajectory for the reward model without hurting performance (see Fig. 9 in the paper where performance jumps after each iteration even with a 10 % replay). |

In other words, 10 % provides a *memory‑efficient* approximation of “full history” that still supplies a grounding signal preventing catastrophic forgetting of earlier behaviours.

---

### (iii) How does this replay strategy affect stability and prevent reward drift?

Reward drift—where successive updates cause the reward model to move away from its original intent—is a known failure mode in iterative RL pipelines. The authors mitigate it in three complementary ways:

1. **Baseline anchored by group averages:** GRPO computes advantages \(\hat A_{i,t}\) by subtracting the group mean reward from each sample’s score (outcome supervision) or by summing normalized step rewards (process supervision). This *relative* scoring automatically centres each batch, so even if the absolute scale of rewards drifts, the advantage signal stays stable.

2. **Replay of historic data:** By mixing in 10 % of past pairs, the loss sees both “new” and “old” comparisons at every update. This regularises \(r_{\varphi}\): it cannot simply over‑fit to whatever pattern appears in the current batch because it must also continue to honour older comparisons that may span different regions of answer space.

3. **KL regularisation on the policy:** GRPO adds an explicit KL term \(\beta \,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) to the objective (Equation 2). The reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is set to the *previous* policy after each outer iteration, which together with replay ensures that both policy and reward evolve gradually rather than making abrupt jumps.

The empirical evidence in Section 5.2 shows that after one outer iteration performance rises sharply, while after subsequent iterations gains become smaller but still positive – indicating that neither component collapses. Moreover, Figure 10 (“iterative RL”) demonstrates that using replay yields smoother learning curves than a naïve “re‑train from scratch” baseline, confirming that replay curbs oscillations caused by drifting rewards.

---

### Putting it all together

In each outer iteration:

1. **Generate fresh answer–reward pairs** by sampling many outputs per question with the current policy.
2. **Update the reward model** on those fresh pairs plus a modest (10 %) replay buffer of older pairs.
3. **Treat this newly trained reward model as fixed** while performing one step of GRPO on the policy; KL regularisation ties the updated policy back to its predecessor.
4. **Replace the reference model** with the newly updated policy and repeat.

The 10 % replay is therefore not an arbitrary heuristic; it is a lightweight form of experience replay that gives enough historical context to stabilise both reward‑model learning and policy updates while keeping computational cost low. This design lets DeepSeekMath achieve *iterative* improvements—e.g., GSM8K accuracy jumps from 82.9 % → 88.2 %—without suffering from runaway reward drift or instability across iterations.
