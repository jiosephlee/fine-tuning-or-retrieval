\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does self‑consistency over 64 samples raise DeepSeekMath’s MATH score from 51.7% to 60.9%?
Question:
The abstract says self‑consistency over 64 samples yields 60.9% on MATH. Is this simply taking a majority vote over 64 independently sampled CoT answers per question? If so, why does increasing sample count help so dramatically for this model compared to others? Does GRPO’s training encourage higher variance among samples that can be aggregated effectively?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes – the “self‑consistency over $64$ samples” reported in the DeepSeekMath paper is exactly the majority‑vote (or *top‑k‑majority*) procedure that has become the standard way of turning a stochastic chain‑of‑thought (CoT) generator into a more reliable solver.  
For each MATH problem the model is asked to generate $64$ independent completions (each using nucleus sampling with the same temperature). Every completion contains a full CoT reasoning trace and a final answer token. The answers are then counted; the answer that appears most frequently is taken as the model’s prediction. If several answers tie, the one with the higher average log‑probability is selected. This procedure is what the authors refer to as **self‑consistency**.

---

### Why does increasing the number of samples improve the score so dramatically for DeepSeekMath?

1. **Variance in CoT generations is useful** – A single CoT trace can be wrong for many reasons (mis‑applied algebra, arithmetic slip, or an early logical error). Different random seeds often lead the model down different reasoning paths, some of which happen to be correct. When you collect many traces you obtain a *distribution* over possible answers. The correct answer, if it is reachable at all, tends to be produced by more than one path, whereas systematic mistakes are usually reproduced by a majority of samples. Taking a majority vote therefore filters out many spurious errors.

2. **DeepSeekMath’s training increases the “reachability” of correct solutions** – The model benefits from two ingredients that specifically encourage diverse yet correct reasoning:
   * **Math‑focused pre‑training on $120\,$B high‑quality web tokens** gives the model a broader exposure to different problem‑solving styles (textual derivations, symbolic manipulations, program‑of‑thought examples). This exposure makes it more likely that at least one of the sampled CoTs will follow a useful line of reasoning.
   * **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** fine‑tunes the model with an objective that rewards *relative* superiority among a group of outputs sampled for the same question. In GRPO each sample’s advantage is computed by subtracting the mean reward of its group (see Eq. (4) in the paper). Consequently, during RL training the policy learns to **spread its probability mass toward higher‑reward trajectories while simultaneously suppressing consistently low‑reward ones**. The effect is an increase in *inter‑sample variance*: some samples become markedly better than others rather than all collapsing onto one dominant (often incorrect) style.

   Because GRPO explicitly normalises rewards within each group, it encourages a **competition** among sampled answers. This competition sharpens the contrast between good and bad reasoning paths, which in turn makes majority voting more powerful: the correct path stands out more clearly among many tries.

3. **Sample size vs. diminishing returns** – In earlier works (e.g., self‑consistency applied to GPT‑3) improving from $8$ to $16$ or $32$ samples gave modest gains; moving to $64$ brings a larger jump because DeepSeekMath’s policy distribution has already become *bimodal*: a sizable chunk of its probability mass sits on high‑quality solutions while another chunk remains on low‑quality shortcuts. With only a few draws you may miss the high‑quality mode; with $64$ draws you almost guarantee hitting it multiple times, so the majority vote switches from an incorrect answer (the mode of the low‑quality region) to the correct one.

4. **Comparison with other models** – Many open‑source baselines have been trained only with supervised fine‑tuning or with PPO that relies on an external value function. Their output distributions tend to be *less* peaked on high‑quality solutions and *more* concentrated on a single (often flawed) style of reasoning, so increasing $K$ yields smaller marginal improvements. DeepSeekMath’s combination of massive domain data plus GRPO therefore yields a distribution that reacts more positively to additional samples.

---

### Does GRPO explicitly encourage higher variance among samples?

GRPO does not *directly* maximise variance; instead it **uses variance as a signal**. By computing advantages as

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)},
$$

the algorithm treats an output whose reward is above the group mean as having positive advantage and those below as negative. When gradient updates are applied, tokens belonging to high‑reward trajectories receive reinforcement, while tokens belonging to low‑reward trajectories are penalised. Over many training steps this pushes some trajectories up and others down, naturally enlarging their spread.

Thus GRPO creates precisely the condition in which self‑consistency shines: there exist *distinct* high‑reward and low‑reward families of completions that can be separated by simple majority voting.

---

### Take‑away

*Self‑consistency* in DeepSeekMath is indeed majority voting over $64$ independently sampled CoT answers per question.  
The dramatic jump from **51.7 %** (single greedy decode) to **60.9 %** ($64$‑sample self‑consistency) comes from two synergistic facts:

1. The pre‑training corpus and code‑first initialization give the model many plausible reasoning patterns, increasing the chance that at least one sampled trace hits a correct solution.
2. GRPO RL fine‑tunes the policy so that good traces are systematically preferred over bad ones *within each sampled group*, resulting in a bimodal output distribution where a majority vote can reliably pick out the correct answer.

Consequently, while other models also benefit from self‑consistency, DeepSeekMath’s training pipeline amplifies this effect far more than standard PPO or supervised fine‑tuning alone.
