\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How are outcome supervision and process supervision implemented in GRPO?
Question:
The paper describes outcome supervision as assigning the same normalized reward to all tokens of an output, while process supervision provides step‑wise rewards. I am unsure how "steps" are detected in generated text (e.g., via special tokens or line breaks), how the per‑step rewards are normalized across a group, and how the advantage \( \hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} \) propagates back to earlier tokens. Could someone clarify with an example of a CoT answer containing three reasoning steps?
Answer:
**Answer**

In GRPO the reward that drives the policy update can be given either **once at the end of the whole answer** (outcome supervision) or **after every reasoning step** (process supervision).  
Both ways use the same mathematical machinery – the advantage of token *t* in sampled answer *i* is  

\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{step }j:\; \text{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} ,
\]

where $\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$ is a *normalized* reward for step *j*. The only difference lies in how we define a “step’’ and what rewards we attach to it.

---

### 1. How a “step’’ is detected in generated text  

The papers’ implementation follows the practice of most chain‑of‑thought (CoT) datasets: a step is demarcated by a **special separator token** that is inserted during data preparation. Concretely:

* In the SFT data each CoT solution is written as  

```
Answer: 12
Step 1: … (first sub‑calculation)
Step 2: … (second sub‑calculation)
Step 3: … (final reasoning)
```

* When the model is trained, every line that begins with “Step *k*:” or a line break after a period and a space is replaced by a **single sentinel token** (e.g. `<step>`). The token sequence therefore looks like  

```
… <step> … <step> … <step> …
```

During generation the same sentinel appears whenever the model decides to start a new reasoning chunk, so the reward model can later locate it easily.

If your own data do not contain an explicit sentinel, you can treat a newline or a “**;**” that follows a complete statement as a step boundary – the paper only requires that all tokens belonging to one logical chunk share the same end‑of‑step index.

---

### 2. Normalising rewards across a *group*  

GRPO samples **$G$** completions $\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}$ for the same question $q$. The raw scores from the reward model are

\[
r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \quad\text{(outcome) or}\quad
r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \quad\text{(process)},
\]

where $i$ indexes the sampled answer and $\text{index}(j)$ is the token position of step *j*.  
All scores in the group are pooled into one list  

\[
\mathbf R = \bigl\{ r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \mid i=1,\dots ,G,\; j=1,\dots ,K_i\bigr\},
\]

and then **standardised**:

\[
\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} = \frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)} - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)}
{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)} .
\]

This has two effects:

1. It removes any absolute scale that depends on the particular question, making the advantage comparable across groups.
2. It centres the distribution at zero, so that tokens belonging to *better* answers receive positive advantage while tokens from *worse* answers obtain negative advantage.

For outcome supervision there is only one reward per answer, i.e. $K_i=1$; for process supervision there may be several $K_i>1$.

---

### 3. From normalised step‑wise rewards to token‑level advantages  

Consider an example CoT with three steps (the sentinel `<step>` marks each boundary):

```
Question: …
<step> Compute A = 7×8 = 56.
<step> Use A to find B = A – 10 = 46.
<step> The final answer is B = 46.
```

Assume GRPO samples $G=4$ completions and the process‑reward model gives the following *raw* step scores (higher = better):

| answer | step 1 | step 2 | step 3 |
|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| o₁    | 0.6   | 0.7   | 0.9 |
| o₂    | 0.4   | 0.5   | 0.8 |
| o₃    | 0.5   | 0.6   | 0.6 |
| o₄    | 0.3   | 0.4   | 0.5 |

All nine numbers constitute $\mathbf R$. Their mean ≈ 0.55, std ≈ 0.18, so after normalisation we obtain $\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$.  
For answer $o_2$ we might get (rounded)

| step 1 | step 2 | step 3 |
|-------|-------|-------|
| –0.83 | –0.28 | 1.39 |

Now compute advantages for each token:

* Tokens belonging to **step 3** (the last chunk) see only their own normalised reward, e.g. for token “46” in step 3 of $o_2$

\[
\hat A_{2,t}= \widetilde r_2^{\text{index}(3)} = 1.39 .
\]

* Tokens in **step 2** receive the sum of rewards of step 2 **and** step 3, because any later improvement should also be credited to earlier decisions:

\[
\hat A_{2,t}= \widetilde r_2^{\text{index}(2)} + \widetilde r_2^{\text{index}(3)}
            = -0.28 + 1.39 = 1.11 .
\]

* Tokens in **step 1** get all three rewards summed:

\[
\hat A_{2,t}= -0.83 -0.28 + 1.39 = 0.28 .
\]

Thus earlier tokens that eventually lead to a high final score obtain a *positive* cumulative advantage, while those that contribute to a low final score would have a negative sum.

These per‑token advantages are plugged into the GRPO surrogate loss (Eq.​ (4) of the paper). The policy gradient therefore nudges the probability of tokens with large positive $\hat A_{i,t}$ upward and those with negative $\hat A_{i,t}$ downward.

---

### 4. Summary of differences between outcome and process supervision  

| Aspect | Outcome supervision | Process supervision |
|--------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Reward granularity | One scalar per answer (`r_i`) placed at the final token only | One scalar per reasoning step (`r_i^{index(j)}`) placed at each `<step>` token |
| Advantage computation | Same normalised reward copied to every token: $\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i$ | Cumulative sum of later‑step rewards: $\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{index(j)}$ |
| Signal richness | Coarse; only final correctness matters | Fine‑grained; tells which intermediate reasoning moves were helpful |
| Implementation requirement | Only need an end‑of‑sequence reward model | Need a process‑reward model that can evaluate partial outputs up to each `<step>` token |

In practice you will see process supervision yielding larger gains on difficult math problems because it tells the model *where* it went wrong, not merely whether it succeeded overall.

--- 

**Take‑away:**  
To reproduce GRPO’s process supervision you must (1) annotate your CoT data with an explicit step delimiter (a sentinel token), (2) train or fine‑tune a reward model that can score a prefix ending at any delimiter, (3) normalise all step scores within each sampled group, and (4) compute token‑level advantages by summing all normalised scores from the current step onward as shown above. The same pipeline collapses to outcome supervision when you simply set $K_i=1$ and copy the normalised score to every token of an answer.
