\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What exactly is the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) objective and how does it differ from standard PPO?
Question:
I read the section introducing GRPO and see Equation (4) defining \( \mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta) \). However, I am unclear on three points: (1) how the advantage \( \hat{A}_{i,t} \) is computed using group-relative rewards, (2) why the KL regularization term is added directly to the loss instead of being part of the reward as in PPO, and (3) what practical benefits (e.g., memory savings, stability) arise from removing the critic/value network. Could someone walk through a concrete example of a single question with a group of sampled outputs?
Answer:
**Answer**

`Group‑Relative Policy Optimization` (GRPO) is the reinforcement‑learning (RL) variant that the authors use to finetune *DeepSeekMath‑Instruct* after supervised instruction tuning.  It can be read as a “PPO without a critic” in which the baseline is obtained **from the other samples that belong to the same question**.  Below I walk through the three points you asked about and illustrate them with a tiny concrete example.

---

### 1.  How the advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ is computed from group‑relative rewards  

In ordinary PPO the advantage at token $t$ is  

$$
A_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t} r_k - V_\psi(o_{<t}),
$$

where a learned value network $V_\psi$ supplies a baseline for variance reduction.  
GRPO discards this value network and replaces the baseline with the **average reward of a *group* of outputs that are sampled for the same question**.

For a given question $q$ we draw $G$ completions  

$$
\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid q).
$$

Each completion receives a scalar score from the reward model $r_\varphi$:

$$
r_i = r_\varphi(q,o_i)\quad (i=1,\dots ,G).
$$

The paper describes two ways of turning these scores into advantages.

| Supervision | Advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ |
|--------------|---------------------------|
| **Outcome supervision** (reward only at the end of a generation) | First normalise the group scores  

$$
\widetilde r_i = \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)},
$$  

and then set $\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i$ for *all* tokens $t$ of that output. |
| **Process supervision** (reward at each reasoning step) | The reward model is evaluated after each step, giving a sequence $\{r_i^{\text{step}(j)}\}_{j=1}^{K_i}$. After normalising across *all* step‑wise scores in the group, we define  

$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{step}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{step}(j)},
$$  

i.e. the sum of normalised future step rewards, which is exactly what GAE would compute if we treated each step as an “episode”. |

Thus the baseline is simply “the group mean” (or, more precisely, a z‑score around that mean).  Because every sample in the group is produced by the **same old policy**, this relative comparison is unbiased and has very low variance—no extra critic is needed.

---

### 2.  Why KL regularisation appears as a separate term instead of being added to the reward  

In PPO the usual practice (e.g. ChatGPT‑style RLHF) is to subtract a per‑token KL penalty from the reward before computing advantages:

$$
r_t = r_\varphi(q,o_{\le t}) - \beta\,\log \frac{\pi_\theta(o_t|\,)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_t|\,)} .
$$

GRPO instead **keeps the KL term outside the reward** and adds it directly to the loss:

```math
L_{\text{GRPO}} = 
-\mathbb{E}\Big[ \underbrace{\text{clipped surrogate}}_{\text{policy gain}} - 
      \beta\, D_{\!KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big)\Big].
```

There are two practical reasons for this design:

1. **Cleaner advantage estimation** – The advantage now depends only on *relative* rewards inside a group, which are already normalised. If we mixed a KL penalty into those scalar rewards we would have to re‑normalise per token, complicating both outcome and process supervision. By pulling KL out we keep $\hat A_{i,t}$ simple (just a z‑score or step‑sum).

2. **Memory / computation savings** – Computing a per‑token KL term requires evaluating both policies at every generation step, which already happens when we compute the probability ratios in the PPO‐style surrogate. Adding it as an extra additive loss does not require any extra forward pass or storage; we simply reuse those ratios to obtain an unbiased estimator of $D_{\!KL}$. The paper notes that this estimator  

   $$
   D_{\!KL}\big[\pi_\theta||\pi_{\text{ref}}\big]
   = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}
     -\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_\theta}-1
   $$

   is positive and cheap to compute.

Hence GRPO treats KL regularisation as a **policy‑level** constraint rather than part of the scalar reward signal.

---

### 3.  What we actually gain by removing the critic/value network?  

| Aspect | PPO (with critic) | GRPO (no critic) |
|--------|-------------------|------------------|
| **Memory footprint** | Two large transformer heads (policy + value) ≈ twice the model’s activation memory; on a 7 B LLM this often forces batch‑size reduction or gradient checkpointing. | Only one forward pass of the policy; memory usage drops by roughly 40–50 % (the exact number depends on how many layers are duplicated for value). |
| **Computation time** | Each training step evaluates both networks → ~2× forward cost; also requires an extra optimisation loop for $V_\psi$. | One forward pass, one ratio computation → ~1× cost; training becomes roughly twice as fast per GPU hour. |
| **Stability / variance** | The learned value can be noisy when only an end‑of‑sequence reward exists, leading to high variance gradients and sometimes destabilising updates, especially for long LLM generations. | The baseline is an exact empirical mean across samples, so its variance is zero by construction; gradients are therefore less noisy and PPO’s clipping works more reliably. |
| **Implementation complexity** | Need to maintain two optimisers, replay buffers for value targets, GAE hyper‑parameters ($\lambda,\gamma$). | Simpler code path: just sample groups, compute normalized rewards and apply clipped surrogate plus KL term. |

In practice, the authors observed that with GRPO they could fit *four* times more sampled outputs per GPU (they use $G=64$) while keeping batch size constant, which translates directly into better coverage of diverse completions and thus richer learning signals.

---

### Concrete miniature example

Suppose our training prompt is a simple algebra problem:

```
Q: Solve for x: 2x + 3 = 7.
```

During one RL iteration we ask the *old* policy ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) to generate $G=3$ completions:

| i | Generated output $o_i$ | Reward model score $r_i=r_\varphi(Q,o_i)$ |
|---|--------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| 1 | “$x=2.$”               | 0.9 |
| 2 | “$x=2.$ Explanation …”| 0.8 |
| 3 | “Cannot solve.”          | 0.2 |

(These scores come from a learned reward model that prefers correct solutions and penalises nonsense.)

**Outcome supervision**  
Compute group statistics: mean$=0.6333$, std$\approx0.3578$.  
Normalised rewards:

* $\widetilde r_1 = (0.9-0.6333)/0.3578 \approx 0.75$
* $\widetilde r_2 \approx 0.46$
* $\widetilde r_3 \approx -1.21$

Set $\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i$ for every token of each output (the same advantage applies to all positions because we only have an end‑of‑sequence reward).

Now plug into Eq.(4): for each token we compute the probability ratio  

$$
\rho_{i,t}= \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}|Q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|Q,o_{i,<t})},
$$

clip it between $1-\varepsilon$ and $1+\varepsilon$, multiply by $\hat A_{i,t}$, sum over tokens, and finally subtract  

$ \beta D_{\!KL}[\pi_\theta||\pi_{\text{ref}}]$.

The gradient will push up probabilities for tokens belonging to output 1 (positive advantage ≈ +0.75), give a milder push to output 2 (+0.46), and push down tokens of output 3 (negative advantage ≈ −1.21). Because these advantages are already centred on zero, there is no need for an additional value network.

If we had used *process supervision*, each reasoning step (“$2x+3=7 ⇒ 2x=4 ⇒ x=2.$”) would obtain its own intermediate reward; those would be summed forward to give token‑wise advantages that gradually increase as correct steps accumulate.

---

### Summary

* **GRPO’s advantage** comes from taking *relative* scores within a sampled group as a baseline, eliminating the need for a separate value head.
* The KL penalty is kept as an explicit loss term so that advantage calculation stays clean and because it can be evaluated cheaply from already‑computed probability ratios.
* By removing the critic we halve memory usage, speed up each update, and obtain variance‑free baselines—especially beneficial when fine‑tuning large language models where GPU memory is at premium.

This explains why GRPO can achieve comparable or better performance than PPO while using substantially fewer resources, exactly what Table 6 and Section 4 of the DeepSeekMath paper report.

### How is the DeepSeekMath corpus de‑duplicated and filtered to avoid benchmark contamination?
Question:
The paper mentions URL‑based deduplication, near‑deduplication, and a 10‑gram exact‑match filter for GSM8K/MATH/CMATH etc. I am confused about the exact pipeline: after deduplication, are pages scored by the fastText classifier and then ranked? At what point is the 10‑gram filter applied, and does it operate on raw HTML or extracted text? Also, how does the "10% domain" rule for identifying math‑related domains work in practice?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “DeepSeekMath Corpus” is built from Common‑Crawl (CC) in a *four‑stage* pipeline that is deliberately designed to keep the final token stream free of any material that could give the model an unfair advantage on the evaluation benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, …).  
Below I walk through the steps in the order they are applied, explain where the fast‑Text scores are used, when the 10‑gram exact‑match filter runs, what text it operates on, and how the “10 % domain” rule is implemented.

---

### 1.  Raw CC → URL‑based deduplication / near‑deduplication  

CC contains many billions of HTML pages, many of which are exact copies (mirrored sites, archive snapshots, etc.).  
The authors first collapse these duplicates by two cheap heuristics:

* **URL‑based deduplication** – pages that share an identical URL after normalising scheme, host and path are considered duplicates and only one copy is kept.  
* **Near‑deduplication** – a fingerprint (e.g., Simhash/MinHash) is computed on the *raw HTML* of each page; if two fingerprints are within a pre‑specified Hamming distance the later page is discarded.

After these two passes the corpus shrinks from ≈ 200 B raw pages to **≈ 40 B deduplicated HTML pages**. No language‑model‑specific information is used yet; this step only reduces storage and later compute cost.

---

### 2.  FastText classification and ranking  

With the deduplicated HTML set in hand, the authors train a **fastText** linear text classifier:

* **Positive examples** – 500 k snippets drawn randomly from OpenWebMath (the seed corpus).  
* **Negative examples** – 500 k random CC pages that survived the deduplication stage.

The classifier uses 256‑dim word‑ngram vectors (n≤3) and is trained for three epochs.  
Once trained, it is applied *page‑wise* to every **deduplicated HTML document**.  

The classifier outputs a **probability score** $s(p)\in[0,1]$ indicating how “math‑like’’ a page $p$ is.  
All pages are then **sorted descending by $s(p)$** and only the top‑scoring portion is retained. The amount retained is decided empirically: experiments were run with the top 40 B, 80 B … tokens and the authors finally fixed on **the top 40 B tokens for iteration 1**, later expanding to 120 B after four iterations.

Thus ranking happens *after* deduplication but *before* any textual cleaning or decontamination.

---

### 3.  Domain‑level expansion and “10 % domain” rule  

After iteration 1 many math pages are still missing because the seed set was too narrow. To harvest more positives the pipeline analyses **domains** (the part of a URL up to the base host, e.g. `mathoverflow.net`). For each domain $D$ it computes

$$
\rho(D)=\frac{\#\text{pages from }D\text{ kept in iteration 1}}{\#\text{total pages from }D\text{ in CC}} .
$$

If $\rho(D) > 0.10$ (i.e., more than ten percent of that domain’s pages survived the fastText ranking) the domain is labelled **math‑related**. This heuristic works because a domain whose majority of URLs contain math content will naturally yield a high keep‑rate under a math classifier.

Human annotators then manually pick **sub‑paths** inside those domains that definitely host math material (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/…`). All uncollected pages matching those annotated URLs are added to the seed corpus for the next training round, thereby enriching the positive set and allowing a stronger fastText model in iteration 2. The process repeats four times until marginal gains vanish (≈ 98 % of data already collected by iteration 3).

---

### 4.  Text extraction  

Before any decontamination can be performed we need plain text rather than raw HTML. The authors use a simple extractor that strips tags, decodes entities and keeps only visible text blocks (no code blocks or tables). The result for each page $p$ is a string $t(p)$. All subsequent filters—including the exact‑match filter—operate on this **extracted plain text**, not on HTML markup.

---

### 5.  Benchmark de‑contamination (10‑gram/3‑gram filter)

The final safety net removes any page that could leak evaluation data:

| Condition | What triggers removal |
|-----------|-----------------------|
| **10‑gram exact match** | If any contiguous ten‑word sequence in $t(p)$ appears verbatim in any benchmark reference (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval …) → discard page. |
| **Shorter strings (3–9 grams)** | For benchmarks whose excerpts are shorter than ten tokens, an exact match of any ≥3‑gram subsequence also triggers removal. |

Implementation details from the paper:  
* They slide a window of size 10 over each extracted text and compare each window against a pre‑computed hash set of all ten‑grams occurring in the benchmark corpora.  
* The same procedure with window sizes 3–9 is applied only to those benchmark files whose total length is <10 words (e.g., some Chinese CMATH problems).  

Because filtering happens *after* text extraction, it works purely on natural language tokens; HTML tags or attribute strings never get matched.

---

### 6.  Final token count  

After de‑duplication, fastText ranking, domain expansion and exact‑match cleaning, the pipeline yields **≈ 35.5 M web pages**, which correspond to **≈ 120 B tokens** of high‑quality math content (the “DeepSeekMath Corpus”). The authors stop after four iterations because iteration 4 adds <2 % new tokens relative to iteration 3.

---

## Putting it together – What happens to a page?

1. **Raw CC download → URL dedup → near‑dedup** (still HTML).  
2. **FastText score** computed on raw HTML → page kept if its score ranks within the desired token budget.  
3. If required for later iterations: check whether its domain satisfies $\rho(D)>0.10$; otherwise it may be ignored until new annotated URLs appear.  
4. **HTML → plain text extraction** ($t(p)$).  
5. **10‑gram / ≥3‑gram exact match** against benchmark n‑grams; if any match → discard page completely.  
6. Surviving pages are concatenated into sharded token files for pre‑training.

Therefore:

* **FastText scoring precedes ranking**, which determines which pages proceed to step 4.  
* The **10‑gram filter operates on extracted plain text**, not on raw HTML.  
* The “10 % domain” rule is purely a *statistics‐driven heuristic* used to discover new math domains; it does not directly affect ranking but expands the seed set for subsequent fastText training.

This pipeline guarantees that by the time training begins no token can be traced back to a question or solution that appears in any of the public math benchmarks, thus eliminating benchmark contamination while retaining as much genuine mathematical web material as possible.

### Why does pre‑training on arXiv papers not improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
The authors report that training on MathPile or ArXiv‑RedPajama gave no gains (or even hurt) performance on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, and miniF2F. Is this because the arXiv data lacks the step‑by‑step reasoning style found in benchmark problems, or could it be due to token distribution differences? How was the arXiv‑only corpus prepared (e.g., LaTeX removal) and could different preprocessing or larger model scales change this conclusion?
Answer:
The short answer that the authors of **DeepSeekMath** give is that the *type* of text that dominates arXiv papers does not provide the kinds of signals a language model needs to become a good “step‑by‑step” problem solver.  In the ablation experiments described in §4.2 (“ArXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning”), training on either **MathPile** ($\approx 85\%$ arXiv) or on a clean **ArXiv‑RedPajama** dump actually leaves the model’s scores on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM and miniF2F unchanged or even slightly worse compared with a baseline that receives no arXiv data at all.  

Below is a concise synthesis of the factors the paper points to, together with the concrete preprocessing pipeline they used, and a brief discussion of what might change the outcome.

### 1. What the arXiv data look like after cleaning

1. **Source** – Full LaTeX source files from the arXiv repository ($\approx 28\text{B}$ tokens for RedPajama, $\approx 8.9\text{B}$ tokens for MathPile).  
2. **LaTeX stripping** – All preambles, comments, macro definitions and bibliography sections are removed; the remaining body text is kept as plain‑text tokens.  
3. **De‑duplication & filtering** – The same URL‑based deduplication used for the Common‑Crawl pipeline is applied, and any passage that contains an exact 10‑gram match to any benchmark question/answer is discarded (shorter n‑grams are also filtered if they appear in the benchmark).  

The result is a large collection of *expository* mathematics: definitions, theorem statements, proof sketches, and discussion of prior work.  The content is highly compact, dense, and often assumes background knowledge; it rarely contains the explicit “think‑aloud” or chain‑of‑thought (CoT) style that benchmark problems require.

### 2. Why this type of data does not boost CoT reasoning

| Aspect | What arXiv provides | What benchmarks need |
|--------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Narrative structure** | Implicit reasoning; most proofs are written in a tersely logical style with occasional “We show that …” but rarely break down each algebraic manipulation into separate sentences. | Benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH are formatted as *question → multi‑step solution* in natural language (often with intermediate equations explicitly described). |
| **Token distribution** | Heavy use of symbols, formulas embedded in LaTeX (after stripping they become long token sequences like “int 0 1” for $ \int_{0}^{1} $) and domain‑specific jargon; relatively low proportion of connective words (“therefore”, “hence”). | Chain‑of‑thought generation benefits from a balanced mix of connective discourse and numeric/token reasoning; excess formula tokens can even overwhelm the model’s ability to learn proper conditioning. |
| **Learning signal** | The model sees many statements of results without seeing the *process* that leads to them; during pre‑training it only learns to predict next tokens given a long technical paragraph, not how to decompose a problem into sub‑steps. | CoT training rewards precisely the ability to *decompose* a problem into logical pieces; without exposure to such decompositions during pre‑training there is little downstream gain. |
| **Domain coverage** | Predominantly research‑level material (advanced algebra, topology, optimization) that bears little lexical overlap with elementary school problems appearing in GSM8K/MATH. | Benchmarks consist mostly of elementary to undergraduate problems; a mismatch in topic frequency reduces transferability. |

Because the model’s objective during pre‑training is simply next‑token prediction, it will allocate capacity to memorize theorem statements rather than learning how to *derive* them step by step.  This explains why adding arXiv tokens sometimes even harms performance: they introduce noise with respect to the downstream distribution (the model’s probability mass shifts toward “high‑level” jargon at the expense of the simpler reasoning patterns required by the benchmarks).

### 3. Could different preprocessing or larger scale change this?

The authors explicitly acknowledge three open questions:

1. **Alternative tokenization / formula handling** – The current pipeline treats LaTeX symbols as ordinary text after stripping macros. A more sophisticated approach (e.g., separating formula tokens from surrounding prose, or using a math‑aware tokenizer) might reduce token‐distribution skew and give the model clearer cues about where reasoning steps should be inserted.

2. **Combining arXiv with other corpora** – In isolation arXiv hurts performance, but when mixed with high‑quality web math pages (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) it does not dominate. The paper’s main corpus ($\approx 120\text{B}$ tokens) already contains $\approx 10\%$ arXiv; at that proportion it does not degrade results, suggesting that a modest amount of well–filtered arXiv can be harmless but still insufficient to provide extra benefit.

3. **Scale effects** – All experiments were run on models up to 7 B parameters (or a 1.3 B probe). It is possible that much larger models (≥ 70 B) could absorb both code/CC data and massive scientific text without catastrophic forgetting, enabling them to leverage arXiv’s richer mathematical vocabulary. The authors note this as future work.

In short, what matters most for improving CoT style reasoning is *data that exhibit explicit stepwise reasoning*, not merely “more math”.  The DeepSeekMath team’s own web‑page mining pipeline deliberately selects pages containing such explanatory walkthroughs (e.g., tutorials, solved examples), which explains why their custom Corpus yields large gains whereas raw arXiv does not.

### Takeaway

- **ArXiv papers are largely *expository* rather than *procedural***; after standard cleaning they provide few examples of the chain‑of‑thought format used by GSM8K/MATH.
- The resulting token distribution biases the model toward high‑level theorem statements and away from intermediate reasoning steps.
- The paper’s experiments show no benefit (and sometimes a slight drop) when training on arXiv alone; this holds across several benchmark families.
- Improvements could potentially come from more nuanced preprocessing (e.g., preserving proof steps), mixing arXiv with stepwise web data in modest amounts, or scaling up model size—areas left for future investigation.

Thus the observed lack of gain is not an artifact of a single stray implementation detail but reflects a fundamental mismatch between what arXiv contains and what mathematical reasoning benchmarks demand.

### What is the role of code pre‑training before math pre‑training, and why does it help?
Question:
The paper conducts two‑stage experiments where a 1.3B model first sees 400B code tokens then 150B math tokens, and also a mixed one‑stage variant. The results show improvements in program‑aided reasoning but mixed training sometimes harms pure reasoning. I am trying to understand: (a) what aspects of code data (e.g., syntax, execution semantics) transfer to symbolic math reasoning, (b) why catastrophic forgetting occurs when code is only in the first stage for a small model, and (c) whether larger models would benefit more from mixed code+math training.
Answer:
**Answer**

The authors of *DeepSeekMath* deliberately trained a 1.3 B‑parameter language model in two stages – first on a very large **code** corpus (≈ 400 B tokens) and then on a dedicated **math** corpus (≈ 150 B tokens).  They also compared this “code‑first → math‑second” pipeline with a single‑stage mixture of code + math tokens.  The experiments (Tables §4.1‑4.2) show three systematic patterns that answer your three sub‑questions.

---

### (a) What is transferred from code to symbolic‑math reasoning?

Even though the model never sees explicit mathematics while it is learning to write code, the **structure** of programming languages is highly aligned with the structure of formal mathematical reasoning:

1. **Syntax and tree‑like composition.**  
   Code is written in a strict grammar (e.g., Python, C++) where every statement can be parsed into an abstract syntax tree (AST).  When the model learns to predict the next token it must internalise notions of *balanced parentheses*, *indentation*, *operator precedence* and *nested scopes*.  These are exactly the same constraints that appear in symbolic expressions such as $ \int_{0}^{1} x^{2}\,dx $ or $ \forall x\in\mathbb{R},\;x^{2}\ge 0 $.  Consequently the model’s embedding space for “operator‑operand” patterns becomes reusable for math symbols.

2. **Execution semantics and step‑by‑step thinking.**  
   To generate correct code the model implicitly learns a notion of *state transition* – each token changes the program’s execution state in a predictable way.  This mirrors the way a mathematical proof or calculation proceeds: each line transforms the previous state (e.g., applying an algebraic rule, calling a function such as `sympy.simplify`).  The paper’s program‑of‑thought (PoT) experiments show that this latent procedural knowledge makes the model far better at writing Python programs that solve GSM8K / MATH problems.

3. **Error‑checking and fine‑grained feedback.**  
   During code pre‑training the model repeatedly encounters a binary signal: the generated program either compiles/runs or crashes.  Even though DeepSeekMath’s pre‑training does not include an execution loop, the statistical correlation between syntactically correct code and higher downstream likelihood encourages the model to prefer well‑formed token sequences.  That bias survives into the math stage and helps it avoid “ill‑formed” derivations such as mismatched brackets or undefined symbols.

Taken together, these properties give the model a head start on handling *structured* reasoning, which explains why code pre‑training improves both **pure text‑based CoT** performance (the model already knows how to organise multi‑step arguments) and **program‑aided PoT** performance (it can emit runnable Python more reliably).

---

### (b) Why does catastrophic forgetting appear for a small model when code is only in the first stage?

Catastrophic forgetting occurs when a network is trained on a new domain and its parameters drift away from representations useful for the earlier domain. The paper reports that for the 1.3 B model, training **only** on code first and then on math leads to:

* A drop in coding ability after the math stage (the model no longer solves HumanEval as well).  
* Only modest gains on pure CoT math tasks when compared with a single-stage mixed curriculum.

The underlying reasons are:

1. **Capacity bottleneck.**  
   A 1.3 B network has limited hidden dimensionality; it cannot simultaneously allocate distinct subspaces for two very different token distributions (source‑code vs. mathematical prose). When it later sees大量的数学文本, gradient updates push many weights toward representing frequent math tokens ($\sum$ , $≈$ , $⇒$) at the expense of code‑specific tokens (`def`, `self`, `:`).  

2. **Distribution shift without rehearsal.**  
   In a two‑stage schedule the second stage never revisits any code examples, so there is no rehearsal signal that reminds the optimizer how to keep code competence alive. The optimizer therefore follows the steepest descent direction for the current loss—purely math—and erodes previously learned code patterns.

3. **Different token vocabularies and token frequencies.**  
   The tokenizer used by DeepSeek‑LLM treats many punctuation symbols as separate tokens; their frequencies differ drastically between code (lots of `{`, `}`, `:`) and math (lots of Greek letters, operators). After many updates on math data, embedding vectors for those high‑frequency code symbols receive far fewer gradient steps, causing them to become under‑trained.

The authors’ mitigation experiment – mixing code and math tokens in one stage – supplies continual rehearsal for code symbols while still exposing the model to mathematics, which dramatically reduces forgetting (see Table 4).  For larger models this effect is weaker because they possess enough capacity to carve out separate “expert” subspaces; however, even at 7 B some degradation is still observed if code is entirely removed after stage 1.

---

### (c) Would larger models benefit more from mixed code + math training?

The paper’s primary large scale experiment uses a **7 B** model that starts from *DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5*.  The observations are:

* When training *only* on math tokens, performance on program‑aided benchmarks drops sharply (the model forgets how to emit syntactically correct Python).  
* When training on a **mixed** curriculum (≈ 400 B code + 150 B math tokens in one pass), both coding ability and mathematical reasoning improve simultaneously, and no catastrophic forgetting is seen.

For a **larger transformer**, we can reason as follows:

1. **Higher capacity reduces interference.**  
   With more layers/heads, the model can allocate dedicated attention heads to capture programming grammar while other heads learn mathematical semantics. Empirically this translates into smaller performance swings when switching domains, which suggests mixed training would be at least as beneficial as in the 7 B case.

2. **Scale amplifies data efficiency gains.**  
   The paper shows that adding high‑quality web math data yields disproportionate gains even for a small base (“DeepSeekMath–Base 7B matches Minerva 540B”).  Because larger models already make better use of each token, providing them with *both* domains concurrently lets them exploit synergies earlier: they learn to reuse AST–like representations across domains rather than relearn them from scratch.

3. **Empirical hint from related works.**  
   Prior studies on *code→math* transfer (e.g., Minerva, CodeLlama + MathInstruct) report that models ≥ 30 B benefit from an initial coding phase but do not suffer severe forgetting when later exposed to massive math corpora—larger models simply retain coding competence longer.

Thus it is reasonable to expect that **larger models would gain even more from a mixed curriculum**, because they can keep both skill sets alive while still extracting richer cross‑domain patterns (e.g., recognizing that solving an integral often reduces to calling `sympy.integrate`).  Nevertheless, if one insists on strict two‑stage training even at large scale, some rehearsal mechanism (e.g., occasional replay of code samples or interleaved fine‑tuning) would still be advisable to avoid any residual forgetting.

---

### Putting it all together

*Code pre‑training supplies structural priors—grammar, hierarchical composition, and procedural thinking—that are directly useful for symbolic mathematics.*  
When you later expose the same network to pure mathematical text, those priors accelerate learning of CoT reasoning and make PoT generation more reliable.

*Catastrophic forgetting arises because a small model cannot simultaneously encode two divergent token distributions without continual rehearsal.*  The second stage overwrites many parameters learned during the first stage.

*Mixing the two data streams during pre‑training solves both problems: it preserves coding skill through constant exposure while still delivering massive math gains.*  Larger models are expected to reap even larger benefits because they have enough capacity to maintain separate expert subspaces and therefore can fully exploit synergies between programming and mathematics.

In practice, if you are training a new LLM for mathematical reasoning you should either:

1. start from a strong **code‐oriented checkpoint**, then continue training on a large, high‑quality math corpus while **interleaving a modest amount of code data**, or  
2. adopt an explicit replay strategy (e.g., periodic sampling of saved code batches) when performing strictly sequential stages.

Both approaches follow exactly what the DeepSeekMath experiments demonstrate and explain why they work.

### How are outcome supervision and process supervision implemented in GRPO?
Question:
The paper describes outcome supervision as assigning the same normalized reward to all tokens of an output, while process supervision provides step‑wise rewards. I am unsure how "steps" are detected in generated text (e.g., via special tokens or line breaks), how the per‑step rewards are normalized across a group, and how the advantage \( \hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} \) propagates back to earlier tokens. Could someone clarify with an example of a CoT answer containing three reasoning steps?
Answer:
**Answer**

In GRPO the reward that drives the policy update can be given either **once at the end of the whole answer** (outcome supervision) or **after every reasoning step** (process supervision).  
Both ways use the same mathematical machinery – the advantage of token *t* in sampled answer *i* is  

\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{step }j:\; \text{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} ,
\]

where $\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$ is a *normalized* reward for step *j*. The only difference lies in how we define a “step’’ and what rewards we attach to it.

---

### 1. How a “step’’ is detected in generated text  

The papers’ implementation follows the practice of most chain‑of‑thought (CoT) datasets: a step is demarcated by a **special separator token** that is inserted during data preparation. Concretely:

* In the SFT data each CoT solution is written as  

```
Answer: 12
Step 1: … (first sub‑calculation)
Step 2: … (second sub‑calculation)
Step 3: … (final reasoning)
```

* When the model is trained, every line that begins with “Step *k*:” or a line break after a period and a space is replaced by a **single sentinel token** (e.g. `<step>`). The token sequence therefore looks like  

```
… <step> … <step> … <step> …
```

During generation the same sentinel appears whenever the model decides to start a new reasoning chunk, so the reward model can later locate it easily.

If your own data do not contain an explicit sentinel, you can treat a newline or a “**;**” that follows a complete statement as a step boundary – the paper only requires that all tokens belonging to one logical chunk share the same end‑of‑step index.

---

### 2. Normalising rewards across a *group*  

GRPO samples **$G$** completions $\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}$ for the same question $q$. The raw scores from the reward model are

\[
r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \quad\text{(outcome) or}\quad
r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \quad\text{(process)},
\]

where $i$ indexes the sampled answer and $\text{index}(j)$ is the token position of step *j*.  
All scores in the group are pooled into one list  

\[
\mathbf R = \bigl\{ r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \mid i=1,\dots ,G,\; j=1,\dots ,K_i\bigr\},
\]

and then **standardised**:

\[
\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} = \frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)} - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)}
{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)} .
\]

This has two effects:

1. It removes any absolute scale that depends on the particular question, making the advantage comparable across groups.
2. It centres the distribution at zero, so that tokens belonging to *better* answers receive positive advantage while tokens from *worse* answers obtain negative advantage.

For outcome supervision there is only one reward per answer, i.e. $K_i=1$; for process supervision there may be several $K_i>1$.

---

### 3. From normalised step‑wise rewards to token‑level advantages  

Consider an example CoT with three steps (the sentinel `<step>` marks each boundary):

```
Question: …
<step> Compute A = 7×8 = 56.
<step> Use A to find B = A – 10 = 46.
<step> The final answer is B = 46.
```

Assume GRPO samples $G=4$ completions and the process‑reward model gives the following *raw* step scores (higher = better):

| answer | step 1 | step 2 | step 3 |
|-------|-------|-------|-------|
| o₁    | 0.6   | 0.7   | 0.9 |
| o₂    | 0.4   | 0.5   | 0.8 |
| o₃    | 0.5   | 0.6   | 0.6 |
| o₄    | 0.3   | 0.4   | 0.5 |

All nine numbers constitute $\mathbf R$. Their mean ≈ 0.55, std ≈ 0.18, so after normalisation we obtain $\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$.  
For answer $o_2$ we might get (rounded)

| step 1 | step 2 | step 3 |
|-------|-------|-------|
| –0.83 | –0.28 | 1.39 |

Now compute advantages for each token:

* Tokens belonging to **step 3** (the last chunk) see only their own normalised reward, e.g. for token “46” in step 3 of $o_2$

\[
\hat A_{2,t}= \widetilde r_2^{\text{index}(3)} = 1.39 .
\]

* Tokens in **step 2** receive the sum of rewards of step 2 **and** step 3, because any later improvement should also be credited to earlier decisions:

\[
\hat A_{2,t}= \widetilde r_2^{\text{index}(2)} + \widetilde r_2^{\text{index}(3)}
            = -0.28 + 1.39 = 1.11 .
\]

* Tokens in **step 1** get all three rewards summed:

\[
\hat A_{2,t}= -0.83 -0.28 + 1.39 = 0.28 .
\]

Thus earlier tokens that eventually lead to a high final score obtain a *positive* cumulative advantage, while those that contribute to a low final score would have a negative sum.

These per‑token advantages are plugged into the GRPO surrogate loss (Eq.​ (4) of the paper). The policy gradient therefore nudges the probability of tokens with large positive $\hat A_{i,t}$ upward and those with negative $\hat A_{i,t}$ downward.

---

### 4. Summary of differences between outcome and process supervision  

| Aspect | Outcome supervision | Process supervision |
|--------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Reward granularity | One scalar per answer (`r_i`) placed at the final token only | One scalar per reasoning step (`r_i^{index(j)}`) placed at each `<step>` token |
| Advantage computation | Same normalised reward copied to every token: $\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i$ | Cumulative sum of later‑step rewards: $\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{index(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{index(j)}$ |
| Signal richness | Coarse; only final correctness matters | Fine‑grained; tells which intermediate reasoning moves were helpful |
| Implementation requirement | Only need an end‑of‑sequence reward model | Need a process‑reward model that can evaluate partial outputs up to each `<step>` token |

In practice you will see process supervision yielding larger gains on difficult math problems because it tells the model *where* it went wrong, not merely whether it succeeded overall.

--- 

**Take‑away:**  
To reproduce GRPO’s process supervision you must (1) annotate your CoT data with an explicit step delimiter (a sentinel token), (2) train or fine‑tune a reward model that can score a prefix ending at any delimiter, (3) normalise all step scores within each sampled group, and (4) compute token‑level advantages by summing all normalised scores from the current step onward as shown above. The same pipeline collapses to outcome supervision when you simply set $K_i=1$ and copy the normalised score to every token of an answer.

### Why does reinforcement learning mainly improve Maj@K rather than Pass@K according to Figure 8?
Question:
The authors state that RL makes the output distribution more robust, boosting correct responses from TopK but not changing fundamental capability. How is Maj@K computed in this context (majority vote over multiple samples per question?), and why would GRPO’s group‑relative baseline lead to higher probability mass on already good answers rather than generating better new solutions? Is this an artifact of using only outcome supervision with a reward model trained on correctness?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DeepSeekMath paper the authors evaluate the effect of their reinforcement‑learning (RL) stage by reporting two families of metrics:

* **Pass@K** – the probability that at least one of *K* sampled answers is *exactly* correct.
* **Maj@K** – the accuracy obtained when we take a *majority vote* over the *K* sampled answers (i.e., we pick the answer that appears most often among the *K* samples and check whether that answer is correct).

Both metrics are computed on a **fixed set of test questions**; for each question we generate *K* completions from the model (the same *K* is used for Pass@K and for Maj@K).  Pass@K therefore measures “does the model ever produce a correct solution”, while Maj@K measures “does the model tend to put most of its probability mass on a correct solution”.

---

### Why RL lifts Maj@K but leaves Pass@K almost unchanged

The RL algorithm used in the paper is **Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.  Its essential ingredients are:

1. **Outcome‑only reward** – a binary reward (correct / incorrect) that is supplied only at the end of a generated answer.
2. **Group‑relative baseline** – for each question a *group* of $G$ samples (64 in the experiments) is drawn from the current policy; the baseline used to compute advantages is simply the mean reward of that group.
3. **KL regularisation against a fixed reference model** – this prevents the policy from drifting arbitrarily far from the SFT model.

Because the advantage for every token in a sample is proportional to  

$$\hat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)},$$  

all tokens belonging to a *correct* answer receive **a positive, identical signal**, while tokens belonging to an *incorrect* answer receive **a negative, identical signal**.  In other words, GRPO does not distinguish *how much better* one correct answer is compared with another; it only learns to increase the total probability mass assigned to any answer that happens to be correct.

Consequences:

| What GRPO reinforces | Effect on distribution |
|----------------------|------------------------|
| “Any correct answer” | Pushes probability from low‑scoring (incorrect) outputs toward *any* high‑scoring output |
| “Correctness only”   | Does not create new, previously unseen solution strategies; it only makes existing good solutions more likely |

When we look at **Pass@K**, it already saturates fairly early because even the SFT model can produce a correct answer in at least one of 64 samples for many questions.  Adding GRPO does not create *new* correct answers, so the probability that “at least one” sample is correct changes little.

In contrast, **Maj@K** depends on how much of the total probability mass sits on a single correct answer.  Before RL, the distribution over samples may be fairly flat: a question might yield several distinct correct solutions and many incorrect ones, so no single answer dominates and a majority vote often selects an incorrect answer.  After GRPO, because all correct samples receive a uniform positive boost and all incorrect samples are uniformly penalised, the mass concentrates on whichever correct answer appears most frequently in the group.  Hence, when we take a majority vote over the same *K$, we are now much more likely to pick that dominant correct answer, raising Maj@K.

---

### Intuition behind “majority‑vote” vs “any‑correct”

Think of each question as a roulette wheel whose slices correspond to different generated completions.  The SFT wheel has many small slices for both correct and incorrect answers; spinning it 64 times gives you many different colours, so you might hit a green slice (correct) once but you seldom see green dominate.  GRPO rewrites the wheel by merging all green slices into a few larger ones while shrinking red slices.  The chance that at least one spin lands on green (Pass@K) barely changes—the green area was already non‑zero—but now green occupies enough area that most spins land on it, so a majority of spins are green (higher Maj@K).

---

### Relation to “outcome‑only” supervision

Because GRPO’s reward signal comes only from an outcome model trained on binary correctness, it cannot reward intermediate reasoning steps that lead to *novel* solution ideas.  The authors themselves note in §4.3 (“Why RL works?”) that this explains why RL mainly makes the output distribution **more robust** rather than expanding fundamental capability: it redistributes probability towards already‑good solutions instead of discovering new ones.  If one were to employ a richer process‑level reward (as they also experiment with), or use more diverse question distributions during exploration, the RL signal could potentially create new high‑reward regions and thus improve Pass@K as well.

---

### Summary

* **Maj@K** is computed by majority voting over *K* model outputs per test question.
* GRPO uses group‑relative advantages that give every *correct* sample an equal positive push and every *incorrect* sample an equal negative push.
* This pushes probability mass onto existing correct solutions, making them dominate the sampled set—hence Maj@K rises.
* Pass@K measures merely “does any sample hit a correct answer”; because SFT already has non‑negligible chance of doing so, uniformly up‑weighting all correct answers does not markedly increase this chance.
* The effect is therefore an artefact of using only outcome supervision with a binary correctness reward; richer rewards or more diverse exploration could change both metrics.

Thus, Figure 8 reflects that RL as implemented improves *robustness* of the distribution rather than expanding its *capability*.

### What does "iterative RL with GRPO" entail and how does replaying 10% of historic data affect training?
Question:
Algorithm 1 mentions updating the reward model after each iteration using a replay buffer that contains 10% historical samples. I am confused about: (i) how new training pairs for the reward model are generated from the current policy, (ii) why only 10% of old data is kept instead of full history, and (iii) what impact this has on stability or preventing reward drift across iterations.
Answer:
**Answer**

Algorithm 1 in the DeepSeekMath paper describes an *iterative* reinforcement‑learning loop built on the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) objective.  
The loop consists of two tightly coupled moving parts:

1. **the policy (the LLM) that generates new answers**, and  
2. **the reward model that judges those answers**.

At the end of each GRPO iteration the reward model is refreshed with a small replay buffer that contains *10 %* of the samples that were used to train it in previous iterations. Below we unpack how the new training pairs are obtained, why only a fraction of the old data is retained, and what effect this design has on stability and reward drift.

---

### (i) How are new training pairs for the reward model generated?

Each GRPO iteration proceeds as follows (see Algorithm 1):

1. **Sample a batch of questions** \(q\) from the instruction‑tuning set \(\mathcal D\) (the same GSM8K/MATH prompts used for SFT).  
2. **Generate a *group* of outputs** \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\) for every question from the *current* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). In the experiments \(G=64\); the generation uses nucleus sampling with a maximum length of 1024 tokens.  
3. **Score each output** with the *current* reward model \(r_{\varphi}\), obtaining scalar rewards \(r_i\). In outcome‑supervision these scores are the final‑step rewards; in process‑supervision each reasoning step is also scored, but the pairwise data needed for training the reward model are still whole answer‑reward pairs.  
4. **Form training pairs** \((q,o_i,r_i)\). For each question we now have up to 64 new (question, answer, reward) triples that reflect how the latest policy behaves on that prompt.

These triples are then fed to the reward‑model update step (the “continuous training” line in Algorithm 1). The loss is a standard pairwise preference loss: for two answers \(o_a, o_b\) to the same question we encourage the model to assign a higher score to the one with larger empirical reward (e.g., higher correctness or higher process‑reward). Thus each iteration creates fresh supervision data directly from the policy’s own behaviour.

---

### (ii) Why keep only 10 % of historic samples instead of the whole history?

The replay buffer is deliberately *small* for two practical reasons that are discussed implicitly in Section 5.2 (“Iterative RL with GRPO”) and reflected in the experimental design:

| Reason | What happens if we keep everything? |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| **Memory / compute budget** – The reward model is trained repeatedly after every iteration. Storing millions of past (question, answer, reward) triples quickly overwhelms GPU memory and would make each update orders of magnitude slower. |
| **Staleness of old data** – As training progresses, newer policy outputs are *systematically better* (or at least different) than early‑stage outputs. Old pairs become less informative for refining a model that now operates in a different region of answer space; they can even bias the reward model toward rewarding obsolete behaviours. |
| **Diversity vs. redundancy** – A random 10 % sample preserves a representative slice of earlier behaviours while discarding duplicated or near‑identical examples that add little signal. Empirically this small slice is enough to keep a smooth trajectory for the reward model without hurting performance (see Fig. 9 in the paper where performance jumps after each iteration even with a 10 % replay). |

In other words, 10 % provides a *memory‑efficient* approximation of “full history” that still supplies a grounding signal preventing catastrophic forgetting of earlier behaviours.

---

### (iii) How does this replay strategy affect stability and prevent reward drift?

Reward drift—where successive updates cause the reward model to move away from its original intent—is a known failure mode in iterative RL pipelines. The authors mitigate it in three complementary ways:

1. **Baseline anchored by group averages:** GRPO computes advantages \(\hat A_{i,t}\) by subtracting the group mean reward from each sample’s score (outcome supervision) or by summing normalized step rewards (process supervision). This *relative* scoring automatically centres each batch, so even if the absolute scale of rewards drifts, the advantage signal stays stable.

2. **Replay of historic data:** By mixing in 10 % of past pairs, the loss sees both “new” and “old” comparisons at every update. This regularises \(r_{\varphi}\): it cannot simply over‑fit to whatever pattern appears in the current batch because it must also continue to honour older comparisons that may span different regions of answer space.

3. **KL regularisation on the policy:** GRPO adds an explicit KL term \(\beta \,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) to the objective (Equation 2). The reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is set to the *previous* policy after each outer iteration, which together with replay ensures that both policy and reward evolve gradually rather than making abrupt jumps.

The empirical evidence in Section 5.2 shows that after one outer iteration performance rises sharply, while after subsequent iterations gains become smaller but still positive – indicating that neither component collapses. Moreover, Figure 10 (“iterative RL”) demonstrates that using replay yields smoother learning curves than a naïve “re‑train from scratch” baseline, confirming that replay curbs oscillations caused by drifting rewards.

---

### Putting it all together

In each outer iteration:

1. **Generate fresh answer–reward pairs** by sampling many outputs per question with the current policy.
2. **Update the reward model** on those fresh pairs plus a modest (10 %) replay buffer of older pairs.
3. **Treat this newly trained reward model as fixed** while performing one step of GRPO on the policy; KL regularisation ties the updated policy back to its predecessor.
4. **Replace the reference model** with the newly updated policy and repeat.

The 10 % replay is therefore not an arbitrary heuristic; it is a lightweight form of experience replay that gives enough historical context to stabilise both reward‑model learning and policy updates while keeping computational cost low. This design lets DeepSeekMath achieve *iterative* improvements—e.g., GSM8K accuracy jumps from 82.9 % → 88.2 %—without suffering from runaway reward drift or instability across iterations.

### How does self‑consistency over 64 samples raise DeepSeekMath’s MATH score from 51.7% to 60.9%?
Question:
The abstract says self‑consistency over 64 samples yields 60.9% on MATH. Is this simply taking a majority vote over 64 independently sampled CoT answers per question? If so, why does increasing sample count help so dramatically for this model compared to others? Does GRPO’s training encourage higher variance among samples that can be aggregated effectively?
Answer:
**Answer**

Yes – the “self‑consistency over $64$ samples” reported in the DeepSeekMath paper is exactly the majority‑vote (or *top‑k‑majority*) procedure that has become the standard way of turning a stochastic chain‑of‑thought (CoT) generator into a more reliable solver.  
For each MATH problem the model is asked to generate $64$ independent completions (each using nucleus sampling with the same temperature). Every completion contains a full CoT reasoning trace and a final answer token. The answers are then counted; the answer that appears most frequently is taken as the model’s prediction. If several answers tie, the one with the higher average log‑probability is selected. This procedure is what the authors refer to as **self‑consistency**.

---

### Why does increasing the number of samples improve the score so dramatically for DeepSeekMath?

1. **Variance in CoT generations is useful** – A single CoT trace can be wrong for many reasons (mis‑applied algebra, arithmetic slip, or an early logical error). Different random seeds often lead the model down different reasoning paths, some of which happen to be correct. When you collect many traces you obtain a *distribution* over possible answers. The correct answer, if it is reachable at all, tends to be produced by more than one path, whereas systematic mistakes are usually reproduced by a majority of samples. Taking a majority vote therefore filters out many spurious errors.

2. **DeepSeekMath’s training increases the “reachability” of correct solutions** – The model benefits from two ingredients that specifically encourage diverse yet correct reasoning:
   * **Math‑focused pre‑training on $120\,$B high‑quality web tokens** gives the model a broader exposure to different problem‑solving styles (textual derivations, symbolic manipulations, program‑of‑thought examples). This exposure makes it more likely that at least one of the sampled CoTs will follow a useful line of reasoning.
   * **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** fine‑tunes the model with an objective that rewards *relative* superiority among a group of outputs sampled for the same question. In GRPO each sample’s advantage is computed by subtracting the mean reward of its group (see Eq. (4) in the paper). Consequently, during RL training the policy learns to **spread its probability mass toward higher‑reward trajectories while simultaneously suppressing consistently low‑reward ones**. The effect is an increase in *inter‑sample variance*: some samples become markedly better than others rather than all collapsing onto one dominant (often incorrect) style.

   Because GRPO explicitly normalises rewards within each group, it encourages a **competition** among sampled answers. This competition sharpens the contrast between good and bad reasoning paths, which in turn makes majority voting more powerful: the correct path stands out more clearly among many tries.

3. **Sample size vs. diminishing returns** – In earlier works (e.g., self‑consistency applied to GPT‑3) improving from $8$ to $16$ or $32$ samples gave modest gains; moving to $64$ brings a larger jump because DeepSeekMath’s policy distribution has already become *bimodal*: a sizable chunk of its probability mass sits on high‑quality solutions while another chunk remains on low‑quality shortcuts. With only a few draws you may miss the high‑quality mode; with $64$ draws you almost guarantee hitting it multiple times, so the majority vote switches from an incorrect answer (the mode of the low‑quality region) to the correct one.

4. **Comparison with other models** – Many open‑source baselines have been trained only with supervised fine‑tuning or with PPO that relies on an external value function. Their output distributions tend to be *less* peaked on high‑quality solutions and *more* concentrated on a single (often flawed) style of reasoning, so increasing $K$ yields smaller marginal improvements. DeepSeekMath’s combination of massive domain data plus GRPO therefore yields a distribution that reacts more positively to additional samples.

---

### Does GRPO explicitly encourage higher variance among samples?

GRPO does not *directly* maximise variance; instead it **uses variance as a signal**. By computing advantages as

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)},
$$

the algorithm treats an output whose reward is above the group mean as having positive advantage and those below as negative. When gradient updates are applied, tokens belonging to high‑reward trajectories receive reinforcement, while tokens belonging to low‑reward trajectories are penalised. Over many training steps this pushes some trajectories up and others down, naturally enlarging their spread.

Thus GRPO creates precisely the condition in which self‑consistency shines: there exist *distinct* high‑reward and low‑reward families of completions that can be separated by simple majority voting.

---

### Take‑away

*Self‑consistency* in DeepSeekMath is indeed majority voting over $64$ independently sampled CoT answers per question.  
The dramatic jump from **51.7 %** (single greedy decode) to **60.9 %** ($64$‑sample self‑consistency) comes from two synergistic facts:

1. The pre‑training corpus and code‑first initialization give the model many plausible reasoning patterns, increasing the chance that at least one sampled trace hits a correct solution.
2. GRPO RL fine‑tunes the policy so that good traces are systematically preferred over bad ones *within each sampled group*, resulting in a bimodal output distribution where a majority vote can reliably pick out the correct answer.

Consequently, while other models also benefit from self‑consistency, DeepSeekMath’s training pipeline amplifies this effect far more than standard PPO or supervised fine‑tuning alone.

### Can the unified paradigm (Equation 9) be used to derive DPO as a special case of PPO/GRPO?
Question:
The authors claim that supervised fine‑tuning, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO can all be expressed with gradient coefficient \(GC_{\mathcal{A}}\). I would like to see an explicit derivation showing how DPO’s pairwise loss fits into this framework—what choices of data source, reward function \(\pi_{rf}\), and algorithm produce the DPO gradient coefficient? This would help me understand the connections between these methods.
Answer:
Below is a step‑by‑step sketch of how the pair‑wise Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss emerges as a special‑case of the **unified gradient‑coefficient formulation** presented in Equation $(9)$ of the DeepSeekMath paper.  
The goal is to identify which **data source** $\mathcal D$, which **reward function** $\pi_{\text{rf}}$ (the “reference” or “reward” policy), and which **algorithmic processing** $\mathcal A$ produce exactly the DPO gradient coefficient $GC_{\text{DPO}}$.

---

### 1. The unified gradient‑coefficient view

Equation $(9)$ writes the gradient of any training objective $\mathcal J_{\mathcal A}$ as  

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal J_{\mathcal A}(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal D}
\Bigl[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
GC_{\mathcal A}(q,o,t,\pi_{\text{rf}})\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})\Bigr].
\tag{9}
\]

The three ingredients are:

* **Data source $\mathcal D$** – a distribution over question–output pairs.  
* **Reward function / reference policy $\pi_{\text{rf}}$** – supplies a scalar signal that the algorithm turns into a coefficient.  
* **Algorithm $\mathcal A$** – the rule that maps the raw reward into $GC_{\mathcal A}$.

When we instantiate these three components appropriately we recover the known losses (SFT, RFT, PPO, …).  

---

### 2. What DPO actually optimises

The original DPO paper (Huang et al., 2024) maximises the *pairwise* likelihood  

\[
\max_\theta \;\; 
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(r_\varphi(q,y^+) - r_\varphi(q,y^-)\Bigr)
+ \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|q)}{\pi_\theta(y^-|q)},
\tag{D}
\]

where  

* $y^+$ is a preferred response, $y^-$ a dispreferred one (both sampled from the current policy or from an offline policy),  
* $r_\varphi(q,\cdot)$ is a learned reward model (the “preference model”), and  
* $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ is the sigmoid.

Differentiating $(D)$ with respect to $\theta$ gives a gradient that can be written in the same shape as $(9)$:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{DPO}}
= \mathbb{E}_{(q,y^+,y^-)}\Bigl[
GC_{\text{DPO}}(q,y^\pm)\,
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y^+|q)
- GC_{\text{DPO}}(q,y^\pm)\,
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y^-|q)
\Bigr],
\]

with a *single* coefficient applied symmetrically to the preferred and dispreferred terms.

---

### 3. Mapping DPO onto the unified paradigm

#### 3.1 Data source $\mathcal D_{\text{DPO}}$

DPO uses **offline sampled pairs**:  

1. Start from an already‑trained SFT model (the “base policy” $\pi_{\text{SFT}}$).  
2. For each question $q$ draw two outputs, $y^+$ and $y^-$, from the *same* base policy (or from a mixture of base‑policy and a modestly updated policy).  
3. Keep the pair only if a *preference* label is known – in practice this label comes from a reward model $r_\varphi$: if $r_\varphi(q,y^+)> r_\varphi(q,y^-)$ we treat $(y^+,y^-)$ as a preferred/dispreferred pair.

Hence  

\[
(q,y^+,y^-)\sim \underbrace{\bigl(P(Q),\,\pi_{\text{SFT}}(Y|Q),\,\pi_{\text{SFT}}(Y|Q)\bigr)}_{\text{offline sampling}}.
\]

This matches the “offline” column in Table 1 of the paper.

#### 3.2 Reward function / reference policy $\pi_{\text{rf}}$

In Equation $(9)$ the reward function appears only through its *gradient coefficient*.  
For DPO we set  

* **Reference policy** $\pi_{\text{rf}} = \pi_{\text{SFT}}$.  
* **Reward signal** = the *preference score* produced by the learned reward model:  

  \[
  s(q,y^+,y^-)=r_\varphi(q,y^+)-r_\varphi(q,y^-).
  \]

Because DPO’s loss contains a sigmoid of this difference, the coefficient will be proportional to  

\[
\sigma(s)- (1-\sigma(s)) = 2\sigma(s)-1.
\]

Thus we can view the reward function as mapping a pair of outputs to a *scalar advantage*  

\[
A_{q,y^\pm}= 2\sigma\!\bigl(r_\varphi(q,y^+)-r_\varphi(q,y^-)\bigr)-1.
\tag{1}
\]

#### 3.3 Algorithmic transformation → Gradient coefficient

The algorithmic step for DPO simply **multiplies** each log‑probability term by this advantage:

\[
GC_{\text{DPO}}(q,y^\pm)=A_{q,y^\pm}.
\tag{2}
\]

There is no extra clipping, baseline subtraction or KL penalty; it is a *direct* scaling of the log‑prob gradient by the signed preference signal.

Putting (1)–(2) back into Equation $(9)$ yields exactly the DPO update:

```
∇θJ_DPO = E_{q,y+,y-}[ A(q,y+,y-) ∇θ log πθ(y+|q)
                    - A(q,y+,y-) ∇θ log πθ(y-|q) ] .
```

Because each pair appears once with a + sign for the preferred output and once with a – sign for the dispreferred output, we recover the *pairwise* formulation of DPO without any extra terms.

---

### 4. Intuition behind each component

* **Why offline sampling?**  
  DPO treats preferences as static observations that do not depend on how the current policy behaves during training. This makes it cheap: no on‑policy roll‑outs, no critic, no value function—exactly what Equation $(9)$’s “offline” branch captures.

* **Why use a sigmoid of reward differences?**  
  The sigmoid turns an unbounded difference $r_\varphi(y^+)-r_\varphi(y^-)$ into a probability‑like weight lying in $(-1,1)$. When the preference is strong ($|s|\gg0$) we get coefficients close to $+1/-1$; when preferences are ambiguous ($s\simeq0$) we get coefficients near zero, meaning those pairs have little influence—mirroring how humans treat weakly decided comparisons.

* **Why no KL term?**  
  In PPO/GRPO an explicit KL penalty stabilises learning against catastrophic drift from the reference model. DPO’s coefficient already shrinks to zero for uncertain pairs, which implicitly regularises updates; consequently an extra KL term is unnecessary in its original formulation.

### 5. Summary table (conceptual)

| Component | DPO instantiation | Equation $(9)$ slot |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Data source $\mathcal D$ | Offline sampled triplets $(q,y^+,y^-)$ from SFT policy | first expectation in $(9)$ |
| Reward / reference policy $\pi_{\text{rf}}$ | Fixed SFT policy; reward model provides scalar differences | appears inside $GC_{\mathcal A}$ |
| Algorithmic mapping → $GC_{\mathcal A}$ | Pairwise advantage $A=2\sigma(r_\varphi(y^+)-r_\varphi(y^-))-1$ | Equation $(9)$’s coefficient |

Thus, by choosing an **offline data source**, fixing the **reference policy to be the SFT model**, and defining **$GC_{\text{DPO}}$** exactly as above, Equation $(9)$’s general gradient recovers the DPO pairwise loss. Consequently, DPO can indeed be seen as a special case of the unified paradigm described in the DeepSeekMath paper, sitting between pure supervised fine‑tuning (where $GC=1$) and full PPO/GRPO (where $GC$ involves advantages computed from per‑token rewards and baselines). This derivation clarifies that all those methods share a common mathematical backbone; they differ only in *how* they generate data, *what* reward signal they use, and *how* they translate that signal into a scalar multiplier for each token’s log‑probability gradient.$
