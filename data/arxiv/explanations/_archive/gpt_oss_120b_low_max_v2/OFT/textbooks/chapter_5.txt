Chapter 5: # Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) – Adding a Radius Constraint  

In the previous chapter we introduced **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, a principled way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models by learning a layer‑wise orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \). OFT guarantees that the hyperspherical energy – the sum of pairwise angular similarities among neurons – remains exactly unchanged, because an orthogonal transformation preserves all inner products on the unit sphere. While this invariance gives COFT a solid theoretical footing, it still leaves a degree of freedom: any orthogonal matrix can be applied, ranging from the identity (no change) to a full rotation or reflection.  

In practice, when we finetune a model for a downstream task such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation, we would like to **limit how far the finetuned model can drift** from its pretrained parent. If the transformation is too large, subtle but crucial aspects of the pretrained distribution may be overwritten, leading to instability or collapse. Conversely, if the transformation is too restrictive, the model may not acquire enough capacity to satisfy the new task. **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** augments OFT with an explicit radius constraint that directly controls this drift.

The central idea is to keep \( \mathbf{R} \) orthogonal **and** force it to stay within an \( \varepsilon \)-ball around the identity matrix:
\[
\| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \| \;\le\; \varepsilon .
\]
Here \( \| \cdot \| \) denotes a matrix norm (typically the Frobenius norm) and \( \varepsilon > 0\) is a hyperparameter set by the practitioner. The resulting formulation writes the forward pass of a finetuned linear layer as
\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\! \mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I},\;
\|\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\| \le \varepsilon .
\tag{1}
\]
In what follows we explain how this constraint is embedded within a differentiable training pipeline, why it matters for stability, and how to pick an appropriate \( \varepsilon\) for different downstream scenarios.

---  

## The “\( \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\)-ball” Constraint: Why It Matters  

### Intuition from Geometry  
Consider each layer’s weight matrix as a set of vectors attached at the origin of a \(d\)-dimensional space. An orthogonal matrix rotates or reflects all vectors simultaneously; geometrically it moves each vector along a great‑circle on the unit hypersphere while preserving pairwise angles. If we allow any rotation, these vectors can end up arbitrarily far from their original locations on that sphere, albeit still maintaining relative distances.  

The **\( \varepsilon\)-ball** constraint says: *the whole set of vectors must not travel farther than a prescribed angular radius*. Since for small perturbations,
\[
\| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \|_{\!F} \approx 2\,\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_2,
\]
where \( \boldsymbol{\theta}\) encodes the rotation angles (via exponential map), bounding \( \| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I}\|\) directly bounds the maximal rotation angle applied to any neuron. Thus COFT limits **how much directional information** can be altered while still guaranteeing that pairwise angles stay intact.

### Stability vs. Flexibility Trade‑off  
If \( \varepsilon\) is set **very small**, COFT behaves almost like conventional fine‑tuning with a tiny learning rate: weights move only minimally, preserving most of the pretrained generation quality but possibly failing to incorporate new control signals. When \( \varepsilon\) is **large**, COFT approaches unrestricted OFT: the model enjoys maximal flexibility to adapt its representation but may experience instability akin to naive finetuning (e.g., mode collapse or loss of subject identity). The proper choice therefore sits somewhere in between, offering enough freedom for task‑specific adaptation while keeping the model anchored to its pretrained manifold.

## Embedding the Constraint via Cayley Parameterization and Projected Gradient Descent  

### Cayley Transform Recap  
Recall that any orthogonal matrix with determinant \(+1\) can be expressed via the **Cayley transform**:
\[
\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q}^{\!\top}= -\,\mathbf{Q}.
\tag{2}
\]
Here \( \mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric and serves as an unconstrained latent variable. When we update \( \mathbf{Q}\) with gradient descent, Eq. (2) automatically yields an orthogonal \( \mathbf{R}\).

### Translating the Radius Constraint  
The constraint in Eq. (1) involves \( \mathbf{R}\), not directly \( \mathbf{Q}\). To enforce it efficiently we first approximate the Cayley transform with its **Neumann series**:
\[
(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1}
 =  \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\!   \mathbf{Q}^{\,k}
   =  \mathbf{I}+  \mathbf{Q}+  O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}),
\]
so that for sufficiently small \( \|\mathbf{Q}\|\):
\[
\mathbf{R}
   = (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}+  \mathbf{Q}+O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}))
   =  \mathbf{I}+2\,\mathbf{Q}+ O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}). 
   \tag{3}
\]
Dropping higher‑order terms (reasonable because COFT explicitly limits how far we move from identity), we obtain an approximate linear relationship between deviation in \( R\) and magnitude of \( Q:\)
\[
   \|\!\;  \underbrace{\bigl(\,\mathbf R-\!\,\mathbf I\,\bigr)}_{\approx\,2\,\mathbf Q}\;\|_F
   ~~\approx~~ 2\,  \|\!\,\mathbf Q\,\|_F .
   \tag{4}
\]
Thus enforcing
\[
    \|\!\,\bigl(\,\mathbf R-\!\,\mathbf I\,\bigr)\!\|_F    ~~\le~~    ε
    ~~  ⇔  ~~   2\,\|\!\,\mathbf Q\,\|_F   ~~≤~~ ε,
    ~~⇔~~   ‖ 𝑄 ‖_F ≤ ε' := ε/2 .
    \tag{5}
\]

Consequently we can **embed the radius constraint directly on \( 𝑄 )**: after each gradient step on \(𝑄,\) we project it back onto the Frobenius ball of radius \(ε'\):
```python
# pseudo‑code for one update step
Q ← Q - η * ∇_Q L            # ordinary gradient descent on loss L
if ‖Q‖_F > ε' :
    Q ← ε' * Q / ‖Q‖_F       # projection onto Frobenius ε'-ball
```
Because projection onto a convex ball is differentiable almost everywhere (the scaling operation), this procedure integrates seamlessly into standard automatic‑differentiation frameworks (PyTorch, JAX, etc.). Moreover, initializing \(𝑄=0_{d\times d}\) guarantees that at training start we have exactly \( R = I.\)

### Maintaining Exact Orthogonality  
While Eq. (3) provides an approximation useful for setting \(ε',`)`the actual forward pass continues to use Eq. (2). That is, after projection we recompute 
\( R = (\boldsymbol I + Q)(\boldsymbol I - Q)^{-1}\)
for every forward pass. Because this expression yields an exact orthogonal matrix regardless of how large \( Q )\), COFT respects both constraints simultaneously:

1. **Orthogonality** – guaranteed analytically by Cayley construction.
2. **Radius bound** – enforced approximately via projected updates on \( Q.\)

Empirically (see Figure 9 in the original paper), this two‑step scheme yields stable training even when ε is moderately large (e.g., ε≈0.3). The residual error introduced by neglecting higher orders in Eq. (3) becomes negligible after projection because any overshoot would push ‖𝑄‖ beyond ε′ and be immediately rescaled.

## Effect of \( ε\) on Flexibility vs. Stability  

To illustrate how varying \(ε)\ influences model behavior, consider three representative values:

| ε | Expected Rotation Angle (≈ ‖θ‖₂ ) | Observed Behaviour |
|---|----------------------------------|--------------------|
| **0.05** | < 5° | Model hardly departs from pretrained distribution; subject identity preserved but control signal often ignored; training loss may stagnate early. |
| **0.15** | ≈ 15° | Balanced regime: model learns to inject new control cues while maintaining high fidelity to pretrained semantics; convergence is fast and stable. |
| **0.35** | ≈ 35° | Near‑unconstrained OFT; higher adaptability but occasional spikes in loss; risk of mode collapse if learning rate or batch size are not tuned carefully. |

A conceptual plot (analogous to Figure 10 in the paper) would show three curves of validation CLIP‑text similarity versus epoch:

- The **low‑ε curve** rises quickly but saturates at a suboptimal value.
- The **mid‑ε curve** rises similarly fast but reaches a higher plateau.
- The **high‑ε curve** may initially lag due to larger updates but eventually surpasses others if training proceeds long enough—at cost of larger variance.

These observations underscore that **ε works as a knob for regularization strength**: smaller values provide stronger regularization (more similar to classic low‑learning‑rate fine‑tuning), while larger values grant greater representational freedom akin to OFT.

## Practical Guidelines for Choosing \( ε)\  

### 1️⃣ Identify Task Demands  

- **Subject‑Driven Generation (DreamBooth style)** – preserving fine subject details is paramount; modest angular changes are sufficient because text prompts already supply most semantic directionality. Recommended range:  
  $$ ε ∈ [0.10,\;0.20] $$  
  In experiments this range yielded stable convergence within 400–800 iterations and avoided identity loss observed in unconstrained OFT.

- **Controllable Generation (ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter style)** – additional modalities (edges, segmentation maps, landmarks) require more drastic reorientation of feature space so that new conditioning can be absorbed effectively. A slightly larger value works better:  
  $$ ε ∈ [0.15,\;0.30] $$  
  For extremely fine-grained control such as facial landmark manipulation, practitioners have reported optimal results near ε≈0.25.

### 2️⃣ Consider Data Regime  

- **Few‑shot settings (< 10 images)** – over‑parameterization can cause overfitting if transformations are too large; keep ε toward the lower end of its recommended interval.
- **Large datasets (> few thousand samples)** – model can safely explore bigger rotations; raising ε improves final generation quality without risking collapse.

### 3️⃣ Relate ε to Learning Rate  

Because both learning rate η and radius bound govern step size on the manifold, they should be balanced:

- With a *high* η (e.g., η=1e-3), choose *smaller* ε to prevent overshooting.
- With *low* η (e.g., η=5e-5), you may enlarge ε up to ~0.35 without destabilizing training.

A practical rule-of-thumb:
```text
if η ≥ 5×10⁻⁴ → set ε ≤ 0.15
else                → allow ε up to 0.30
```

### 4️⃣ Monitor Diagnostic Signals  

During training watch two quantities every epoch:

1. **Hyperspherical Energy Difference**:  
   $$ ΔHE = |\text{HE}(W)-\text{HE}(W^0)| $$
   Should stay around zero by construction; sudden spikes indicate projection failure or numerical issues inside Cayley inversion.

2. **Radius Violation Ratio**:  
   $$ r_{\text{viol}} = 
      {\frac{\max_{l}\bigl(\lVert R_l - I\rVert_F\bigr)}{\varepsilon}} $$
   where index *l* runs over all finetuned layers.
   If `r_viol` consistently hits 1 across many layers, you might have chosen an overly restrictive ε; consider increasing it modestly.

When both metrics remain stable and `r_viol` stays comfortably below 1 (e.g., < 0.8), training is proceeding within intended bounds.

## Summary  

Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning augments OFT with an explicit radius constraint
\(‖R-I‖ ≤ ε.\)
By recasting this bound in terms of the skew‑symmetric Cayley parameter \(Q,\)
the constraint becomes a simple Frobenius‐norm ball projection that integrates cleanly into stochastic gradient descent pipelines.
The hyperparameter \(ε)\ governs a smooth trade‑off between preserving pretrained semantic structure and granting enough flexibility for new downstream control signals:

- Small \(ε)\ → strong regularization → high fidelity but limited adaptation.
- Moderate \(ε)\ → balanced performance → fast convergence and stable control.
- Large \(ε)\ → near‑unconstrained OFT → maximal adaptability at risk of instability.

Practical recommendations suggest using ε between 0.10–0.20 for subject preservation tasks and between 0.15–0.30 for controllable generation tasks, adjusting further according to data size and learning rate.

With these tools in place, researchers and engineers can confidently finetune massive text‑to‑image diffusion models while keeping them anchored to their powerful pretrained knowledge—a crucial step toward reliable, controllable generative AI systems.
