Chapter 4: # Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices  

In modern deep‑learning systems, orthogonal transformations have emerged as a powerful tool for finetuning large pretrained models while preserving important geometric properties of the weight space.  An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ satisfies  
\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I},
\]  
so that it represents a rotation (determinant $+1$) or a reflection (determinant $-1$) of the underlying vector space.  Applying an orthogonal matrix to the weight matrix of a layer leaves the pair‑wise angular relationships among its neurons unchanged, and consequently the **hyperspherical energy**—the sum of inverse pairwise distances on the unit hypersphere—remains invariant.  This invariance is the key ingredient behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) for text‑to‑image diffusion models.

While the theory is clean, naively storing an arbitrary orthogonal matrix incurs a quadratic parameter cost $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$, which quickly becomes prohibitive for the high‑dimensional linear layers that dominate diffusion backbones.  In this chapter we develop practical parameterizations that dramatically reduce that cost while retaining full orthogonality.  We introduce three ideas in increasing order of efficiency: the **Cayley transform**, a **block‑diagonal decomposition**, and finally **parameter sharing** across blocks.  We also discuss how these schemes compare to low‑rank updates such as LoRA, and present practical tips for stable implementation.

---

## 1. Cayley Transform: From Skew‑Symmetric Matrices to Orthogonal Matrices  

A classic way to generate an orthogonal matrix without ever performing an explicit QR or SVD decomposition is the **Cayley transform**.  Let $\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ be a *skew‑symmetric* matrix, i.e. $\mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q}$.  The Cayley transform maps $\mathbf{Q}$ to an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ via  
\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\;}
\tag{1}
\]  
provided that $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ is invertible.  Equation (1) guarantees $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}$ because  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
&= \big[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top}\big] (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\\
&= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}^{\top})(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\\
&= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-\top}(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}= \mathbf I.
\end{aligned}
\]  

Because $\mathbf Q$ is skew‑symmetric, its degrees of freedom are exactly $d(d-1)/2$, matching the dimension of the orthogonal group $\mathrm O(d)$.  Hence by learning the independent entries of $\mathbf Q$, we can explore the entire set of orthogonal matrices *except* those with determinant $-1$ (the Cayley transform maps only to the special orthogonal group $\mathrm {SO}(d)$).  In practice this limitation does not harm performance: any reflection can be expressed as a rotation followed by a sign flip on a single coordinate, which can be absorbed into subsequent linear layers.

### 1.1 Parameter Count and Learning Dynamics  

If we store all unique entries of $\mathbf Q$, we need $d(d-1)/2$ trainable parameters.  For a moderately sized layer with $d=1024$, this amounts to roughly $5\times10^{5}$ parameters, already far fewer than a full $d\times d$ matrix ($>10^{6}$).  Moreover, gradients with respect to $\mathbf Q$ are obtained by differentiating through (1); modern automatic‑differentiation frameworks handle the matrix inverse efficiently using back‑propagation rules for linear solves.

### 1.2 Numerical Stability  

The inversion in (1) can become unstable when eigenvalues of $\mathbf Q$ approach $+1$, because $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)$ becomes near singular.  A common safeguard is to **clip** the spectral norm of $\mathbf Q$ during training:

```python
# PyTorch-style pseudocode
max_norm = 0.9
norm = torch.linalg.norm(Q, ord=2)
if norm > max_norm:
    Q = Q * (max_norm / norm)
```

Alternatively one can add a tiny damping term $\delta \,\mathbf I$ inside the inverse:
$\;(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q + \delta \,\mathbf I)^{-1}$, with $\delta \sim 10^{-5}$.

## 2. Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Reducing $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$  

Even $d(d-1)/2$ parameters can be excessive when $d$ reaches several thousands, as is typical for transformer feed‑forward or attention projection matrices in diffusion models.  A natural way to cut down the parameter budget is to restrict $\mathbf R$ to a **block‑diagonal** form:

\[
\boxed{\;
\mathbf R = \operatorname {diag}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\!\!\!\!\!\! \;\;\; \!\!\! \underbrace{\;\;}_{
r~{\text {blocks}}}\big)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
   \!\!\!\! \displaystyle \underbrace{\;\;\; \color {gray}{ \!\! R_{1}}}_{d/r\times d/r}
   &       &        \\
        & \ddots &        \\
        &       &   \displaystyle \underbrace{\;\;\; \color {gray}{ R_{r}}}_{d/r\times d/r}
   \\[4pt]
   &       &
)
}
$
where each block $\displaystyle R_{i}\in {\rm O}\bigl(\tfrac d r\bigr)$ is itself an orthogonal matrix.  

### 2.1 Parameter Savings  

A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters.  When we split it into $r$ equal blocks, each block has size $d/r$, so its parameter count is $(d/r)(d/r-1)/2$. Summing over all blocks yields

\[
N_{\rm blk}= r\, \frac {d}{r}\Bigl(\frac d r -1 \Bigr)\! /2
= \frac {d}{2}\Bigl(\frac d r -1 \Bigr)
= \mathcal O \!\left(\frac {d^{2}} {r}\right).
\]

Thus increasing $r$ linearly reduces the quadratic term: if $r=8$, we retain only $12.5\,\%$ of the original parameters.

### 2.2 Expressive Trade‑off  

Restricting the transformation to block‑diagonal form means that neurons belonging to different blocks never interact via the orthogonal rotation.  Consequently, each block can only re‑orient its own subset of dimensions independently.  Empirically this restriction still provides enough flexibility for finetuning tasks such as subject‑driven generation or control signal injection, while substantially alleviating memory pressure.

### 2.3 Construction via Independent Cayley Transforms  

Each block $R_i$ can be generated independently using its own skew‑symmetric matrix $Q_i$, i.e.

\[
R_i = (\boldsymbol I_{d/r}+ Q_i)(\,\boldsymbol I_{d/r} - Q_i)^{-1}.
\]

All blocks share the same code path; during back‑propagation gradients only flow through their respective $Q_i$, keeping implementation clean and parallelizable.

## 3. Parameter Sharing Across Blocks: From $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$ to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$

When storage is at an absolute premium—e.g., when deploying on edge devices or when finetuning millions of layers simultaneously—we can push efficiency further by *sharing* parameters across blocks.  

### 3.1 Shared Block Matrix  

Instead of learning distinct $Q_i$ for each block, we enforce  
\[
Q_1 = Q_2 = \dots = Q_r =: Q_{\text shared},
\]
which yields identical orthogonal submatrices

\[
R_1 = R_2 = \dots = R_r =: R_{\text shared}.
\]

Now only one skew‑symmetric matrix needs to be stored, costing $(d/r)(d/r-1)/2$ parameters overall:

\[
N_{\rm shared}= \frac {d}{r}\Bigl(\frac d r -1 \Bigr)\! /2
= \mathcal O \!\left(\frac {d^{2}} {r^{2}}\right).
\]

For example, with $d=1024$ and $r=16$, we go from roughly $32\,000$ parameters (independent blocks) down to about $500$ parameters—an order‐of‐magnitude reduction.

### 3   .  3   Practical Implications  

Sharing makes every block rotate in exactly the same way; effectively we are applying a *structured* orthogonal transformation that respects a pre‑defined partitioning of dimensions (e.g., channel groups in convolutional layers).  This structure can be interpreted as a depthwise rotation applied independently across groups—a useful perspective when adapting convolutional kernels where each group corresponds to one input channel.

Although sharing reduces expressivity compared with independent blocks, experiments reported in the OFT literature show that even heavily shared configurations retain enough capacity to achieve state‑of‑the‑art controllability on diffusion models while dramatically cutting down memory usage.

## 4. Comparison with LoRA and Full‑Matrix Updates  

| Approach | Trainable Parameters | Structural Constraint | Typical Use Case |
|----------|---------------------|-----------------------|------------------|
| **Full weight update** ($W=W^0+\Delta W$) | $dn$ (for layer $W^0\in{\mathbb R}^{d\times n}$) | None (maximal flexibility) | Fine‐grained editing but high risk of collapse |
| **LoRA** ($W=W^0 + A B^\top,\; A,B$ low rank) | $r'(d+n)$ where rank $r'\ll \min(d,n)$ | Low‑rank additive change | Memory efficient yet still Euclidean‑based |
| **OFT (full Cayley)** | $d(d-1)/2$ | Orthogonal multiplicative change → preserves hyperspherical energy | Semantic preserving finetuning |
| **OFT – Block diagonal** | $\displaystyle d\,(\tfrac d r -1)/2 = {\cal O}(d^2/r)$ | Orthogonal per block → intra‐block angle preservation | Tradeoff between capacity and parameter budget |
| **OFT – Shared blocks** | ${\cal O}(d^2/r^2)$ | One common orthogonal transformation applied across groups | Extreme compression; still suitable for control tasks |

The key distinction lies in **what geometric property is preserved**: LoRA constrains updates to lie in a low-dimensional subspace but does not guarantee any invariance on angular relationships among neurons; OFT explicitly enforces orthogonality, which mathematically guarantees that pairwise cosine similarities—and thus hyperspherical energy—are unchanged.

## 5. Implementation Details and Numerical Considerations  

### 5.1 Initialization at the Identity  

Finetuning should start from the pretrained weights untouched. To achieve this we initialize every skew‑symmetric matrix as zero:

```python
# shape (block_dim, block_dim)
Q = torch.zeros(block_dim, block_dim)
# enforce skew symmetry explicitly
Q = Q - Q.T   # still zero but maintains constraint during updates
```

Because $(\boldsymbol I+0)(\boldsymbol I-0)^{-1}= \boldsymbol I$, each block produces an identity orthogonal matrix at step 0, ensuring that the forward pass initially replicates exactly the pretrained model.

### 5.2 Enforcing Skew Symmetry During Optimization  

Gradient updates will inevitably break strict skew symmetry if we treat `Q` as an unrestricted tensor. Two common remedies:

* **Projection after each optimizer step:**  
```python
with torch.no_grad():
    Q.copy_(0.5 * (Q - Q.T))
```
* **Parameterization via an unconstrained matrix `U`:**  
`Q = U - U.T`. The optimizer sees `U`, which has no constraints; `Q` is constructed on-the-fly before forming `R`. This approach yields cleaner autograd graphs.

### 5.3 Stable Inverse Computation  

The inverse $(\boldsymbol I-\boldsymbol Q)^{-1}$ can be obtained efficiently by solving a linear system rather than forming an explicit inverse:

```python
def cayley(Q):
    I = torch.eye(Q.shape[0], device=Q.device)
    num = I + Q
    den = I - Q          # den will be used as coefficient matrix
    # solve den @ X = num for X
    R = torch.linalg.solve(den, num)
    return R
```

Solvers such as `torch.linalg.solve` employ LU factorization with partial pivoting and are numerically robust even for moderately ill-conditioned matrices—as long as we keep ‖Q‖ below 1 (see Section 1.2).

### 5.4 Gradient Scaling  

Because $(I-\!Q)^{-1}$ amplifies gradients when `$I-\!Q`' is close to singular, it is advisable to scale gradients or use adaptive optimizers (AdamW). Additionally, clipping the Frobenius norm of `Q` after each update prevents it from drifting into regions where inversion becomes dangerous.

### 5.5 Integration Into Existing Diffusion Frameworks  

In diffusion pipelines such as Stable Diffusion, most trainable linear layers are either

* attention projection matrices (`W_q`, `W_k`, `W_v`, `W_o`) of shape `(hidden_dim, hidden_dim)`
* feed­forward MLP weights (`W_↑`, `W_↓`) also typically square.

For each target layer we replace `W` with `R·W⁰`. The code pattern is:

```python
class OFTLinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, W0, block=4, share=False):
        super().__init__()
        self.W0 = nn.Parameter(W0.clone().detach(), requires_grad=False) # frozen
        d = W0.shape[0]
        self.block_dim = d // block

        if share:
            self.U = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(self.block_dim,
                                              self.block_dim))
        else:
            self.U = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(block,
                                              self.block_dim,
                                              self.block_dim))

    def forward(self, x):
        # build orthogonal R from U
        if self.U.dim() == 3:                              # independent blocks
            Rs = [cayley(self.U[i] - self.U[i].T) 
                  for i in range(self.U.shape[0])]
        else:                                               # shared block
            Q_shared = self.U - self.U.T
            R_shared = cayley(Q_shared)
            Rs = [R_shared] * (self.W0.shape[0] // self.block_dim)

        R = torch.block_diag(*Rs)                          # assemble full R
        W = torch.matmul(R.t(), self.W0.t()).t()           # equivalent weight
        return F.linear(x, W)
```

All hyperparameters (`block`, `share`, learning rate for `U`) are tunable; typical settings used in OFT experiments are `block = 8` and `share=False`.

## Summary  

We have presented three complementary strategies for representing large orthogonal matrices efficiently:

1. **Cayley Transform** – a differentiable bijection from skew–symmetric matrices to special orthogonal matrices.
2. **Block‑Diagonal Decomposition** – partitions the full rotation into independent sub‑rotations, cutting parameter complexity from $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r)$.
3. **Parameter Sharing Across Blocks** – forces all sub‑rotations to be identical, further shrinking complexity to $\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.

These techniques enable Orthogonal Finetuning to be applied at scale on high‑dimensional layers found in state‑of‑the‑art text‑to‑image diffusion models while preserving hyperspherical energy and thus maintaining generative semantics. Compared with low‑rank additive schemes such as LoRA, OFT’s multiplicative orthogonal updates trade off some flexibility for strong geometric invariance and empirical stability—qualities essential for downstream tasks like subject driven generation and controllable image synthesis.

Practical tips—including identity initialization, skew–symmetry enforcement, spectral clipping and solving linear systems rather than forming explicit inverses—ensure numerically stable training even on GPUs with limited precision. Armed with these tools, researchers and engineers can incorporate principled orthogonal transformations into any large neural architecture without incurring prohibitive memory costs or inference overheads.
