\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# From Preferences to Policies: How DPO Turns Human Choices Into Model Behavior  

*Why a seemingly modest change of variables lets us skip reinforcement learning altogether.*

---

## 1. The problem we’re trying to solve  

Large language models (LLMs) are trained on massive corpora of text.  
During pre‑training they pick up all sorts of knowledge **and** a mixture of behaviours—some useful, some not.  
When we want the model to *behave* in a particular way (be helpful, safe, witty, or generate only positive reviews) we need a way to **steer** it.

The dominant recipe today is **RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback).  
In short:

1. **Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT)** – give the model a few high‑quality examples.  
2. **Reward modelling** – collect pairs of completions \((y_w, y_l)\) for the same prompt \(x\); ask humans which one they prefer. Fit a neural reward function \(r_\phi(x,y)\) that predicts those preferences.  
3. **RL optimisation** – treat \(r_\phi\) as a reward and run a policy‑gradient algorithm (usually PPO) to maximise it while staying close to the SFT model via a KL‑penalty.

While RLHF works, it is **heavy**: you need a separate reward network, you have to sample from the model during training, and PPO hyper‑parameters are notoriously finicky.

Enter **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** – a method that uses exactly the same preference data but *directly* updates the language model itself with a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss. No extra reward head, no RL loop, no sampling‑while‑training.

---

## 2. A quick refresher: the Bradley‑Terry model  

Human preferences are often recorded as binary comparisons:

\[
y_w \succ y_l \mid x \quad\text{( “\(y_w\) is better than \(y_l\) for prompt \(x\)” )}
\]

The classic statistical model for such data is the **Bradley‑Terry (BT) model**:

\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=
\frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)}
     {\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^*(x,y_2)\bigr)} .
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(r^*(x,y)\) is an *unknown* latent reward that assigns higher scores to better completions.  
If we knew \(r^*\), training would be trivial: just increase the probability of the higher‑scoring answer.

In RLHF we *approximate* \(r^*\) with a neural network \(r_\phi\), train it with the logistic loss derived from (1), and then plug it into an RL objective.

---

## 3. The RLHF objective in equations  

The KL‑constrained optimisation that PPO tries to solve is

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}
\;\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \pi_\theta}\!\bigl[ r_\phi(x,y) \bigr]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\,|x)\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

- \(\pi_\theta\) is the policy we are fine‑tuning (the LM we want).  
- \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is typically the SFT model, acting as a “reference” distribution we don’t want to stray too far from.  
- \(\beta>0\) controls how conservative we stay.

Equation (2) isn’t differentiable w.r.t. token probabilities because of the expectation over discrete generations, which is why we resort to REINFORCE/PPO.

---

## 4. A hidden closed‑form solution  

If we treat \(r(x,y)\) as any reward function (not necessarily learned), (2) has an analytical optimum:

\[
\boxed{
\pi_r(y|x)=
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)}
     {Z(x)}
},
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)\,\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y')\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

(You can find the derivation in many control‑as‑inference papers.)  
Equation (3) says: *the optimal policy is just the reference policy re‑weighted by an exponentiated reward*.  

Now take logs on both sides and rearrange:

\[
r(x,y)=
\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\tag{4}
\]

The pesky partition function \(Z(x)\) depends only on the prompt, not on any particular completion; it will cancel out when we consider **differences** of rewards between two candidates.

---

## 5. From rewards back to preferences – the DPO trick  

Plug (4) into the BT preference probability (1):

\[
p(y_w \succ y_l | x)
=
\sigma
  \Bigl(
    \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
   -\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
  \Bigr),
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\).

Crucially, **the unknown partition term vanished**. The probability that a human prefers one completion now depends *only* on ratios of policy probabilities and reference probabilities.  

Thus, if we treat our *policy* \(\pi_\theta\) as the variable we want to learn, we can directly maximise the likelihood of observed preferences:

```python
# Pseudocode for one minibatch
logit = beta * (
    torch.log(pi_theta.logprob(y_w, x)) - torch.log(pi_ref.logprob(y_w, x))
  - torch.log(pi_theta.logprob(y_l, x)) + torch.log(pi_ref.logprob(y_l, x))
)
loss = -torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logit, torch.ones_like(logit))
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
```

That’s it – a **binary cross‑entropy loss** applied to the language model’s own logits! No separate reward head, no Monte‑Carlo rollouts.

We call this loss **DPO’s objective**:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
   \Bigl[
   \log\sigma
   \Bigl(
   \beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
 - \beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
   \Bigr)
   \Bigr].
}
\tag{6}
\]

When you expand gradients of (6), you see exactly what intuition suggests: increase the log‑probability of “good” completions and decrease that of “bad” ones, weighted by how badly the current model ranks them. The weighting term,
\( \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) \),
acts like an *automatic curriculum*: if the model already prefers \(y_w\), this term is tiny and the pair contributes little; if it gets them backwards, the gradient spikes.

---

## 6. Visual analogy – “pulling on a rope”  

Imagine each prompt \(x\) as an anchoring post with two strings attached: one leads to \(y_w\), another to \(y_l.\)  
The reference model gives each string a baseline tension (its log‑probability).  
DPO measures how much extra pull our current model applies relative to that baseline and nudges it until the preferred string is tauter than the dispreferred one by an amount proportional to \(\beta.\)

Because every pair contributes only its **relative** tension, we never need to know all possible strings—just those two that humans have compared.

---

## 7. Why DPO works better than PPO (empirically)

| Aspect | PPO | DPO |
|--------|-----|-----|
| Reward modelling | Separate network → extra variance | Implicit inside policy |
| Sampling during training | Required for on‑policy gradients | Not needed |
| Hyper‑parameters | KL target, clipping range, learning rate schedule | Single scalar β (and optimizer settings) |
| Stability | Sensitive to value‑function bias & KL estimate | Closed‑form optimum → no variance from Monte Carlo |
| Compute | Often needs many GPU hours per epoch | Essentially just supervised fine‑tuning cost |

In benchmark experiments (sentiment control, Reddit TL;DR summarisation, single‑turn dialogue), DPO matched or surpassed PPO while using far fewer resources and virtually no hyper‑parameter sweep.

## 8. Code sketch – training loop in practice  

Below is a minimal PyTorch–style snippet that shows how you could plug DPO into an existing 🤗 Transformers trainer.

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")
ref   = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")   # copy before fine tuning
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("gpt2-large")

beta = 0.1                # tradeoff between reward strength & KL penalty
opt  = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-5)

def logprob(model, inputs):
    """Returns sum_i log p(token_i | prefix_i)."""
    logits = model(**inputs).logits
    # shift so that logits[i] predicts token[i]
    shift_logits = logits[:, :-1].contiguous()
    shift_labels = inputs["input_ids"][:, 1:].contiguous()
    log_probs   = torch.nn.functional.log_softmax(shift_logits, dim=-1)
    token_logp  = log_probs.gather(2, shift_labels.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)
    return token_logp.sum(dim=1)       # shape: [batch]

for batch in dataloader:               # each entry: x, y_w, y_l as raw texts
    # Tokenize everything once
    enc_x   = tokenizer(batch["prompt"],      return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
    enc_ww  = tokenizer(batch["chosen"],      return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
    enc_ll  = tokenizer(batch["rejected"],    return_tensors="pt", padding=True)

    # Concatenate prompt + completion for logprob calculation
    inp_w = {k: torch.cat([enc_x[k], enc_ww[k][:,:-1]], dim=1) for k in enc_x}
    inp_l = {k: torch.cat([enc_x[k], enc_ll[k][:,:-1]], dim=1) for k in enc_x}

    # Log probabilities under current policy and reference
    logp_w_cur = logprob(model, inp_w)
    logp_l_cur = logprob(model, inp_l)
    with torch.no_grad():
        logp_w_ref = logprob(ref , inp_w)
        logp_l_ref = logprob(ref , inp_l)

    # DPO logits → binary cross entropy
    dpo_logit = beta * ((logp_w_cur - logp_w_ref) - (logp_l_cur - logp_l_ref))
    loss = torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        dpo_logit,
        torch.ones_like(dpo_logit)          # label=1 i.e. w should beat l
    )

    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
```

A few practical notes:

- `ref` can be either the original SFT checkpoint or a *pseudo* reference obtained by maximising likelihood on all chosen completions when an SFT checkpoint isn’t available.
- The loss automatically respects the KL constraint because any deviation from `ref` appears in the numerator/denominator terms.
- You can still use mixed precision (`torch.cuda.amp`) or gradient accumulation; DPO behaves like ordinary supervised fine‐tuning.

---

## 9. When does DPO shine?  

- **Low‑resource setups:** If you have only a few hundred thousand preference pairs and limited GPU budget, DPO reaches good performance in a single epoch.
- **Rapid prototyping:** Because there’s no RL loop you can experiment with new prompts or datasets without worrying about PPO hyper‑parameters.
- **Stability critical applications:** The closed‑form objective removes variance coming from Monte Carlo rollouts or value function approximations—helpful for safety‑critical fine‑tuning where divergence would be unacceptable.

---

## 10. Open questions & future directions  

1️⃣ **Out-of-distribution generalisation** – early results show DPO behaves similarly to PPO on unseen domains (e.g., CNN/DailyMail after training on Reddit TL;DR), but systematic studies remain needed.  

2️⃣ **Self‑labelled data** – can we generate new preference pairs by prompting our own DPO policy (“self‐ranking”) and keep training? This would mirror how PPO leverages large amounts of unlabeled prompts through its value estimator.  

3️⃣ **Scaling** – experiments have proven DPO up to ~6 B parameters; extending to >100 B will test whether numerical stability or memory constraints appear in the log‐ratio terms.

4️⃣ **Beyond text** – any generative system where preferences can be expressed as pairwise comparisons (images, music, code snippets) could adopt DPO by simply swapping out `logprob` for an appropriate likelihood estimator.

---

## 11. Takeaway

The heart of RLHF was always about aligning two distributions: “what humans like” vs “what the model emits”.  
What DPO shows us is that ***the alignment can be expressed directly in terms of those same distributions***—no extra reward head required and no reinforcement learning loop needed.

By reparameterising rewards as log‐ratios between policy and reference probabilities,
the Bradley–Terry likelihood becomes a simple binary cross entropy over language-model logits.
The result is an algorithm that is:

- **Simple** – one line of loss code,
- **Stable** – no variance from sampling,
- **Efficient** – comparable compute to ordinary supervised fine‐tuning,
- **Effective** – matches or exceeds PPO on sentiment control, summarisation and dialogue tasks.

If you’ve been hesitant to dive into RLHF because of its engineering overhead,
give DPO a try: collect some preference pairs (or reuse existing datasets), load your favourite LM as both `model` and `reference`, set a β value you’re comfortable with, and start training with that cross–entropy loss block.
Your language model will start behaving exactly as your human annotators intended—without ever stepping foot in an RL gym.
