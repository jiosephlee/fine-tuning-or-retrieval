\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GRPO’s token‑level importance weight violate the principle of importance sampling?
Question:
In the GRPO objective (Eq. 2) the importance ratio is defined as \(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\). The paper claims this is a mis‑application of importance sampling because it is computed on a single token sample rather than an average over many samples. Could someone explain in detail how the standard importance sampling identity works for sequence‑level distributions and why applying it token‑wise leads to a biased or high‑variance estimator? A concrete example with a short sequence would be helpful.
Answer:
In GRPO the *importance ratio* that appears inside the PPO‑style surrogate loss is  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\,,
\]

i.e. the ratio of the *next‑token* probabilities of the current policy and the behaviour (old) policy.  
The paper claims that using this ratio **per token** is a misuse of importance sampling because the classical importance‑sampling identity works on whole **samples** drawn from a distribution, not on a single component of a factorised sample. Below we first recall the standard identity, then show why applying it token‑wise gives a biased or very high‑variance estimator, and finally illustrate the problem with a tiny example.

---

### 1.  Importance sampling for sequence‑level expectations  

Let  

\[
p_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\text{old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}),\qquad 
p_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})
\]

be the behaviour and target policies respectively, and let $f(y)$ be any scalar function defined on whole responses (e.g. the reward or an advantage that depends on the complete sequence).  
The expectation of $f$ under the target policy can be written as an importance‑sampling estimator over samples drawn from the old policy:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[f(y)]
&= \sum_{y} p_{\theta}(y|x) f(y)                                   \\
&= \sum_{y} p_{\text{old}}(y|x)\,
   \underbrace{\frac{p_{\theta}(y|x)}{p_{\text{old}}(y|x)}}_{\displaystyle 
   \text{sequence‑level ratio}} f(y)                                 \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{y\sim p_{\text{old}}(\cdot|x)}
   \Bigl[\, r(y)\; f(y) \Bigr],\qquad 
   r(y)=\frac{p_{\theta}(y|x)}{p_{\text{old}}(y|x)} .
\end{aligned}
\]

The crucial point is that the *weight* $r(y)$ is a **function of the whole sampled sequence** $y$. To obtain an unbiased estimate of the target expectation we need many independent draws $y^{(1)},\dots , y^{(N)}\sim p_{\text{old}}$ and then average

\[
\hat{\mu}=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N} r(y^{(n)})\, f(y^{(n)}).
\]

Both bias‑correction (the ratio) and variance reduction rely on averaging over many *full* samples.

---

### 2.  What happens if we replace $r(y)$ by a token‑wise factor?

Because the language model factorises its distribution into a product of conditional next‑token distributions, one may be tempted to rewrite

\[
r(y)=\frac{\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}
          {\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}
     = \prod_{t=1}^{T} w_{t}(\theta),
\qquad 
w_{t}(\theta)=
   \frac{p_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{p_{\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}.
\]

GRPO then inserts each factor $w_{t}(\theta)$ inside the surrogate loss **individually** and clips it token‑by‑token. From an importance‑sampling standpoint this is incorrect for two reasons.

#### (a) Missing averaging over the conditional distribution  

For a fixed prefix $x,y_{<t}$, the quantity  

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=
   \frac{p_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{p_{\text{old}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})}
\]

is a random variable whose expectation under the old next‑token distribution equals

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim p_{\text{old}}(\cdot|x,y_{<t})}[ w_t(\theta) ]
     = \sum_{v} p_{\text{old}}(v|x,y_{<t})
       \frac{p_{\theta}(v|x,y_{<t})}{p_{\text{old}}(v|x,y_{<t})}
     = 1 .
\]

Thus **only after averaging over many draws of $y_t$** does this factor become an unbiased correction term. In GRPO we have just one draw per position – the token that actually appeared in the sampled response – so we are using a single noisy realisation of a random variable whose mean would be 1. The estimator

\[
\hat g = w_t(\theta)\, f(y)
\]

is therefore unbiased only in expectation over *all possible* continuations at that step, not over the single continuation we actually observed.

#### (b) Accumulation of variance across time steps  

Because each token’s weight is independent (conditioned on its prefix), the variance of their product grows quickly with sequence length:

\[
\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\prod_{t=1}^{T} w_t\bigr]
   = \Bigl(\operatorname{Var}[w]\!+\! (\mathbb{E}[w])^2 \Bigr)^T - 1
   \approx (\sigma_w^2+1)^T-1,
\]

where $\sigma_w^2=\operatorname{Var}[w_t]$. Even modest per‑token variance ($\sigma_w^2 \approx 0.2$) leads to an exponential blow‑up when $T=50~–~200$. By clipping each token separately GRPO does not rescue this explosion; instead it discards many extreme tokens, which changes the *expected* weight away from 1 and introduces systematic bias.

In short, **the token‑wise weight does not satisfy the importance‑sampling identity** because it does not integrate (average) over the full conditional distribution, and its product yields a high‑variance estimator that is further distorted by per‑token clipping.

---

### 3.  A concrete toy example  

Consider a vocabulary consisting of only two tokens, “A” and “B”, and a query $x$.  
Suppose under the old policy

* $p_{\text{old}}(A|x)=0.9,\; p_{\text{old}}(B|x)=0.1.$

The new policy shifts probability mass slightly:

* $p_{\theta}(A|x)=0.8,\; p_{\theta}(B|x)=0.2.$

Take a single‑step “sequence’’ (so $T=1$).  
The true importance weight for any sample drawn from $p_\text{old}$ is

\[
r(y)=
   \frac{p_\theta(y)}{p_{\text{old}}(y)}=
   \begin{cases}
      0.8/0.9 \approx 0.89 & y=A\\[4pt]
      0.2/0.1 = 2           & y=B .
   \end{cases}
\]

If we draw many responses from $p_\text{old}$ and average $r(y)$, we obtain

\[
0.9\times0.89 + 0.1\times2 = 1,
\]

as required by importance sampling.

Now suppose we treat this as a *token* weight in GRPO: we get exactly the same numbers because there is only one token, so everything is fine for length = 1.

Add another step with identical conditional distributions (so total length = 2).  
The true sequence weight is now

\[
r(y_1 y_2)= w_1 w_2,\quad
w_k=
   \begin{cases}
      0.89 & \text{if token }k=A\\
      2    & \text{if token }k=B .
   \end{cases}
\]

The expectation over all four possible length‑2 sequences equals again 1, but consider **one specific sampled sequence**, say “AA”. Its per‑token weights are both 0.89, so GRPO multiplies them implicitly by using each token’s weight inside its loss; nevertheless each token’s contribution is weighted by 0.89 instead of by the *average* weight ≈ 1 that would appear after marginalising over all possibilities at that position.

If we now clip each token with $\varepsilon = 0.2$ (i.e., clamp to [0.8, 1.2]), both tokens become **clipped to 0.8** (the lower bound). The product weight used for “AA” becomes $0.8\times0.8 = 0.64,$ which underestimates the true likelihood ratio (≈ 0.79). For a different sampled sequence like “AB”, clipping yields $0.8\times1.2 = 0.96,$ again deviating from the true product $0.89\times2 = 1.78.$ Because clipping is performed *before* multiplying, GRPO’s estimator no longer has expectation 1; it is biased downward for sequences containing many low‑probability tokens and upward for those containing many high‑probability tokens, depending on where the clipping thresholds lie.

When sequences become longer (e.g., $T=50$), these distortions compound dramatically: most tokens will be pushed toward one side of the clipping interval, and their product will either vanish (if many are clipped downwards) or explode (if many are clipped upwards). This explains why GRPO exhibits **high variance** that grows with response length and why training can diverge even when clipping ranges appear generous.

---

### 4.  Why GSPO fixes the issue  

GSPO replaces per‑token ratios with

\[
s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta (y_i|x)}
                {\pi_{\text{old}} (y_i|x)} 
   \Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
 = \exp{\Bigl[\frac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr]} .
\]

Notice two important facts:

1. **Sequence-level weighting:** The exponentiation converts the product of token ratios into an *average* in log space; thus every token contributes equally to a single scalar weight for that whole response.
2. **Unbiased correction:** When we average $s_i^{\,|y_i|}=r(y_i)$ over many responses drawn from the old policy, we recover exactly 1, i.e.
   \[
   \mathbb{E}_{y_i\sim p_\text{old}}
       r(y_i)=
   \mathbb{E}_{y_i}[s_i^{\,|y_i|}] = 1 .
   \]
   Consequently clipping applied to $s_i(\theta)$ operates on a quantity whose expectation is already correctly normalised, avoiding systematic bias.

Because only one importance weight per response appears inside the surrogate loss, variance grows only linearly with sequence length (through the log‑averaging), not exponentially as in GRPO’s token-wise product.

---

### 5.  Takeaway  

The classical importance–sampling identity requires **averaging over many full samples**, not over components of a factorised sample taken only once each. In GRPO each token’s ratio is computed from a single draw of its next‑token distribution; this breaks unbiasedness and leads to exploding variance when long sequences are clipped token‑wise. GSPO restores proper importance sampling by moving the correction to **sequence level**, where an average over full responses can be taken and where clipping interacts with a well‑behaved scalar weight rather than with every individual token.

Hence GRPO’s token-level importance weight violates the principle of importance sampling, while GSPO’s sequence-level weight satisfies it and explains empirically why GSPO achieves far better stability and efficiency, especially for long responses and large MoE models as reported in the paper.

### How is the sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) derived and why is length normalization used?
Question:
GSPO defines \(s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right)\).
Why do the authors raise the likelihood ratio to the power \(1/|y_i|\)? How does this length normalization affect the scale of the ratio and the clipping range? Would it be possible to omit this step, and if so, what practical issues would arise?
Answer:
The importance ratio that appears in GSPO is  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
          =\exp\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                 \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
$$

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ is called *length‑normalisation* (or *geometric‑mean* normalisation). It is introduced for three intertwined reasons:

1. **Stabilising the numerical range of the ratio**  
   The raw sequence‑level importance weight
   $$
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
   =\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   $$
   is a product of many per‑token ratios. Even when each token‑ratio stays close to 1 (e.g. between 0.9 and 1.1), the product can explode or collapse exponentially with the length of the response. Consequently, long answers would have importance ratios that are orders of magnitude larger (or smaller) than short answers, and a single token whose probability changes dramatically would dominate the whole product.

   By raising the product to the power $1/|y_i|$ we take the **geometric mean** of the per‑token ratios instead of their product. The geometric mean lies between the minimum and maximum token‑ratio, so its magnitude stays roughly in the same interval as an individual token‑ratio, irrespective of how many tokens are in the sequence. In practice this means that $s_i(\theta)$ typically lives in a compact range such as $[0.5,2]$ rather than $[10^{-6},10^{6}]$.  

2. **Making clipping independent of sequence length**  
   GSPO uses PPO‑style clipping on the importance ratio:

   $$
   \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr).
   $$

   If we omitted the normalisation, the clipping thresholds would have to be chosen *per‑length*: short responses could be safely clipped with a small $\varepsilon$, while long responses would need a much larger $\varepsilon$ just to keep any samples inside the window. This would either over‑clip short responses (making optimisation too conservative) or under‑clip long ones (allowing highly off‑policy samples to influence gradients). Length normalisation puts all $s_i(\theta)$ on roughly the same scale, so a single pair of clipping constants works for every response length.

3. **Reducing variance of the estimator**  
   Importance sampling theory tells us that, when we re‑weight samples from an old distribution to estimate expectations under a new distribution, *the variance of the estimate increases with the variance of the importance weights*. Because products of many random variables have much higher variance than their individual factors, using an un‑normalised sequence weight would dramatically increase gradient variance—especially for long sequences where even tiny per‑token changes compound. The geometric mean dramatically damps this effect: it is equivalent to averaging log‑ratios and then exponentiating, which yields a much lower‑variance estimator.

---

### What happens if we drop the $1/|y_i|$ factor?

If we defined  

$$
\tilde s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
$$

the following practical issues would arise:

| Issue | Why it appears |
|------|----------------|
| **Exploding/vanishing ratios** | For a response of length 100, even modest per‑token odds (e.g. 0.95 → 1.05) can turn into $\tilde s_i\approx 10^{4}$ or $10^{-4}$. This makes most samples fall outside any reasonable clipping interval, so almost every update would be clipped to the boundary and carry almost no gradient information. |
| **Length‑dependent clipping** | To keep a reasonable fraction of samples unclipped we would need a clipping range that grows with length (e.g. $\varepsilon\propto |y_i|$). This defeats the purpose of PPO’s fixed “trust region” and forces us to tune a separate hyperparameter for each batch’s length distribution. |
| **High gradient variance** | The gradient term contains $\tilde s_i(\theta)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)$. When $\tilde s_i$ is huge, a single sample dominates the minibatch update and introduces large stochastic noise; when it is tiny, it effectively disappears, wasting data. Both behaviours impair learning stability and efficiency. |
| **Numerical instability** | Multiplying thousands of probabilities can underflow to zero in float16/float32, breaking back‑propagation or producing NaNs after exponentiation. Using logs (as in GSPO) avoids this but still leaves us with an exponentiation step; normalising before exponentiation keeps values well within representable ranges. |

Empirically, the authors observed that without normalisation “the likelihood changes of a few tokens can result in dramatic fluctuations of the sequence‑level importance ratio”, which directly leads to unstable training and even catastrophic collapse on long responses.

---

### Summary

*The exponent $1/|y_i|$ transforms the product of token‑wise likelihood ratios into their geometric mean.* This simple operation achieves three crucial goals:

1. **Keeps importance ratios in a bounded, length‑independent range**, so a single clipping window works for all responses.
2. **Reduces variance** of the importance weights, yielding more reliable gradient estimates.
3. **Prevents numerical overflow/underflow** that would otherwise arise from multiplying many probabilities.

Consequently, length normalisation is not an optional cosmetic tweak; it is essential for making sequence‑level importance sampling practical in RL fine‑tuning of large language models. Omitting it would force you either to accept wildly unstable training dynamics or to introduce cumbersome per‑length hyper‑parameter tuning—both undesirable in large‑scale pipelines.

### What exactly is the “group‑based advantage” \(\widehat{A}_i\) and how is it computed in practice?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO use \(\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}\).
I am confused about a few details: (1) Is the standard deviation computed across the whole group for each query separately? (2) What happens if all rewards in a group are identical (zero std)? (3) Are rewards ever normalized further (e.g., to keep them within [‑1, 1]) before being used as advantages?
Answer:
The quantity  

\[
\widehat A_i \;=\;
\frac{\,r(x,y_i)-\displaystyle\operatorname{mean}\bigl\{r(x,y_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^G\,}
     {\displaystyle\operatorname{std}\bigl\{r(x,y_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^G}
\]

is called the *group‑based advantage* in both GRPO and GSPO.  
It is simply a **z‑score** that measures how much the reward of a particular response \(y_i\) deviates from the average reward obtained by the other responses generated for the same query \(x\).  The “group’’ refers to the set of \(G\) responses \(\{y_1,\dots ,y_G\}\) sampled from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for that query.

### (1)  What is the scope of the standard‑deviation computation?

For every query \(x\) we collect a **single group** of \(G\) responses.  
The mean and standard deviation are computed **over that group only**:

* mean\(= \frac1G\sum_{j=1}^{G} r(x,y_j)\);
* std\(= \sqrt{\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^{G}\bigl(r(x,y_j)-\text{mean}\bigr)^2}\).

Thus each query has its own normalisation statistics, and they are *not* shared across queries or across different batches.  In other words, for each query we treat the \(G\) rewards as a small sample from which we estimate a location (mean) and a scale (standard deviation); these statistics are then used to turn the raw reward into an advantage that is comparable across queries of differing difficulty or reward range.

### (2)  What if every reward in a group is identical (zero variance)?

If all rewards are equal, the denominator \(\operatorname{std}(\cdot)\) becomes zero, which would make \(\widehat A_i\) undefined.  The paper does not describe an elaborate fallback; in practice one follows the usual trick employed when normalising by a sample standard deviation:

* **Add a tiny constant \(\varepsilon** (e.g., \(10^{-8}\)) to the denominator before division, or
* **Skip normalisation** for that group and set \(\widehat A_i = 0\) for every \(i\).

Both alternatives amount to treating the group as offering no relative preference among its members – every response receives the same advantage, so its contribution to the gradient vanishes.  Because groups are constructed from stochastic policy roll‑outs, exact ties are rare unless the reward function is extremely coarse (e.g., binary success/failure).  Consequently, the simple epsilon‑padding used in most implementations suffices and does not affect stability.

### (3)  Are rewards further normalised (e.g., clamped to \([-1,1]\)) before forming \(\widehat A_i\)?

No additional clipping or scaling is performed in the algorithm as described.  The only transformation applied to raw rewards is the group‑wise z‑score above.  The authors deliberately keep the reward range unaltered because:

1. The reward model already outputs values in \([0,1]\) (the paper states \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\)).
2. Z‑scoring already rescales them to have zero mean and unit variance *within each group*, which makes them comparable across groups regardless of absolute magnitude.
3. Further bounding would destroy this relative information; for instance, if two responses obtain rewards \(0.99\) and \(0.98\), clipping them both to \(1.0\) would erase their distinction.

Consequently, after computing \(\widehat A_i\) you feed it directly into the PPO‑style clipped objective (either GRPO or GSPO).  No extra “advantage‑clipping’’ step is mentioned in the paper.

---

#### Putting it together

When training with GRPO or GSPO you first sample a batch of queries, then for each query you generate a **group** of \(G\) completions using the old policy.  
For each group you:

1. Collect the scalar rewards \(r(x,y_1),…,r(x,y_G)\).
2. Compute their mean and standard deviation.
3. Convert each reward into a z‑score \(\widehat A_i\), handling a possible zero‑std case by adding an infinitesimal constant.
4. Use this advantage together with either token‑level importance weights (GRPO) or sequence‑level importance ratios (GSPO) inside the clipped surrogate loss.

The resulting \(\widehat A_i\) therefore captures *relative* quality within its own group – exactly what is needed for “relative’’ policy optimisation without relying on an external value model.

### Why does clipping a larger fraction of tokens in GSPO still lead to higher training efficiency than GRPO?
Question:
Figure 3 in the paper shows that GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO, yet its sample efficiency is better. Intuitively, discarding more data should hurt learning. Can someone clarify why sequence‑level clipping provides a cleaner learning signal despite using fewer token‑level gradients? Is this related to variance reduction of the importance weights or to alignment between reward granularity and optimization unit?
Answer:
In GSPO the **unit of the importance ratio and of the clipping operation is the whole response**, not each token.  
Because the reward that drives learning is defined for an entire sequence, weighting the gradient by a *sequence‑level* importance weight

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
        \tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr)
\]

captures **exactly the same distributional mismatch that the reward measures**: how far the old policy’s sampled response is from the current policy as a whole.  
When a response is far from the current policy, \(s_i(\theta)\) will lie outside the narrow clipping interval \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\) (the paper uses \(\varepsilon\approx3\times10^{-4}\)). Those responses are discarded entirely from the gradient estimate.

In GRPO, by contrast, every token receives its own importance weight

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

and clipping is applied **independently** at each position.  
Since each token is drawn from a different next‑token distribution, a single sample cannot reliably estimate the required importance ratio; the weight fluctuates wildly from token to token. The gradient therefore becomes a sum of many *noisy* terms, and clipping only damps but does not eliminate this noise. When responses are long, the variance accumulates (the paper notes this “high‑variance training noise” that eventually leads to collapse).

Because GSPO discards an entire response whenever its sequence‑level ratio is off‑policy, it **throws away far more tokens** (the paper’s Figure 4 shows two orders of magnitude more clipped tokens). This looks wasteful only if one thinks of tokens as independent training examples. In reality those clipped tokens belong to responses whose overall likelihood under the new policy is already mismatched with the old one; keeping any of their token‑gradients would only reinforce an inaccurate off‑policy direction. By removing them wholesale, GSPO eliminates a large source of variance.

Two theoretical facts explain why this yields higher *sample efficiency*:

1. **Variance reduction of importance weights** – The variance of an importance‑sampling estimator depends on the spread of the weight distribution. A single sequence‑level weight has much lower variance than a product (or average) of many token‑level weights because the logarithms are averaged across tokens (the length‑normalisation in \(s_i(\theta)\)). Consequently, after clipping, the remaining unbiased term has a tighter distribution and provides a cleaner learning signal.

2. **Alignment between reward granularity and optimization unit** – The advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) is computed from the *whole‑response* reward and is shared by all tokens in that response. When we weight each token by a different \(w_{i,t}\), we are mismatching a scalar signal with a vector of heterogeneous coefficients, which injects bias and noise. Using a single scalar weight \(s_i(\theta)\) preserves this alignment: every token in a given response contributes equally to increase or decrease the total log‑likelihood according to the same advantage value.

The gradient analysis in §4.2 makes this precise. In GRPO we have

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
 = \mathbb E\!\bigl[\,\widehat A_i
   \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t}\! w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
   \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\bigr],
\]

where each term is scaled by a different noisy factor \(w_{i,t}\).  
GSPO’s gradient reads

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
 = \mathbb E\!\bigl[\,s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
   \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t}\! \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\bigr],
\]

i.e., all tokens are multiplied by exactly the same scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). The only remaining stochasticity comes from sampling whole responses, not from per‑token importance ratios.

Therefore, even though GSPO discards many more *tokens*, it discards whole *responses* that would otherwise add high‑variance noise. The gradients that survive are **far less noisy**, which translates into faster and more stable learning—exactly what Figure 3 shows. The phenomenon is not paradoxical once we recognise that sample efficiency is governed by gradient *quality* (variance) rather than raw token count.

In short, sequence‑level clipping gives a cleaner signal because:

* it respects the natural granularity of the reward,
* it yields low‑variance importance weights,
* it removes entire off‑policy responses instead of polluting gradients with noisy token‑wise weights.

These factors together explain why GSPO can clip two orders of magnitude more tokens yet still achieve superior training efficiency compared with GRPO.

### How does expert‑activation volatility in MoE models break GRPO’s token‑level importance ratios?
Question:
The authors report that after a gradient update, about 10 % of the experts activated for a given token change, causing large fluctuations in \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\. Why does a change in expert routing affect the token probability ratio so dramatically? Is this because different experts have vastly different output distributions, or because routing decisions are not differentiable? How does GSPO avoid this problem by using only sequence likelihoods?
Answer:
In GRPO the importance weight that appears in the loss is

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta (y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} (y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

i.e. the ratio of the *token‑level* probabilities under the new and the old policy.  
When we train a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language model, each token is processed by a small subset of experts that are chosen by the routing network. After one gradient step the parameters of the routing network change, so for many tokens a different set of experts becomes active. In the 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B experiment the authors observed that about *10 %* of the experts that were active for a given token under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) are replaced by other experts under \(\pi_\theta\).

Why does this modest change in routing blow up the token‑level ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\)?

1. **The probability of a token is largely determined by the activated experts.**  
   In an MoE layer each expert contributes a separate feed‑forward transformation; the final hidden state (and thus the logit distribution over the next token) is a weighted sum of the active experts’ outputs. If a token switches from expert set \(E\) to a very different set \(E'\), its hidden representation can change dramatically, and consequently \(\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) can differ by orders of magnitude from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). Because \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is exactly this ratio, even a 10 % routing mismatch may produce values far outside \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\).

2. **Routing decisions are not differentiable and are recomputed each forward pass.**  
   The routing function selects experts via a hard top‑\(k\) argmax on a scoring network, which does not share gradients across different routing choices. When the policy parameters change, the routing decision for a token can flip abruptly; there is no smooth interpolation that would keep the two distributions close. Hence the importance weight, which assumes an *infinitesimal* change in distribution, no longer satisfies its theoretical premise.

Because GRPO multiplies every token’s log‑likelihood gradient by its own \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), these occasional huge ratios inject high‑variance noise into the gradient estimate. The clipping mechanism only caps *the magnitude* of each ratio but cannot prevent large fluctuations in their *distribution*: many tokens get clipped on one side while a few retain very large (or very small) weights, which accumulates over long sequences and eventually drives the policy to collapse.

### How GSPO avoids this problem

GSPO replaces token‑level ratios with a *sequence‑level* importance weight

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
           =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
               \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
\]

Two observations are crucial:

* **Averaging over tokens smoothes out routing volatility.**  
  Even if some individual \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) are extreme because their routing changed, they are summed inside a logarithm and then divided by sequence length. The resulting exponentiated average is much more stable; for a typical 100‑token response a 10 % routing change alters only about ten terms in the sum, which is diluted by the remaining ninety.

* **The objective no longer depends on *which* experts produced each token.**  
  The sequence likelihood \(\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)=\prod_t \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) is computed after the forward pass of the *entire* MoE network. Whether expert A was used at position t or expert B at position t+1 does not matter as long as the final product remains similar. Because training optimizes the full likelihood, GSPO tolerates the inevitable re‑routing that occurs after each update.

Consequently GSPO’s gradient (Eq. (9) in the paper) weights *all* token log‑likelihood gradients equally by \(s_i(\theta)/|y_i|\). The weighting factor no longer fluctuates token‑by‑token; it is identical for every token in a given response. This eliminates the high‑variance term that plagued GRPO and removes any dependence on preserving exact expert activation patterns across updates.

In short, expert‑activation volatility breaks GRPO because GRPO’s token‐level importance ratios assume a smooth, almost unchanged conditional distribution—a condition violated when hard routing switches abruptly. GSPO sidesteps this issue by moving to sequence‑level importance sampling, where averaging over tokens neutralises routing changes and yields stable importance weights that align with the sequence‐level reward signal. This is why GSPO can train MoE models without additional tricks such as Routing Replay, while GRPO cannot.

### What is the ‘Routing Replay’ strategy and why does it add memory/communication overhead?
Question:
In Section 5.4 the paper mentions caching the routing decisions of \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\) and replaying them when computing \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\. I would like to understand exactly how this works: (a) what information needs to be stored per token (expert IDs, gating scores?), (b) how is it re‑used during the forward pass of \(\pi_θ\), and (c) why does this increase memory usage compared to standard forward passes? Also, does Routing Replay interfere with learning new routing patterns?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the GRPO implementation the importance ratio of a token is  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,\;y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,\;y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
$$

When the policy is a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model each token is processed by a *routing* step that selects a small set of experts (e.g. 2 out of 64) and forwards the hidden state through those expert feed‑forward networks. The routing decision depends on the current parameters $\theta$. Consequently, after a gradient update the set of activated experts for a given token can change, sometimes substantially (the paper reports ≈10 % of experts differ after one update). If the routing changes between the numerator $\pi_{\theta}$ and the denominator $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the two likelihoods are computed by *different* sub‑networks, making their ratio unstable and noisy. This volatility destroys the “off‑policy correction” that $w_{i,t}$ is supposed to provide and prevents GRPO from converging on MoE models.

The **Routing Replay** trick restores stability by *forcing both sides of the ratio to use exactly the same expert activation pattern* as the old policy. The procedure works as follows.

---

### (a) What is cached per token?

For every token $y_{i,t}$ that appears in a rollout generated by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ we store

1. **The IDs of the selected experts** (typically an integer vector of length equal to the number of routing slots, e.g. `[12, 37]` for a 2‑expert top‑k routing).  
2. **The gating scores** (the softmax probabilities that were used to weight those experts). In practice most implementations keep only the *raw* gating logits or the normalized weights; they are needed later only to reconstruct exactly the same forward path.

No other information (such as hidden states or gradients) is required because the forward pass can be recomputed from scratch once we know which experts to activate.

---

### (b) How are they re‑used during the forward pass of $\pi_{\theta}$?

When we later compute $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ we need $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$. Instead of letting $\pi_{\theta}$ perform its own routing, we *inject* the cached routing decisions:

1. **Deterministic routing** – before feeding the token through the model we replace the routing module’s usual inference (`top‑k(gating(x))`) with a “replay” mode that simply reads the stored expert IDs and gating scores and routes the hidden state through exactly those experts.  
2. **Forward computation** – with this forced expert set, we compute the logits for $y_{i,t}$ using the *current* parameters $\theta$. Because only the expert weights have changed (the routing pattern is fixed), the numerator of $w_{i,t}$ now reflects how much the *same sub‑network* has changed under $\theta$.  
3. **Denominator** – $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ can be obtained either from a cached logit vector computed at rollout time, or by re‑running a forward pass with $\theta_{\text{old}}$ but still using the cached routing (the latter is cheaper because it avoids another routing computation).

Thus each token’s importance ratio is evaluated on a *consistent* expert activation, eliminating abrupt changes caused by different experts being selected after an update.

---

### (c) Why does this increase memory/communication overhead?

The extra cost comes from two sources:

| Source | Reason |
|--------|--------|
| **Cache size** | For every token in every rollout we must store at least a few bytes per expert ID (e.g., 2 × int16 = 4 B) plus a few floats for gating weights (e.g., 2 × float16 = 4 B). A batch containing millions of tokens easily consumes several gigabytes of additional memory compared with a plain forward pass that only keeps activations for back‑propagation. |
| **Data movement** | In distributed training (tensor‑ or pipeline‑parallel MoE), routing decisions are normally computed locally on each device that owns its expert table. When we replay, every device that holds a token’s hidden state must also receive the list of expert IDs belonging to other devices so it can route correctly. This requires an extra all‑to‑all communication step per training iteration, roughly comparable to the communication needed for scattering/scattering activations in ordinary MoE but now *duplicated* because it occurs both during rollout generation and during replay. |
| **Extra forward pass** | Even though we skip an expensive top‑k selection, we still have to run a forward pass for every token using $\theta$. Since GRPO already needs this forward pass for gradient computation, Routing Replay does not add additional compute, but it prevents us from discarding intermediate activations after rollout generation – they must be retained until replay is performed, further raising memory pressure. |

All together these factors make Routing Replay noticeably heavier than an ordinary dense forward pass.

---

### Does Routing Replay hinder learning new routing patterns?

No – it does **not** block the model from eventually discovering better routes. The replay mechanism is only applied when computing the importance ratios *for that particular batch*. The actual gradient update still updates all parameters, including those governing future routing decisions (the gating network). After an update finishes, subsequent rollouts are generated with the *new* policy $\pi_{\theta}$; therefore fresh routing decisions will be sampled according to whatever pattern $\theta$ now produces. In other words:

1. **During replay** we “freeze” routing for *the current batch* so that off‑policy correction remains well defined.
2. **Between batches** normal training proceeds; next generation step uses unconstrained routing based on updated $\theta$.

Hence Routing Replay merely stabilises one training step; it does not prevent convergence towards new expert assignments.

---

### Summary

*Routing Replay* works by caching per‑token expert IDs and gating scores generated by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. When evaluating $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ it forces $\pi_{\theta}$ to use exactly those cached routes, guaranteeing that numerator and denominator are computed by identical subnetworks. This eliminates volatility in token‑level importance ratios caused by MoE expert‑activation changes. The price is higher memory consumption (to store IDs + gates for every token) and extra all‑to‑all communication in distributed settings because routing information must be shipped across devices. The technique does not stop the model from learning new routing patterns; it only stabilises each individual gradient step while allowing future steps to explore new routes.

In practice this overhead motivated the authors to adopt GSPO, whose sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ does not depend on per‑token likelihoods and therefore removes the need for Routing Replay altogether.

### Can GSPO be applied to multi‑turn RL without modification?
Question:
The paper introduces GSPO‑token as a token‑level variant for multi‑turn settings where per‑token advantages may be needed. If I have a dialogue where each turn receives its own reward, should I use GSPO‑token with separate \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) for each turn, or can I still apply plain GSPO by aggregating turn rewards into a single sequence reward? What are the trade‑offs in terms of stability and credit assignment?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
If each turn of a dialogue receives its own reward (e.g. turn‑level feedback, turn‑wise user ratings, or intermediate scores), you should use **GSPO‑token** and assign a separate advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to each turn (or even to each token inside the turn).  Using plain **GSPO** is still possible – you can sum or average the turn rewards into a single scalar per‑response advantage – but you will lose fine‑grained credit assignment and may obtain a less stable learning signal when the rewards vary heavily across turns.

---

### Why the choice matters

#### 1.  What GSPO optimises

GSPO’s objective (Eq. (6) in the paper) treats the *entire* response $y_i$ as a single unit:

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\Big[\,\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\; \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta))\widehat A_i\big)\Big],
$$

where the advantage $\widehat A_i$ is computed from the **sequence‑level** reward
$r(x,y_i)$.  Hence every token in $y_i$ receives exactly the same weight
$s_i(\theta)$ (the length‑normalised sequence importance ratio).  The algorithm is deliberately designed to match the *unit of reward* (the whole sequence) with the *unit of importance sampling* (the whole sequence likelihood).  

If you collapse several turn‑level scores into a single number (e.g. sum, average, or discounted sum), GSPO will still be mathematically correct; the only difference is that the resulting $\widehat A_i$ no longer reflects where in the response the model did well or poorly.

#### 2.  What GSPO‑token adds

GSPO‑token introduces a token‑wise term $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ while **detaching** the sequence‑level factor $s_i(\theta)$ from its gradient:

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{sg}\!\big[s_i(\theta)\big]\;
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\operatorname{sg}\!\big[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\big]} .
$$

Because $\operatorname{sg}[\,]$ stops gradients, the numerical value of
$s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is still exactly $s_i(\theta)$; the only effect of the
formulation is that each token can now be paired with its own advantage
$\widehat A_{i,t}$.  In a multi‑turn dialogue you can set

$$
\widehat A_{i,t}
=
\begin{cases}
\text{advantage derived from turn 1 reward}, & t \in \text{turn 1},\\[4pt]
\text{advantage derived from turn 2 reward}, & t \in \text{turn 2},\\
\;\;\vdots &
\end{cases}
$$

so that tokens belonging to different turns receive different scaling factors.
When all $\widehat A_{i,t}$ are equal, GSPO‑token collapses back to plain GSPO,
as shown in Eq. (14–15).

#### 3.  Credit assignment

* **Plain GSPO**: credit (or blame) for a high‑reward response is spread uniformly over every token. If turn 2 was excellent but turn 1 was terrible, the uniform weighting may cause the model to reinforce some bad token predictions from turn 1 simply because they appear in an overall high‑scoring response.

* **GSPO‑token**: by giving each turn its own advantage, gradients arising from a good turn are amplified for its tokens while gradients from a bad turn are attenuated (or even reversed if a negative advantage is used). This aligns more closely with how humans think about multi‑turn interactions—reward at step k should primarily affect actions taken at step k.

#### 4.  Stability considerations

The paper’s stability analysis focuses on *variance of importance weights*. Token‑level importance weights in GRPO caused exploding variance because each token weight was treated independently. GSPO resolved this by using a **single sequence weight** $s_i(\theta)$, which varies much less across tokens and lengths.

GSPO‑token inherits this stability because it **reuses the same sequence weight for every token**, only differing in the attached advantages. The gradient expression (Eq. (15)) shows that every token’s gradient is multiplied by the *same* factor  
$s_i(\theta)= (\pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|}$,
so there is no extra variance introduced by per‑token importance ratios. The only source of extra variance comes from having multiple advantages $\widehat A_{i,t}$; however, these are usually low‑variance estimates because they are computed from observed scalar rewards (e.g., a turn score), not from a learned value network.

Consequently, **stability is essentially unchanged** between GSRO and GSRO‑token; you gain finer credit assignment without paying an additional variance penalty.

#### 5.  Practical trade‑offs

| Aspect | Plain GSPO (single advantage per response) | GSPO‑token (per‑turn/token advantage) |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Implementation** | Simpler: compute one $\widehat A_i$ per response | Slightly more bookkeeping: map each token (or turn) to its own $\widehat A_{i,t}$ |
| **Memory / Compute** | Same, because both use length‑normalised sequence ratio; extra advantage tensor is negligible | Same order; only the advantage tensor grows linearly with tokens |
| **Credit assignment** | Coarse – all tokens share reward signal | Fine – each turn can receive its own signal |
| **Stability** | Very stable (as shown empirically) | Equally stable; no extra variance from importance ratios |
| **When to prefer** | Rewards are naturally defined at whole‑response level (e.g., final task success, overall human rating) | Rewards are naturally defined per interaction step (e.g., turn‑wise ratings, delayed user feedback, multi‑objective scoring where different turns optimise different criteria) |

#### 6.  Recommended recipe for multi‑turn RL

1. **Collect per‑turn scores** $r_k(x,y^{(k)})$, where $k$ indexes turns.
2. **Aggregate if you truly want one scalar** (e.g., discounted sum) and feed it to plain GSPO; this works well when later turns dominate overall quality.
3. **Otherwise**, build a vector of advantages:
   $$
   \widehat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_{\text{turn}(t)}(x,y)-\mu_{\text{turn}(t)}}{\sigma_{\text{turn}(t)}},
   $$
   where $\mu_{\text{turn}(t)}$ and $\sigma_{\text{turn}(t)}$ are mean/std computed over the group for that particular turn.
4. Use **GSPO‑token** with those advantages; keep clipping ranges identical to those used for plain GSPO (the paper uses $3\times10^{-4}$/$4\times10^{-4}$), because clipping operates on the same sequence ratio.
5. Monitor *fraction of clipped responses* rather than clipped tokens—the paper shows that even with >90 % of tokens being clipped, GSPO still learns efficiently; you’ll see similar behaviour with GSPO‑token.

---

### Bottom line

- **Yes**, GSPO can be applied without modification by collapsing multi‑turn rewards into one scalar, but you will lose per‑turn credit assignment.
- **No**, you should not use plain GSPO *if* you care about distinguishing good versus bad turns; in that case switch to **GSRO‑token** and give each turn its own advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$.
- The stability guarantees proved for GSRO carry over to GSRO‑token because both share the same sequence importance ratio; thus the main trade‑off is between simplicity (plain GSRO) and finer granularity (GSRO‑token).

### Why does GSPO allow direct use of inference‑engine likelihoods despite precision mismatches?
Question:
The authors claim that because GSPO uses only sequence‑level likelihoods, it tolerates precision differences between training (e.g., fp16 on Megatron) and inference engines (e.g., fp32 on vLLM). How significant are these precision discrepancies for token‑wise versus sequence‑wise likelihoods? Is there any empirical evidence that using inference probabilities directly does not degrade performance, or is this just an intuition?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s claim that GSPO can “directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine” rests on two facts that follow from the way importance ratios are defined in GSPO:

1. **The importance ratio is a *sequence‑level* quantity**,  
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
   \]
   Because the ratio is *averaged* over all tokens (the exponent \(1/|y_i|\) is a length‑normalisation), a small perturbation of any single token probability has only a \(1/|y_i|\) effect on the final scalar. In contrast, GRPO’s token‑wise ratio
   \[
   w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   \]
  enters the gradient **without any averaging**; a single token that is computed with lower numerical precision can dominate the whole update.

2. **Sequence‑level likelihoods are far less sensitive to floating‑point noise** than token‑level likelihoods.  
   When the model is evaluated in fp16 (training engine) and the same response is evaluated in fp32 (inference engine), each token log‑probability can differ by a few ulps (units in the last place). Summing thousands of such differences and then dividing by the length yields a quantity that typically differs by less than \(10^{-4}\). This magnitude is comparable to, or smaller than, the clipping tolerances used in GSPO (the paper uses \(\epsilon_{\text{left}}=3\times10^{-4}\) and \(\epsilon_{\text{right}}=4\times10^{-4}\)). Consequently, the clipped importance ratio rarely changes its side of the clipping interval simply because of precision mismatch.

---

### Why precision matters much more for token‑wise ratios

The variance of an importance weight grows multiplicatively with the number of factors it contains. For a token‑wise weight we have

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{t}\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,k}\mid x,y_{i,<k})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,k}\mid x,y_{i,<k})},
\]

so any relative error on an early token is amplified exponentially for later tokens. Even a tiny systematic bias (e.g., fp16 under‑estimates probabilities by \(5\times10^{-5}\)) can make \(w_{i,T}(\theta)\) differ by an order of magnitude when \(T\) reaches several hundred tokens, which directly affects clipping decisions and gradient magnitudes.

GSPO’s design deliberately avoids this cascade: after taking the product over all tokens to obtain the full sequence likelihood, it immediately applies a *geometric mean* (the \(1/|y_i|\) power). The geometric mean turns a product into an average in log‑space, turning multiplicative errors into additive ones that shrink with length.

---

### Empirical evidence presented in the paper

The paper does **not** include a dedicated ablation that measures performance when using fp32 versus fp16 likelihoods inside GSPO; instead, it provides indirect evidence:

* **Section 5.4 (“Benefit of GSPO for RL infrastructure”)** explicitly states that “GSPO makes it possible to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for optimization, thereby avoiding the need for recomputation with the training engine.” The surrounding discussion points out that because only sequence‑level likelihoods are required, “the former is much more tolerant of precision discrepancies.”

* **Figure 4 (clipping fractions)** shows that despite clipping a far larger fraction of tokens than GRPO, GSPO still attains higher training efficiency. This suggests that the remaining signal—derived from sequence‑level ratios—remains robust even when those ratios are supplied by a different precision engine.

* **Stability across MoE models** (Section 5.3) illustrates that GSPO works without “Routing Replay,” a technique needed precisely because token‑level ratios become unstable when expert routing changes (which also changes per‑token probabilities). The fact that GSPO succeeds without any extra engineering indicates that its reliance on sequence‑level numbers tolerates noisy per‑token values.

Taken together, these observations support the intuition that *the algorithmic design itself dampens precision noise*, rather than providing a formal quantitative comparison.

---

### Bottom line

* **Token‑wise importance ratios amplify precision errors**, because each token’s probability appears directly in the gradient weight and any error propagates multiplicatively.
* **Sequence‑wise importance ratios average over tokens**, turning many tiny errors into a negligible shift that stays inside GSPO’s clipping window.
* The paper does not present a dedicated numeric benchmark of fp16 vs fp32 likelihoods, but several empirical findings (stable MoE training without routing replay, higher efficiency despite heavy clipping, and successful use of inference‑engine logits) are consistent with the claim that sequence‑level likelihoods are sufficiently robust to allow direct use of inference engine probabilities.

Therefore, while a formal experiment would be useful for completeness, the evidence and theoretical arguments given in *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* convincingly explain why GSPO tolerates precision mismatches and why practitioners can safely feed inference‑engine likelihoods straight into the RL optimizer.

### How does the gradient expression in Eq. (9) differ qualitatively from that of GRPO?
Question:
In Eq. (9) the gradient contains a factor \(s_i(θ) = (π_θ(y_i|x)/π_{θ_{old}}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|}\) multiplied by an average over token log‑gradients. In contrast, GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its own importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(θ)\. Could someone walk through a simple numerical example (e.g., two tokens, two responses) showing how these two gradients behave differently when one token’s probability changes drastically while the other stays stable?
Answer:
Both GSPO (the gradient in Eq.~$(9)$) and GRPO try to re‑weight the log‑likelihood gradients of the tokens that appear in a sampled response, but they do it at a **different granularity**.  
In GRPO each token receives its own importance weight  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$

so the contribution of token $\(t\)$ to the overall gradient is multiplied by $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.  
In GSPO the whole sequence gets a single weight  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
           =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr),
$$

and **every token in that response is multiplied by the same factor** $s_i(\theta)$.  
The difference is most apparent when one token in the response changes dramatically while the others stay almost unchanged.  

---

### A tiny numerical illustration  

Suppose we have one query $x$ and we generate two candidate responses (group size $G=2$):

* Response 1: tokens $[a,b]$ (length $|y_1|=2$)
* Response 2: tokens $[c,d]$ (length $|y_2|=2$)

Assume the old policy probabilities are  

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a|x)=0.4,\qquad
 \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(b|x,a)=0.5,\\
&\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(c|x)=0.6,\qquad
 \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(d|x,c)=0.7 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Now we change the current policy only for token $b$; everything else stays the same:

$$
\pi_{\theta}(a|x)=0.4,\qquad
\pi_{\theta}(b|x,a)=0.05,      % huge drop
\pi_{\theta}(c|x)=0.6,\qquad
\pi_{\theta}(d|x,c)=0.7 .
$$

Hence the token‑level importance ratios are  

$$
w_{1,1}=1.0,\quad w_{1,2}= \frac{0.05}{0.5}=0.10,\qquad
w_{2,1}=1.0,\quad w_{2,2}=1.0 .
$$

The **GRPO gradient** (ignoring clipping and the advantage factor for simplicity) is proportional to  

$$
g^{\text{GRPO}}=
\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[
\,w_{1,1}\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(a)
+ w_{1,2}\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(b)
+ w_{2,1}\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(c)
+ w_{2,2}\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(d)\Bigr].
$$

Because $w_{1,2}=0.10$, the update contributed by token $b$ is **down‑weighted by a factor of ten**; the other three tokens keep their full weight.

Now compute the **sequence‑level weight** for each response:

* For response 1:  
  $$
  s_1(\theta)=
  \Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(a)\pi_{\theta}(b)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a)\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(b)}\Bigr)^{1/2}
   =\Bigl( \frac{0.4\times 0.05}{0.4\times 0.5} \Bigr)^{1/2}
   = (0.10)^{1/2}=0.316 .
  $$
* For response 2: $s_2(\theta)=1.0$ because all token probabilities are unchanged.

The **GSPO gradient** (again omitting clipping and advantages) is  

$$
g^{\text{GSPO}}=
\frac{1}{2}\Bigl[
s_1(\theta)\bigl(
\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(a)+
\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(b)\bigr)
+ s_2(\theta)\bigl(
\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(c)+
\nabla \log \pi_{\theta}(d)\bigr)
\bigr].
$$

Both tokens *a* and *b* are multiplied by the same factor $s_1=0.316$.  
Thus the contribution of token $b$ is not suppressed to 0.10 as in GRPO; instead it receives roughly **three times larger weight** (0.316 vs 0.10). The reduction is shared evenly across all tokens of that sequence.

---

### What does this mean in practice?

*When a single token’s probability collapses* (as often happens for long generations or for MoE models where routing changes), GRPO’s per‑token weight can become extremely small or extremely large depending on whether the probability went down or up. Those extreme weights are then multiplied by the corresponding log‑likelihood gradients, producing a **high‑variance gradient estimate**: one token can dominate or be almost completely ignored.

GSPO, by averaging the log‑ratio over the whole sequence and then exponentiating, effectively **takes a geometric mean** of all token‑level ratios. A dramatic change in one token is diluted by the other tokens that stayed stable, so the sequence weight stays in a moderate range (here 0.316 instead of 0.10). Consequently

* the variance of the importance weight across different samples is much lower,
* clipping can be performed with a much tighter interval (the paper uses $3\times10^{-4}$ vs GRPO’s ≈ 0.2),
* gradients from all tokens of a response are scaled uniformly, avoiding “spiky’’ updates that can push the policy into pathological regions.

In short, Eq.~$(9)$’s gradient differs qualitatively from GRPO’s gradient because it **does not let any single token dictate the magnitude of the update**; it treats the whole response as a single unit whose importance is measured by its overall likelihood change, leading to more stable and efficient learning—especially for long responses and mixture‑of‑experts models where individual token probabilities can fluctuate wildly.
