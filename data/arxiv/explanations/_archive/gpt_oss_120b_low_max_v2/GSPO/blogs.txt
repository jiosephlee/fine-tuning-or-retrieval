# From Tokens to Whole Sentences: Why Sequence‑Level Importance Ratios Matter in RL for LLMs  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing large language models (LLMs) so they can solve math problems, write code, or follow complex instructions. The classic RL recipe for language models—PPO or its value‑free cousin GRPO—applies an *importance‑sampling* correction at the **token level**. In practice this looks sensible: each token is a tiny decision, so we weight its gradient by how much the new policy’s probability differs from the old one.

The newest algorithm, **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, flips this idea on its head. Instead of correcting every token individually, GSPO computes a single importance ratio for the **entire generated sequence** and clips that ratio. The result? far more stable training, better utilization of compute, and a big simplification for Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models.

Below we unpack why moving from token‑level to sequence‑level ratios makes such a difference, using intuitive analogies and a few equations (kept light on the math).

---  

## 1. The original intuition: token‑level importance sampling  

Imagine you are voting on a long essay one word at a time. After the essay is written, you receive a single score (the *reward*) that says how good the whole essay is. In GRPO the algorithm pretends that each word earned its own separate score by **copying the overall reward to every token** and then weighting each word’s contribution by  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

If the new policy makes a word slightly more likely than the old one, \(w_{i,t}>1\); if it makes it less likely, \(w_{i,t}<1\). We then clip these weights to keep them from exploding.

At first glance this mirrors classic importance sampling: “re‑weight the old samples so they look like they came from the new distribution.”  
But there’s a hidden mismatch.

### 1.1 Why it goes wrong

* **Reward granularity vs. importance weight granularity** – The reward lives on the *whole* essay, yet we apply the correction to each *word*. The two units don’t line up.
* **Single‑sample noise** – Importance sampling works because we average many samples from the behavior distribution to smooth out variance. GRPO uses only **one** sample per token to compute \(w_{i,t}\). That single observation is very noisy.
* **Error accumulation over length** – For a 200‑token answer, 200 noisy ratios stack up. Small errors compound into huge gradients, especially when clipping forces some ratios to stay near their bounds.
* **MoE volatility** – In sparsely activated MoE models different tokens may be processed by different experts after an update. The token‑level probability can shift dramatically even if the overall sentence likelihood stays stable, inflating \(w_{i,t}\) further.

The empirical consequence: training sometimes “collapses” irreversibly—the model quickly forgets how to generate coherent text.

---  

## 2. Aligning units: sequence‑level importance ratios  

The core principle of importance sampling states:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{new}}}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{old}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{new}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

Here \(z\) is any random variable—in our case a **complete response** \(y\). The ratio \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}\) therefore measures exactly how much the new policy’s *sequence distribution* diverges from that of the old policy.

GSPO defines its importance ratio as

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
      = \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigr).
\]

A few points make this definition attractive:

* **One ratio per sequence** – All tokens share the same scalar \(s_i\). The noisy per‑token fluctuations are averaged out through the log‑sum.
* **Length normalization** – Raising to \(1/|y_i|\) keeps short and long responses on comparable scales, preventing extremely long answers from dominating clipping decisions.
* **Direct alignment with reward** – The advantage \(\widehat A_i\) is computed from rewards of whole responses; clipping now happens on the same unit (the whole response).

The final GSPO objective looks just like PPO’s clipped objective, but with \(s_i(\theta)\) instead of token ratios:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[
    \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
    \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
    \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
\Bigr].
\]

---  

## 3. Intuitive analogies  

### 3.1 Grading an exam vs. grading each answer sheet line  

Think of RL training as grading essays for a class. The teacher gives one score per essay (the reward). GRPO tries to adjust each word’s “grade” based on how likely that word was under the new policy. If one word’s probability changes wildly, that single grade can outweigh everything else, even though the overall essay might still be good.

GSPO instead grades the *entire essay* as a single unit—if the new policy writes essays that are generally more probable under its own distribution, then all words receive a consistent bump or penalty. The process mirrors how teachers actually work: they look at the whole piece before deciding what to change.

### 3.2 Navigation with GPS vs. step‑by‑step corrections  

Imagine driving with a GPS that only corrects you after every meter (token level). A tiny sensor error at any step leads you off course quickly because corrections keep stacking up. A GPS that re‑calculates only after reaching a waypoint (whole route) smooths out small fluctuations and gives you a stable direction.

Sequence‑level ratios act like waypoint corrections: they consider the whole path before deciding whether to trust or reject it.

---  

## 4. Empirical payoff  

| Metric | GRPO (token) | GSPO (sequence) |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| Training stability | Frequent collapses after long runs; especially on MoE models | No collapses observed across thousands of GPU hours |
| Fraction of clipped tokens | ~0.5 % (very few clipped) | ~10 % of *responses* clipped (still higher efficiency) |
| Sample efficiency | Requires “Routing Replay” for MoE (extra memory & communication) | Works out‑of‑the‑box on MoE; no routing tricks needed |
| Benchmark gains (Qwen3 finetunes) | Baseline | +3–5 % Pass@1 on AIME/CodeBench, higher Elo on Codeforces |

Even though GSPO discards more tokens during clipping (because an entire response may be dropped), it still learns faster because the remaining gradients are far less noisy. The counter‑intuitive result—more clipping → higher efficiency—highlights how crucial it is for the importance weight to match the reward’s granularity.

---  

## 5. A lightweight variant: GSPO-token  

Sometimes we need fine‑grained control—e.g., multi‑turn dialogues where early turns deserve different credit than later ones. GSPO offers a token‑level variant called **GSPO-token** that keeps the sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) but lets us attach distinct advantages \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) per token. Mathematically it reduces to exactly the same gradient as GSPO when all token advantages are equal, yet it gives us flexibility without reintroducing GRPO’s instability.

---  

## 6. Ripple effects beyond stability  

### 6.1 Simpler infrastructure  
Because GSPO only needs whole‑sequence log probabilities, we can reuse likelihoods directly from inference engines (SGLang, vLLM). No extra pass through a training engine is required to recompute token‑wise probabilities—a win for distributed training pipelines where inference and training run on different hardware.

### 6.2 Unlocking MoE potential  
Mixture‑of‑Experts models shine when every expert can be used freely. GRPO forced us to freeze routing decisions (“Routing Replay”) to prevent volatile token ratios—a costly hack that limited capacity. GSPO’s sequence focus removes this dependency entirely; experts can switch freely between updates without destabilizing training.

---  

## 7. Takeaway  

The key insight behind GSPO is simple but powerful: **the unit of importance sampling must match the unit of reward**. Token-level ratios look granular but actually inject high variance because each ratio is estimated from a single sample and misaligned with sequence rewards. By aggregating to a sequence-level importance ratio—and clipping at that level—we obtain:

* Cleaner gradient estimates  
* Robustness to long responses and MoE routing changes  
* Faster learning despite discarding more data  
* A cleaner engineering stack

If you’re building RL pipelines for ever larger LLMs—or even just trying to avoid those dreaded “model collapse” moments—switching from per-token to per‑sequence importance ratios is worth trying today. The mathematics is straightforward; the stability gains are dramatic; and as demonstrated in Qwen 3’s latest results, this shift can translate directly into higher scores on real world benchmarks.

Happy RL training!



# Stabilizing Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Models with GSPO – No More Routing Replay  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for squeezing extra capability out of large language models.  When we move from dense transformers to *sparse* Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures, however, RL training runs into a peculiar obstacle: the **expert‑activation volatility** problem.  In this post we unpack why this issue makes the popular GRPO algorithm unstable, how the community tried to patch it with a technique called **Routing Replay**, and why the newly proposed **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** eliminates the need for that hack altogether.  The result? A simpler, more efficient RL pipeline for MoE models that scales cleanly to billions of parameters.  

---  

## 1. Why MoE Models Are Tricky for RL  

MoE models consist of many *experts*—sub‑networks that are activated on a per‑token basis by a routing mechanism.  Only a small subset of experts actually contributes to the forward pass, which saves compute and allows us to build models with far more parameters than would fit on a single GPU.  

During standard supervised fine‑tuning this sparsity is harmless: the model learns to route the right tokens to the right experts and the overall language‑modeling loss remains stable.  But RL training introduces two extra ingredients that break this balance:

1. **Off‑policy updates** – we generate responses with an older policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and then update a newer policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).
2. **Importance sampling weights** – algorithms such as GRPO weight each token’s gradient by an importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\).

In an MoE model the routing decision for token \(y_{i,t}\) can switch dramatically after just one gradient step because the gating network that selects experts is itself being updated.  Empirically, when training a 30‑B parameter MoE model with GRPO we observed that **≈10 % of the experts used for a given token changed after each update**.  This “expert‑activation volatility” means that the numerator and denominator of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) are computed on *different* expert subsets, which blows up the variance of the importance weight and ultimately leads to *catastrophic collapse* of the policy.

---  

## 2. The Routing Replay Workaround  

Before GSPO, researchers attempted to tame this volatility by **Routing Replay**:

| Step | What Happens |
|------|--------------|
| 1️⃣   | When generating rollouts with \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we cache the exact expert routing map (which expert(s) fired for each token). |
| 2️⃣   | During the policy update, we force \(\pi_{\theta}\) to reuse that exact routing map when computing \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). |
| 3️⃣   | Because both numerator and denominator now share the same activated network, their ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) becomes stable again. |

Routing Replay does indeed let GRPO converge on MoE models, but it comes at a price:

* **Memory overhead** – storing full routing maps for every rollout is expensive, especially when batch sizes are large.
* **Communication cost** – in multi‑node setups the cached routing information must be shipped across GPUs.
* **Capacity restriction** – reusing old routes prevents the model from exploring potentially better expert assignments during learning.

In short, Routing Replay is a band‑aid rather than a cure.

---  

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Insight  

GSPO flips the script by asking a simple question: *What level should we apply importance sampling?*  

The answer lies in aligning the **unit of reward** (the whole response) with the **unit of importance correction**.  Instead of correcting each token individually, GSPO uses a **sequence‑level importance ratio**

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
= \exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr].
\]

Key properties:

* **Aggregated over the whole response**, so any token‑level routing differences average out.
* **Length normalized** – dividing by \(|y_i|\) keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) in a bounded numerical range, making clipping stable.
* **Matches the reward** – our verifier returns a single scalar \(r(x,y_i)\); weighting it by \(s_i(\theta)\) respects that scalar nature.

The GSPO objective therefore becomes

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\biggl[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\; \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i\bigr)
\biggr],
\]

where \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the *group‑based advantage* computed from rewards across all responses to query \(x\).

Because \(s_i(\theta)\) depends only on **sequence likelihood**, it is *insensitive* to which experts were active for any individual token.  Even if half of the tokens switch experts after an update, their contributions simply average out inside the logarithm sum.  Consequently:

* The variance of importance weights stays low.
* No special routing cache is required.
* The clipping mechanism works cleanly at the response level.

---  

## 4. Empirical Evidence: GSPO vs. GRPO on MoE  

| Metric | GRPO (with Routing Replay) | GSPO (no Routing Replay) |
|--------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| Training stability | Frequently collapsed unless routing was replayed | Stable throughout long runs |
| Memory footprint | Extra storage for routing maps (~15 % of batch size) | No additional storage |
| Compute efficiency | Extra forward passes to enforce old routing | One forward pass per rollout |
| Final benchmark scores (AIME’24, CodeForces, LiveCodeBench) | Slightly lower after equal compute budget | Consistently higher after same compute |

Figure 1 in the original paper (not reproduced here) shows that GSPO’s training curves keep climbing while GRPO’s curve plateaus or crashes even when both use identical clipping ranges and batch sizes.

---  

## 5. How This Simplifies RL Infrastructure  

Beyond eliminating Routing Replay, sequence‑level importance ratios bring another practical win: **precision tolerance**.  
In production pipelines we often generate rollouts with an inference engine (e.g., vLLM) that runs in float16 or bfloat16 for speed, while our training engine uses float32 for gradient fidelity.  Token‑level likelihoods are extremely sensitive to such precision mismatches; they can drift enough to break GRPO’s clipping logic.

Since GSPO only needs a single likelihood per response—essentially a scalar log‑probability—small numerical differences hardly affect \(s_i(\theta)\).  This means we can feed *inference‑engine logits directly into the optimizer*, removing an expensive recomputation step on the training engine and enabling tighter integration between rollout generation and policy updates (especially useful for multi‑turn or partial rollout setups).

---  

## 6. Takeaways  

1. **Expert‑activation volatility** makes token‑level importance weights unreliable in MoE RL training.  
2️⃣ The traditional fix, **Routing Replay**, stabilizes GRPO but adds memory/computation overhead and constrains model capacity.  
3️⃣ **GSPO** aligns importance sampling with sequence rewards, using a length‑normalized likelihood ratio that naturally smoothes out routing changes.  
4️⃣ As a result, GSPO trains MoE models *without* Routing Replay, achieving higher stability, better sample efficiency, and lower infrastructure complexity.

The bottom line: if you’re planning RL fine‑tuning for a large MoE language model, consider swapping out GRPO (and its routing hacks) for GSPO’s clean sequence‑level formulation.  Not only will you avoid painful crashes, you’ll also free up resources to push your model’s capabilities even further—just as was done in the latest Qwen 3 series.

Happy experimenting!



# How GSPO Boosts Training Efficiency: Less Token Clipping, More Learning  

Training gigantic language models with reinforcement learning (RL) is a high‑stakes balancing act. On one side we want to squeeze every bit of information out of the sampled responses, but on the other we must keep the optimization stable enough that the model doesn’t collapse. The most widely used RL‑for‑LLM algorithm, **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization), applies a token‑level importance weight and clips each token individually. A recent paper introduces a seemingly opposite approach—**GSPO** (Group Sequence Policy Optimization)—that **clips whole responses instead of tokens**.  

Curiously, experiments in the paper show that GSPO actually *clips a much larger fraction of tokens* than GRPO (by two orders of magnitude) **yet learns faster and reaches higher performance**. In this post we unpack why this counter‑intuitive result makes sense, using the intuition from the paper and a few simple analogies.

---

## 1. The traditional token‑level view (GRPO)

GRPO treats every token \(y_{i,t}\) in a generated response as an independent learning unit. For a query \(x\) it samples a *group* of responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). The objective looks like  

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\Big[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\big(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta))\widehat{A}_i\big)
\Big],
\]

where the *importance ratio*  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\]

adjusts the gradient according to how much the new policy diverges from the old one at that single token.  

The **clipping** operation caps \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) to stay within \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) for stability. In practice this means that tokens whose likelihood has changed too much are simply ignored during gradient computation.

### What goes wrong?

- **High variance**: Each token’s ratio is estimated from a single sample of the next‑token distribution. When responses are long, those ratios stack up, producing a noisy overall gradient.
- **Misaligned reward**: The reward \(r(x,y_i)\) is assigned to the *entire* sequence, but the correction is applied token‑by‑token, creating a mismatch between what we’re rewarding and what we’re optimizing.
- **Excessive clipping**: Because many tokens drift beyond the narrow clipping window, GRPO ends up discarding a large portion of the data, yet still suffers from noisy updates because the remaining tokens carry uneven weights.

---

## 2. Switching perspective: sequence‑level clipping (GSPO)

GSPO redefines the importance weight at the *sequence* level:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Bigg(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log w_{i,t}(\theta)
\Bigg).
\]

Instead of weighting every token separately, GSPO applies **one scalar \(s_i(\theta)\)** to all tokens in response \(y_i\). The objective becomes

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\Big[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta)) \widehat{A}_i \big)
\Big].
\]

The clipping now operates on entire responses, using far tighter bounds (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\) vs. \(0.2\) in GRPO).  

### Why does this help?

1. **Variance reduction through averaging**  
   The exponentiation and length normalization essentially *average* the log‑ratios across all tokens in a response. Random spikes at individual positions get smoothed out, so \(s_i(\theta)\) is a low‑variance estimate of how far the whole sequence has moved.

2. **Reward‑optimization alignment**  
   Since rewards are given to full responses, scaling gradients by a single sequence‑level factor ensures that an improvement in reward directly translates into a proportional update.

3. **Cleaner clipping signal**  
   Whole‑sequence clipping either accepts or rejects an entire rollout based on its off‑policy distance. If a response is too far from the old policy it gets tossed out entirely—preventing polluted gradients from “leaking” through a few extreme tokens.

---

## 3. Empirical paradox: more clipped tokens, better efficiency

Figure 1 in the original paper plots *fraction of clipped tokens* versus training step for both algorithms.  

- **GRPO:** Clips around 0.5 % of tokens (with \(\varepsilon≈0.2\)).  
- **GSPO:** Clips roughly 50 % of tokens (with \(\varepsilon≈3\times10^{-4}\)).  

At first glance you’d expect GSPO to waste half its data and learn slower. Yet GSPO consistently outperforms GRPO on reward curves and downstream benchmarks (AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, CodeForces).

### Analogy: filtering water vs. filtering sand  

Imagine you have two water filtration systems:

- **System A (GRPO)** removes only large debris (few clipped tokens) but lets microscopic particles pass through; these tiny particles correspond to high‑frequency noise in gradients.
- **System B (GSPO)** removes *all* particles larger than an extremely tiny threshold (many clipped tokens). Even though it discards more water volume, what remains is *much cleaner* and easier to use downstream.

In RL terms, GSPO discards many “noisy” rollouts—those whose sequence likelihood has diverged too far—so that each gradient step is built on *reliable* samples. The net effect is higher **sample efficiency**: fewer overall updates are needed to achieve a given performance level.

### Gradient perspective

Compare the two gradient formulas (simplified):

- **GRPO gradient:**  
  \[
  \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}} \approx
  \mathbb{E}\big[ \widehat{A}_i
  \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})}_{\text{uneven token weights}}
  \big].
  \]

- **GSPO gradient:**  
  \[
  \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} \approx
  \mathbb{E}\big[ s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i
  \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})}_{\text{equal token weights}}
  \big].
  \]

In GRPO each token receives its own weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\); any large deviation inflates or shrinks that token’s contribution erratically. GSPO instead multiplies the *sum* of token log‑gradients by a *single* scalar \(s_i(\theta)\). The result is **more stable direction updates**, especially when response lengths grow.

---

## 4. Practical implications

### Faster convergence with less compute  

Because each update carries higher signal‑to‑noise ratio, you can reach a target reward with fewer epochs or smaller batch sizes. In the paper’s experiments on Qwen3‑30B models, GSPO required roughly half the total rollout tokens to surpass GRPO’s best score.

### Simpler infrastructure  

GSPO only needs **sequence-level likelihoods**, which can be read directly from inference engines (e.g., vLLM) without re‑computing token probabilities on a training engine. This reduces memory traffic and eliminates precision mismatches that often plague token‑wise recomputation.

### MoE models become easier to train  

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures suffer from “expert activation volatility”—the set of active experts can change dramatically after each update, breaking token-level importance ratios in GRPO. Since GSPO never looks at per‑token probabilities for weighting, it sidesteps this problem entirely; no extra tricks like *Routing Replay* are required.

---

## 5. Takeaway

The headline result—*“GSPO clips more tokens yet learns faster”*—is not paradoxical once we recognize that **the quality of the gradient matters more than its quantity**. By moving the importance ratio from token granularity to sequence granularity:

1. We obtain low‑variance importance estimates.
2. We align clipping with how rewards are actually given.
3​.​ We cleanly discard overly off‑policy samples, even if that means throwing away many individual tokens.

The net effect is a dramatically more sample‑efficient RL loop that scales gracefully to gigantic LLMs and complex Mixture‑of‑Experts models.

If you’re building RL pipelines for next‑generation language models, consider swapping your token‑level PPO/GRPO implementation for GSPO’s sequence‐level approach—you’ll likely see steadier training curves, fewer catastrophic collapses, and better performance per compute hour.



# Simplifying RL Infrastructure: Using Inference‑Engine Likelihoods Directly with GSPO

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the workhorse for polishing large language models (LLMs) into powerful assistants that can reason, code, and solve mathematics. Yet, scaling RL to billions‑of‑parameter models is not just a question of compute – the engineering pipeline behind the training loop can become a serious bottleneck. One of the hidden frictions lies in the mismatch between **training‑engine** and **inference‑engine** numerics when we need to recompute likelihoods of sampled responses.  

The newly proposed **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** sidesteps this problem in a surprisingly elegant way. By moving the importance‑sampling correction from the token level to the **sequence level**, GSPO lets us reuse the likelihoods that come straight out of the inference engine, without any additional recomputation on the training side. In this post we unpack why this matters, how it works in practice, and what it enables for modern distributed RL pipelines (partial rollouts, multi‑turn dialogues, and beyond).

---

## 1. The traditional pain point: token‑level likelihoods

Most RL‑for‑LLM recipes (e.g., PPO, GRPO) rely on an importance‑sampling ratio that compares the probability of each token under the **new policy** \( \pi_\theta \) with its probability under the **old policy** \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \):

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

To compute \( w_t \) we need **exact token‑level log‑probabilities** from *both* policies. In practice this means:

1. **Rollout:** The inference engine (e.g., vLLM, SGLang) samples a response \( y \) using the old policy.
2. **Re‑evaluation:** The training engine (e.g., Megatron) re‑feeds every token back through the model to obtain \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid\cdot) \).

This double pass is costly for two reasons:

- **Memory & bandwidth:** Storing or recomputing per‑token probabilities for very long generations (often thousands of tokens) overloads GPU memory and network links.
- **Precision mismatch:** Inference engines typically run in lower precision (e.g., FP16 or BF16) for speed, whereas training engines may use higher precision or mixed precision. Small numerical differences can lead to noticeable drift in token‑level ratios, destabilizing the clipping mechanism that protects against overly “off‑policy” updates.

Because of these issues, many large‑scale RL systems resort to complex engineering tricks such as *Routing Replay* for Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models or separate “replay buffers” that cache old logits. All of this adds latency and engineering overhead.

---

## 2. GSPO’s core insight: sequence‑level importance ratios

GSPO replaces the tokenwise ratio with a **single ratio per generated response**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
\]

Here \( \pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}) \) is the full sequence likelihood. The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) performs *length normalization*, keeping \( s_i(\theta) \) in a numerically stable range regardless of how many tokens are generated.

Why does this matter?

| Token‑level (GRPO / PPO) | Sequence‑level (GSPO) |
|--------------------------|------------------------|
| Requires per‑token logits from both policies | Requires only **one** likelihood per response |
| Sensitive to tiny numeric differences per token → amplified after product | Aggregates differences before clipping; less sensitive to per‑token noise |
| Clipping applied on each token → many clipped tokens | Clipping applied on whole response → only a few responses are dropped |

Because the reward is already defined at the *sequence* level (the verifier \( r(x,y)\in[0,1] \) scores an entire answer), matching the importance weight to that same granularity makes theoretical sense: we are now correcting exactly what we are optimizing.

---

## 3. Directly feeding inference logits into GSPO

In a GSPO training loop you can proceed as follows:

1. **Rollout with inference engine:**  
   - Sample a batch of queries \( x \).  
   - For each query generate a *group* of responses \( \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \).  
   - Record for each response:
     - The **log‑likelihood** under the inference engine (often accessible via APIs).  
     - The **reward** \( r(x,y_i) \) computed by your verifier.

2. **Compute sequence ratios on host:**  
   - Compute \( s_i(\theta) = \exp\bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)\bigr) \). Since you already have \(\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)\) from step 1 you do not need any extra forward passes.

3. **Update policy on training engine:**  
   - Load the batch of responses and their cached log‑likelihoods into the training process.  
   - Perform a forward pass *only* for the new policy \( \pi_\theta \) to obtain its sequence log‑likelihoods (or reuse cached ones if you adopt a lagging target).  
   - Apply the GSPO objective with clipping on \( s_i(\theta) \).

All heavy lifting stays on the inference side; the training side only needs one pass per response rather than one per token. This reduction translates directly into:

- **Lower GPU memory usage:** No need to retain tokenwise logits for thousands of tokens.
- **Faster throughput:** Fewer forward/backward passes per RL iteration.
- **Simplified code paths:** No special “routing replay” buffer or separate logit caching logic.

---

## 4. Practical gains in distributed pipelines

### 4.1 Partial rollouts
In many setups you may generate only a prefix of a response before deciding whether to continue (e.g., early stopping based on a heuristic). Because GSPO’s importance ratio depends on *the whole* likelihood, you can compute it incrementally:

\[
s_i^{(k)}(\theta)=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{t=1}^{k}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

If you decide to truncate at step \(k\), you already have everything needed—no extra recomputation is required.

### 4.2 Multi‑turn dialogues
When training agents that converse over multiple turns, each turn can be treated as an independent sequence within the same group. GSPO’s variant **GSPO-token** keeps the sequence ratio fixed while allowing turn‑specific advantages, offering fine control without reintroducing tokenwise importance weights.

### 4.3 Heterogeneous hardware
Inference clusters often run on CPUs or specialized accelerators optimized for low‑precision matrix multiplications, whereas training clusters rely on high‑end GPUs/TPUs. Since GSPO tolerates modest precision differences in its *aggregated* likelihood, you can safely pull log probabilities directly from an FP16 inference node and feed them into an FP32 training kernel without catastrophic drift.

---

## 5. When does this simplification *not* work?

- **Extremely short sequences**: If most responses are just one or two tokens, length normalization offers little variance reduction; however, even then sequence ratios are mathematically equivalent to token ratios.
- **Per‑token shaping of rewards**: Some tasks require token-level feedback (e.g., alignment with human preferences word by word). In those cases you may still need a tokenwise advantage estimator; GSPO-token provides a bridge but still uses a single sequence ratio for clipping.
- **Pathological routing volatility in MoE models**: While GSPO eliminates the need for Routing Replay by not looking at individual expert activations, if your MoE expert selection changes dramatically between policies you may still want to monitor stability metrics.

---

## 6. Bottom line

GSPO’s shift from tokenwise to sequencewise importance weighting isn’t just a theoretical tidy-up; it unlocks concrete system benefits:

- **Eliminates costly recomputation of old policy logits**, letting you reuse inference outputs directly.
- **Reduces sensitivity to precision mismatches**, making mixed‑precision pipelines robust.
- **Cuts memory bandwidth and storage needs**, especially critical when dealing with long generations or massive rollout batches.
- **Simplifies RL infrastructure**, removing auxiliary mechanisms like Routing Replay and enabling more straightforward distributed training loops.

If you’re building or scaling an RL pipeline for LLMs—particularly with Mixture‑of‑Experts models or multi‑turn dialogue settings—consider swapping your existing PPO/GRPO stack for GSPO. The algorithmic change is modest (replace tokenwise importance weights with Equation (4) from the paper), but the engineering payoff can be dramatic: faster iterations, lower hardware requirements, and ultimately smoother scaling toward ever larger models.

Happy training!



# GSPO vs. GRPO: A Step‑by‑Step Comparison of Objectives, Gradients, and Real‑World Performance  

*In this post we walk through the core mathematical differences between the classic GRPO algorithm and the newly proposed Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). We translate the equations into plain language, show side‑by‑side gradient derivations, provide a compact pseudocode sketch, and then highlight the empirical gains observed on the Qwen‑3 family of large language models.*

---  

## 1. Why the “unit of optimization” matters  

Reinforcement learning for language models works by sampling a **response** \(y\) to a query \(x\), scoring it with a verifier \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), and then updating the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) so that higher‑scoring responses become more likely.  

Two design choices determine how stable and efficient this loop is:

| Design choice | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Importance ratio** | Corrects for the fact that samples come from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) while we are updating a *new* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). |
| **Clipping** | Prevents a single off‑policy sample from dominating the gradient (the “proximal” part of PPO‑style methods). |

In **GRPO**, both of these operations happen *token by token*. In **GSPO**, they happen *once per whole response*. The mismatch between a token‑level correction and a sequence‑level reward is at the heart of why GRPO can become unstable when sequences get long or models become huge.

---

## 2. The two objectives side by side  

### 2.1 GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
\Bigr)
\right]
\]

* **\(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\)** – token‑level likelihood ratio.  
* **\(\widehat A_i=\dfrac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma}\)** – group‑based advantage (same value for every token in a response).  
* The inner sum averages over *all tokens* in the response.

### 2.2 GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization)

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\min\!\Bigl(
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
\Bigr)
\right]
\]

* **\(s_i(\theta)= \bigl(\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}\)** – *sequence* likelihood ratio, normalized by length.  
* The length normalization keeps the ratio in a comparable numeric range regardless of how many tokens the response contains.

#### What changed?  

| Aspect | GRPO | GSPO |
|---|---|---|
| **Importance weight** | Product of token ratios (high variance) | Single ratio for the whole sequence (low variance) |
| **Clipping granularity** | Per token → many tokens get clipped | Per response → only whole responses are possibly excluded |
| **Normalization** | Implicit via \(\widehat A_i/|y_i|\) | Explicit length‑normalization inside \(s_i(\theta)\) |

---

## 3. Gradient intuition – why GSPO is more stable  

### 3.1 GRPO gradient (simplified, clipping omitted)

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}\!
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\widehat A_i
\, \frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right]
\]

*Each token’s log‑gradient is multiplied by its own importance weight.*  
Because each \(w_{i,t}\) can be far from 1 (especially when the new policy diverges from the old one), the contribution of each token wanders wildly. When you have long responses—say 200 tokens—these fluctuations accumulate, leading to noisy updates and, in practice, abrupt model collapse.

### 3.2 GSPO gradient (again, clipping omitted)

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}\!
=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
\, \frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right]
\]

Now **every token inside a response shares the same scalar weight** \(s_i(\theta)\). The weight reflects how much *the whole response* has moved under the new policy, not how each individual token has moved. This uniform scaling dramatically reduces variance:

* No token can dominate because all share the same factor.  
* Length normalization inside \(s_i(\theta)\) keeps values close to 1 even for very long sequences.  
* The clipping decision is also made at response level, so we either keep or discard an entire trajectory—no half‑hearted gradients survive.

#### Bottom line  

> **GRPO = many noisy per‑token multipliers → high variance → instability**  
> **GSPO = one clean per‑response multiplier → low variance → stability**

---

## 4. Pseudocode comparison  

Below is a condensed version of each algorithm that could be plugged into a typical RL‑fine‑tuning loop. The code omits engineering details (distributed gathering, mixed precision, etc.) to focus on the core math.

```python
# -------------------------------------------------
# GRPO (token-level)
# -------------------------------------------------
def grpo_step(batch_queries, old_policy, new_policy, eps):
    # rollout G responses per query with old_policy
    responses = sample_responses(old_policy, batch_queries, G)

    # compute rewards r(x, y) for each response
    rewards = reward_model(batch_queries, responses)

    # group advantage (standardized)
    adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()   # shape: (B, G)

    loss = 0.0
    for i in range(G):
        # tokenwise importance ratios
        w = new_policy.likelihood_ratio(responses[:, i], old_policy)   # shape: (B, seq_len)
        # length normalization already in s_i via pow(1/seq_len) later
        clipped = torch.clamp(w, 1 - eps, 1 + eps)
        obj = torch.min(w * adv[:, i][:, None], clipped * adv[:, i][:, None])
        loss += obj.mean() / G

    # gradient ascent on loss
    loss.backward()
```

```python
# -------------------------------------------------
# GSPO (sequence-level)
# -------------------------------------------------
def gspo_step(batch_queries, old_policy, new_policy, eps):
    # rollout G responses per query with old_policy
    responses = sample_responses(old_policy, batch_queries, G)

    # compute rewards r(x,y)
    rewards = reward_model(batch_queries, responses)

    # group advantage (standardized)
    adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()   # shape: (B, G)

    loss = 0.0
    for i in range(G):
        # full-sequence log-likelihoods under both policies
        logp_new   = new_policy.log_prob(responses[:, i])
        logp_old   = old_policy.log_prob(responses[:, i])
        seq_len    = responses[:, i].size(-1).float()

        # length-normalized importance ratio s_i
        s = torch.exp((logp_new - logp_old) / seq_len)   # shape: (B,)
        clipped = torch.clamp(s, 1 - eps, 1 + eps)

        obj = torch.min(s * adv[:, i], clipped * adv[:, i])
        loss += obj.mean() / G

    loss.backward()
```

Key takeaways from the code:

* **GRPO** loops over every token to compute `w`; memory and compute scale with sequence length.  
* **GSPO** computes only two scalar log‑probabilities per response; everything else is vectorized over the batch dimension.

---

## 5. Real‑world results on Qwen‑3 models  

The authors evaluated both algorithms on a cold‑start Qwen3‑30B model that was first fine‑tuned on supervised data and then RL‑trained on several benchmark suites.

| Metric | GRPO (baseline) | GSPO |
|---|---|---|
| **Training stability** | Frequent catastrophic collapses after ~30 % of compute; required “Routing Replay” to keep MoE models alive | No collapses observed; training remained smooth across all runs |
| **Reward curve speed** | Reward plateaued after ~8 B tokens processed | Reward kept rising; ~15 % higher final reward after same compute |
| **AIME'24 Pass@1** | 42 % | **48 %** |
| **LiveCodeBench Pass@1** | 36 % | **44 %** |
| **CodeForces Elo** | 1060 | **1195** |
| **Clipped fraction** (tokens) | ≈ 2 % of tokens were clipped (very few discarded) | ≈ 0.02 % of *responses* were clipped – corresponds to two orders of magnitude fewer usable samples but still higher overall efficiency |

A striking observation is that despite clipping *far more tokens* in GRPO (because every off‑policy token is removed), GSPO still learns faster. This confirms that noisy token‑level gradients are far less useful than clean sequence‑level signals.

### MoE specific benefit  

When training Mixture‑of‑Experts models with GRPO we needed an extra “Routing Replay” buffer to freeze expert activation patterns across updates—adding memory pressure and complicating distributed pipelines. With GSPO this extra machinery disappears entirely because only whole-sequence likelihoods are needed; expert routing changes do not destabilize `s_i`. Consequently:

* Training memory drop: ~15 % less per GPU.
* No extra communication for routing caches.
* Higher final performance on all benchmarks.

---

## 6. TL;DR – When should you switch to GSPO?  

* You are fine‑tuning a large language model (30 B+ parameters or a deep MoE).  
* Your RL objective gives a scalar reward at **the end of a generated answer**—the usual case for instruction following or code generation.  
* You see exploding variance in PPO/GRPO training curves or you have to resort to tricks like Routing Replay.  

In those settings simply replace the token‐level importance ratio with the length‐normalized sequence ratio `s_i`, keep the same group advantage computation and clipping logic—and you’ll get:

* More stable training without extra tricks,
* Faster convergence for the same amount of compute,
* Cleaner infrastructure because you can reuse inference‐engine likelihoods directly.

---

## 7. Closing thoughts  

GSPO’s elegance stems from respecting a fundamental statistical principle: *importance sampling must match what you are actually estimating.* The reward lives on whole sequences; correcting distribution shift at token granularity only adds noise. By re-aligning importance weights with sequence likelihoods—and by normalizing across length—the authors turned an unstable algorithm into a robust workhorse that powers today’s Qwen‑3 models.

If you’re building RL pipelines for next‑generation LLMs, give GSPO a try; the math is simple enough to drop into existing PPO frameworks while delivering real gains in stability and performance. Happy fine‑tuning!
