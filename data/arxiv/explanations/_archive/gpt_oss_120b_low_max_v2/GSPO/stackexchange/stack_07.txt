\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Can GSPO be applied to multi‑turn RL without modification?
Question:
The paper introduces GSPO‑token as a token‑level variant for multi‑turn settings where per‑token advantages may be needed. If I have a dialogue where each turn receives its own reward, should I use GSPO‑token with separate \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) for each turn, or can I still apply plain GSPO by aggregating turn rewards into a single sequence reward? What are the trade‑offs in terms of stability and credit assignment?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
If each turn of a dialogue receives its own reward (e.g. turn‑level feedback, turn‑wise user ratings, or intermediate scores), you should use **GSPO‑token** and assign a separate advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to each turn (or even to each token inside the turn).  Using plain **GSPO** is still possible – you can sum or average the turn rewards into a single scalar per‑response advantage – but you will lose fine‑grained credit assignment and may obtain a less stable learning signal when the rewards vary heavily across turns.

---

### Why the choice matters

#### 1.  What GSPO optimises

GSPO’s objective (Eq. (6) in the paper) treats the *entire* response $y_i$ as a single unit:

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\Big[\,\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\; \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta))\widehat A_i\big)\Big],
$$

where the advantage $\widehat A_i$ is computed from the **sequence‑level** reward
$r(x,y_i)$.  Hence every token in $y_i$ receives exactly the same weight
$s_i(\theta)$ (the length‑normalised sequence importance ratio).  The algorithm is deliberately designed to match the *unit of reward* (the whole sequence) with the *unit of importance sampling* (the whole sequence likelihood).  

If you collapse several turn‑level scores into a single number (e.g. sum, average, or discounted sum), GSPO will still be mathematically correct; the only difference is that the resulting $\widehat A_i$ no longer reflects where in the response the model did well or poorly.

#### 2.  What GSPO‑token adds

GSPO‑token introduces a token‑wise term $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ while **detaching** the sequence‑level factor $s_i(\theta)$ from its gradient:

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{sg}\!\big[s_i(\theta)\big]\;
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\operatorname{sg}\!\big[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\big]} .
$$

Because $\operatorname{sg}[\,]$ stops gradients, the numerical value of
$s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is still exactly $s_i(\theta)$; the only effect of the
formulation is that each token can now be paired with its own advantage
$\widehat A_{i,t}$.  In a multi‑turn dialogue you can set

$$
\widehat A_{i,t}
=
\begin{cases}
\text{advantage derived from turn 1 reward}, & t \in \text{turn 1},\\[4pt]
\text{advantage derived from turn 2 reward}, & t \in \text{turn 2},\\
\;\;\vdots &
\end{cases}
$$

so that tokens belonging to different turns receive different scaling factors.
When all $\widehat A_{i,t}$ are equal, GSPO‑token collapses back to plain GSPO,
as shown in Eq. (14–15).

#### 3.  Credit assignment

* **Plain GSPO**: credit (or blame) for a high‑reward response is spread uniformly over every token. If turn 2 was excellent but turn 1 was terrible, the uniform weighting may cause the model to reinforce some bad token predictions from turn 1 simply because they appear in an overall high‑scoring response.

* **GSPO‑token**: by giving each turn its own advantage, gradients arising from a good turn are amplified for its tokens while gradients from a bad turn are attenuated (or even reversed if a negative advantage is used). This aligns more closely with how humans think about multi‑turn interactions—reward at step k should primarily affect actions taken at step k.

#### 4.  Stability considerations

The paper’s stability analysis focuses on *variance of importance weights*. Token‑level importance weights in GRPO caused exploding variance because each token weight was treated independently. GSPO resolved this by using a **single sequence weight** $s_i(\theta)$, which varies much less across tokens and lengths.

GSPO‑token inherits this stability because it **reuses the same sequence weight for every token**, only differing in the attached advantages. The gradient expression (Eq. (15)) shows that every token’s gradient is multiplied by the *same* factor  
$s_i(\theta)= (\pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|}$,
so there is no extra variance introduced by per‑token importance ratios. The only source of extra variance comes from having multiple advantages $\widehat A_{i,t}$; however, these are usually low‑variance estimates because they are computed from observed scalar rewards (e.g., a turn score), not from a learned value network.

Consequently, **stability is essentially unchanged** between GSRO and GSRO‑token; you gain finer credit assignment without paying an additional variance penalty.

#### 5.  Practical trade‑offs

| Aspect | Plain GSPO (single advantage per response) | GSPO‑token (per‑turn/token advantage) |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Implementation** | Simpler: compute one $\widehat A_i$ per response | Slightly more bookkeeping: map each token (or turn) to its own $\widehat A_{i,t}$ |
| **Memory / Compute** | Same, because both use length‑normalised sequence ratio; extra advantage tensor is negligible | Same order; only the advantage tensor grows linearly with tokens |
| **Credit assignment** | Coarse – all tokens share reward signal | Fine – each turn can receive its own signal |
| **Stability** | Very stable (as shown empirically) | Equally stable; no extra variance from importance ratios |
| **When to prefer** | Rewards are naturally defined at whole‑response level (e.g., final task success, overall human rating) | Rewards are naturally defined per interaction step (e.g., turn‑wise ratings, delayed user feedback, multi‑objective scoring where different turns optimise different criteria) |

#### 6.  Recommended recipe for multi‑turn RL

1. **Collect per‑turn scores** $r_k(x,y^{(k)})$, where $k$ indexes turns.
2. **Aggregate if you truly want one scalar** (e.g., discounted sum) and feed it to plain GSPO; this works well when later turns dominate overall quality.
3. **Otherwise**, build a vector of advantages:
   $$
   \widehat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_{\text{turn}(t)}(x,y)-\mu_{\text{turn}(t)}}{\sigma_{\text{turn}(t)}},
   $$
   where $\mu_{\text{turn}(t)}$ and $\sigma_{\text{turn}(t)}$ are mean/std computed over the group for that particular turn.
4. Use **GSPO‑token** with those advantages; keep clipping ranges identical to those used for plain GSPO (the paper uses $3\times10^{-4}$/$4\times10^{-4}$), because clipping operates on the same sequence ratio.
5. Monitor *fraction of clipped responses* rather than clipped tokens—the paper shows that even with >90 % of tokens being clipped, GSPO still learns efficiently; you’ll see similar behaviour with GSPO‑token.

---

### Bottom line

- **Yes**, GSPO can be applied without modification by collapsing multi‑turn rewards into one scalar, but you will lose per‑turn credit assignment.
- **No**, you should not use plain GSPO *if* you care about distinguishing good versus bad turns; in that case switch to **GSRO‑token** and give each turn its own advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$.
- The stability guarantees proved for GSRO carry over to GSRO‑token because both share the same sequence importance ratio; thus the main trade‑off is between simplicity (plain GSRO) and finer granularity (GSRO‑token).
