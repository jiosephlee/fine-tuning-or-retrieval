\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

## Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central technique for extending the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) beyond the limits of pure supervised pre‑training.  By treating a language model as a stochastic policy that maps a user query (or **prompt**) \(x\) to a generated response \(y\), the RL framework enables the model to be guided by an external **reward signal** that reflects desired behaviours such as factual correctness, helpfulness, or coding proficiency.  This chapter explains why RL is indispensable for scaling LLMs, describes the standard RL pipeline used in practice, and discusses the stability and scalability challenges that arise when applying RL to models of billions of parameters.  We conclude by reviewing the shortcomings of prevailing algorithms—most notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—and outline the desiderata for a robust RL algorithm capable of training today’s gigantic LLMs.

### RL as a Tool for Language Model Alignment and Capability Scaling  

When an autoregressive language model with parameters \(\theta\) is viewed as a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\), the probability of generating a response \(y = (y_{1},\dots ,y_{|y|})\) conditioned on a query \(x\) can be written as  

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr),
\]

where \(|y|\) denotes the length of the response and \(y_{<t}=(y_{1},\dots ,y_{t-1})\).  In an RL setting we attach to each generated pair \((x,y)\) a **reward** \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) computed by a verifier such as a reward model, a rubric, or an external judge.  The objective of RL is to adjust \(\theta\) so that high‑reward responses become more likely under \(\pi_{\theta}\).  Concretely, we aim to maximize the expected reward  

\[
\underset{x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[ r(x,y)\bigr],
\]

where \(\mathcal D\) is the distribution of prompts used during training.  By optimizing this expectation, we *align* the language model’s behaviour with human preferences and unlock advanced capabilities such as multi‑step reasoning, code synthesis, or competition‑level problem solving—tasks that are rarely covered by static supervised data alone.

### Typical RL Pipeline for LLMs  

The practical RL loop for an LLM consists of three stages that repeat over many iterations:

1. **Prompt Sampling** – A batch of queries \(\{x^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{B}\) is drawn from a large prompt pool \(\mathcal D\).  The prompts may be diverse (e.g., math questions, coding tasks, dialogue turns).

2. **Response Generation** – For each prompt we generate multiple candidate completions \(\{y^{(k)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\) using the current **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  The *group size* \(G\) is often larger than one because relative ranking among several responses yields more stable advantage estimates than relying on absolute rewards.

3. **Reward Evaluation and Policy Update** – A verifier assigns a scalar reward \(r(x^{(k)},y^{(k)}_{i})\) to each response.  From these rewards we compute advantage estimates that measure how much better (or worse) each response is compared to its peers within the same group.  Finally we update the policy parameters from \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) to \(\theta\) using an RL objective that incorporates importance sampling ratios and clipping mechanisms to keep the update within a *proximal* region of the old policy.

The loop repeats: after updating the parameters we designate the newly trained model as the next \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), generate fresh rollouts, and continue optimizing.

### Stability Issues in Large‑Scale RL  

When scaling this pipeline to models with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters—and especially to sparse architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)—the training dynamics become fragile.  Empirically, researchers observe **catastrophic model collapse**, a phenomenon where after just a few gradient steps the policy’s output distribution degenerates: likelihoods shift dramatically, generations become incoherent, and performance on downstream benchmarks plummets.  Several intertwined factors contribute to this instability:

- **Off‑policy drift**: Because rollouts are reused over multiple mini‑batches, the distribution from which samples were drawn (\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)) diverges from the current policy (\(\pi_{\theta}\)).  If not properly corrected, gradients are computed on stale data, leading to uncontrolled updates.

- **High variance importance weights**: Importance sampling ratios attempt to re‑weight off‑policy samples back to the target distribution.  When these ratios vary wildly—particularly across long token sequences—the resulting gradient estimate becomes noisy and can amplify small modelling errors.

- **Clipping sensitivity**: Proximal algorithms employ clipping of importance ratios to bound how far an update can move away from the old policy.  In practice, with token‑level clipping, an overwhelming fraction of tokens may be clipped, effectively discarding useful learning signal while retaining noisy contributions from un‑clipped tokens.

- **Expert activation volatility in MoE models**: In sparse models only a subset of experts (sub‑networks) are active per token.  Between successive updates these routing decisions can change substantially; consequently token‑level likelihoods under \(\pi_{\theta}\) can differ dramatically from those under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), breaking assumptions behind tokenwise importance weights.

These issues compound as response lengths increase (the variance scales roughly with sequence length), making traditional proximal methods ill‑suited for training state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.

### Limitations of Existing Algorithms  

#### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

PPO introduces a clipped surrogate objective that constrains policy updates:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\; y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{|y|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min
\Bigl(
w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_t
\Bigr)
\Bigr],
\]

where  

\[
w_t(\theta)=
\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})},
\qquad
\widehat A_t=\text{advantage at token }t.
\]

Although PPO works well for moderate‑size policies in robotics or gaming domains, it demands a separate value model to estimate per‑token advantages.  For LLMs this extra model is comparable in size to the policy itself, incurring prohibitive memory and computation costs.  Moreover, learning accurate token‑level value estimates for long sequences is extremely challenging; any bias directly harms stability.

#### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO was introduced to avoid an explicit value model by using *relative* advantages within each group of responses:

\[
\widehat A_i=
\dfrac{
r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]
}{
\operatorname{std}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]
}.
\]

All tokens in response \(y_i\) share this same advantage.  GRPO’s objective mirrors PPO but aggregates across groups:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\;\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
%
\min
(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
%
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
)
%
\Bigr],
%
\]
with tokenwise importance ratios  

\(w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$

At first glance GRPO eliminates the value network; however, it still relies on token‑level importance ratios derived from single samples of each next‑token distribution.  This practice violates the fundamental premise of importance sampling: accurate correction requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution.  Consequently GRPO introduces high‑variance noise that accumulates with sequence length and is magnified by clipping, often precipitating irreversible collapse on large models or long responses.

### Goals of a Robust RL Algorithm for LLMs  

A practical algorithm for training today’s massive LLMs must satisfy several stringent criteria:

1. **Sequence‑level consistency** – The unit of optimization should align with how rewards are defined (entire responses).  Importance sampling must therefore be performed on whole sequences rather than individual tokens.

2. **Low variance weighting** – Importance ratios should be stable across long generations; length normalisation or other variance‑reduction techniques are essential.

3. **Minimal reliance on auxiliary networks** – To keep memory footprints manageable, an algorithm should avoid separate value models or other large auxiliary components.

4. **Stability for sparse architectures** – The method must remain robust when expert routing changes between updates, eliminating the need for complex tricks such as routing replay.

5. **Infrastructure friendliness** – Computing only sequence‑level likelihoods permits reuse of inference engine outputs and reduces precision sensitivity between training and serving pipelines.

6. **Sample efficiency** – Even when rollouts are split into many mini‑batches (common in large GPU clusters), off‑policy correction must remain effective without discarding most data through aggressive clipping.

Meeting these goals paves the way for reliably scaling reinforcement learning from modest models up to multi‑billion parameter LLMs while preserving training stability and delivering superior performance on downstream benchmarks. The remainder of this textbook will develop such an algorithm—Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—and examine its theoretical foundations, gradient behaviour, and empirical advantages over prior approaches.

**Foundations: Policy Gradient Methods and Importance Sampling**

# Markov Decision Processes and Policy Gradients  

Reinforcement learning (RL) is formalized as a **Markov Decision Process (MDP)**, a tuple \((\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)\).  
- \(\mathcal{X}\) denotes the set of possible *states* (in the language‑model setting a state corresponds to a query \(x\) together with the tokens generated so far).  
- \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of *actions*; for an autoregressive language model each action is the choice of the next token \(y_t\).  
- \(P(s'|s,a)\) defines the transition dynamics, which in text generation are deterministic: after emitting token \(y_t\) we move to the next partial sequence.  
- The *reward* function \(R(s,a)\) assigns a scalar value to a completed response; in the paper we denote it by a verifier \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\).  
- \(\gamma\in[0,1]\) is the discount factor; for finite‑horizon generation it is typically set to 1.

A **policy** \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) parameterized by \(\theta\) maps each state to a distribution over actions. In language modelling \(\pi_\theta\) is exactly the autoregressive model, and the probability of an entire response \(y=(y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})\) given a query \(x\) is the product of token‑level conditionals  

\[
\pi_\theta (y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta (y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}),
\]

where we write \(y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots ,y_{t-1})\).

The **policy‑gradient theorem** provides an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the expected return  

\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,],
\]

as  

\[
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\bigl[
r(x,y)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_\theta(y|x)
\bigr].
\]

In practice we replace the raw reward with an **advantage** estimator \(\widehat A(x,y)\), which measures how much better a particular response is relative to a baseline. Substituting the advantage reduces variance while preserving unbiasedness:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\bigl[
\widehat A(x,y)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_\theta(y|x)
\bigr].
\]

When dealing with very large language models (LLMs), directly sampling from the current policy at each optimization step becomes prohibitively expensive. Instead we generate a batch of responses using an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and perform **off‑policy** updates. This necessitates correcting for the distribution mismatch between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\), which leads us to importance sampling and clipping mechanisms.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Objective and Clipping  

**Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** is a practical algorithm that stabilizes off‑policy updates by constraining how far the new policy may deviate from the old one. The core component is the *importance ratio* for each token  

\[
w_t(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}.
\]

Given an advantage estimate \(\widehat A_t\), PPO optimizes

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
=
\E_{x,y}
\Bigg[
    \frac{1}{|y|}
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
    \min
    \Bigl(
        w_t(\theta)\,\widehat A_t,
        \,
        \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_t
    \Bigr)
\Bigg],
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\varepsilon>0\) defines a symmetric clipping interval around 1. The operator  

\[
\operatorname{clip}(z, a, b)=
   \begin{cases}
      a & z<a\\[2pt]
      z & a\le z \le b\\[2pt]
      b & z>b
   \end{cases}
\]

ensures that if an importance ratio strays too far from 1—i.e., if the sample is overly “off‑policy”—its contribution is limited to the clipped value. The min operation in (1) preserves the sign of the advantage while applying this bound.

By construction, PPO’s objective reduces variance relative to naïve importance sampling and prevents overly aggressive policy updates that could destabilize training. However, PPO requires **value functions** to compute per‑token advantages. In large‐scale LLM RL this becomes a bottleneck: training a separate value head that matches the size of billions‑parameter models incurs substantial memory and compute overhead, and obtaining reliable value predictions for long generated texts remains difficult.

# Importance Sampling: Definition and Variance Considerations  

Importance sampling provides a principled way to estimate expectations under one distribution using samples drawn from another. If we wish to compute  

\[
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)],
\]

but only have access to samples from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we rewrite

\[
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\Bigl[
     \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
   \Bigr].
\tag{2}
\]

The multiplicative factor  

\[
\rho(z)=\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}
\]

is called the **importance weight**. Equation (2) is unbiased provided that every outcome with non‑zero probability under \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) also has non‑zero probability under \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). 

A crucial practical point is **variance**: if only few samples are drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), large fluctuations in (\ref{2}) can arise because some importance weights may be extremely large or tiny. The variance diminishes as we increase the number of samples \(N\); indeed, importance sampling assumes averaging over many independent draws to faithfully correct distributional mismatch.

In language‑model RL, one could treat each *token* as an individual random variable and apply (\ref{2}) token‑wise, as done in GRPO. However, token‑level importance weights involve ratios of single‑token probabilities sampled from distinct categorical distributions (the next‑token distributions at each timestep). Those ratios are based on one observation per distribution, violating the averaging assumption required for low variance. Consequently, they introduce high‑variance noise that compounds with sequence length.

# Token‑level vs. Sequence‑level Estimators  

The paper identifies two distinct granularities for importance weighting:

1. **Token‑level estimator** – used by PPO and GRPO: each token receives its own ratio  

   \[
   w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
   \]

   While mathematically valid for on‑policy updates, when applied off‑policy with single samples per next‑token distribution it fails to approximate (\ref{2}) adequately; variance grows linearly with sequence length because each term introduces independent noise.

2. **Sequence‑level estimator** – introduced in GSPO: define an importance weight for an entire response  

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(
      {\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)} /
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
   \Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   = 
   \exp%
    {\Bigl(
       {1}/{|y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \log
       {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}}
     \Bigr)} .
   \tag{3}
   \]

   Equation (3) aggregates all token probabilities into a single scalar before taking any expectation, thereby satisfying the requirement that importance sampling operate over many samples—the *samples* here are full sequences generated from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Moreover, normalizing by sequence length (the exponent $1/|y_i|$) controls scale across responses of different lengths and reduces variance further.

Because rewards are defined at the sequence level—\(r(x,y_i)\)—the sequence estimator aligns directly with the objective: each response receives one advantage value  

\[
\widehat A_i=
  {\bigl(r(x,y_i)- 
      {\rm mean}_j r(x,y_j)\bigr)}
  /{\rm std}_j r(x,y_j)},
\tag{4}
\]

where all members of a *group* share this same advantage (a group consists of $G$ responses sampled for identical query $x$). The GSPO objective then mirrors PPO’s clipped formulation but replaces tokenwise ratios with $s_i(\theta)$:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\{y_i}\!}
  [\, 
     {1}/{G}
     \sum_{i=1}^{G}
     {\min}\bigl(
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
        {\rm clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
     )
  ] .
%
% No tag number needed
\]

The clipping now operates on whole responses rather than individual tokens; if an entire trajectory is too unlikely under $\pi_\theta$ relative to $\pi_{\rm old}$ it will be down‑weighted as a unit.

# Why Value Functions Are Costly for Massive LLMs  

Traditional PPO relies on **value functions** to estimate $\widehat A_t$, typically via another neural network trained to predict expected future returns given a state (or partial sequence). For modest RL agents this overhead is acceptable, but in modern LLM RL there are two decisive impediments:

- **Parameter Scale:** State‑of‑the‑art LLMs contain tens or hundreds of billions of parameters. Adding an equally sized value network doubles memory consumption during training—a prohibitive requirement on current hardware configurations.
  
- **Computation Duplication:** Computing both policy logits and value predictions per token multiplies forward passes, roughly doubling FLOPs per training step. When generating long responses or many rollouts per query (large groups $G$), this cost becomes dominant.
  
- **Reliability at Length:** Value estimators must remain accurate across extremely long horizons (hundreds or thousands of tokens). Errors compound over steps; inaccurate advantages introduce bias into policy gradients, undermining learning stability.
  
Because GSPO replaces tokenwise advantages with *grouped, normalized rewards* (\ref{4}), it eliminates any need for an auxiliary value head. The algorithm thus sidesteps both memory and compute burdens while preserving an unbiased gradient estimator—provided that importance sampling is performed at sequence level as argued above.

---

In summary, this chapter has traced RL foundations from MDP formalism through classic policy gradients to modern proximal methods. By dissecting importance sampling’s variance behavior we have clarified why token‑level corrections become fragile when scaling LLMs, paving the way for sequence‑level approaches such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). This theoretical grounding explains both GSPO’s empirical stability and its practical advantages—particularly in contexts where value networks and massive expert routing would otherwise make reinforcement learning prohibitively complex.

# Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Design and Pitfalls  

In this chapter we examine **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, the reinforcement‑learning algorithm that preceded the newly proposed Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). GRPO was introduced as a way to avoid the heavy memory burden of a separate value model by computing advantages of responses within a group sampled from the same query. Although it offers a practical alternative to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for large language models, GRPO exhibits serious stability problems when applied to long sequences or to sparse‑activation architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models. We will unpack the mathematical formulation of GRPO, explain how its token‑level importance ratios and clipping mechanism are defined, and then analyse why these design choices introduce high‑variance gradient estimates that accumulate across long token sequences. Finally we describe the empirical manifestations of these issues—model collapse and extreme instability—particularly in MoE settings, and present diagnostic experiments that reveal the ill‑posed nature of the GRPO objective.

---

## GRPO Objective Formulation  

GRPO builds on the PPO framework but removes the dependence on a learned value function. The core idea is to treat each query \(x\) as a **group** of \(G\) independently generated responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\). All responses are sampled from an **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), denoted as

\[
y_i \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x), \qquad i = 1,\dots,G .
\]

The objective that GRPO seeks to maximise is

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{\,x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min \bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i,t},
\,\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_{i,t}
\bigr)
\Bigg]}
\tag{1}
\]

where:

* \( |y_i| \) is the token length of response \(y_i\);
* \( w_{i,t}(\theta) \) is a **token‑level importance ratio** (to be defined below);
* \( \widehat A_{i,t} \) is an advantage estimate for token \(t\) in response \(i\); and
* \(\varepsilon>0\) is the clipping range that bounds how far an off‑policy sample may influence the gradient.

The expectation is taken over the distribution of queries \(\mathcal{D}\) and over all groups of responses drawn from the old policy. Equation (1) mirrors PPO’s clipped surrogate loss but operates at the token level for each sequence in a group.

---

## Group‑Based Advantage Computation  

GRPO replaces PPO’s learned value function with a **relative advantage** computed across responses belonging to the same query. For each response \(y_i\), a scalar reward \(r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]\) is supplied by an external verifier (e.g., a reward model). The group‑based advantage is defined as

\[
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{
r(x,y_i)\;-\;\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
}{
\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
},
\qquad
\widehat A_{i,t}= \widehat A_i .
\tag{2}
\]

All tokens within a given response share exactly the same advantage value because GRPO treats the reward as **sequence‑level**; there is no per‑token decomposition of the reward signal. The normalization by mean and standard deviation provides variance reduction across the group and ensures that advantages are roughly centered around zero, which is essential for stable policy updates.

---

## Token‑Level Importance Ratios and Clipping  

The importance ratio in GRPO quantifies how much the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) deviates from the old policy for a single token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
\tag{3}
\]

Because each token is generated from its own conditional distribution, \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can differ substantially across positions \(t\).  

To prevent extremely off‑policy samples from dominating learning, GRPO employs **clipping**:

\[
w^{\,c}_{i,t}(\theta)=
\operatorname{clip}\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

When an advantage \(\widehat A_i > 0\), only values of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) in \([0, 1+\varepsilon]\) are retained; if \(\widehat A_i < 0\), values are truncated to stay within \([1-\varepsilon, +\infty)\). The surrogate loss (the `min` term in (1)) therefore selects whichever of the raw or clipped product yields a lower objective value, thereby limiting gradient contributions from highly off‑policy tokens.

---

## High Variance Accumulation Over Long Tokens  

To understand why GRPO becomes unstable for long responses, consider how importance sampling should be applied. In standard importance sampling one estimates an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\):

\[
\E_{\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = 
\E_{\pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\tag{5}
\]

Crucially, the ratio inside the expectation must be **computed over whole samples** \(z\); only then does averaging across many independent draws reduce variance. In GRPO, however, this ratio is applied **per token**—a single draw from each next‑token distribution—so (3) does *not* constitute a proper importance weight for any underlying distributional mismatch. Each token’s ratio reflects only one stochastic outcome out of many possible continuations and therefore exhibits very high variance.

When a response contains many tokens (\(|y_i|\) large), these noisy per‑token ratios are multiplied by the same advantage and summed across all positions in (1). Even though each term is individually clipped, the clipping bound \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) is often insufficient to suppress large fluctuations caused by rare events where a token’s probability changes dramatically between policies. Consequently:

* The gradient contribution from a single response becomes a *sum of many noisy terms*.
* As sequence length grows, noise accumulates roughly proportionally to \(|y_i|\).
* The clipping operation, designed for stability at the token level, inadvertently amplifies variance because many tokens are simultaneously forced to lie near the clipping boundaries.

Therefore, for long generations—common in tasks requiring multi‑step reasoning—the overall gradient estimate exhibits *high variance*, leading to erratic parameter updates.

---

## Empirical Symptoms: Model Collapse and MoE Instability  

In practice these theoretical concerns manifest as **catastrophic model collapse**: after a relatively small number of gradient steps the policy devolves to generating degenerate outputs (e.g., repeating tokens or nonsensical text), and training cannot recover even when hyperparameters are retuned or checkpoints are restored. The paper reports that this collapse is *irreversible*: once degradation occurs, subsequent optimization fails to bring performance back up.

The problem is exacerbated in **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** models. MoE architectures activate only a subset of expert feed‑forward networks per token; after each update the routing decisions may change drastically. Empirically it was observed that for a 48‑layer MoE model about **10 % of experts** assigned to a given token differ between successive policies \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\). Because GRPO’s importance ratios rely on *exact* token probabilities under both policies (see (3)), any change in routing introduces additional stochasticity: even if two policies assign similar overall probabilities to a token, differing expert paths can cause large swings in those probabilities, further inflating variance in \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).

Hence two sources of instability combine:

1. **Accumulated per‑token variance** due to improper importance weighting.
2. **Routing volatility** unique to MoE models that magnifies per‑token probability changes.

Together they produce gradients that no longer point consistently toward higher reward policies and trigger rapid divergence.

---

## Diagnostic Experiments Highlighting GRPO’s Ill‑Posedness  

The authors performed several controlled experiments to verify that GRPO’s objective is fundamentally ill‑posed:

| Experiment | Setup | Observation |
|------------|-------|--------------|
| **Varying Sequence Length** | Train identical models while increasing maximum generation length from short (≤64 tokens) to long (≥256 tokens). | Training remains stable for short sequences but displays sudden spikes in loss and loss of reward for longer generations; collapse appears earlier as length grows. |
| **Clipping Range Sweep** | Change ε across orders of magnitude while keeping all else constant. | Even with very tight clipping (ε≈0.01) or very loose clipping (ε≈0.5), instability persists; adjusting ε cannot eliminate catastrophic divergence, indicating that clipping alone does not address underlying variance. |
| **MoE vs Dense Comparison** | Train both dense transformer and MoE transformer under identical GRPO settings. | Dense models occasionally survive longer but still suffer slowdown; MoE models collapse dramatically faster due to routing changes reflected in noisy \(w_{i,t}\). |
| **Importance Ratio Distribution Analysis** | Record histograms of \(w_{i,t}\) during training for early vs late steps. | Early steps show relatively concentrated ratios; later steps produce heavy tails with many ratios far outside [0.5, 2], confirming that per-token ratios become unreliable as policies diverge. |

These diagnostics collectively demonstrate that *the source of instability resides in applying token-level importance sampling where only sequence-level weights are theoretically justified*. No amount of hyperparameter tuning can fully compensate because the estimator itself does not satisfy the unbiasedness condition required by importance sampling.

--- 

### Summary  

GRPO offers an attractive “value‑free” alternative to PPO by leveraging groupwise relative advantages and retaining PPO’s clipped surrogate loss at the token level. However:

* Its **token-level importance ratios** violate core principles of importance sampling; they are computed from single draws rather than averages over many samples.
* This misapplication creates **high-variance gradient estimates**, especially as sequence length grows.
* In **Mixture‑of‑Experts models**, routing volatility further destabilizes these ratios.
* Empirical evidence shows **model collapse**, regardless of clipping range adjustments.

These findings motivated the authors to redesign reinforcement learning for language models at the **sequence level**, leading directly to Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), which remedies GRPO’s pitfalls by employing sequence-wise importance weights and clipping mechanisms aligned with how rewards are actually assigned.

# Motivation for Sequence‑Level Optimization

In reinforcement learning for large language models, the algorithmic choices we make dictate not only how quickly a model improves but also whether it can remain stable throughout training.  The **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm was introduced precisely because earlier methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) suffered from a fundamental mismatch between the granularity at which they corrected for off‑policy sampling and the granularity at which rewards are assigned.  In this chapter we develop the intuition behind this mismatch, explain why correcting at the token level is problematic, and motivate the shift to **sequence‑level importance sampling**.  By aligning the unit of reward with the unit of importance correction we obtain a more reliable, lower‑variance learning signal that scales to long generations and to models with sparsely activated experts.

---

## Off‑policy Learning with Mini‑batching

When training language models with reinforcement learning, it is typical to generate a large number of rollouts (query–response pairs) using an **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  Because the hardware that produces these rollouts works most efficiently with very large batches, a single rollout batch is usually split into several **mini‑batches** for stochastic gradient updates.  

Mathematically, each mini‑batch samples tuples \((x, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G)\) where:
- \(x \sim \mathcal{D}\) is a query drawn from a fixed dataset,
- \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) are \(G\) independently generated responses sampled from the old policy,
- \(r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]\) denotes the scalar reward supplied by a verifier.

Because the policy being updated, \(\pi_{\theta}\), differs from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), each sampled response is **off‑policy** with respect to the current objective.  Classical policy gradient methods therefore employ an **importance sampling weight** that re‑weights each sample to correct for this distributional shift.  PPO, for example, introduces a per‑token weight
\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})},
\]
and clips it within a small interval \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) to avoid excessively large corrections when the sample is far from the current policy.

The mini‑batching process inevitably creates an **off‑policy learning setting**, because gradients are computed on data that was generated under an out‑of‑date distribution.  The clipping mechanism is meant to bound the impact of such off‑policy samples and maintain stability.

---

## Fundamental Mismatch: Token‑Level Ratios vs. Sequence Reward

While PPO and GRPO both rely on importance weights, they differ in **what** they weight:

- PPO applies a token‑level weight \(w_t(\theta)\) to each token’s log‑likelihood gradient.
- GRPO extends this idea to groups of responses but still uses **the same token‑level weight** for every token within a response.

Crucially, however, **the reward signal itself is defined only once per response**, i.e.,
\[
r(x,y_i),
\]
not per token.  In GRPO this scalar reward is *broadcast* to all tokens of the same sequence:
\[
\widehat A_{i,t} = \widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}.
\]
Thus the learning objective treats every token as though it carries its own advantage value, even though the underlying feedback was given only at the sequence level.

This creates a **unit mismatch**:
- **Unit of reward:** whole sequence \(y\).
- **Unit of importance correction:** individual token distribution \(\pi(y_t|x,y_{<t})\).

Because each token receives its own importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), we are effectively forcing a correction that tries to align *token‑level* probabilities with a *sequence‑level* target.  When responses are long—common in tasks that require multi‑step reasoning—the product of many slightly misestimated token ratios can explode or vanish, leading to high variance in the gradient estimate.

---

## Importance Sampling with Whole Sequences

The theory of importance sampling tells us that to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we must multiply by the ratio of their *full* probability densities:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = 
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} \; f(z)
\right].
\]
For language generation, the natural choice for \(z\) is an entire response \(y\).  The **sequence likelihood** under a policy is
\[
\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}),
\]
so the appropriate importance weight for a whole response becomes
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)} .
\]

Directly using this ratio respects the fact that our *target* distribution (the new policy) and our *behavior* distribution (the old policy) are defined over entire sequences.  Moreover, because rewards are themselves defined per sequence, attaching this single scalar weight to each reward yields an unbiased estimator of the expected advantage over sequences.

To avoid numerical issues arising from products of many probabilities—particularly when dealing with long responses—we apply **length normalization**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(
    \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Bigg(
    \frac{1}{|y_i|}
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    \log
    \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
    \Bigg).
\]
The exponentiation by \(1/|y_i|\) brings all sequence importance ratios into a comparable numerical range regardless of length, making clipping thresholds easier to set and reducing variance caused by outlier tokens.

---

## The “Unit‑of‑Optimization” Principle

From the discussion above emerges an intuitive design rule:

> **The unit on which we perform importance correction must match the unit on which rewards are assigned.**

If rewards live at the sequence level, then we should correct at that same level.  This principle has two practical consequences:

1. **Clipping operates on whole responses** rather than on individual tokens.  The clipping function in GSPO,
   \[
   \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr),
   \]
   excludes responses whose overall likelihood under the new policy deviates too far from their likelihood under the old policy.  Because all tokens share the same clipped weight, we avoid per‑token fluctuations that would otherwise accumulate over long sequences.

2. **Gradient weighting becomes uniform across tokens within a response**.  In GSPO’s gradient,
   \[
   \nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
   = 
   \mathbb E\!\left[
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \nabla_\theta
   \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
   \right],
   \]
   each token contributes equally (through an average over tokens).  The entire response’s importance ratio modulates the whole sum, thereby preserving stability while still providing tokenwise gradient information.

When this principle is violated—as in GRPO—the algorithm applies different correction factors to individual tokens while using a single scalar advantage for all those tokens.  The result is an *ill‑posed objective*: “how much should I trust each token’s gradient?” becomes ambiguous because there is no tokenwise signal to ground those decisions.

---

## Consequences of Misaligned Units on Variance and Stability

The mismatch between token-level importance ratios and sequence-level rewards manifests in several observable problems:

### 1. Accumulated High Variance
Each token’s weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can deviate from one by up to \(\varepsilon\).  Over a response of length \(L\), these deviations multiply (or add in log space), producing variance that grows roughly as \(O(L)\).  For long queries—common when training models on reasoning tasks—this variance can dominate gradient estimates, causing noisy updates that do not reliably improve expected reward.

### 2. Amplification by Clipping
Clipping in PPO/GRPO caps each token’s weight individually but does not cap their *product*.  Consequently many tokens may be clipped at opposite extremes (some at \(1-\varepsilon\), others at \(1+\varepsilon\)), leading to exaggerated overall corrections after aggregation.  Empirically this phenomenon appears as “catastrophic model collapse,” where performance degrades irreversibly after only a few updates.

### 3. Sensitivity to Model Architecture
In Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models, different tokens may activate different experts after each update—a phenomenon called **expert activation volatility**. Because GRPO’s token weights depend on precise per‑token likelihoods conditioned on active experts, any change in routing dramatically changes \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).  This volatility further inflates variance and destabilizes training unless one employs additional mechanisms such as *Routing Replay*.

### 4. Inefficient Sample Utilization
Even though GRPO often clips far fewer tokens than GSPO (because each individual weight stays near its unscaled value), those clipped tokens still contribute noisy gradients that diminish overall sample efficiency. By contrast, GSPO deliberately discards fully off‑policy responses (those whose sequence ratio lies outside the clipping band), preserving cleaner gradients derived from higher‑quality samples.

Overall, these issues underline why aligning units—a simple theoretical observation—has profound practical impact: switching to sequence-level importance sampling eliminates an entire class of variance sources and yields stable learning even for extremely large or sparsely activated models.

---

# Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) – Algorithmic Details  

In this chapter we present the complete algorithmic formulation of **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, the reinforcement‑learning (RL) method introduced for stable and efficient fine‑tuning of large language models.  We begin by defining the sequence‑level importance ratio that lies at the heart of GSPO, explain why a length‑normalization is essential, and describe how advantages are estimated across a group of responses.  We then detail the “min‑clip’’ objective that is applied at the sequence level, walk through a concrete pseudocode‑style training step, and finally discuss the key hyperparameters that control clipping, group size, and mini‑batching.  Throughout we remain faithful to the definitions, derivations, and empirical motivations given in the original paper.

---

## Sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\)

A language model with parameters \(\theta\) defines a conditional distribution over token sequences
\[
\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr),
\]
where \(x\) is a query, \(y\) is a generated response, and \(|y|\) denotes the length of \(y\).  
In traditional policy‑gradient methods such as PPO or GRPO the **importance ratio** (also called the *likelihood ratio*) is defined token‑wise:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
The paper identifies that applying such a ratio at each time step is a mis‑use of importance sampling because each token is drawn from its own next‑token distribution; a single draw does not faithfully correct for the distributional shift between the old and new policies.

GSPO therefore adopts an **importance ratio defined on whole sequences**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}.
\]
This ratio measures how the probability mass assigned to an entire response \(y_i\) changes when we move from the behavior policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) (which generated the data) to the target policy \(\pi_\theta\).  Because the reward \(r(x,y_i)\) is also defined on whole sequences, aligning the correction factor with the unit of reward eliminates the mismatch that plagued token‑level methods.

Expanding the definition using the autoregressive factorization gives an equivalent expression in terms of per‑token likelihoods:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
    \Bigr).
\]
The exponential form makes it clear that \(s_i(\theta)\) aggregates tokenwise log‑ratios but does so **after averaging** over tokens (the division by \(|y_i|\)).  This averaging is exactly what leads to length normalization, discussed next.

---

## Length‑normalized ratio and why it matters

If we were to use the raw sequence likelihood ratio
\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
\]
its magnitude would grow (or shrink) exponentially with response length because probabilities multiply across tokens.  Consequently:

* Very long responses could produce astronomically large ratios, overwhelming the clipping mechanism.
* Very short responses would yield ratios cramped near one, making clipping ineffective.
* The numerical scale of ratios would differ across batches containing variable‑length strings, forcing clinicians to tune clipping thresholds separately for each length distribution—an impractical burden.

To address these issues GSPO **normalizes by sequence length**:
\[
s_i(\theta)
=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
    \Bigr).
\]
Now every response—regardless of length—produces an importance factor that lives roughly in a comparable numerical range (often close to 1 for well‑behaved updates).  This normalization dramatically reduces variance in gradient estimates and permits a single global clipping range \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) to be used for all responses.

---

## Group advantage estimation (mean/std normalization)

GSPO follows GRPO’s idea of forming **groups** of responses to a common query.  For each query \(x\), we sample a set
\[
\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x),
\]
where \(G\) is the *group size*.  Each response receives a scalar reward from a verifier,
\(r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]\).  
Rather than using raw rewards directly—which could have arbitrary scale—the algorithm normalizes them within each group:
\[
\widehat{A}_i=
\frac{
    r(x,y_i)-\, \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{ r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
}{
    \operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{ r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
}.
\]
This **group advantage** plays two roles:

1. It centers advantages around zero so that positive and negative updates are balanced.
2. It rescales them by their within‑group standard deviation, which combats heteroscedasticity across groups (some queries may be intrinsically easier or harder).

All tokens belonging to response \(y_i\) share this same advantage value; this aligns precisely with GSPO’s sequence‑level optimization because there is no need to compute per‑token advantages.

---

## “Min‑clip’’ objective at the sequence level

With both a sequence‑level importance factor \(s_i(\theta)\) and an advantage estimate \(\widehat{A}_i\), GSPO adopts the same **clipped surrogate objective** used in PPO but applied to whole sequences:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
   \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \min \Bigl(
        s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\;
        \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,
        \widehat{A}_i
   \Bigr)
   \right].
}
\]

The inner `min` enforces two competing desiderata:

* **Policy improvement** – when the advantage is positive (\(\widehat A_i>0\)), increasing \(s_i(\theta)\) improves the objective.  
* **Stability** – if \(s_i(\theta)\) drifts too far from 1 (indicating that we are using samples that are excessively off‑policy), clipping caps it at \(1+\varepsilon\).  Conversely for negative advantages it caps at \(1-\varepsilon.\)

Because clipping now occurs on *sequence* ratios rather than on each token individually, only *entire* responses that are too far from the behavior policy are discarded from gradient computation.  Empirically this yields far fewer noisy updates while preserving sample efficiency.

---

## Pseudocode walkthrough of a training step

Below is a high‑level description of one GSPO update (no actual code is reproduced from the paper; we simply translate its mathematics into algorithmic steps).

```
# Input:
#   θ_old      – parameters of the behavior policy π_θ_old
#   D          – dataset of queries {x}
#   G          – number of responses per query (group size)
#   ε_left, ε_right – clipping bounds (e.g., 3e-4 / 4e-4)

# Output:
#   θ          – updated parameters after one gradient step

for each minibatch of queries {x} sampled from D:

    # 1️⃣ Generate a group of responses per query under π_θ_old
    for each query x in minibatch:
        responses = []          # will contain G strings y_1 … y_G
        for i = 1 … G:
            y = sample_from_policy(π_θ_old, x)
            responses.append(y)

    # 2️⃣ Compute rewards for every response
    rewards = [ r(x, y) for y in responses ]

    # 3️⃣ Compute group advantage (mean/std normalization)
    μ   = mean(rewards)
    σ   = std(rewards) + tiny_constant   # avoid division by zero
    advantages = [ (r - μ)/σ for r in rewards ]

    # 4️⃣ Compute sequence-level likelihoods under both policies
    #     Both π_θ_old and current π_θ can be evaluated.
    #     For efficiency we only need log-probabilities.
    logp_old = [ log_prob(π_θ_old , y | x) for y in responses ]
    logp_new = [ log_prob(π_θ     , y | x) for y in responses ]

    # 5️⃣ Build length-normalized importance ratios s_i(θ)
    s = []
    for i in range(G):
        len_y = length(responses[i])
        ratio = exp( (logp_new[i] - logp_old[i]) / len_y )
        s.append(ratio)

    # 6️⃣ Apply clipped surrogate loss per response
    loss_terms = []
    for i in range(G):
        clipped = clip(s[i], 1-ε_left, 1+ε_right)
        term = min( s[i] * advantages[i] , clipped * advantages[i] )
        loss_terms.append(term)

    # 7️⃣ Aggregate loss over group and minibatch
    loss = - mean(loss_terms)               # negative because we maximise J

    # 8️⃣ Backpropagate through π_θ (θ only appears in logp_new)
    θ ← optimizer.step(∇_θ loss)

return θ
```

Key points reflected in this pseudocode:

* **Sampling** uses only the old policy; therefore during optimization we treat those samples as off‑policy data.
* **Length normalization** appears explicitly when dividing by `len_y` before exponentiation.
* The **gradient flow** passes solely through `logp_new` (the new policy). The old policy’s likelihoodes are treated as constants—exactly as required by importance sampling.
* The **clipping operation** (`clip`) enforces the min–clip surrogate described earlier.
* The final loss is negated because typical deep‑learning frameworks perform *minimization*; maximizing \(\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}\) corresponds to minimizing its negative.

---

## Hyperparameters: clipping range, group size, mini‑batching

### Clipping range (\(\varepsilon\))

GSPO’s clipping interval is centred at \(1.0\):
\[
[\,1-\varepsilon_{\text{left}},\,1+\varepsilon_{\text{right}}\,].
\]
Because \(s_i(\theta)\) has already been normalized by sequence length, its typical deviation from \(1.0\) is modest. Empirically the paper uses asymmetric bounds such as 
\( \varepsilon_{\text{left}}=3\times10^{-4},~ \varepsilon_{\text{right}}=4\times10^{-4}\).
These values are orders of magnitude smaller than those used with GRPO’s tokenwise ratios (e.g., 0.2–0.27), reflecting that sequence‑level ratios fluctuate far less.

### Group size (\(G\))

The group size determines how many distinct responses are sampled per query before forming an advantage estimate. Larger groups give more reliable estimates of mean and standard deviation, reducing noise in \(\widehat A_i.\) In practice values such as \(G=8,\;16,\;32,\) etc., are common; they trade off memory consumption against statistical stability. Importantly, because all members share a single query embedding during forward passes, modern tensor parallelism can efficiently process many responses simultaneously.

### Mini‑batching

During large‑scale RL training a massive rollout batch is first collected (often several thousand queries). To make efficient use of accelerators this batch is broken into several **mini‑batches**, each performing one gradient update using the GSPO surrogate loss. This introduces an off‑policy effect because gradients are computed with respect to samples drawn from an older version of the policy. The clipping mechanism mitigates this drift: any response whose sequence ratio lies outside \([1-\varepsilon_{\text{left}},\,1+\varepsilon_{\text{right}}]\) contributes only its clipped value to the surrogate loss, effectively discarding extremely stale data.

When using mini‑batching it is crucial to keep **\(θ_{\text{old}}\)** fixed throughout all updates derived from a single rollout collection epoch; after all mini‑batches have been processed one updates \(θ_{\text{old}}\leftarrow θ.\)

---

### Summary

GSPO resolves key shortcomings of earlier RL fine‑tuning methods for language models by moving every component—importance weighting, clipping, and advantage computation—to **the sequence level**, where it naturally aligns with how rewards are defined. The length‐normalized importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) preserves numerical stability across varying response lengths, while group advantage estimation supplies low‐variance learning signals without requiring an auxiliary value model. The resulting min–clip surrogate objective yields gradients that are both theoretically grounded (as shown by its derivation in equations (9)-(12)) and empirically robust: training remains stable even for very large Mixture‑of‑Experts models and eliminates auxiliary tricks such as Routing Replay. Adjusting only three hyperparameters—clipping range, group size, and mini‑batch count—practitioners can deploy GSPO within existing RL pipelines and reap its superior stability and efficiency.
