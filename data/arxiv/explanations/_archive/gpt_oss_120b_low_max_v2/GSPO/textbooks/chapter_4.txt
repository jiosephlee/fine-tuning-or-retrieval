\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: # Motivation for Sequence‑Level Optimization

In reinforcement learning for large language models, the algorithmic choices we make dictate not only how quickly a model improves but also whether it can remain stable throughout training.  The **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm was introduced precisely because earlier methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) suffered from a fundamental mismatch between the granularity at which they corrected for off‑policy sampling and the granularity at which rewards are assigned.  In this chapter we develop the intuition behind this mismatch, explain why correcting at the token level is problematic, and motivate the shift to **sequence‑level importance sampling**.  By aligning the unit of reward with the unit of importance correction we obtain a more reliable, lower‑variance learning signal that scales to long generations and to models with sparsely activated experts.

---

## Off‑policy Learning with Mini‑batching

When training language models with reinforcement learning, it is typical to generate a large number of rollouts (query–response pairs) using an **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  Because the hardware that produces these rollouts works most efficiently with very large batches, a single rollout batch is usually split into several **mini‑batches** for stochastic gradient updates.  

Mathematically, each mini‑batch samples tuples \((x, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G)\) where:
- \(x \sim \mathcal{D}\) is a query drawn from a fixed dataset,
- \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) are \(G\) independently generated responses sampled from the old policy,
- \(r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]\) denotes the scalar reward supplied by a verifier.

Because the policy being updated, \(\pi_{\theta}\), differs from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), each sampled response is **off‑policy** with respect to the current objective.  Classical policy gradient methods therefore employ an **importance sampling weight** that re‑weights each sample to correct for this distributional shift.  PPO, for example, introduces a per‑token weight
\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})},
\]
and clips it within a small interval \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) to avoid excessively large corrections when the sample is far from the current policy.

The mini‑batching process inevitably creates an **off‑policy learning setting**, because gradients are computed on data that was generated under an out‑of‑date distribution.  The clipping mechanism is meant to bound the impact of such off‑policy samples and maintain stability.

---

## Fundamental Mismatch: Token‑Level Ratios vs. Sequence Reward

While PPO and GRPO both rely on importance weights, they differ in **what** they weight:

- PPO applies a token‑level weight \(w_t(\theta)\) to each token’s log‑likelihood gradient.
- GRPO extends this idea to groups of responses but still uses **the same token‑level weight** for every token within a response.

Crucially, however, **the reward signal itself is defined only once per response**, i.e.,
\[
r(x,y_i),
\]
not per token.  In GRPO this scalar reward is *broadcast* to all tokens of the same sequence:
\[
\widehat A_{i,t} = \widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}.
\]
Thus the learning objective treats every token as though it carries its own advantage value, even though the underlying feedback was given only at the sequence level.

This creates a **unit mismatch**:
- **Unit of reward:** whole sequence \(y\).
- **Unit of importance correction:** individual token distribution \(\pi(y_t|x,y_{<t})\).

Because each token receives its own importance ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), we are effectively forcing a correction that tries to align *token‑level* probabilities with a *sequence‑level* target.  When responses are long—common in tasks that require multi‑step reasoning—the product of many slightly misestimated token ratios can explode or vanish, leading to high variance in the gradient estimate.

---

## Importance Sampling with Whole Sequences

The theory of importance sampling tells us that to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we must multiply by the ratio of their *full* probability densities:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = 
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} \; f(z)
\right].
\]
For language generation, the natural choice for \(z\) is an entire response \(y\).  The **sequence likelihood** under a policy is
\[
\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}),
\]
so the appropriate importance weight for a whole response becomes
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)} .
\]

Directly using this ratio respects the fact that our *target* distribution (the new policy) and our *behavior* distribution (the old policy) are defined over entire sequences.  Moreover, because rewards are themselves defined per sequence, attaching this single scalar weight to each reward yields an unbiased estimator of the expected advantage over sequences.

To avoid numerical issues arising from products of many probabilities—particularly when dealing with long responses—we apply **length normalization**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(
    \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Bigg(
    \frac{1}{|y_i|}
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    \log
    \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
    \Bigg).
\]
The exponentiation by \(1/|y_i|\) brings all sequence importance ratios into a comparable numerical range regardless of length, making clipping thresholds easier to set and reducing variance caused by outlier tokens.

---

## The “Unit‑of‑Optimization” Principle

From the discussion above emerges an intuitive design rule:

> **The unit on which we perform importance correction must match the unit on which rewards are assigned.**

If rewards live at the sequence level, then we should correct at that same level.  This principle has two practical consequences:

1. **Clipping operates on whole responses** rather than on individual tokens.  The clipping function in GSPO,
   \[
   \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr),
   \]
   excludes responses whose overall likelihood under the new policy deviates too far from their likelihood under the old policy.  Because all tokens share the same clipped weight, we avoid per‑token fluctuations that would otherwise accumulate over long sequences.

2. **Gradient weighting becomes uniform across tokens within a response**.  In GSPO’s gradient,
   \[
   \nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
   = 
   \mathbb E\!\left[
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \nabla_\theta
   \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
   \right],
   \]
   each token contributes equally (through an average over tokens).  The entire response’s importance ratio modulates the whole sum, thereby preserving stability while still providing tokenwise gradient information.

When this principle is violated—as in GRPO—the algorithm applies different correction factors to individual tokens while using a single scalar advantage for all those tokens.  The result is an *ill‑posed objective*: “how much should I trust each token’s gradient?” becomes ambiguous because there is no tokenwise signal to ground those decisions.

## Consequences of Misaligned Units on Variance and Stability

The mismatch between token-level importance ratios and sequence-level rewards manifests in several observable problems:

### 1. Accumulated High Variance
Each token’s weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can deviate from one by up to \(\varepsilon\).  Over a response of length \(L\), these deviations multiply (or add in log space), producing variance that grows roughly as \(O(L)\).  For long queries—common when training models on reasoning tasks—this variance can dominate gradient estimates, causing noisy updates that do not reliably improve expected reward.

### 2. Amplification by Clipping
Clipping in PPO/GRPO caps each token’s weight individually but does not cap their *product*.  Consequently many tokens may be clipped at opposite extremes (some at \(1-\varepsilon\), others at \(1+\varepsilon\)), leading to exaggerated overall corrections after aggregation.  Empirically this phenomenon appears as “catastrophic model collapse,” where performance degrades irreversibly after only a few updates.

### 3. Sensitivity to Model Architecture
In Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models, different tokens may activate different experts after each update—a phenomenon called **expert activation volatility**. Because GRPO’s token weights depend on precise per‑token likelihoods conditioned on active experts, any change in routing dramatically changes \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).  This volatility further inflates variance and destabilizes training unless one employs additional mechanisms such as *Routing Replay*.

### 4. Inefficient Sample Utilization
Even though GRPO often clips far fewer tokens than GSPO (because each individual weight stays near its unscaled value), those clipped tokens still contribute noisy gradients that diminish overall sample efficiency. By contrast, GSPO deliberately discards fully off‑policy responses (those whose sequence ratio lies outside the clipping band), preserving cleaner gradients derived from higher‑quality samples.

Overall, these issues underline why aligning units—a simple theoretical observation—has profound practical impact: switching to sequence-level importance sampling eliminates an entire class of variance sources and yields stable learning even for extremely large or sparsely activated models.
