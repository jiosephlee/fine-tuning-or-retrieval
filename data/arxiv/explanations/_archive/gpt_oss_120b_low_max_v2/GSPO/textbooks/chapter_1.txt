Chapter 1: ## Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central technique for extending the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) beyond the limits of pure supervised pre‑training.  By treating a language model as a stochastic policy that maps a user query (or **prompt**) \(x\) to a generated response \(y\), the RL framework enables the model to be guided by an external **reward signal** that reflects desired behaviours such as factual correctness, helpfulness, or coding proficiency.  This chapter explains why RL is indispensable for scaling LLMs, describes the standard RL pipeline used in practice, and discusses the stability and scalability challenges that arise when applying RL to models of billions of parameters.  We conclude by reviewing the shortcomings of prevailing algorithms—most notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—and outline the desiderata for a robust RL algorithm capable of training today’s gigantic LLMs.

### RL as a Tool for Language Model Alignment and Capability Scaling  

When an autoregressive language model with parameters \(\theta\) is viewed as a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\), the probability of generating a response \(y = (y_{1},\dots ,y_{|y|})\) conditioned on a query \(x\) can be written as  

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr),
\]

where \(|y|\) denotes the length of the response and \(y_{<t}=(y_{1},\dots ,y_{t-1})\).  In an RL setting we attach to each generated pair \((x,y)\) a **reward** \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) computed by a verifier such as a reward model, a rubric, or an external judge.  The objective of RL is to adjust \(\theta\) so that high‑reward responses become more likely under \(\pi_{\theta}\).  Concretely, we aim to maximize the expected reward  

\[
\underset{x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[ r(x,y)\bigr],
\]

where \(\mathcal D\) is the distribution of prompts used during training.  By optimizing this expectation, we *align* the language model’s behaviour with human preferences and unlock advanced capabilities such as multi‑step reasoning, code synthesis, or competition‑level problem solving—tasks that are rarely covered by static supervised data alone.

### Typical RL Pipeline for LLMs  

The practical RL loop for an LLM consists of three stages that repeat over many iterations:

1. **Prompt Sampling** – A batch of queries \(\{x^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{B}\) is drawn from a large prompt pool \(\mathcal D\).  The prompts may be diverse (e.g., math questions, coding tasks, dialogue turns).

2. **Response Generation** – For each prompt we generate multiple candidate completions \(\{y^{(k)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\) using the current **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  The *group size* \(G\) is often larger than one because relative ranking among several responses yields more stable advantage estimates than relying on absolute rewards.

3. **Reward Evaluation and Policy Update** – A verifier assigns a scalar reward \(r(x^{(k)},y^{(k)}_{i})\) to each response.  From these rewards we compute advantage estimates that measure how much better (or worse) each response is compared to its peers within the same group.  Finally we update the policy parameters from \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) to \(\theta\) using an RL objective that incorporates importance sampling ratios and clipping mechanisms to keep the update within a *proximal* region of the old policy.

The loop repeats: after updating the parameters we designate the newly trained model as the next \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), generate fresh rollouts, and continue optimizing.

### Stability Issues in Large‑Scale RL  

When scaling this pipeline to models with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters—and especially to sparse architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)—the training dynamics become fragile.  Empirically, researchers observe **catastrophic model collapse**, a phenomenon where after just a few gradient steps the policy’s output distribution degenerates: likelihoods shift dramatically, generations become incoherent, and performance on downstream benchmarks plummets.  Several intertwined factors contribute to this instability:

- **Off‑policy drift**: Because rollouts are reused over multiple mini‑batches, the distribution from which samples were drawn (\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)) diverges from the current policy (\(\pi_{\theta}\)).  If not properly corrected, gradients are computed on stale data, leading to uncontrolled updates.

- **High variance importance weights**: Importance sampling ratios attempt to re‑weight off‑policy samples back to the target distribution.  When these ratios vary wildly—particularly across long token sequences—the resulting gradient estimate becomes noisy and can amplify small modelling errors.

- **Clipping sensitivity**: Proximal algorithms employ clipping of importance ratios to bound how far an update can move away from the old policy.  In practice, with token‑level clipping, an overwhelming fraction of tokens may be clipped, effectively discarding useful learning signal while retaining noisy contributions from un‑clipped tokens.

- **Expert activation volatility in MoE models**: In sparse models only a subset of experts (sub‑networks) are active per token.  Between successive updates these routing decisions can change substantially; consequently token‑level likelihoods under \(\pi_{\theta}\) can differ dramatically from those under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), breaking assumptions behind tokenwise importance weights.

These issues compound as response lengths increase (the variance scales roughly with sequence length), making traditional proximal methods ill‑suited for training state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.

### Limitations of Existing Algorithms  

#### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

PPO introduces a clipped surrogate objective that constrains policy updates:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\; y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{|y|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min
\Bigl(
w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_t
\Bigr)
\Bigr],
\]

where  

\[
w_t(\theta)=
\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})},
\qquad
\widehat A_t=\text{advantage at token }t.
\]

Although PPO works well for moderate‑size policies in robotics or gaming domains, it demands a separate value model to estimate per‑token advantages.  For LLMs this extra model is comparable in size to the policy itself, incurring prohibitive memory and computation costs.  Moreover, learning accurate token‑level value estimates for long sequences is extremely challenging; any bias directly harms stability.

#### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO was introduced to avoid an explicit value model by using *relative* advantages within each group of responses:

\[
\widehat A_i=
\dfrac{
r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]
}{
\operatorname{std}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]
}.
\]

All tokens in response \(y_i\) share this same advantage.  GRPO’s objective mirrors PPO but aggregates across groups:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\;\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
%
\min
(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
%
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
)
%
\Bigr],
%
\]
with tokenwise importance ratios  

\(w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$

At first glance GRPO eliminates the value network; however, it still relies on token‑level importance ratios derived from single samples of each next‑token distribution.  This practice violates the fundamental premise of importance sampling: accurate correction requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution.  Consequently GRPO introduces high‑variance noise that accumulates with sequence length and is magnified by clipping, often precipitating irreversible collapse on large models or long responses.

### Goals of a Robust RL Algorithm for LLMs  

A practical algorithm for training today’s massive LLMs must satisfy several stringent criteria:

1. **Sequence‑level consistency** – The unit of optimization should align with how rewards are defined (entire responses).  Importance sampling must therefore be performed on whole sequences rather than individual tokens.

2. **Low variance weighting** – Importance ratios should be stable across long generations; length normalisation or other variance‑reduction techniques are essential.

3. **Minimal reliance on auxiliary networks** – To keep memory footprints manageable, an algorithm should avoid separate value models or other large auxiliary components.

4. **Stability for sparse architectures** – The method must remain robust when expert routing changes between updates, eliminating the need for complex tricks such as routing replay.

5. **Infrastructure friendliness** – Computing only sequence‑level likelihoods permits reuse of inference engine outputs and reduces precision sensitivity between training and serving pipelines.

6. **Sample efficiency** – Even when rollouts are split into many mini‑batches (common in large GPU clusters), off‑policy correction must remain effective without discarding most data through aggressive clipping.

Meeting these goals paves the way for reliably scaling reinforcement learning from modest models up to multi‑billion parameter LLMs while preserving training stability and delivering superior performance on downstream benchmarks. The remainder of this textbook will develop such an algorithm—Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—and examine its theoretical foundations, gradient behaviour, and empirical advantages over prior approaches.
