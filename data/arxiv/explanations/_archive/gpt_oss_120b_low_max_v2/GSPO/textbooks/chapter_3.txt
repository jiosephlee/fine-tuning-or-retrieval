\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: # Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Design and Pitfalls  

In this chapter we examine **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, the reinforcement‑learning algorithm that preceded the newly proposed Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). GRPO was introduced as a way to avoid the heavy memory burden of a separate value model by computing advantages of responses within a group sampled from the same query. Although it offers a practical alternative to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) for large language models, GRPO exhibits serious stability problems when applied to long sequences or to sparse‑activation architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models. We will unpack the mathematical formulation of GRPO, explain how its token‑level importance ratios and clipping mechanism are defined, and then analyse why these design choices introduce high‑variance gradient estimates that accumulate across long token sequences. Finally we describe the empirical manifestations of these issues—model collapse and extreme instability—particularly in MoE settings, and present diagnostic experiments that reveal the ill‑posed nature of the GRPO objective.

---

## GRPO Objective Formulation  

GRPO builds on the PPO framework but removes the dependence on a learned value function. The core idea is to treat each query \(x\) as a **group** of \(G\) independently generated responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\). All responses are sampled from an **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), denoted as

\[
y_i \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x), \qquad i = 1,\dots,G .
\]

The objective that GRPO seeks to maximise is

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{\,x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\Bigg[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min \bigl(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i,t},
\,\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_{i,t}
\bigr)
\Bigg]}
\tag{1}
\]

where:

* \( |y_i| \) is the token length of response \(y_i\);
* \( w_{i,t}(\theta) \) is a **token‑level importance ratio** (to be defined below);
* \( \widehat A_{i,t} \) is an advantage estimate for token \(t\) in response \(i\); and
* \(\varepsilon>0\) is the clipping range that bounds how far an off‑policy sample may influence the gradient.

The expectation is taken over the distribution of queries \(\mathcal{D}\) and over all groups of responses drawn from the old policy. Equation (1) mirrors PPO’s clipped surrogate loss but operates at the token level for each sequence in a group.

---

## Group‑Based Advantage Computation  

GRPO replaces PPO’s learned value function with a **relative advantage** computed across responses belonging to the same query. For each response \(y_i\), a scalar reward \(r(x,y_i)\in[0,1]\) is supplied by an external verifier (e.g., a reward model). The group‑based advantage is defined as

\[
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{
r(x,y_i)\;-\;\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
}{
\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
},
\qquad
\widehat A_{i,t}= \widehat A_i .
\tag{2}
\]

All tokens within a given response share exactly the same advantage value because GRPO treats the reward as **sequence‑level**; there is no per‑token decomposition of the reward signal. The normalization by mean and standard deviation provides variance reduction across the group and ensures that advantages are roughly centered around zero, which is essential for stable policy updates.

---

## Token‑Level Importance Ratios and Clipping  

The importance ratio in GRPO quantifies how much the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) deviates from the old policy for a single token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}.
\tag{3}
\]

Because each token is generated from its own conditional distribution, \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) can differ substantially across positions \(t\).  

To prevent extremely off‑policy samples from dominating learning, GRPO employs **clipping**:

\[
w^{\,c}_{i,t}(\theta)=
\operatorname{clip}\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

When an advantage \(\widehat A_i > 0\), only values of \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) in \([0, 1+\varepsilon]\) are retained; if \(\widehat A_i < 0\), values are truncated to stay within \([1-\varepsilon, +\infty)\). The surrogate loss (the `min` term in (1)) therefore selects whichever of the raw or clipped product yields a lower objective value, thereby limiting gradient contributions from highly off‑policy tokens.

---

## High Variance Accumulation Over Long Tokens  

To understand why GRPO becomes unstable for long responses, consider how importance sampling should be applied. In standard importance sampling one estimates an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\):

\[
\E_{\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = 
\E_{\pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\tag{5}
\]

Crucially, the ratio inside the expectation must be **computed over whole samples** \(z\); only then does averaging across many independent draws reduce variance. In GRPO, however, this ratio is applied **per token**—a single draw from each next‑token distribution—so (3) does *not* constitute a proper importance weight for any underlying distributional mismatch. Each token’s ratio reflects only one stochastic outcome out of many possible continuations and therefore exhibits very high variance.

When a response contains many tokens (\(|y_i|\) large), these noisy per‑token ratios are multiplied by the same advantage and summed across all positions in (1). Even though each term is individually clipped, the clipping bound \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) is often insufficient to suppress large fluctuations caused by rare events where a token’s probability changes dramatically between policies. Consequently:

* The gradient contribution from a single response becomes a *sum of many noisy terms*.
* As sequence length grows, noise accumulates roughly proportionally to \(|y_i|\).
* The clipping operation, designed for stability at the token level, inadvertently amplifies variance because many tokens are simultaneously forced to lie near the clipping boundaries.

Therefore, for long generations—common in tasks requiring multi‑step reasoning—the overall gradient estimate exhibits *high variance*, leading to erratic parameter updates.

---

## Empirical Symptoms: Model Collapse and MoE Instability  

In practice these theoretical concerns manifest as **catastrophic model collapse**: after a relatively small number of gradient steps the policy devolves to generating degenerate outputs (e.g., repeating tokens or nonsensical text), and training cannot recover even when hyperparameters are retuned or checkpoints are restored. The paper reports that this collapse is *irreversible*: once degradation occurs, subsequent optimization fails to bring performance back up.

The problem is exacerbated in **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** models. MoE architectures activate only a subset of expert feed‑forward networks per token; after each update the routing decisions may change drastically. Empirically it was observed that for a 48‑layer MoE model about **10 % of experts** assigned to a given token differ between successive policies \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\). Because GRPO’s importance ratios rely on *exact* token probabilities under both policies (see (3)), any change in routing introduces additional stochasticity: even if two policies assign similar overall probabilities to a token, differing expert paths can cause large swings in those probabilities, further inflating variance in \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\).

Hence two sources of instability combine:

1. **Accumulated per‑token variance** due to improper importance weighting.
2. **Routing volatility** unique to MoE models that magnifies per‑token probability changes.

Together they produce gradients that no longer point consistently toward higher reward policies and trigger rapid divergence.

## Diagnostic Experiments Highlighting GRPO’s Ill‑Posedness  

The authors performed several controlled experiments to verify that GRPO’s objective is fundamentally ill‑posed:

| Experiment | Setup | Observation |
|------------|-------|--------------|
| **Varying Sequence Length** | Train identical models while increasing maximum generation length from short (≤64 tokens) to long (≥256 tokens). | Training remains stable for short sequences but displays sudden spikes in loss and loss of reward for longer generations; collapse appears earlier as length grows. |
| **Clipping Range Sweep** | Change ε across orders of magnitude while keeping all else constant. | Even with very tight clipping (ε≈0.01) or very loose clipping (ε≈0.5), instability persists; adjusting ε cannot eliminate catastrophic divergence, indicating that clipping alone does not address underlying variance. |
| **MoE vs Dense Comparison** | Train both dense transformer and MoE transformer under identical GRPO settings. | Dense models occasionally survive longer but still suffer slowdown; MoE models collapse dramatically faster due to routing changes reflected in noisy \(w_{i,t}\). |
| **Importance Ratio Distribution Analysis** | Record histograms of \(w_{i,t}\) during training for early vs late steps. | Early steps show relatively concentrated ratios; later steps produce heavy tails with many ratios far outside [0.5, 2], confirming that per-token ratios become unreliable as policies diverge. |

These diagnostics collectively demonstrate that *the source of instability resides in applying token-level importance sampling where only sequence-level weights are theoretically justified*. No amount of hyperparameter tuning can fully compensate because the estimator itself does not satisfy the unbiasedness condition required by importance sampling.

--- 

### Summary  

GRPO offers an attractive “value‑free” alternative to PPO by leveraging groupwise relative advantages and retaining PPO’s clipped surrogate loss at the token level. However:

* Its **token-level importance ratios** violate core principles of importance sampling; they are computed from single draws rather than averages over many samples.
* This misapplication creates **high-variance gradient estimates**, especially as sequence length grows.
* In **Mixture‑of‑Experts models**, routing volatility further destabilizes these ratios.
* Empirical evidence shows **model collapse**, regardless of clipping range adjustments.

These findings motivated the authors to redesign reinforcement learning for language models at the **sequence level**, leading directly to Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), which remedies GRPO’s pitfalls by employing sequence-wise importance weights and clipping mechanisms aligned with how rewards are actually assigned.
