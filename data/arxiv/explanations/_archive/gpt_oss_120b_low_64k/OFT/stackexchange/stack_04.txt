\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How is the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix parameterized and why does it improve parameter efficiency?
Question:
Section 3.2 describes splitting \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(r\) blocks of size \(d/r \times d/r\). I am confused about how many learnable parameters this introduces compared to a full orthogonal matrix, and how sharing blocks further reduces parameters. Could someone compute the exact parameter count for a concrete example, e.g., \(d=256\) with \(r=4\) and with/without block sharing?
Answer:
In the paper the orthogonal transformation $\mathbf R$ is introduced as a way to rotate all neurons of a layer while keeping the pair‑wise angles (and therefore the hyperspherical energy) unchanged.  
If we were to store an arbitrary orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ we would need  

$$
\#\text{params}_\text{full}= \frac{d(d-1)}{2},
$$

because an orthogonal matrix is completely described by a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ ($\mathbf Q^\top=-\mathbf Q$), which has exactly $d(d-1)/2$ independent entries.

---

### Block‑diagonal parameterisation

The paper reduces the number of degrees of freedom by forcing $\mathbf R$ to be block‑diagonal with $r$ identical (or different) square blocks

$$
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}\bigr), \qquad 
\mathbf R_{i}\in O\!\left(\frac{d}{r}\right).
$$

Each block has size $b=d/r$.  A single $b\times b$ orthogonal block needs  

$$
\frac{b(b-1)}{2} = \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}
$$

free parameters.  If we keep all $r$ blocks independent, the total count becomes  

$$
\#\text{params}_{\text{block}} 
= r \times \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}
= \frac{d\,(\,d/r-1\,)}{2}.
$$

When we **share** the same block across all positions ($\mathbf R_{1}=\dots=\mathbf R_{r}$), only one block has to be stored, so the parameter count drops further to  

$$
\#\text{params}_{\text{shared}}
= \frac{(d/r)\bigl(d/r-1\bigr)}{2}.
$$

---

### Concrete numbers for $d=256,\; r=4$

* **Full orthogonal matrix**  
  $$
  \#_{\text{full}} = \frac{256\times255}{2}=32\,640.
  $$

* **Block‑diagonal with four *different* blocks** ($b=256/4=64$)  
  $$
  \#_{\text{block}} = \frac{256\times(64-1)}{2}
                  = \frac{256\times63}{2}
                  = 8\,064.
  $$

* **Block‑diagonal with *shared* blocks** (only one $64\times64$ orthogonal matrix)  
  $$
  \#_{\text{shared}} = \frac{64\times63}{2}=2\,016.
  $$

Thus, compared with a generic orthogonal matrix, the block‑diagonal trick reduces the number of learnable parameters by a factor of about 4 (no sharing) or about 16 (with sharing). The reduction is exactly what the authors call “parameter‑efficient”: we retain the desirable property that every row of $\mathbf R$ is still an orthonormal basis (hence hyperspherical energy is preserved), but we limit the degrees of freedom, making finetuning both cheaper and more stable.

In practice these three settings correspond to:

| Setting | Trainable parameters | Relative cost vs. full |
|---------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Full orthogonal ($r=1$) | 32 640 | 100 % |
| Block‑diagonal, $r=4$, no sharing | 8 064 | ≈ 25 % |
| Block‑diagonal, $r=4$, shared | 2 016 | ≈ 6 % |

The paper’s experiments use values of $r$ that give a similar order of magnitude reduction, which explains why OFT can be applied to large diffusion models while still keeping the parameter budget comparable to—or even smaller than—other low‑rank adapters such as LoRA.
