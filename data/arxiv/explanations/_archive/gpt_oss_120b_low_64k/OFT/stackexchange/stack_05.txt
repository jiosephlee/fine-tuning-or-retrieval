\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How is the \(\epsilon\)-constraint enforced in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)?
Question:
COFT adds a deviation constraint \(‖\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}‖ \leq \epsilon\. The paper suggests moving this constraint to the skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) via a Neumann series approximation. How exactly is the projection onto the feasible set performed during training? Is it simply clipping the Frobenius norm of \(\mathbf{Q}\), and does this guarantee that the resulting \(\mathbf{R}\) satisfies the original bound?
Answer:
In COFT the optimisation variable is the orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) that is applied to the pretrained weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
The two constraints that must hold for every update are  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},\qquad 
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\le \epsilon .
\]

Because we parametrise \(\mathbf{R}\) by a Cayley transform  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad \mathbf{Q}^{\top}=-\mathbf{Q},
\]

the orthogonality is automatically satisfied for any skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\) (the Cayley map sends the Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{so}(d)\) to the special‑orthogonal group).  
The remaining deviation constraint can therefore be expressed directly in terms of \(\mathbf{Q}\).
Expanding the Cayley transform as a Neumann series (which converges when \(\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{2}<1\)) gives  

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}
        = \mathbf{I}+2\mathbf{Q}+O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2}),
\]

so that, up to higher‑order terms,  

\[
\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\;\approx\;2\,\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}.
\]

Hence enforcing  

\[
\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\le \epsilon' := \frac{\epsilon}{2}
\]

is sufficient to guarantee that the original bound on \(\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}\) holds (the neglected \(O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{2})\) term becomes negligible when \(\epsilon'\) is small, which is exactly the regime used in COFT).

During training we therefore perform **projected gradient descent** on the skew‑symmetric parameters:

1. **Gradient step** – compute the usual stochastic gradient of the loss w.r.t. the unconstrained entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\).  
2. **Skew‑symmetrisation** – enforce \(\mathbf{Q}\leftarrow (\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{Q}^{\top})/2\); this guarantees that the update stays in the Lie algebra.  
3. **Norm projection** – if the Frobenius norm exceeds \(\epsilon'\), we rescale:
   \[
   \mathbf{Q}\leftarrow \epsilon'\,\frac{\mathbf{Q}}{\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}} .
   \]
   This operation is exactly a Euclidean projection onto the ball
   \(\{\mathbf{Q}:\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F}\le \epsilon'\}\).

Because the projection step is a true Euclidean projection, it does not alter the direction of the gradient beyond what is necessary to stay inside the feasible set; consequently training remains stable.

After each update, the orthogonal matrix used in the forward pass is recomputed by applying the Cayley formula with the projected \(\mathbf{Q}\). By construction, every such matrix satisfies both constraints:

* orthogonality follows from the Cayley map,
* deviation from identity is bounded because
  \[
  \|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\|_{F}
  = \bigl\| (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} - \mathbf{I} \bigr\|_{F}
  = 2\,\|\mathbf{Q}\|_{F} + O(\|\mathbf{Q}\|^{3})
  \le 2\,\epsilon' = \epsilon .
  \]

Thus **clipping the Frobenius norm of \(\mathbf{Q}\)** (after symmetrisation) is exactly how COFT enforces the “radius” constraint. The approximation introduced by truncating the Neumann series does not break the guarantee as long as \(\epsilon'\) is chosen small enough—this is precisely why COFT reports good stability for modest values of \(ε\) (e.g., \(ε≈0.1–0.3\) in their experiments). In practice the higher‑order terms are negligible, and empirical results show that the resulting \(R\) always respects the original bound.
