\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Can multiple OFT matrices be composed for sequential downstream tasks?
Question:
The conclusion mentions compositionality: if we fine‑tune on task A obtaining \(\mathbf{R}_A\) and later on task B obtaining \(\mathbf{R}_B\), their product remains orthogonal. Does applying both transforms sequentially preserve both tasks’ knowledge? Are there any experimental results or theoretical insights on catastrophic forgetting when stacking orthogonal finetunes?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper shows that an orthogonal fine‑tuning (OFT) step can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{(t)} = \mathbf{R}^{(t)}\mathbf{W}^{0},
\qquad \mathbf{R}^{(t)\!\top}\mathbf{R}^{(t)}=\mathbf{I},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) are the pretrained weights and \(\mathbf{R}^{(t)}\) is the layer‑shared orthogonal matrix learned for downstream task \(t\).  
If we later fine‑tune the same model on a second task \(B\) we obtain another orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) and the final weights become  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{AB}= \mathbf{R}^{(B)}\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\mathbf{W}^{0}.
\]

Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is again orthogonal, \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\in\mathcal O(d)\) and the hyperspherical energy of the network is still exactly preserved. Hence the **compositionality property** that the authors mention holds mathematically: stacking OFT steps never destroys the pair‑wise angular relationships that the method is designed to protect.

---

### Does stacking preserve both tasks’ knowledge?

Preserving hyperspherical energy guarantees that **the geometric structure of the weight space** (i.e. all pair‑wise cosine similarities between neurons) stays unchanged up to a global rotation/reflection. This is a *necessary* condition for retaining the pretrained semantics, but it is **not sufficient** to guarantee that the specialised knowledge acquired for each downstream task survives unchanged.

Think of an orthogonal transform as rotating a coordinate system. After task A, we have rotated the network by \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\); after task B we rotate it again by \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\). The final representation space is therefore different from both intermediate spaces unless \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) happens to commute with \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\) (i.e. they are rotations about the same axis or one is the identity). In general, two arbitrary rotations do **not** commute, so the second fine‑tuning can move representations away from those that were optimal for task A.

Consequently, stacking OFT matrices can cause **interference** between tasks:

1. **If tasks are compatible** – e.g., both require only a small adjustment of neuron directions in roughly the same subspace – then \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) will be close to identity in the basis defined by \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\), and performance on A will degrade only slightly.
2. **If tasks are conflicting** – e.g., subject‑driven generation (which learns a subject‑specific rotation) followed by a control‑signal task that needs a very different rotation of the same layers – then \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}\) may undo or overwrite part of the transformation introduced by \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}\), leading to catastrophic forgetting of A.

The paper itself lists this compositionality question as an **open problem** (Section “Concluding Remarks and Open Problems”) and does not provide empirical evidence that stacking OFT steps works without forgetting. The authors only prove that the product remains orthogonal; they do **not** experimentally verify whether knowledge from multiple sequential fine‑tunings is retained.

---

### Theoretical intuition

- **Preserved pairwise angles:** For any two neurons \(i,j\),
  \[
  \cos\!\bigl(\langle \mathbf{R}^{AB}\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},
               \mathbf{R}^{AB}\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle\bigr)
  = \cos\!\bigl(\langle \mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},
               \mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle\bigr),
  \]
  because orthogonal matrices preserve inner products. This guarantees that *the distribution of angles* across a layer stays identical to the pretrained model.

- **Task‑specific loss landscapes:** Each downstream loss \(L_{A}(\mathbf{R})\) or \(L_{B}(\mathbf{R})\) induces a different optimum on the orthogonal manifold. The manifold contains many points (all possible rotations). Optimising for A moves us to a point \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}_{\star}\); optimising subsequently for B moves us to another point \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}_{\star}\). Unless those optima coincide, reaching \(\mathbf{R}^{(B)}_{\star}\) will move us away from \(\mathbf{R}^{(A)}_{\star}\).

- **Absence of an explicit multi‑task regulariser:** In multi‑task learning with OFT one would have to add a regularisation term such as
  \[
  \lambda\,\|\,\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{(A)}_{\star}\|_{F}^{2}
  \]
  during training on task B to keep the new rotation close to the previous one. The current formulation does not contain such a term.

---

### Practical take‑aways

1. **You can multiply OFT matrices safely from a numerical standpoint** – the result is still orthogonal and will not break the hyperspherical‑energy guarantee.
2. **Do not expect perfect knowledge retention without additional constraints.** Empirically you may observe degradation on earlier tasks, especially when tasks are dissimilar.
3. **If you need true compositionality**, consider one of these strategies (all of them are consistent with the paper’s framework):
   - *Joint optimisation*: fine‑tune on all tasks simultaneously, learning a single orthogonal matrix that balances all losses.
   - *Regularised sequential training*: when fine‑tuning on B, add a penalty that keeps the new matrix close to the previously learned one.
   - *Separate adapters*: keep each task’s OFT matrix separate and switch between them at inference time (exactly analogous to LoRA adapters), at the cost of storing multiple matrices.

4. **Future work** cited by the authors includes systematic experiments on stacking OFT matrices and measuring forgetting; until such results appear, any claim about “catastrophic forgetting does not happen” would be speculative.

In short, stacking orthogonal fine‑tuning matrices preserves the mathematical property of orthogonality, but it does not automatically guarantee preservation of each downstream task’s performance. Empirical validation and possibly additional multi‑task regularisation are needed to avoid forgetting when composing OFT transforms.
