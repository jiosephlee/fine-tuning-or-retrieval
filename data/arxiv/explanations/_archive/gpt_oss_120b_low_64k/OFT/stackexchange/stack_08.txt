\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does OFT compare to LoRA in terms of effective rank of the weight update?
Question:
(a) The paper rewrites OFT’s update as \((
        \mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^0\). If \(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\) is low‑rank because of block‑diagonal structure, what rank can we expect? (b) LoRA explicitly parametrizes a low‑rank matrix \(\Delta=W_aW_b^T\). How do these two approaches differ in capturing expressive updates, and can we theoretically bound one’s expressivity relative to the other?
Answer:
**Answer**

Both OFT and LoRA modify a pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ by adding a *structured* perturbation, but they do so in very different ways.  
In OFT the perturbation is written as  

$$
\Delta_{\text{OFT}}=(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\,\mathbf W^{0},
$$

where $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix that is forced to be **block‑diagonal** (Section 3.2).  
In LoRA the perturbation has the familiar low‑rank factorisation  

$$
\Delta_{\text{LoRA}}=\mathbf A\,\mathbf B^{\!\top},\qquad
\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{d\times r'},\;\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{n\times r'} ,
$$

with $r'\ll \min(d,n)$.

Below we analyse the effective rank of $\Delta_{\text{OFT}}$ and compare the expressive power of the two updates.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### (a) Expected rank of $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)\mathbf W^{0}$

Because $\mathbf R$ is block‑diagonal with $r$ blocks, each block has size $d/r\times d/r$.  
Write  

$$
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}), \qquad
\mathbf I=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf I_{1},\dots,\mathbf I_{r}),
$$

so that  

$$
\mathbf R-\mathbf I=
\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\mathbf R_{1}-\mathbf I_{1}}_{\text{size }d/r},
\dots,
\underbrace{\mathbf R_{r}-\mathbf I_{r}}_{\text{size }d/r}).
$$

Each block $\mathbf R_{k}-\mathbf I_{k}$ is a **difference of two orthogonal matrices**. For a generic orthogonal matrix the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle; subtracting the identity moves them away from zero, and unless the block happens to be exactly $\mathbf I_{k}$ the block will be full rank. Consequently each block contributes at most $d/r$ to the rank, and in practice—because we start from $\mathbf R=\mathbf I$ and move only a small amount during training—most blocks are *approximately* low‑rank.  

If we denote by $\rho=\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$, then a simple bound follows from basic linear‑algebra:

$$
\rho \le \frac{d}{r}\; r = d,
$$

but because of the block structure we can tighten this to  

$$
\rho \le r\,\, \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf R_{k}-\mathbf I_{k}) 
      \;\approx\, r\,\,c,
$$

where $c<d/r$ is the typical numerical rank of a single block (often on the order of a few dozen when $d=1024, r=8$).  
Thus **the effective rank of the OFT update is proportional to the number of blocks**, not to the full dimension $d$. In practice the authors report using $r=8,16,\dots$; with a 1024‑dimensional layer this yields an update whose numerical rank is roughly $8-16\times c\simeq 50-200$, i.e. far lower than $d=1024$.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### (b) Expressivity comparison with LoRA

Both methods can be seen as low‑rank modifications of the original weights, but their *source* of low‑rankness is different.

|                     | Source of low‑rankness                                   | Typical parameter count                                   |
|---------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| **LoRA**            | Explicit factorisation $\Delta = \mathbf A \mathbf B^{T}$ with rank $r'$. | $r'(d+n)$.                                               |
| **OFT**             | Implicit low‑rankness because $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$ is block‑diagonal; each block contributes at most its size. | With sharing: $\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2}$ (or $\frac{d(d/r-1)}{2r}$ if blocks are shared). |

*Expressivity* can be interpreted as the set of matrices that can be realised as an update.

* **LoRA** can represent *any* matrix of rank at most $r'$. Its span is exactly all matrices whose column space lies in the span of the columns of $\mathbf A$ (or rows of $\mathbf B^{T}$). This gives a *dense* subspace in the space of low‑rank matrices.

* **OFT** can represent any matrix that can be written as a product of a *block‑diagonal orthogonal perturbation* with the full‑rank pretrained matrix. Because $\mathbf W^{0}$ is usually full rank, the rank of $\Delta_{\text{OFT}}$ cannot exceed the rank of $(\mathbf R-\mathbf I)$. Hence every OFT update lives in a subspace that is *restricted by the block pattern*: only directions that are linear combinations of columns belonging to the same block can be altered together.

Consequently, **LoRA is strictly more expressive for a given numerical rank**: any OFT update with effective rank $k$ can be reproduced by LoRA with $r'\ge k$, simply by setting $\mathbf A=\Delta_{\text{OFT}}$ and $\mathbf B^{T}=I_{n}$. The converse does **not** hold in general, because LoRA can mix columns across arbitrary rows whereas OFT mixes columns only within each block.

However, this extra freedom comes at a cost. The paper argues (Section 3.4) that preserving pair‑wise neuron angles—i.e. enforcing orthogonality—prevents destructive drift of hyperspherical energy and yields far better finetuning stability. In other words, *the restriction imposed by OFT is a useful inductive bias*: it discards many directions that would otherwise corrupt the pretrained relational structure while still providing enough degrees of freedom to rotate neuron directions efficiently.

From a theoretical standpoint we can state:

*Let $r_{\text{eff}} = \operatorname{rank}((\mathbf R-\mathbf I)) = O(r)$. Then for any downstream task that can be solved by an additive low‑rank update of rank at most $r_{\text{eff}}$, there exists an OFT solution that achieves comparable training loss.*  
Conversely, there exist tasks whose optimal additive update has rank larger than $r_{\text{eff}}$; those tasks would require either increasing the number of blocks (larger $r$) or resorting to a less constrained method such as LoRA.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take‑away

* The **effective rank** of OFT’s update equals roughly *(number of blocks × numerical rank of a single block)*; with typical settings it lies in the low‑hundreds even for thousand‑dimensional layers.
* **LoRA** offers an unrestricted low‑rank space; any OFT update can be mimicked by LoRA with equal or larger rank, but LoRA can also produce updates that break hyperspherical energy.
* The *expressivity gap* therefore reflects a trade‑off: OFT sacrifices some degrees of freedom in exchange for preserving angular relationships among neurons, which empirically yields faster convergence and better stability on diffusion finetuning tasks.

In practice you should pick OFT when you need strong preservation of pretrained semantics and are happy to tune only a modest number of blocks; pick LoRA when you suspect that a richer, possibly non‑block‑structured low‑rank modification is required.
