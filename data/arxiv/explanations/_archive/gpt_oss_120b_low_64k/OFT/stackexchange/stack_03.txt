\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What is the role of the Cayley transform and why does it restrict \(\det(\mathbf{R})=1\)?
Question:
The paper uses Cayley parameterization \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf{Q}\). I understand that this yields an orthogonal matrix, but why does it only generate matrices with determinant +1 (the special orthogonal group)? Does this limitation affect the ability to represent reflections, and if so, why do the authors claim it does not harm performance?
Answer:
The Cay‑Cayley (or simply *Cayley*) transform is a classic way of turning a **skew‑symmetric** matrix  

\[
\mathbf Q\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},\qquad \mathbf Q^{\!\top}=-\mathbf Q
\]

into an **orthogonal** matrix  

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
\]

Because the denominator \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)\) must be invertible, the map is defined only when \(-1\) is **not** an eigenvalue of \(\mathbf R\).  This restriction has a concrete algebraic consequence: the determinant of every matrix obtained from the Cayley transform is **always \(+1\)**, i.e. the image of the map lies in the *special orthogonal group*  

\[
\operatorname{SO}(d)=\{\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\mid \mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I,\; \det \mathbf R=1\}.
\]

---

### Why does the determinant become \(+1\)?

Let \(\lambda\) be any (possibly complex) eigenvalue of the skew‑symmetric \(\mathbf Q\).  
Skew‑symmetry implies that the eigenvalues are purely imaginary and come in conjugate pairs:
\( \lambda = \pm i\,\mu \) with \(\mu\in \mathbb R\).  
If we apply the Cayley formula to such an eigenvalue we obtain the corresponding eigenvalue of \(\mathbf R\):

\[
\rho = \frac{1+\lambda}{\,1-\lambda\,}
      = \frac{1+i\mu}{\,1-i\mu\,}
      = \frac{(1+i\mu)^2}{1+\mu^2}.
\]

Since \(|1+i\mu|^2 = 1+\mu^2\), we have \(|\rho|=1\); every eigenvalue of \(\mathbf R\) lies on the unit circle in the complex plane.  Moreover,
the product of all eigenvalues equals

\[
\det(\mathbf R)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\rho_k
               = \prod_{k=1}^{d}\frac{1+\lambda_k}{1-\lambda_k}
               = \frac{\det(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)}{\det(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)}.
\]

Because the characteristic polynomial of a real skew‑symmetric matrix is an even polynomial,
the determinants in numerator and denominator are equal, giving  

\[
\det(\mathbf R)=+1 .
\]

The only orthogonal matrices that cannot be written in this way are those that possess an eigenvalue equal to \(-1\); those are precisely the matrices whose determinant is \(-1\) (reflections or improper rotations).

---

### What does this mean for reflections?

A *reflection* (or any orthogonal matrix with \(\det=-1)\) can be written as a product of a rotation and a single Householder‑type flip:

```
R_reflection = H · R_rotation,
```

where \(H=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots ,1)\) has determinant \(-1\).  
When we restrict ourselves to matrices generated by the Cayley transform we simply lose the ability to *explicitly* insert that final flip inside the same parameterisation.

In a neural network, however, this loss is harmless for two reasons.

* **Sign ambiguity can be absorbed elsewhere.**  
  The output of a linear layer is
  \( \mathbf z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x
                = (\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\! (\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x)\).
  Multiplying a column of `\(\mathbf W^{0}\)` by ‑1 (or equivalently flipping one coordinate of `\(\mathbf x``) does not change any downstream non‑linearities that depend only on inner products or cosine similarities, because those operations are invariant under a global sign change of one neuron.  Hence a missing reflection can be “absorbed’’ by re‑scaling a single neuron or by an equivalent sign change in a subsequent layer.

* **The training objective does not distinguish proper from improper rotations.**  
  The paper’s loss functions are based on *hyperspherical energy*, i.e. on pairwise cosine similarities between normalized weight vectors.  Cosine similarity depends only on the angle between two vectors, not on their absolute orientation in space.  Flipping all vectors in one subspace (the effect of a reflection) leaves all pairwise angles unchanged, so the hyperspherical energy stays exactly the same.  Consequently the optimisation landscape treats a reflection and its corresponding rotation as equivalent solutions.

Because OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) merely seeks an orthogonal transformation that preserves these angles, it never needs to venture outside `\(\operatorname{SO}(d)\)` to achieve its goal.  Empirically, the authors report that using Cayley‑parameterised matrices does **not** hurt generation quality or control performance; they simply obtain a subset of all possible orthogonal maps that is sufficient for preserving hyperspherical energy while still providing enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks.

---

### Summary

* The Cayley transform maps any skew‑symmetric matrix `\(\mathbf Q` to an orthogonal matrix `\(\mathbf R` whose determinant is forced to be `+1`.  
* This happens because eigenvalues of `\(\mathbf Q` are purely imaginary, which makes every eigenvalue of `\(\mathbf R` lie on the unit circle but never equal to `-1`.  
* Matrices with determinant `-1` (reflections) cannot be produced directly, but in deep networks their effect can be absorbed by sign changes in other layers without altering pairwise angles.  
* Since OFT’s objective is invariant under such sign flips, restricting to `SO(d)` does not impair performance, which explains why the authors observe no degradation despite the theoretical limitation.

Thus, the Cayley parameterisation provides an efficient way to enforce orthogonality while remaining expressive enough for the tasks considered in the paper.
