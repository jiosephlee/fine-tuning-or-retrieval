# From LSTM to xLSTM: Why Reviving Recurrent Networks Matters for Today's Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs) have become synonymous with Transformers, but the humble recurrent neural network (RNN) that powers the classic Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) still hides a lot of untapped potential. A recent paper, **xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory**, shows that by addressing three core weaknesses of the original LSTM, we can scale recurrent models to billions of parameters and compete head‑to‑head with state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models. In this post we walk through the original LSTM, its historical impact, the three fundamental limitations that have kept it from the limelight, and how the new xLSTM architecture tackles each problem.

---

## A Quick Refresher: What Is an LSTM?  

The LSTM was introduced in the early 1990s to solve the *vanishing gradient* problem that plagued vanilla RNNs. Its key ideas are:

* **Constant error carousel** – an additive update to a cell state $c_t$ that preserves gradients over long time horizons.  
* **Gating mechanisms** – three sigmoid‑controlled gates (input $i_t$, forget $f_t$, output $o_t$) that regulate what information enters, stays in, or leaves the memory.

Mathematically, a single LSTM cell works as follows:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t, \\
h_t &= o_t \,\psi(c_t),
\end{aligned}
\]

where $z_t$ is the transformed input, $\psi$ is typically a $\tanh$ squashing function, and each gate is a sigmoid of an affine transformation of the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$.

Because gradients can flow through many time steps unchanged by the multiplicative forget gate, LSTMs quickly became the go‑to architecture for any sequence problem: language modeling, machine translation, speech synthesis, video captioning, and even reinforcement‑learning agents such as AlphaStar. For a decade LSTMs were **the** deep learning workhorse for sequential data.

---

## Why LSTMs Fell Out of Favor  

When Transformers arrived in 2017 with their fully parallel self‑attention, they eclipsed RNNs on large‑scale language tasks. The main reasons were practical rather than theoretical:  

1. **Irreversible storage decisions** – Once an LSTM writes a value into its cell state it cannot later “undo” that decision without overwriting everything else. In tasks like nearest‑neighbor search where newer evidence should replace older memory, classic LSTMs struggle.  
2. **Scalar memory bottleneck** – Each cell holds a single scalar (or a small vector) that must compress all information seen so far. Rare tokens or fine‑grained details get lost because there isn’t enough capacity to store them separately.  
3. **Sequential bottleneck** – The hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence forces strict step‑by‑step processing; you cannot parallelize across time steps. This makes training on long sequences slower and less hardware‑efficient compared to the parallel self‑attention of Transformers.

These three limitations are precisely why the community shifted toward attention‑based models and later toward linear attention or State‑Space Models (SSMs). However, they also suggest *opportunities*: if we could fix these issues while keeping the linear time complexity of recurrent updates, RNNs might make a comeback.

---

## Introducing xLSTM: Extending What Already Works  

The xLSTM paper proposes two orthogonal extensions that directly target the three problems above:

### 1️⃣ Exponential Gating + Normalization  

Instead of restricting gates to sigmoid outputs (which lie in $[0,1]$), xLSTM allows **exponential activations** ($\exp(\cdot)$). This lets the model amplify or attenuate signals much more aggressively:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t) \;\text{or}\; \exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]

Because exponential values can explode, the authors introduce a *normalizer state* $n_t$ that accumulates the product of past forget gates and current input gates:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t,\\
n_t &= f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
h_t = o_t\, \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]

A stabilizer state $m_t$ further prevents overflow by re‑centering the logarithms of $i_t$ and $f_t$. The result is **revisable storage**: later inputs can effectively “overwrite” earlier memories without destroying everything else—a direct fix for the irreversible storage problem.

### 2️⃣ New Memory Structures  

#### • sLSTM (scalar memory with mixing)  
Keeps the classic scalar cell but adds *memory mixing* across multiple heads/cells. The exponential gates make mixing more expressive while preserving the ability to revise stored values.

#### • mLSTM (matrix memory)  
Boosts capacity dramatically by replacing the scalar $c_t$ with a full matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Updates follow a **covariance rule** reminiscent of associative memories:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

where $\mathbf{k}_t$ (key) and $\mathbf{v}_t$ (value) are linear projections of $\mathbf{x}_t$. Retrieval uses a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$, giving

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,\,1)}.
\]

Because $\mathbf{C}_t$ is updated additively and does not involve any learned parameters beyond the key/value/query projections, it acts like an *infinite* high‑dimensional buffer—solving the limited capacity issue while still being trainable end‑to‑end.

Crucially, mLSTM discards hidden‑to‑hidden connections (“memory mixing”), making its recurrence *fully parallelizable*: all time steps can be computed in one large matrix operation akin to FlashAttention for Transformers.

### 3️⃣ Residual xLSTM Blocks  

Both sLSTM and mLSTM are wrapped inside residual blocks that perform either *post* or *pre* up‑projection (similar to Transformer or SSM backbones). Stacking these blocks with pre‑LayerNorm yields deep **xLSTM architectures** that resemble modern LLM stacks but retain linear time/constant memory complexity with respect to sequence length.

## How Do These Changes Pay Off?  

Empirical results from the paper paint a clear picture:

| Setting | Model | Validation Perplexity (15 B tokens) |
|---------|-------|------------------------------------|
| Baseline LSTM | – | ∼73 |
| Transformer (GPT‑3 sized) | – | 49 |
| State‑Space Model (Mamba) | – | 45 |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (mostly mLSTM blocks) | – | **38** |
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (pure sLSTM) | – | **39** |

* The exponential gating alone shrinks perplexity by ≈ 10 points; adding matrix memory gives another ≈ 5–6 point gain.  
* On synthetic tasks requiring *state tracking* (e.g., parity languages), only models with memory mixing—i.e., sLSTM—solve them perfectly, confirming that exponential gating truly enables revisable storage.  
* On associative recall benchmarks with up to 256 key/value pairs, mLSTM matches or outperforms Transformers despite using far fewer parameters for explicit attention.  
* Scaling experiments up to 300 B tokens show that xLSTMs maintain low perplexity even when extrapolating context length from 2 048 to 16 384 tokens—something Transformers struggle with due to quadratic attention cost.

In short, xLSTM delivers **Transformer‑level performance**, **linear scaling**, and **far better long‐context handling**, all while keeping memory usage constant in sequence length.

---

## Why Reviving Recurrent Networks Matters Today  

1. **Hardware Efficiency** – Linear time complexity means less GPU memory pressure for very long sequences; this is especially valuable on edge devices or when serving ultra‐long prompts.  
2. **Better Long‑Range Reasoning** – Because hidden states are updated incrementally rather than recomputed via attention over all previous tokens, xLSTMs naturally retain information across tens of thousands of steps without exploding compute cost.  
3. **New Algorithmic Capabilities** – Exponential gating gives RNNs true *revision* abilities—think of a dynamic knowledge base that can overwrite outdated facts on the fly.  
4. **Complementary Design Space** – Researchers now have three viable large‑scale families—Transformers, SSMs, and recurrent models—with distinct tradeoffs; future systems may combine them for specialized tasks (e.g., RNN cores for streaming data plus attention heads for global reasoning).

---

## Takeaway  

The classic LSTM was revolutionary because it let gradients flow through time; decades later its core ideas still hold immense promise. By equipping LSTMs with exponential gates, a normalizer state, and richer memory structures (scalar mixing or matrix covariance), xLSTM revives recurrent networks as first‑class citizens in the era of massive language models. If you’re building next‑generation LLMs—or simply curious about alternatives to self‑attention—giving xLSTMs a look could be well worth your time.
