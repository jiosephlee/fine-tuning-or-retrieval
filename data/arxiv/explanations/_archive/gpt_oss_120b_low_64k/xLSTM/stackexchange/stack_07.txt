\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Can you clarify the relationship between the covariance update rule and Fast Weight Programmers?
Question:
In Section 2.3 it is mentioned that the matrix update \(\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top\) is equivalent to Fast Weight Programmers (FWP). How does the addition of decay/learning rates via forget and input gates map onto the classic FWP formulation? Are there any subtle differences in training dynamics?
Answer:
The update that appears in the mLSTM cell  

$$
\mathbf C_{t}=f_{t}\,\mathbf C_{t-1}+i_{t}\,\mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{\top}
\tag{1}
$$

is exactly the “fast‑weight” rule that was proposed in the original Fast‑Weight Programmer (FWP) papers \citep{Schmidhuber:92ncfastweights,Schlag:21}.  
In a classic FWP the fast‑weight matrix $\mathbf W^{(f)}$ is modified at every time step by an additive outer‑product of a *key* (or “parameter”) vector and a *value* (or “activation”) vector:

$$
\mathbf W^{(f)}_{t}= \underbrace{(1-\eta)}_{\text{decay}}\mathbf W^{(f)}_{t-1}+ \underbrace{\lambda}_{\text{learning‑rate}}\;\mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{\top}.
\tag{2}
$$

If we identify  

* the **forget gate** $f_{t}$ with the factor $(1-\eta)$, i.e. it determines how much of the previous fast‑weight matrix is retained, and  
* the **input gate** $i_{t}$ with the effective learning‑rate $\lambda$,

then (1) is precisely (2) with the additional twist that both $f_{t}$ and $i_{t}$ are *dynamic*: they are computed from the current input $\mathbf x_t$ (and optionally from the previous hidden state) via a sigmoidal or exponential activation.  This makes the decay and learning‑rate **data‑dependent**, a capability that ordinary FWP formulations did not have.

### How the gates map onto FWP components

| FWP component | mLSTM gate | Role in (1) |
|---------------|------------|--------------|
| Decay factor  | $f_{t}\in[0,1]$ (sigmoid or $\exp$) | Multiplies $\mathbf C_{t-1}$; when $f_t\approx 0$ the old memory is forgotten, when $f_t\approx 1$ it is retained. |
| Learning rate | $i_{t}>0$ (exponential activation) | Scales the outer product $\mathbf v_t\mathbf k_t^{\!\top}$; larger $i_t$ writes stronger memories. |
| Fast weight matrix | $\mathbf C_t$ | Stores accumulated key–value associations; retrieved by multiplying with a query vector $\mathbf q_t$. |

Because both gates are produced by **exponential activations**, they can take values far larger than one.  The paper therefore introduces a stabiliser state (eq. (5)) that normalises the gates without changing gradients, ensuring numerical stability while preserving the exact FWP dynamics.

### Subtle differences in training dynamics

1. **State‑dependent decay** – In a vanilla FWP the decay constant is usually fixed, e.g. $(1-\eta)=0.99$.  In mLSTM, $f_t$ can increase or decrease sharply depending on $\mathbf x_t$.  Consequently, the model can *selectively* erase specific parts of its fast‑weight memory while preserving others, which gives it a richer forgetting behaviour than static decay.

2. **Gate‑controlled write strength** – The input gate $i_t$ acts like an adaptive learning rate.  When a particular key–value pair is highly relevant (for example a named entity that must be recalled later), the network can boost $i_t$ to write a strong outer product; for irrelevant inputs it can shrink $i_t$, preventing noise from polluting $\mathbf C_t$.  This dynamic scaling is absent from classic FWP where $\lambda$ is usually constant.

3. **Normalization via the normaliser state** – The normaliser $\mathbf n_t = f_t\,\mathbf n_{t-1}+ i_t\,\mathbf k_t$ accumulates a weighted sum of keys and appears in the denominator of the retrieved hidden state,
   $$
   \tilde{\mathbf h}_t = \frac{\mathbf C_t\,\mathbf q_t}{\max(|\mathbf n_t^{\!\top}\!\mathbf q_t|,\,1)}.
   $$
   This extra division protects against exploding inner products when many keys have been written; classic FWP does not include such a normaliser because it typically assumes bounded keys/values or relies on explicit clipping.

4. **Stabilisation trick** – The paper’s stabiliser state $m_t = \max(\log f_t+m_{t-1},\,\log i_t)$ rescales $f_t,i_t$ so that their product never exceeds numerical limits.  From a training‑dynamics perspective this means that gradients flowing through the gates are unaffected (the rescaling cancels out), but overflow is avoided—something normally omitted in earlier FWP work.

5. **Parallelisable recurrence** – Because there is no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent weight matrix in mLSTM, the whole recurrence can be rewritten as a scan that runs in parallel across time steps (see Appendix A).  Classical FWPs are also often described as "online" updates, but they are usually implemented sequentially; the paper points out that with exponential gating and static matrix memory, one can compute all updates simultaneously on a GPU, achieving transformer‑like throughput.

### Bottom line

The covariance update rule of mLSTM is *identical* to a Fast Weight Programmer if we interpret

* **forget gate** ⇔ **decay factor**, and  
* **input gate** ⇔ **learning‑rate**.

The novelty introduced by xLSTM lies in making those factors *learnable functions of the current input*, adding a normaliser to keep retrieval stable, and providing a numerically safe re‑parameterisation of exponential gates.  These modifications do not change the underlying fast‑weight mathematics but give the model far more expressive control over how memories are written and erased, which in turn leads to smoother training dynamics and better performance on tasks that require selective storage and long‑range recall.
