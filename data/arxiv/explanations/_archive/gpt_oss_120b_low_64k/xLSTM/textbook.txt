# Motivation and Historical Context of LSTMs  

## Vanishing Gradients in Recurrent Neural Networks  

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) process sequences by applying the same transformation at each time step, feeding the hidden state forward so that information can be accumulated over time.  Early RNNs, however, suffered from a fundamental training difficulty known as the *vanishing (or exploding) gradient* problem.  When back‑propagating errors through many time steps, the chain rule multiplies many Jacobian matrices together.  If the spectral norm of these matrices is smaller than one, gradients shrink exponentially; if it is larger than one, they blow up.  In either case, learning long‑range dependencies becomes impossible because the contribution of distant inputs to the loss either disappears or overwhelms the update.  The problem was formally identified in the seminal works on RNN learning \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:00book} and motivated a search for architectures that could preserve error signals over arbitrarily long horizons.

## Constant Error Carousel (CEC) and Gating  

The breakthrough that addressed vanishing gradients was the *Constant Error Carousel* (CEC), introduced together with a set of multiplicative gates.  The CEC is an additive memory update that allows the cell state to retain its value unchanged across many steps, thereby transporting error signals backward without attenuation.  At each time step \(t\) the cell state \(c_t\) is updated according to  

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t ,
\tag{1}
\]  

where \(i_t\) (input gate) and \(f_t\) (forget gate) are scalars in \([0,1]\).  The term \(z_t\) denotes a candidate content generated from the current input and previous hidden state.  Because the update is a convex combination of the previous cell value and a new candidate, gradients can flow through many steps unchanged when \(f_t\approx 1\).  This additive path constitutes the *constant error carousel*.  

Gating serves two complementary purposes.  The input gate controls how much new information is written into memory, while the forget gate determines how much of the existing memory should be retained.  A third gate, the output gate \(o_t\), modulates how much of the transformed cell state is exposed as the hidden output

\[
h_t \;=\; o_t \,\psi(c_t),
\tag{2}
\]  

where \(\psi(\cdot)\) is typically a hyperbolic tangent that squashes the raw cell value into a bounded range before emission.  These three gates—input, forget, and output—are learned functions of both the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\).  Their multiplicative nature allows the network to *selectively* remember or discard information on a per‑time‑step basis, a capability that plain RNNs lack.

## Mathematical Formulation of the Original LSTM  

Putting all components together yields the full set of recurrence equations for a standard LSTM cell:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_t &= f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t , \\[4pt]
h_t &= o_t \,\tilde h_t , \qquad
\tilde h_t = \psi(c_t), \\[4pt]
z_t &= \varphi(\tilde z_t), 
\qquad
\tilde z_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{z}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_z\, h_{t-1} + b_z , \\[4pt]
i_t &= \sigma(\tilde i_t),
\qquad
\tilde i_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_i\, h_{t-1} + b_i , \\[4pt]
f_t &= \sigma(\tilde f_t),
\qquad
\tilde f_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{f}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_f\, h_{t-1} + b_f , \\[4pt]
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde o_t),
\qquad
\tilde o_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{o}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_o\, h_{t-1} + b_o .
\end{aligned}
\]  

Here each gate pre‑activation (\(\tilde i_t,\tilde f_t,\tilde o_t,\tilde z_t\)) consists of an affine transformation of the external input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) plus a recurrent contribution from the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\).  The sigmoid function \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\) restricts gates to the unit interval, while activation functions \(\varphi,\psi\) are usually chosen as hyperbolic tangents to keep values bounded.  This formulation captures both *input‑dependent* gating (through \(\mathbf{w}\)-terms) and *hidden‑dependent* gating (through \(r\)-terms), enabling rich dynamics that adapt both to current observations and to internal context.

When multiple scalar cells are stacked into a vector \(\mathbf{c}_t\in\mathbb{R}^d\), recurrent weight matrices replace scalar recurrence coefficients, allowing communication across cells via hidden‑hidden connections (“memory mixing”).  Ablation studies such as those in \citet{Greff:15} have demonstrated that every component—additive update, three gates, recurrent connections—is essential for achieving high performance.

## Successes of LSTMs Across Domains  

The introduction of constant‑error carousel and gating transformed sequential modeling.  LSTMs quickly became a workhorse for tasks requiring long‑range dependency capture:

* **Natural language generation and modeling** – Early works demonstrated fluent text synthesis from character‑level LSTMs (\citep{Graves:13}) and later word‑level generation (\citep{Karpathy:15blog}).  
* **Handwriting generation** – By coupling an LSTM with mixture density outputs, realistic stroke sequences were produced (\citep{Graves:13}).  
* **Sequence‑to‑sequence translation** – Encoder–decoder architectures built on stacked LSTMs achieved competitive machine translation results (\citep{Sutskever:14nips}).  
* **Program evaluation** – LSTMs learned to execute short programs in synthetic languages (\citep{Zaremba:14arxiv}).  
* **Image captioning and code generation** – Joint visual–text models leveraged LSTM decoders to articulate image content or source code (\citep{Karpathy:15}, \citep{Hossain:19}).  
* **Hydrological forecasting** – In environmental modeling, LSTMs captured complex rainfall–runoff relationships (\citep{Kratzert:18}, \citep{Kratzert:19}) and contributed to flood warning systems (\citep{Nearing:24}).  
* **Reinforcement learning agents** – High‑performing agents such as AlphaStar for StarCraft II (\citep{Vinyals:17short}) and OpenAI Five for Dota 2 (\citep{OpenAI:19}) embedded LSTM cores to maintain strategic memory over long episodes.  

Beyond these examples, analyses have uncovered *semantic neurons*—individual units whose activations correlate strongly with interpretable concepts such as number syntax (\citep{Lakretz:19}), sentiment (\citep{Radford:17arxiv}), or linguistic categories (\citep{Bau:19}).  These discoveries highlight that LSTM memories can learn abstract representations useful across disparate downstream tasks.

## Emergence of Transformers and the Challenge to LSTMs  

Despite their strengths, LSTMs retain three notable drawbacks that eventually motivated a paradigm shift toward attention‑based models:

1. **Inability to revise storage decisions** – Once an element has been written into a scalar cell it cannot be efficiently overwritten without explicit gating tricks.  In tasks like Nearest Neighbor Search where newer evidence should replace older stored values, vanilla LSTMs exhibit high mean‑squared error because they cannot retroactively edit earlier writes.

2. **Limited storage capacity** – Each cell stores a single scalar value; even with many parallel cells this compression can hinder representation of rare events or high‑dimensional features (e.g., rare token prediction on WikiText‑103 suffers higher perplexity).

3. **Sequential processing bottleneck** – Hidden‑to‑hidden recurrences enforce strict time‑step ordering; parallel hardware cannot accelerate inference or training beyond linear time in sequence length.

The *Transformer* architecture introduced in 2017 (\citep{Vaswani:17}) circumvented these issues by replacing recurrence with self‑attention—a mechanism that computes pairwise interactions between all tokens in parallel.  Self‑attention provides:

* **Direct revision** of any earlier token’s contribution during each layer’s computation,
* **Unbounded storage capacity** through learned attention weight matrices,
* **Quadratic parallelizability**, allowing modern GPUs/TPUs to process very long contexts efficiently.

These advantages led Transformers to dominate large language modeling benchmarks and become the foundation of contemporary large language models (LLMs) such as GPT‑3 (\citep{Brown:20short}) and LLaMA (\citep{Touvron:23arxiv}).  Nonetheless, Transformers incur quadratic memory costs with respect to sequence length, prompting ongoing research into linear attention approximations and alternative recurrent structures.

The historical trajectory—from vanishing gradients in early RNNs → constant error carousel & gating in LSTMs → transformer self‑attention—sets the stage for recent attempts to revive recurrent ideas at scale.  By extending LSTM gating (exponential gates), augmenting memory structures (scalar vs.\ matrix memories), and embedding these units in residual blocks (the xLSTM framework), researchers aim to combine the interpretability and linear-time benefits of recurrence with performance competitive with modern Transformers.

# Fundamental Limitations of Classic LSTMs  

In this chapter we dissect three core shortcomings of the original Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture that become pronounced when one attempts to scale the model to the billions of parameters typical of modern large language models.  The discussion is grounded entirely in the observations and experiments reported in the *xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory* paper.  By exposing these weaknesses we motivate the design choices that lead to the two novel extensions—sLSTM and mLSTM—introduced later in the text.

---

## Inability to Revise Stored Information – Nearest‑Neighbor Search Example  

A defining characteristic of the classic LSTM is its *constant‑error carousel*: the cell state $c_t$ is updated according to  

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t ,
\]

where $f_t$ (forget gate) and $i_t$ (input gate) are sigmoidal scalars that determine how much of the previous memory is retained and how much new information $z_t$ is incorporated.  This additive update has two consequences.  First, once a value has been written into $c_{t-1}$, the only way to influence it later is by attenuating it through a forget gate that acts multiplicatively on the *entire* cell.  Second, because each gate is a scalar that modulates a *single* memory slot, there is no mechanism for selectively overwriting a specific piece of information while leaving other parts untouched.

The paper illustrates this rigidity with a synthetic **nearest‑neighbor search** task (see Appendix A).  The setup provides a reference vector at sequence start and then streams a long list of candidate vectors.  The model must output, at the final time step, the value attached to the candidate most similar to the reference.  An optimal solution continually monitors similarity scores and, whenever a more similar vector appears, replaces the previously stored best candidate.  

Classic LSTMs perform poorly on this task: once a relatively similar vector has been stored early in the sequence, later more similar vectors cannot be “revoked” because forgetting would also erase information valuable for subsequent updates.  The mean‑squared error curve (left panel of Figure 1 in the paper) shows a plateau far above zero even after extensive training, indicating that the network cannot learn an effective revision strategy.

The **xLSTM** family mitigates this problem by introducing *exponential gating*: input and forget gates may use $\exp(\cdot)$ instead of $\sigma(\cdot)$, dramatically amplifying their dynamic range.  Coupled with a normalizer state $n_t$ that tracks cumulative gate products, exponential gates allow an LSTM cell to increase or decrease its contribution without saturating at $0$ or $1$.  Consequently, when a more relevant vector arrives, its input gate can grow exponentially while the forget gate attenuates only the obsolete contribution, effectively revising stored information—a capability absent from classic LSTMs.

---

## Scalar Memory Bottleneck – Rare‑Token Prediction Analysis  

Another limitation stems from **scalar memory**: each LSTM cell stores a single scalar $c_t$.  When many such cells are concatenated into a vector $\mathbf{c}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, each component still represents an isolated scalar memory.  This representation forces high‑dimensional contextual information to be compressed into $d$ separate scalar channels.

The paper exposes this bottleneck through a **rare‑token prediction** experiment on the Wikitext‑103 benchmark (right panel of Figure 1).  Tokens are grouped by frequency; perplexity is measured separately for each bucket.  Classic LSTMs excel on frequent tokens—their representations are reinforced often enough that the limited scalar capacity suffices.  However, for low‑frequency (rare) tokens perplexity rises sharply, indicating that rare lexical items are not faithfully encoded in any scalar cell.

The underlying reason is that each scalar cell can retain only one dimension of information at a time; updating it with a rare token’s embedding inevitably overwrites or interferes with other stored signals.  Scaling up hidden size $d$ does not fully solve the problem because it merely adds more isolated scalars rather than providing a richer *structured* memory.

To address this restriction **mLSTM** replaces scalar cells with a *matrix memory* $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$.  Storing proceeds via an outer‑product (covariance) update  

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \,\mathbf{v}_t \,\mathbf{k}_t^\top ,
\]

where $\mathbf{k}_t$ (key) and $\mathbf{v}_t$ (value) are $d$‑dimensional vectors derived from the current input.  Because each update writes an entire rank‑one matrix into $\mathbf{C}_t$, multiple orthogonal pieces of information can coexist without immediate interference.  Retrieval simply multiplies $\mathbf{C}_t$ by a query $\mathbf{q}_t$, yielding a weighted sum over all stored key–value pairs.  This matrix memory dramatically enlarges effective storage capacity while preserving linear time complexity with respect to sequence length.

---

## Sequential Processing & Lack of Parallelism – Hidden‑Hidden Connections  

The third classical drawback concerns **sequential computation** enforced by hidden‑hidden recurrent connections. In standard LSTMs, every gate depends on the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$:

\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_t + r_i\,h_{t-1} + b_i ,\qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_{f}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_t + r_f\,h_{t-1} + b_f ,\;\text{etc.}
\]

This dependence creates *memory mixing*: information from $h_{t-1}$ is mixed into all gates at time $t$, forming an implicit chain that must be evaluated strictly forward in time.  Consequently, processing time grows linearly with sequence length and cannot benefit from modern parallel hardware such as GPUs or TPUs for long contexts.

When scaling a model to billions of parameters—an essential ingredient for contemporary large language models—this sequential bottleneck becomes prohibitive: training throughput drops dramatically compared with Transformer architectures whose self‑attention matrices can be computed in parallel across all positions (after suitable approximations).  

The **mLSTM** variant eliminates hidden‑hidden recurrence entirely. Its gates depend solely on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$, removing terms proportional to $h_{t-1}$.  The recurrence survives only in the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$, whose update uses only scalar gates $i_t$ and $f_t$.  Because these gates are now functions of $\mathbf{x}_t$, one can reformulate the entire forward pass as a series of parallel scans (similar in spirit to FlashAttention).  The resulting implementation processes all time steps simultaneously on GPUs, restoring linear computational cost while preserving recurrent dynamics via the explicit external memory $\mathbf{C}_t$.

In contrast, **sLSTM** retains hidden‑hidden connections in order to support *memory mixing* across multiple scalar cells or heads—a design choice motivated by tasks requiring fine‑grained state tracking (e.g., parity or entity tracking).  Although sLSTM cannot be fully parallelized, the authors report an optimized CUDA kernel that runs at less than twice the cost of parallel mLSTM, demonstrating that even non‑parallelizable recurrent variants can be made tractable for large models.

---

## Implications for Scaling to Billions of Parameters  

Understanding these three limitations clarifies why classic LSTMs have historically lagged behind Transformers when models are scaled:

1. **Revision Capability** – Without exponential gating and normalisation, additional parameters cannot compensate for an inability to overwrite stale memories; performance plateaus regardless of size.

2. **Memory Capacity** – A scalar memory restricts how much distinct information can be stored per parameter; simply widening layers yields diminishing returns on tasks involving rare events or long‑range dependencies.

3. **Parallelism** – The hidden‑hidden recurrence prevents efficient utilization of modern accelerator architectures; training speed becomes a hard bottleneck when parameter counts reach billions.

The **xLSTM** framework addresses each shortcoming directly: exponential gating adds expressive dynamical range; matrix memory expands storage without sacrificing linear time complexity; and removing hidden‑hidden connections enables parallel computation at scale.  Empirical results reported in Sections 4–6 of the paper confirm that once these architectural barriers are lifted, LSTM‑based models can match or surpass state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State Space Models both in perplexity and scaling behaviour—even when trained on hundreds of billions of tokens.

In summary, classic LSTMs are limited not merely by outdated engineering but by fundamental design constraints—inflexible gating, undersized memories, and inherently sequential processing—that become fatal at large scale.  Overcoming these constraints is precisely what motivates sLSTM and mLSTM, paving the way for recurrent architectures capable of competing in today’s massive language modeling landscape.

**Review of Standard LSTM Architecture**

The Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) network, introduced in the early 1990s, remains a cornerstone of sequence modelling.  Its design is built around a single scalar memory cell that is updated additively at each time step, thereby preserving gradients over long horizons—a property often referred to as the *constant‑error carousel*.  In this chapter we unpack the original LSTM equations, describe the role of each parameter, and explain how the architecture has been extended to vector‑valued memories and recurrent weight matrices.  Throughout we follow the notation introduced in the original LSTM literature and in the xLSTM paper.

---

### Cell State Update, Hidden State Generation, and Gate Definitions

At the heart of an LSTM layer lies a **cell state** \(c_t\) that stores information across time steps.  The update rule for the cell state is

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t ,
\tag{1}
\]

where three quantities deserve immediate attention:

1. **Forget gate** \(f_t\in(0,1)\) determines how much of the previous cell value \(c_{t-1}\) is retained.
2. **Input gate** \(i_t\in(0,1)\) controls how much of a newly computed candidate \(z_t\) is written into the cell.
3. **Cell input** \(z_t\) is a transformed version of the current external input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) (and possibly the previous hidden state).

The update (1) is *additive*: the contribution from the past flows unchanged through multiplication by \(f_t\), while new information is injected linearly via \(i_t z_t\).  This additive structure prevents gradients from vanishing because the derivative w.r.t. \(c_{t-1}\) is simply \(f_t\), which can be kept close to one.

The **hidden state** \(h_t\) – the output that is passed to downstream layers or later time steps – is produced by first squashing the cell state through a pointwise non‑linearity \(\psi(\cdot)\) (commonly \(\tanh\)) and then gating it with an **output gate** \(o_t\):

\[
\tilde{h}_t \;=\; \psi(c_t), \qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \,\tilde{h}_t .
\tag{2}
\]

Thus \(o_t\in(0,1)\) decides how much of the normalized memory content should be exposed to the rest of the network.

All three gates (\(i_t,f_t,o_t\)) are computed in an identical fashion: an affine transformation of the current input and optionally the previous hidden state, followed by a sigmoid activation \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\).  The cell input \(z_t\) also undergoes an affine transform but is passed through a separate non‑linearity \(\varphi(\cdot)\), typically also \(\tanh\).

---

### Weight Matrices, Bias Terms, and Activation Functions

Formally, each gate and the cell input are defined as

\[
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), &
\tilde{z}_t &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{z}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{z}\,h_{t-1} + b_{z}, \\[4pt]
i_t &= \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), &
\tilde{i}_t &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{i}\,h_{t-1} + b_{i}, \\[4pt]
f_t &= \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), &
\tilde{f}_t &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{f}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{f}\,h_{t-1} + b_{f}, \\[4pt]
o_t &= \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), &
\tilde{o}_t &= \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{o}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{o}\,h_{t-1} + b_{o}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Each equation contains:

* **Input weight vectors** \(\mathbf{w}_{z}, \mathbf{w}_{i}, \mathbf{w}_{f}, \mathbf{w}_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}\) that map the external input \(\mathbf{x}_t\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}\) into gate‑specific pre‑activations.
* **Recurrent weight scalars** \(r_{z}, r_{i}, r_{f}, r_{o}\in\mathbb{R}\) that connect the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\) (a scalar in the original formulation) to each gate.
* **Bias terms** \(b_z,b_i,b_f,b_o\in\mathbb{R}\) that shift each pre‑activation before applying its non‑linearity.
* **Activation functions**: sigmoid for gates (\(\sigma\)) to restrict values to (0,1); typically hyperbolic tangent for both \(\varphi(\cdot)\) and \(\psi(\cdot)\).

The original LSTM therefore possesses four distinct sets of parameters per layer: three gate vectors plus one cell‑input vector, four corresponding recurrent scalars, and four bias scalars.  In practice these are often concatenated into large weight matrices for computational efficiency; however conceptually they retain their separate functional roles.

---

### Vector‑Valued Memory Cells and Recurrent Weight Matrices

While early LSTMs stored a *single* scalar memory per unit, later work generalized this to a *vector* of cells:

\[
\mathbf{c}_t = (c^{(1)}_t,\dots,c^{(d)}_t)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d}.
\]

In this setting each component follows (1) independently but can also interact through *recurrent weight matrices*:

\[
\mathbf{R} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
r^{(11)} & \dots & r^{(1d)}\\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
r^{(d1)} & \dots & r^{(dd)}
\end{bmatrix}
   \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\]

which replace the scalar recurrences \(r_z , r_i , r_f , r_o\).  For example, the forget gate now becomes

\[
f_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f\,\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_f\,h_{t-1} + b_f),
\]

where \(h_{t-1}=g(\mathbf{c}_{t-1})\) denotes a possibly linear projection of the previous cell vector.  The inclusion of full matrices permits *memory mixing*: information from one cell can affect any other cell in subsequent steps.  Empirical studies (e.g., Greff et al., 2015) showed that all components—including these recurrent matrices—are crucial for achieving strong performance; ablations that remove any gate or set recurrent connections to zero lead to noticeable degradation.

---

### Ablation Insights from Prior Work

Systematic ablation experiments have been conducted on vanilla LSTMs to assess which ingredients are essential:

| Ablated Component | Observed Effect |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Remove input gate (\(i_t=1\)) | Faster forgetting of new information; perplexity rises markedly. |
| Remove forget gate (\(f_t=1\)) | Gradient blow‑up over long sequences; training destabilises. |
| Remove output gate (\(o_t=1\)) | Hidden state no longer modulated; downstream layers receive raw saturated values. |
| Zero recurrent weight matrices (no mixing) | Decrease in expressivity, especially on tasks requiring state tracking across distant timesteps. |
| Replace sigmoid gates with hard‑sigmoid or linear activations | Training becomes less robust; vanishing/exploding gradients reappear. |

These results underline that **all three gates**, their **sigmoid nonlinearities**, and **recurrent mixing** together constitute an indispensable recipe for preserving long‑range dependencies while still allowing flexible information flow.

In summary, the standard LSTM architecture intertwines an additive cell update with multiplicative gates whose parameters are learned via four distinct affine transformations followed by sigmoidal squashing.  Vector‑valued cells and recurrent weight matrices enrich this picture by enabling interactions among multiple memory slots.  Understanding these fundamentals provides the groundwork for appreciating later extensions—such as exponential gating and matrix memories—that constitute the xLSTM family explored later in this text.

# Exponential Gating: Theory and Stabilization  

In this chapter we present the theoretical underpinnings of exponential gating, a central innovation of the extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM) family.  We first motivate why one would replace the traditional sigmoid activation on the LSTM gates with an exponential one, then give the precise mathematical definition of the exponential input and forget gates.  Next we introduce two auxiliary states – the **normalizer** $n_t$ and the **stabilizer** $m_t$ – which are essential for preserving numerical stability when gates can grow without bound.  Finally we outline a proof sketch showing that these auxiliary constructions leave the functional behavior of the network unchanged, both in the forward pass and in back‑propagated gradients.  Throughout, every equation is written in LaTeX syntax so that the reader may translate it directly into an implementation.

---

## Motivation for Exponential Instead of Sigmoid Gates  

The classic LSTM update for a scalar cell state $c_t$ is  
\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t ,
\tag{1}
\]
where $i_t\in(0,1)$ and $f_t\in(0,1)$ are obtained by applying a sigmoid $\sigma(\cdot)=1/(1+e^{-x})$ to affine pre‑activations.  The sigmoid’s bounded range guarantees that each gate contributes a value between $0$ and $1$, which prevents the cell state from exploding but also severely limits how aggressively a gate can **revise** a previously stored quantity.

Consider two contrasting desiderata:

1. **Rapid revision:** If a more relevant piece of information arrives later in a sequence, we would like the network to *override* earlier stored values quickly.  A sigmoid forget gate $f_t$ close to $0$ damps the old state only linearly; an exponential counterpart can reduce $f_t$ many orders of magnitude in a single step.

2. **Dynamic learning rates:** The input gate $i_t$ modulates how much of a new candidate $z_t$ is written into memory.  With a sigmoid, $i_t$ can never exceed $1$, constraining the effective learning rate for each time step.  Allowing $i_t$ to be exponential removes this ceiling, enabling the model to *amplify* salient inputs when needed.

These observations suggest that replacing $\sigma(\cdot)$ by an exponential map $\exp(\cdot)$ could endow LSTMs with *adaptive* gating amplitudes that grow (or shrink) as needed during training, while still preserving differentiability.

However, exponentials are unbounded above and can become extremely small but positive for large negative arguments.  If left unchecked they lead to numerical overflow or underflow during training, especially when many time steps are multiplied together as in Eq. (1).  The remainder of this chapter develops mechanisms that retain the expressive power of exponentials while guaranteeing stable computation.

---

## Definition of Exponential Input & Forget Gates  

Let $\tilde{i}_t$ and $\tilde{f}_t$ denote the usual affine pre‑activations for input and forget gates:
\[
\tilde{i}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_i^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_i\, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_i ,
\qquad
\tilde{f}_t \;=\; \mathbf{w}_f^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t \;+\; r_f\, h_{t-1} \;+\; b_f .
\tag{2}
\]
In the exponential variant we define
\[
i_t \;=\; \exp(\tilde{i}_t) , 
\qquad
f_t \;\in\;\Bigl\{\,\sigma(\tilde{f}_t),\;\exp(\tilde{f}_t)\Bigr\}.
\tag{3}
\]
Thus the input gate is *always* exponential, while the forget gate may be either its traditional sigmoid form or an exponential form depending on experimental preference (the paper reports both alternatives).  The candidate cell input $z_t$ remains defined exactly as in a standard LSTM (e.g., $\varphi(\mathbf{w}_z^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1}+b_z)$), ensuring compatibility with existing architectures.

Plugging these definitions into Eq. (1) yields an **exponential LSTM** update:
\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t .
\tag{4}
\]
If $f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)$ then both multiplicative terms may be arbitrarily large or small, leading directly to possible numerical instability—a problem addressed by the auxiliary states introduced next.

---

## Normalizer State \( n_t \) and Its Role  

When using unbounded gates it is no longer safe to squash $c_t$ directly through a fixed nonlinearity $\psi(\cdot)$.  The *relative magnitude* between contributions from past memory and current input becomes crucial for downstream layers that consume $c_t$.  To obtain a scale‑invariant hidden representation we introduce a **normalizer state** $n_t$, defined analogously to Eq. (4) but without any candidate values:
\[
n_t \;=\; f_t\, n_{t-1} \;+\; i_t .
\tag{5}
\]
Intuitively, $n_t$ aggregates exactly the same multiplicative gate factors as $c_t$, but treats every incoming signal as unit magnitude.  Consequently,
\[
\tilde h_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}
\tag{6}
\]
produces a hidden activation whose scale is automatically compensated for by how “strong” the gates have been over time.  This division replaces the classic squashing function $\psi(\cdot)$ (often $\tanh$) and ensures that even if both numerator and denominator become huge (or tiny), their ratio stays within a numerically tractable range.

The hidden state finally respects an output gate as usual:
\[
h_t = o_t\, \tilde h_t,
\qquad
o_t = \sigma(\tilde o_t), 
\quad 
\tilde o_t = \mathbf{w}_o^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o .
\tag{7}
\]

---

## Stabilizer State \( m_t \) and Log‑Space Re‑parameterization  

Even with normalization, direct multiplication of exponentials across many time steps can cause overflow in floating‑point arithmetic because intermediate products such as $f_1 f_2 \dots f_T$ may exceed representable limits before being divided by $n_T$.  A classic remedy is to work in logarithmic space where products become sums.

Define a **stabilizer state** $m_t$ that tracks, in log‑space, the dominant magnitude among all products of gates up to time $t$:
\[
m_t = \max \bigl( \log f_t + m_{t-1},~ \log i_t \bigr) .
\tag{8}
\]
The recurrence says: either we extend the previous dominant term by multiplying with $f_t$, or we start anew from an input gate $i_t$, whichever yields a larger log‑value.  By storing only the maximum, we avoid underflow from extremely small terms while still capturing the dominant scale.

Using $m_t$, we *re‑scale* both gates before they participate in Eq.s (4)–(5):
\[
i'_t = \exp(\log i_t - m_t)
      = \exp(\tilde i _t - m_t),
\qquad
f'_t = \exp( (\log f_t + m_{t-1}) - m_t )
      = \exp( (\tilde f _t + m_{t-1}) - m_t ).
\tag{9}
\]
Substituting $(i'_t,f'_t)$ for $(i_t,f_t)$ leaves Eq.s (4)–(5) unchanged because we have multiplied both numerator and denominator of $\tilde h _t = c _t / n _t$ by exactly $\exp(-m _t)$:
\[
c'_t = f'_t c'_{t-1} + i'_t z _t
     = e^{-m _t} (f_tc_{t-1}+i_ts=?)=
     e^{-m _t} c _t .
\]
Similarly,
\( n'_t = e^{-m _t} n _t.\)
Consequently their ratio remains identical,
\( c'_t/n'_t = c _t /n _ t,\)
and all downstream quantities are untouched.

This *log‑space re‑parameterization* guarantees that neither $i'_t$ nor $f'_t$ ever exceeds \(e^{\,|\,m_{\max}|}\), where \(m_{\max}\) is bounded by typical floating point ranges (e.g., about 88 for IEEE 754 double precision). In practice one clips or caps \(m_{\max}\) at a modest value (often around 10–20) to stay far away from overflow while preserving sufficient dynamic range for learning.

## Proof Sketch: Invariance of Forward Pass and Gradients  

We now argue informally why replacing $(i,f,n,c)$ by their stabilized counterparts $(i',f',n',c')$ does not alter either the output of the network or its gradients w.r.t. parameters.

### Forward‐pass invariance  

Define scaling factor
\( s _ t:= e^{-m _ t}. \)
From Eq.s (9),
\( i'_ t = s _ t i _ t,\)
\( f'_ t = s _ t f _ t.\)
Insert these into Eq.s (4)-(5):
\[
c' _ t
= f' _ t c'_{ t-1}+ i' _ t z_ t 
= s_ t f_ t (s_{ t-1} c_{ t-1})+ s_ t i_ t z_ t
= s_ t [f_ t c_{ t-1}+ i_ t z_ t]
= s_ t c_ t .
\tag{10}
\]
Analogously,
\( n' _ t = s_ t n_ t.\)
Therefore
\( \frac{c' _ t}{n' _ t}= 
   \frac{s_ tc_ t}{s_ tn_ t}= 
   \frac{c_ t}{n_ t},
   \)
and after multiplication by $o _ t$ (which is unmodified) we obtain exactly the same hidden state:
\( h'_ t = o_ th' _ t = o_ tw'/_?=h_ t.\)

### Gradient invariance  

Consider any loss $\mathcal L$ depending on hidden states $\{h_\tau\}_{\tau=1}^{T}$. Because each $h_\tau'$ equals $h_\tau$, we have $\mathcal L'( {\theta})=\mathcal L({\theta})$, where ${\theta}$ denotes all trainable parameters (weights and biases). Hence,
\( 
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L' = \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L.
$
The chain rule shows that each gradient term contains factors like $\partial c'_t/\partial i'_k$, $\partial n'_t/\partial f'_k$, etc. Using Eq.(10) we see these partial derivatives differ from their unscaled counterparts only by multiplicative factors of \(s_\ell,\ell=t,k,\dots,T.\) But these factors appear both in numerator and denominator of downstream terms exactly as they did in Eq.(10), canceling out when propagated back through the ratio \(c/n.\) A more formal treatment writes out Jacobians for $(c,n)\to h,$ then composes with Jacobians of $(i',f')→(c',n')$, observing that every occurrence of \(s_\cdot\) appears twice with opposite sign because of product rule plus division rule—thus overall gradients match.

Consequently training dynamics are mathematically identical whether we use raw exponentials or their log‑space stabilized versions. The stabilization therefore serves purely as an engineering device: it prevents floating‑point overflow/underflow without altering model expressivity or learning behaviour.

### Summary  

Exponential gating expands the expressive power of recurrent architectures by allowing gates to assume arbitrarily large (or small) values, which facilitates rapid memory revision and adaptive learning rates.  The concomitant risk—numerical instability—is mitigated through two auxiliary constructs:

* **Normalizer state** \(n_{t}\), which accumulates gate magnitudes so that hidden activations can be computed as a scale‑invariant ratio \(c_{t}/n_{t}\).
* **Stabilizer state** \(m_{t}\), which tracks dominant log‑magnitudes and yields log‑space re‑scaled gates \(i'_{t},f'_{t}\).

A simple proof sketch shows that substituting stabilized gates leaves both forward activations and backward gradients unchanged, meaning that training dynamics are unaffected while computational safety is dramatically improved. These ideas constitute a cornerstone of sLSTM and mLSTM—the two novel memory structures introduced in xLSTM—and enable them to overcome several long‑standing limitations of classic LSTMs when scaled to billions of parameters.
