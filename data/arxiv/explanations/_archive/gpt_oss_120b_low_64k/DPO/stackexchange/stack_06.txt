\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What equivalence class of reward functions is represented by the DPO reparameterisation?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims that any reward function consistent with Bradley‑Terry can be written as \(r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{π(y|x)}{π_{ref}(y|x)}+c(x)\). I understand Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 about preference and policy invariance within an equivalence class, but I’m confused about what freedom remains after fixing \(π(y|x)\). Does DPO implicitly fix the additive term \(c(x)\) to zero, and if so, how does this affect identifiability? Could there be useful reward functions that cannot be captured because they require a non‑constant \(c(x)\)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DPO paper the authors show that, for any reward function that is *compatible* with a Bradley‑Terry (or more generally a Plackett‑Luce) preference model, we can rewrite the reward as  

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\;\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+c(x),
\tag{1}
$$

where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is the *optimal* policy for that reward and $c(x)$ is an arbitrary function of the context only.  
The term $c(x)$ does **not** affect either the preference probabilities
$$
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr)
$$
or the optimal policy (Lemma 2).  Hence all rewards that differ only by a context‑dependent constant belong to the same *equivalence class*.  

---

### What does DPO actually optimise?

DPO replaces the unknown reward by the *re‑parameterisation*

$$
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} ,
\tag{2}
$$

i.e. it **chooses the particular member of the equivalence class for which** $c(x)=0$.  
Why is this admissible?  

1. **The partition‑function argument** – Starting from the optimal policy formula  

   $$
   \pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
   \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
   $$

   taking logs gives (1) with  

   $$
   c(x)=\beta \log Z(x).
   $$

   If we *subtract* this constant from the reward we obtain exactly (2).  
   The subtraction does not change any pairwise differences $r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})$, so the induced Bradley‑Terry likelihood is unchanged.

2. **Identifiability** – Because only differences of rewards matter, the Bradley‑Terry model is under‑determined by one degree of freedom per context.  Conventional learning pipelines therefore impose an *identifiability constraint*, e.g. fixing the mean reward for a given prompt to zero or fixing one reference action’s reward to zero.  DPO’s choice $c(x)=0$ is precisely such a constraint: it forces the normalising constant $Z(x)$ to be one, which makes $\pi_{\theta}$ itself a proper probability distribution without any extra scaling.

3. **No loss of expressive power** – Theorem 1 proves that for *any* reward class consistent with Bradley‑Terry there exists a policy $\pi^{*}$ such that after applying the projection  

   $$
   f(r)=r-\beta\log Z_{r},
   \qquad Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y),
   $$

   we obtain a reward of the form (2).  Thus DPO can represent every admissible equivalence class; it simply picks the canonical representative whose additive term has been removed.

---

### Does fixing $c(x)=0$ hurt us?

No.  Because all members of an equivalence class lead to exactly the same preference likelihood and to exactly the same optimal policy, any downstream objective that depends only on those quantities—such as the KL‑constrained RLHF objective (Eq. 3 in the paper)—is unaffected by which particular representative we use.  Consequently, **there are no “useful” reward functions outside the class captured by DPO**; any useful reward can be written as a constant shift of a DPO‑compatible reward.

In practice this means:

* The learned implicit reward $\hat r_{\theta}$ is unique only up to an additive function of $x$.  When we inspect or visualise rewards we should remember that adding any $c(x)$ would not change behaviour.
* The optimisation problem becomes well‑posed because we have removed a degree of freedom that would otherwise make the maximum‑likelihood problem ill‑conditioned (the likelihood surface would be flat along directions that modify $c(x)$).
* When comparing DPO to methods that keep an explicit reward network, you may notice that DPO’s reward values are numerically smaller or centred around zero—this is just the consequence of having set $c(x)=0$.

---

### Summary

* The Bradley‑Terry preference model is invariant to adding any context‑only function $c(x)$ to a reward.
* Lemma 1 & 2 show that such additive terms do not affect preferences nor the KL‑constrained optimal policy.
* Theorem 1 demonstrates that **every** admissible reward class contains a member with $c(x)=0$; DPO explicitly picks this canonical member via the reparameterisation $\hat r_{\theta}= \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$.
* Fixing $c(x)=0$ therefore does **not** reduce expressivity or identifiability; it simply resolves an intrinsic under‑determination and yields a stable optimisation target.

So, DPO does implicitly set the additive term to zero, but this is harmless and indeed desirable because it removes an arbitrary degree of freedom while preserving all information needed to recover the optimal policy.
