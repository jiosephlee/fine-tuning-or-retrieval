\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What role does the reference model \(\pi_{ref}\) play when it is initialized from an SFT model vs. when it is learned from preferred completions only?
Question:
The DPO algorithm requires a reference policy \(\pi_{ref}\). The paper mentions two ways of obtaining it: (1) using the SFT model if it is available, and (2) learning it by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions when no SFT model exists. How does this choice affect the term \(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\) in the loss? In particular, does learning \(\pi_{ref}\) from only preferred data bias the KL‑constraint, and could that explain any performance differences observed in the dialogue experiments?
Answer:
The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the “anchor” that tells DPO how far the fine‑tuned model is allowed to drift.  In the original RLHF objective (Eq. (3) of the paper) the KL‑term  
\[
\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr]
\]  
penalises deviation from a baseline distribution.  When we rewrite the objective in terms of policies only (Eq. (7)), this KL‑term reappears inside the “reward” that DPO actually optimises:  
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\]

Because the KL‑penalty depends only on the *ratio* of the current policy to the reference, the exact choice of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ can change the scale and direction of that ratio, and therefore change the learning signal that DPO receives.

### 1. $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ initialized from an SFT model  
When a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model is available we simply set $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.  This has two immediate consequences:

1. **Correct distributional anchor** – The preference data were collected by sampling from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.  Using the same model as reference means that at training time the denominator in  
   \[
   \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   \]  
   reflects the *true* sampling distribution of the data.  Consequently, the KL term measures exactly how much the new policy is moving away from the data‑generating distribution, which is what the original RLHF formulation intended.

2. **Unbiased KL constraint** – Since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already approximates the “base” behaviour of the language model, any improvement in reward must be earned by changing probabilities *relative* to that base.  The optimisation therefore respects the intended trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to SFT; there is no systematic shift that would, for example, encourage the policy to collapse toward only those completions that appear in the preferred set.

In practice this setting yields a well‑behaved loss surface: preferred completions are up‑weighted because they have a higher ratio $\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$, while dispreferred completions are down‑weighted because their ratio is smaller.  The KL term provides a smooth per‑example importance weight (the sigmoid in Eq. (8)), preventing degenerate solutions such as “unlikelihood” training that simply pushes down all dispreferred tokens.

### 2. $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ learned from preferred completions only  
When no SFT checkpoint exists, the authors propose to fit a model **only on the preferred half** of each comparison:  
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}}=\arg\max_{\pi}\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w})\sim\mathcal D}\bigl[\log \pi(y_{w}|x)\bigr].
\]  
This reference model is therefore *biased* toward high‑quality responses: it places relatively more probability mass on completions that humans already judged as good, and essentially ignores the dispreferred side of each pair.

The bias has three noticeable effects on the term $\displaystyle\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$:

1. **Compressed KL penalty** – Because $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already assigns high probability to many of the preferred completions, their ratios $\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ will be closer to 1 even if $\pi_{\theta}$ changes only slightly.  The KL divergence therefore underestimates how far $\pi_{\theta}$ moves away from an *unbiased* base model; it is effectively a weaker regulariser.

2. **Skewed importance weighting** – The sigmoid weight $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ in Eq. (9) depends on differences of those ratios.  If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already heavily favours $y_{w}$, then even a modest increase in $\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})$ can produce a large positive difference, causing the loss to focus disproportionately on a small set of “already good’’ examples while giving little signal for improving truly bad completions.

3. **Potential over‑fitting to preferred data** – Since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ never sees dispreferred examples, DPO may learn a policy that further concentrates probability on a narrow region of output space (the region covered by the preferred samples).  This can manifest as reduced diversity or as a modest drop in performance when evaluated on out‑of‑distribution prompts, exactly what was observed in the dialogue experiments: DPO with a learned reference achieved comparable win rates on the test set but lagged slightly behind PPO (which uses an SFT reference) when generalising to a different prompt distribution.

### Why does this matter for the dialogue experiments?  

In Section 4.3 of the paper, dialogue data come from Anthropic’s Helpful‑Harmless benchmark where **no dedicated SFT checkpoint exists**; instead, Preferred‑FT is first trained on only preferred responses and then used as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  The authors note that DPO “performs as well or better than PPO” once they use this learned reference, yet they also report a small performance gap relative to an oracle Best‑of‑N baseline and mention that PPO with an SFT reference (when available) can sometimes be more stable.

The gap can be traced back to the biased KL constraint described above: with a reference that already favours good answers, DPO’s optimisation pressure to improve *relative* likelihoods is weaker, so it stops short of moving probability mass from mediocre completions toward better but previously unseen ones.  In contrast, PPO’s KL term is computed against an SFT model that still allocates non‑trivial probability to both sides of each pair; PPO therefore receives a stronger corrective signal that pushes it farther into regions of higher reward while still respecting diversity.

### Summary  

- **When $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$**, the KL term faithfully measures drift from the true data‑generating policy, giving an unbiased regulariser and producing stable importance weights for both preferred and dispreferred examples.
- **When $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is learned only from preferred completions**, it becomes biased toward high‑quality outputs; consequently,
  - the KL penalty is weaker,
  - importance weighting favours already‑good examples,
  - and there is a risk of reduced diversity or poorer out‑of‑distribution generalisation.
- These differences directly affect the log‑ratio term $\displaystyle\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ and can explain why experiments that rely on a learned reference sometimes show slightly lower or less robust performance compared with those that can use an SFT reference.

In practice, if an SFT checkpoint is available it should be used as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  When it isn’t, learning $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from preferred data is still viable—DPO remains stable and improves over pure supervised fine‑tuning—but one should be aware of its milder KL constraint and may need to compensate (e.g., by using a smaller $\beta$ or adding auxiliary diversity regularisers).
