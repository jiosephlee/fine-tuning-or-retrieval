\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does naïve unlikelihood training (without DPO’s weighting) cause model degeneration?
Question:
The paper states that a naïve version of DPO that simply maximises \(\log \pi_θ(y_w|x) - \log \pi_θ(y_l|x)\) (i.e., unlikelihood on dispreferred outputs) leads to degeneration, whereas DPO’s weighted gradient prevents this. What is the precise mechanism behind this degeneration? Is it related to pushing down probabilities of dispreferred tokens too aggressively, causing mode collapse, or something else? Any empirical evidence or toy example would help.
Answer:
In the DPO paper the authors point out that a *naïve* version of the preference loss –  
\[
\mathcal L_{\text{unlik}}(\theta)= -\Bigl[\log \pi_\theta (y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\theta (y_\ell\mid x)\Bigr],
\]
i.e. maximise the log‑ratio of the preferred to the dispreferred completion without any extra weighting – quickly drives the model into a degenerate state.  
The degeneration is not a mysterious “mode‑collapse” that occurs only for large vocabularies; it is a direct consequence of how the gradient of this loss treats *every* comparison equally, regardless of how badly the current policy orders the two candidates.

---

### What the loss is doing

If we write the gradient of the naïve loss for a single preference pair \((x,y_w,y_\ell)\) we obtain  

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{unlik}}=
-\,\bigl[ \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta (y_w\mid x)
          - \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta (y_\ell\mid x)\bigr].
\]

Thus each example contributes **exactly** one unit of “push‑up” on the preferred token probabilities and one unit of “push‑down’’ on the dispreferred ones.  
If the current policy already assigns a *much larger* probability to \(y_w\) than to \(y_\ell\) (which will happen after a few updates), the gradient does **not** become smaller; it still tries to increase \(p(y_w)\) and decrease \(p(y_\ell)\) with exactly the same strength.

---

### Why this leads to degeneration

1. **Over‑penalising low‑probability dispreferred tokens**  
   Suppose after some training steps we have  
   \[
   \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)=0.9,\qquad \pi_\theta(y_\ell\mid x)=10^{-5}.
   \]  
   The naïve gradient still subtracts \(\nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta (y_\ell\mid x)\).  
   Because \(\log \pi_\theta(y_\ell)\) is very negative, its gradient points toward *making that probability even smaller*. In practice this drives a large fraction of the probability mass away from all tokens that ever appeared as a dispreferred answer in any training pair.

2. **Shrinking the support of the distribution**  
   As many dispreferred completions are repeatedly pushed towards zero, the model’s categorical distribution collapses onto a tiny set of tokens that have never been penalised.  Empirically this appears as a **mode‑collapse**: sampling from the model yields repetitions of a handful of high‑probability continuations, and diversity (measured by entropy or distinct‑n) drops dramatically.

3. **Inter‑example interference**  
   Because each pair receives the same unscaled weight, examples where the model already gets the ordering right contribute just as much as those where it gets it wrong.  The optimisation therefore spends a lot of capacity on “already‑solved’’ examples, leaving insufficient gradient signal for hard cases.  In contrast, DPO’s weighting term  

   \[
   w(x,y_w,y_\ell)=\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_\ell)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)
   \]

   (with \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)) is close to zero when the model orders the pair correctly, and close to one when it mis‑orders them.  Hence gradients are **concentrated on mistakes**, preventing wasteful pressure on already‑good pairs.

---

### A tiny toy illustration

Consider a vocabulary of three tokens, \(A,B,C\).  The reference policy (the SFT model) is uniform: \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(A)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(B)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(C)=1/3.\)  

We generate two preference pairs:

| Prompt | Preferred | Dispreferred |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| \(x_1\) | \(A\) | \(B\) |
| \(x_2\) | \(C\) | \(A\) |

Initialize \(\pi_{\theta}\) also uniform. After one step of naïve unlikelihood training with learning rate \(\eta=0.1\),

- For pair 1 we increase \(\log p(A)\) and decrease \(\log p(B)\).  
- For pair 2 we increase \(\log p(C)\) and decrease \(\log p(A)\).

The net update (summing over both pairs) yields  

\[
p(A)' = p(A)+0 = 1/3,\qquad
p(B)' = p(B)-0.1/3,\qquad
p(C)' = p(C)+0.1/3 .
\]

Thus token \(B\)’s probability is driven down while token \(C\)’s probability goes up, even though for prompt \(x_2\) token \(A\) is **not** an error—it was already larger than its competitor in pair 1. Repeating this process quickly pushes \(p(B)\rightarrow 0,\; p(A)\rightarrow 0,\; p(C)\rightarrow 1.\)

If we use DPO’s weighting, after the first step the model already ranks each pair correctly, so each weight \(w\simeq\sigma(0)=0.5.\) The subsequent gradients are halved; moreover, once a pair is correctly ordered (\(w<0.1\)), its contribution becomes negligible, so probabilities stop being driven to zero and the distribution stabilises around a balanced support.

---

### Empirical evidence reported in the paper

Table 7 in Appendix C lists generations from models fine‑tuned with naïve unlikelihood (various values of an auxiliary coefficient $\alpha$).  Even with a tiny $\alpha$ = 0.05, samples become repetitive (“the …”) and lose coherence—clear signs of collapsed probability mass. By contrast, DPO ($\alpha$ = 0, i.e. no extra unlikelihood term but with its importance weight) retains fluency and diversity while satisfying preferences.

The authors also plot KL–reward frontiers (Fig. 2).  Unlikelihood curves lie far above DPO: to reach a modest reward improvement they incur an enormous KL divergence from the reference policy, which is precisely what happens when many tokens are forced toward zero probability.

---

### Bottom line

The naïve objective treats every preference pair identically, relentlessly shrinking probabilities of dispreferred completions regardless of how already unlikely they are. This aggressive “unlikelihood’’ pressure eliminates mass from large portions of the vocabulary, causing mode collapse and exploding KL divergence. DPO avoids this by *weighting* each example with a sigmoid over the current implicit reward gap; only mis‑ordered pairs generate strong gradients, so probabilities are only nudged enough to correct mistakes while preserving overall distributional mass and diversity. This weighted gradient is therefore essential for stable optimisation of preferences without degeneration.
