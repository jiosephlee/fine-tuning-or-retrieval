\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the DPO loss (Eq. 9) avoid the need for a learned baseline or reward normalization?
Question:
In the standard RLHF pipeline, after fitting a reward model we usually need to subtract a baseline (e.g., the mean reward or a value function) when computing the policy gradient to reduce variance. The DPO paper claims that by re‑parameterising the reward as \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\) the loss in Eq. 9 does not require any baseline term. Could someone explain intuitively why the partition function \(Z(x)\) cancels out and why this eliminates the need for an explicit baseline? How does this relate to the weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) in the gradient (Eq. 10)?
Answer:
In the RL‑HF formulation the policy is obtained by solving  

\[
\max _{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb E_{x\sim \mathcal D,\;y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\!\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big) .
\tag{1}
\]

For a *fixed* reward function \(r\) the optimal solution of (1) is known in closed form:

\[
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big), \qquad 
Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
   \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\Big) .
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) shows that the optimal policy is a *soft‑max* over the reference
distribution, where the normalising constant \(Z(x)\) makes the probabilities sum to one.
If we take the logarithm of (2) and solve for \(r(x,y)\) we obtain the re‑parameterisation used by DPO

\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
      + \beta \log Z(x) .
\tag{3}
\]

The Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model that generates the human labels depends only on **differences**
of rewards:

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\big) .
\tag{4}
\]

Substituting (3) for each reward in (4) gives  

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\Big(
  \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
 -\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}
 \Big),
\tag{5}
\]

because the term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) appears **identically** in both
\(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\) and \(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\) and therefore cancels when we take their
difference.  This cancellation is exactly what removes any dependence on a
global normalising constant or on a baseline that would otherwise be required
to centre the reward.

Why does this mean we do not need an explicit baseline in the gradient?
Consider the DPO loss obtained by maximising the likelihood of the BT model
with respect to the policy parameters:

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-
  \mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
  \log\sigma\!\Big(
     \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
    -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
   \Big).
\tag{6}
\]

Differentiating (6) yields precisely Eq. 10 of the paper:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
=
-\beta\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
   \underbrace{\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))}_{w_{\!ij}}
   \Big[\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)
        -   \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)\Big],
\tag{7}
\]
where we defined the *implicit reward*
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\tag{8}
\]

The factor 
\(w_{ij}= \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) 
          = 1-\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l))  \)
plays exactly the role of a variance‑reducing baseline in ordinary REINFORCE.
When the current policy already orders the pair correctly, i.e. 
\( \hat r_\theta(x,y_w) > \hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\), then
\(w_{ij}\approx 0\); the gradient contribution of that example is almost
ignored because it provides little learning signal.  
Conversely, when the policy makes a mistake, \(w_{ij}\approx 1$ and the pair
is weighted heavily.  Thus **the weighting is computed automatically from
the policy itself**, without having to estimate an external baseline or a value
function.

From a probabilistic viewpoint this weighting arises because we are performing
maximum‑likelihood estimation for a *conditional* BT model whose sufficient statistic
is exactly the log‑ratio of policy probabilities.  The log‑ratio already subtracts
the reference log‑probability, which is a fixed function of \(x\); any additive,
\(x$‑only term (the usual “baseline”) would cancel in the log‑odds and therefore does not affect the likelihood.  Hence, after re‑parameterising rewards as in (3), **the loss is already centred**, and no additional baseline or reward normalisation is required.

In summary:

* The partition function \(Z(x)\) depends only on \(x\); when we write preferences in terms of **reward differences** it disappears, which eliminates any need to estimate or subtract a global baseline.
* The gradient weight \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) automatically down‑weights examples that are already correctly ordered and up‑weights those that are mis‑ordered, providing the same variance reduction that a learned baseline would give in standard policy‑gradient methods.
* Because this weighting comes from the re‑parameterised reward itself, DPO can be trained with a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss and does not require an auxiliary value network, reward normalisation, or any hand‑crafted baseline term.
