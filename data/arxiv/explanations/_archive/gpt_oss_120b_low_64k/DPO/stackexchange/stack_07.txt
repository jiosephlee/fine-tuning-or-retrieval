\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is the KL‑target hyperparameter (β) chosen in practice and how does it affect the reward–KL trade‑off?
Question:
In experiments β values such as 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5 are tried. Smaller β seems to enforce stronger KL regularisation while larger β allows more reward optimisation. Is there a principled way to set β (e.g., based on desired KL budget or on properties of the reference model), or is it purely empirical? Also, how does β interact with temperature at generation time?
Answer:
In the DPO formulation the scalar **β** is the only knob that determines how much the fine‑tuned policy is allowed to drift away from the reference (usually the SFT) model.  It appears in two places:

* in the **optimal‑policy equation**  

$$
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr),
$$

* and in the **training loss**  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-
\mathbb E_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}
\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!
\Bigl(\beta\!\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-
\beta\!\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\Bigr)
\Bigr].
$$

Because the KL‑penalty in the original RLHF objective (Eq. 3 of the paper) is multiplied by **β**, larger values of β diminish the penalty and therefore let the policy follow the reward more aggressively; smaller β tighten the KL constraint and keep the policy close to the reference distribution.

---

### How to pick β in practice

The paper does not prescribe a closed‑form rule; instead it suggests **tuning β so that the resulting policy attains a desired average KL‑divergence** from the reference model.  In experiments the authors sweep a small set of candidates ($β ∈ \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$) and plot the *reward–KL frontier* (Figure 2).  Each point on that frontier corresponds to a different β, and because DPO optimises exactly the same constrained objective as PPO, the frontier tells you directly how much reward you gain for a given KL budget.

A pragmatic way to use this is:

1. **Decide on a KL budget** that you are comfortable with (e.g., you want the fine‑tuned model not to diverge more than 6 nats per query from SFT).  
2. Run a short hyper‑parameter sweep over a handful of β values, compute the average KL on a validation set, and pick the β whose KL is closest to your budget.  
3. Keep that β for full training – no further tuning of learning rates or clipping parameters is usually needed (the authors report “almost no tuning”).

Because DPO’s loss is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy, this sweep is cheap: each candidate β requires only one additional fine‑tuning run of a few hundred gradient steps.

---

### Intuition behind small vs large β

* **β ≪ 1** – The term $\,\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\,$ in Eq. (7) becomes tiny, so the sigmoid inside the loss sees almost only the *difference* of those logits.  The gradient therefore heavily rewards any example where the model still prefers $y_{l}$ over $y_{w}$; consequently it pushes probability mass from dispreferred completions back onto the reference distribution.  The resulting policy changes very little and retains most of its original style and diversity.

* **β ≫ 1** – The log‑ratio is amplified, making it easier for the sigmoid to become saturated even when $\pi_{\theta}$ already deviates modestly from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  The loss then behaves more like an *unweighted* preference loss: it mainly tries to increase $\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})$ and decrease $\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})$ without caring much about staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  This yields higher reward scores but at the cost of larger KL and potentially more mode collapse.

---

### Interaction with generation temperature

Temperature $T$ controls how sharply probabilities are turned into tokens at inference time:
$$
p_T(y)=\frac{\exp(\log \pi_{\theta}(y)/T)}{\sum_{y'}\exp(\log \pi_{\theta}(y')/T)}.
$$

When you increase $T$, two effects interact with β:

1. **Effective KL scaling** – Raising $T$ flattens the distribution, which *reduces* the average KL between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  Thus a model trained with a relatively large β (permitting a big KL during training) may behave similarly to a model trained with a smaller β if you later sample with high temperature.

2. **Reward vs fluency trade‑off** – A high temperature injects randomness that can drown out subtle reward signals learned by DPO.  Empirically (Section 5.2) DPO remains robust across a range of temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance degrades noticeably when $T>0.5$.  This robustness stems from DPO’s explicit KL regularisation being baked into each gradient step; PPO must rely on an external value baseline that becomes noisy when sampling is too stochastic.

Consequently, when you plan to generate with a particular temperature, you can *compensate* by adjusting β: if you intend to use a low temperature (e.g., greedy or $T=0.2$) and care about staying close to SFT, pick a smaller β; if you will generate with $T=1.0$ and are willing to accept more divergence for higher reward, a larger β works better.

---

### Summary

* **β is not arbitrary** – it directly controls the average KL‑budget of the fine‑tuned policy.  
* In practice one selects β by **targeting a KL value**: run a short sweep over $\{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$, measure validation KL and pick the one closest to your budget.  
* Small β ⇒ strong KL regularisation (conservative updates); large β ⇒ aggressive reward maximisation (more drift).  
* Generation temperature rescales effective KL; therefore choose β consistent with your intended sampling temperature—low‑temperature generation pairs well with smaller β, high‑temperature generation tolerates larger β.

This procedure matches exactly what authors did in their experiments: they varied β, plotted reward–KL frontiers, and then chose a β that gave them their desired trade‑off without any further RL‑specific tuning.
