\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the DPO loss (Eq. 9) avoid the need for a learned baseline or reward normalization?
Question:
In the standard RLHF pipeline, after fitting a reward model we usually need to subtract a baseline (e.g., the mean reward or a value function) when computing the policy gradient to reduce variance. The DPO paper claims that by re‑parameterising the reward as \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\) the loss in Eq. 9 does not require any baseline term. Could someone explain intuitively why the partition function \(Z(x)\) cancels out and why this eliminates the need for an explicit baseline? How does this relate to the weighting term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) in the gradient (Eq. 10)?
Answer:
In the RL‑HF formulation the policy is obtained by solving  

\[
\max _{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb E_{x\sim \mathcal D,\;y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\!\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big) .
\tag{1}
\]

For a *fixed* reward function \(r\) the optimal solution of (1) is known in closed form:

\[
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big), \qquad 
Z(x)=\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
   \exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\Big) .
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) shows that the optimal policy is a *soft‑max* over the reference
distribution, where the normalising constant \(Z(x)\) makes the probabilities sum to one.
If we take the logarithm of (2) and solve for \(r(x,y)\) we obtain the re‑parameterisation used by DPO

\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
      + \beta \log Z(x) .
\tag{3}
\]

The Bradley–Terry (BT) preference model that generates the human labels depends only on **differences**
of rewards:

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\big(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\big) .
\tag{4}
\]

Substituting (3) for each reward in (4) gives  

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\Big(
  \beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
 -\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}
 \Big),
\tag{5}
\]

because the term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) appears **identically** in both
\(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\) and \(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\) and therefore cancels when we take their
difference.  This cancellation is exactly what removes any dependence on a
global normalising constant or on a baseline that would otherwise be required
to centre the reward.

Why does this mean we do not need an explicit baseline in the gradient?
Consider the DPO loss obtained by maximising the likelihood of the BT model
with respect to the policy parameters:

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-
  \mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
  \log\sigma\!\Big(
     \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
    -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
   \Big).
\tag{6}
\]

Differentiating (6) yields precisely Eq. 10 of the paper:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
=
-\beta\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
   \underbrace{\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))}_{w_{\!ij}}
   \Big[\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w|x)
        -   \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x)\Big],
\tag{7}
\]
where we defined the *implicit reward*
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\tag{8}
\]

The factor 
\(w_{ij}= \sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)) 
          = 1-\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l))  \)
plays exactly the role of a variance‑reducing baseline in ordinary REINFORCE.
When the current policy already orders the pair correctly, i.e. 
\( \hat r_\theta(x,y_w) > \hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\), then
\(w_{ij}\approx 0\); the gradient contribution of that example is almost
ignored because it provides little learning signal.  
Conversely, when the policy makes a mistake, \(w_{ij}\approx 1$ and the pair
is weighted heavily.  Thus **the weighting is computed automatically from
the policy itself**, without having to estimate an external baseline or a value
function.

From a probabilistic viewpoint this weighting arises because we are performing
maximum‑likelihood estimation for a *conditional* BT model whose sufficient statistic
is exactly the log‑ratio of policy probabilities.  The log‑ratio already subtracts
the reference log‑probability, which is a fixed function of \(x\); any additive,
\(x$‑only term (the usual “baseline”) would cancel in the log‑odds and therefore does not affect the likelihood.  Hence, after re‑parameterising rewards as in (3), **the loss is already centred**, and no additional baseline or reward normalisation is required.

In summary:

* The partition function \(Z(x)\) depends only on \(x\); when we write preferences in terms of **reward differences** it disappears, which eliminates any need to estimate or subtract a global baseline.
* The gradient weight \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) automatically down‑weights examples that are already correctly ordered and up‑weights those that are mis‑ordered, providing the same variance reduction that a learned baseline would give in standard policy‑gradient methods.
* Because this weighting comes from the re‑parameterised reward itself, DPO can be trained with a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss and does not require an auxiliary value network, reward normalisation, or any hand‑crafted baseline term.

### Why does the optimal policy for the KL‑constrained RL objective have the form in Eq. 5?
Question:
Equation 5 states that the optimal policy under the objective \( \max_{\pi}\, \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta \mathrm{KL}(\pi||\pi_{ref}) \) is \( \pi_r(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{ref}(y|x)\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr) \). I understand this looks like a Boltzmann distribution, but I’m not sure how the KL‑constraint leads to this exact closed‑form solution. Could anyone provide a short derivation (e.g., using Lagrange multipliers) or point to a reference that shows why this is true?
Answer:
The objective that appears in most RL‑HF papers (including the one you cite) is  

\[
J(\pi)=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,]}_{\text{expected reward}}
\;-\;
\beta\,\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr)}_{\text{conservatism term}} .
\tag{1}
\]

For a **fixed context \(x\)** the expectation over \(x\) can be dropped, so we maximise  

\[
J_x(\pi)=\sum_{y}\pi(y|x)\,r(x,y)-\beta
      \sum_{y}\pi(y|x)\,\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\tag{2}
\]

The policy must be a probability distribution, i.e.  

\[
\sum_{y}\pi(y|x)=1 , \qquad \pi(y|x)\ge 0 .
\tag{3}
\]

Thus the optimisation is a constrained convex problem in the simplex of
distributions over \(y\).  Introducing a Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda(x)\) for the
normalisation constraint we obtain the Lagrangian

\[
\mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)=
  \sum_{y}\pi(y|x) r(x,y)
 - \beta \sum_{y}\!\pi(y|x)\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
 + \lambda(x)\Bigl(1-\sum_{y}\pi(y|x)\Bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

We differentiate w.r.t. each \(\pi(y|x)\) and set the derivative to zero:

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \pi(y|x)}
   = r(x,y)
   - \beta \Bigl[\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+1\Bigr]
   - \lambda(x)=0 .
\]

Rearranging gives

\[
\log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   = \frac{1}{\beta}\bigl(r(x,y)-\lambda(x)-\beta\bigr) .
\tag{5}
\]

Exponentiating both sides yields

\[
\pi(y|x)=
   \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)
   \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{\lambda(x)+\beta}{\beta}\Bigr) .
\tag{6}
\]

The factor that does not depend on \(y\) can be absorbed into a normalising
constant

\[
Z(x)=
   \sum_{y}\!\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
        \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]

so that

\[
\boxed{\;
   \pi^{*}(y|x)=
   \frac{
        \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
        \exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr)
        }{
        Z(x)
        }\;}
    . 
    \tag{7}
\]

Equation (7) is exactly Eq. 5 in the paper.  The derivation shows that the KL‑
penalty simply re‑weights the reference policy by a Boltzmann factor
\(e^{r/\beta}\); the normaliser \(Z(x)\) guarantees that the resulting
distribution sums to one.  Intuitively, actions that receive higher reward are
boosted relative to the reference policy, while the temperature‑like parameter
\(β>0\) controls how sharply the policy follows the reward versus staying close
to the reference distribution.

The same calculation can be carried out for any context‑dependent reward;
the result is a *soft* version of a greedy policy and is often called a
“softmax policy” or “Boltzmann policy”.  In RL‑HF literature it appears under
different names (e.g., “maximum a posteriori” policy or “KL‑constrained optimal”
policy), but all stem from this elementary Lagrange‑multiplier argument.

### What role does the reference model \(\pi_{ref}\) play when it is initialized from an SFT model vs. when it is learned from preferred completions only?
Question:
The DPO algorithm requires a reference policy \(\pi_{ref}\). The paper mentions two ways of obtaining it: (1) using the SFT model if it is available, and (2) learning it by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions when no SFT model exists. How does this choice affect the term \(\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}\) in the loss? In particular, does learning \(\pi_{ref}\) from only preferred data bias the KL‑constraint, and could that explain any performance differences observed in the dialogue experiments?
Answer:
The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the “anchor” that tells DPO how far the fine‑tuned model is allowed to drift.  In the original RLHF objective (Eq. (3) of the paper) the KL‑term  
\[
\beta\,\mathrm{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr]
\]  
penalises deviation from a baseline distribution.  When we rewrite the objective in terms of policies only (Eq. (7)), this KL‑term reappears inside the “reward” that DPO actually optimises:  
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\]

Because the KL‑penalty depends only on the *ratio* of the current policy to the reference, the exact choice of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ can change the scale and direction of that ratio, and therefore change the learning signal that DPO receives.

### 1. $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ initialized from an SFT model  
When a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model is available we simply set $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.  This has two immediate consequences:

1. **Correct distributional anchor** – The preference data were collected by sampling from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$.  Using the same model as reference means that at training time the denominator in  
   \[
   \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   \]  
   reflects the *true* sampling distribution of the data.  Consequently, the KL term measures exactly how much the new policy is moving away from the data‑generating distribution, which is what the original RLHF formulation intended.

2. **Unbiased KL constraint** – Since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already approximates the “base” behaviour of the language model, any improvement in reward must be earned by changing probabilities *relative* to that base.  The optimisation therefore respects the intended trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to SFT; there is no systematic shift that would, for example, encourage the policy to collapse toward only those completions that appear in the preferred set.

In practice this setting yields a well‑behaved loss surface: preferred completions are up‑weighted because they have a higher ratio $\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$, while dispreferred completions are down‑weighted because their ratio is smaller.  The KL term provides a smooth per‑example importance weight (the sigmoid in Eq. (8)), preventing degenerate solutions such as “unlikelihood” training that simply pushes down all dispreferred tokens.

### 2. $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ learned from preferred completions only  
When no SFT checkpoint exists, the authors propose to fit a model **only on the preferred half** of each comparison:  
\[
\pi_{\text{ref}}=\arg\max_{\pi}\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w})\sim\mathcal D}\bigl[\log \pi(y_{w}|x)\bigr].
\]  
This reference model is therefore *biased* toward high‑quality responses: it places relatively more probability mass on completions that humans already judged as good, and essentially ignores the dispreferred side of each pair.

The bias has three noticeable effects on the term $\displaystyle\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$:

1. **Compressed KL penalty** – Because $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already assigns high probability to many of the preferred completions, their ratios $\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ will be closer to 1 even if $\pi_{\theta}$ changes only slightly.  The KL divergence therefore underestimates how far $\pi_{\theta}$ moves away from an *unbiased* base model; it is effectively a weaker regulariser.

2. **Skewed importance weighting** – The sigmoid weight $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ in Eq. (9) depends on differences of those ratios.  If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already heavily favours $y_{w}$, then even a modest increase in $\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})$ can produce a large positive difference, causing the loss to focus disproportionately on a small set of “already good’’ examples while giving little signal for improving truly bad completions.

3. **Potential over‑fitting to preferred data** – Since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ never sees dispreferred examples, DPO may learn a policy that further concentrates probability on a narrow region of output space (the region covered by the preferred samples).  This can manifest as reduced diversity or as a modest drop in performance when evaluated on out‑of‑distribution prompts, exactly what was observed in the dialogue experiments: DPO with a learned reference achieved comparable win rates on the test set but lagged slightly behind PPO (which uses an SFT reference) when generalising to a different prompt distribution.

### Why does this matter for the dialogue experiments?  

In Section 4.3 of the paper, dialogue data come from Anthropic’s Helpful‑Harmless benchmark where **no dedicated SFT checkpoint exists**; instead, Preferred‑FT is first trained on only preferred responses and then used as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  The authors note that DPO “performs as well or better than PPO” once they use this learned reference, yet they also report a small performance gap relative to an oracle Best‑of‑N baseline and mention that PPO with an SFT reference (when available) can sometimes be more stable.

The gap can be traced back to the biased KL constraint described above: with a reference that already favours good answers, DPO’s optimisation pressure to improve *relative* likelihoods is weaker, so it stops short of moving probability mass from mediocre completions toward better but previously unseen ones.  In contrast, PPO’s KL term is computed against an SFT model that still allocates non‑trivial probability to both sides of each pair; PPO therefore receives a stronger corrective signal that pushes it farther into regions of higher reward while still respecting diversity.

### Summary  

- **When $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$**, the KL term faithfully measures drift from the true data‑generating policy, giving an unbiased regulariser and producing stable importance weights for both preferred and dispreferred examples.
- **When $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is learned only from preferred completions**, it becomes biased toward high‑quality outputs; consequently,
  - the KL penalty is weaker,
  - importance weighting favours already‑good examples,
  - and there is a risk of reduced diversity or poorer out‑of‑distribution generalisation.
- These differences directly affect the log‑ratio term $\displaystyle\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ and can explain why experiments that rely on a learned reference sometimes show slightly lower or less robust performance compared with those that can use an SFT reference.

In practice, if an SFT checkpoint is available it should be used as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  When it isn’t, learning $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from preferred data is still viable—DPO remains stable and improves over pure supervised fine‑tuning—but one should be aware of its milder KL constraint and may need to compensate (e.g., by using a smaller $\beta$ or adding auxiliary diversity regularisers).

### How does DPO handle multiple comparisons per prompt (e.g., Plackett‑Luce rankings) instead of binary preferences?
Question:
The derivation in Section 4 mainly discusses binary preferences modeled by Bradley‑Terry. The appendix mentions extensions to Plackett‑Luce models for rankings of more than two answers. Could someone clarify how Eq. 9 would change for a dataset where each prompt has a ranked list of three or more completions? What would be the appropriate loss function and does the same "reward = β log π/π_ref" reparameterisation still hold?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the binary‑preference case the paper models human judgments with the Bradley–Terry (BT) law  

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\frac{\exp r^{*}(x,y_{1})}
                                   {\exp r^{*}(x,y_{1})+\exp r^{*}(x,y_{2})},
\tag{1}
\]

and, after the change‑of‑variables  
\(r(x,y)=\beta\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\)  

the BT probability can be written only in terms of the *optimal* policy \(\pi^{*}\) and the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Substituting (1) into (4) of the paper yields Eq. 9

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})
 =-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
   \!\Bigl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
        \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
       -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
   \Bigr)\Bigr].
\tag{2}
\]

---

### Extending to a **Plackett‑Luce** ranking of \(K>2\) completions

The Plackett‑Luce (PL) model is the natural generalisation of BT to ordered lists.  
Given a prompt \(x\) and a ranking \((y_{1}\succ y_{2}\succ\dots\succ y_{K})\), the PL likelihood under a latent reward function \(r^{*}\) is  

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ\dots\succ y_{K}\mid x)
 =\prod_{k=1}^{K}
   \frac{\exp r^{*}(x,y_{k})}
        {\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp r^{*}(x,y_{j})}.
\tag{3}
\]

Now insert the re‑parameterisation (the analogue of Eq. 5 in the paper)

\[
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + c(x),
\qquad
c(x)=\beta \log Z(x),
\tag{4}
\]

where \(Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\).  
Because \(c(x)\) depends **only on the prompt** it cancels out when we form the ratios that appear in (3).  After a short algebraic manipulation we obtain a *policy‑only* PL likelihood:

\[
p(y_{1}\succ\dots\succ y_{K}\mid x)
 = \prod_{k=1}^{K}
   \frac{
        \displaystyle
        \frac{\pi(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_k|x)}
      }{
        \displaystyle
        \sum_{j=k}^{K}
        \frac{\pi(y_j|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}
      }.
\tag{5}
\]

Equation (5) is exactly Eq. 9 of the paper **generalised** from a binary log‑odds term to a product of \(K-1\) log‑odds terms.

---

### The loss function for ranked data

Given a dataset of rankings  

\[
\mathcal D=\bigl\{
   (x^{(i)},\, y^{(i)}_{1}\succ y^{(i)}_{2}\succ\dots\succ y^{(i)}_{K})
\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

the maximum‑likelihood objective is simply the negative log of (5):

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}^{(K)}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}})
 = -\, \mathbb E_{(x,\mathbf y)\sim \mathcal D}
     \Bigl[
       \sum_{k=1}^{K-1}
         \log
         \sigma
         \Bigl(
           \beta
           \Bigl(
               \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_k|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_k|x)}
              -\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{k+1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{k+1}|x)}
           \Bigr)
         \Bigr)
     \Bigr].
$$

The inner term is exactly the binary DPO log‑sigmoid that appears in Eq. 9, applied **pairwise to successive items in the ranking**.  One can equivalently write it as a sum over all ordered pairs \((y_a , y_b)\) with \(a<b\); this gives the same loss because the PL likelihood factorises into those pairwise odds.

In practice you do not need to enumerate every pair – iterating once over the list and using the telescoping product in (5) is linear in \(K\).  The gradient of this loss is identical in form to Eq. 10 of the paper, but now each comparison contributes its own weight  

\[
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_b)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_a)),
   \quad
   \hat r_\theta(x,y)=
     \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
\]

so that items that are currently ranked incorrectly receive larger updates.

---

### Does the re‑parameterisation still hold?

Yes.  The derivation in Appendix C shows that for *any* Plackett‑Luce model, adding a prompt‑dependent constant \(c(x)\) to the reward does not affect the induced preference distribution because every term in (3) contains a ratio of exponentiated rewards and all constants cancel.  Consequently, every reward function in the equivalence class can be expressed as  

\[
r(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)},
\]

with no extra normalising term needed explicitly during training.  This is precisely the same “reward‑= β log π/π_ref’’ used for binary preferences; it is simply applied to each element of a ranking rather than to a single pair.

---

### Putting it together

* **Modeling assumption:** use a Plackett‑Luce preference model for ranked lists.
* **Policy‑only likelihood:** Eq. (5) – product of ratios of π over π_ref.
* **Loss:** negative log‑likelihood of (5), which expands to a sum of binary DPO terms over successive items (or over all ordered pairs).
* **Reparameterisation:** unchanged – reward equals β log π/π_ref up to an additive prompt‑only constant, which drops out of the PL likelihood.

Thus, extending DPO from binary comparisons to full rankings is completely straightforward: replace Eq. 9 by its product form (5) and train with the corresponding summed cross‑entropy loss.  The algorithmic pipeline—sample candidates from a reference model, collect human rankings, and minimise `L_DPO⁽ᴷ⁾` with respect to θ—remains identical, and all theoretical guarantees derived for BT continue to apply because PL is just a higher‑order BT model.

### Why does naïve unlikelihood training (without DPO’s weighting) cause model degeneration?
Question:
The paper states that a naïve version of DPO that simply maximises \(\log \pi_θ(y_w|x) - \log \pi_θ(y_l|x)\) (i.e., unlikelihood on dispreferred outputs) leads to degeneration, whereas DPO’s weighted gradient prevents this. What is the precise mechanism behind this degeneration? Is it related to pushing down probabilities of dispreferred tokens too aggressively, causing mode collapse, or something else? Any empirical evidence or toy example would help.
Answer:
In the DPO paper the authors point out that a *naïve* version of the preference loss –  
\[
\mathcal L_{\text{unlik}}(\theta)= -\Bigl[\log \pi_\theta (y_w\mid x)-\log \pi_\theta (y_\ell\mid x)\Bigr],
\]
i.e. maximise the log‑ratio of the preferred to the dispreferred completion without any extra weighting – quickly drives the model into a degenerate state.  
The degeneration is not a mysterious “mode‑collapse” that occurs only for large vocabularies; it is a direct consequence of how the gradient of this loss treats *every* comparison equally, regardless of how badly the current policy orders the two candidates.

---

### What the loss is doing

If we write the gradient of the naïve loss for a single preference pair \((x,y_w,y_\ell)\) we obtain  

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{unlik}}=
-\,\bigl[ \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta (y_w\mid x)
          - \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta (y_\ell\mid x)\bigr].
\]

Thus each example contributes **exactly** one unit of “push‑up” on the preferred token probabilities and one unit of “push‑down’’ on the dispreferred ones.  
If the current policy already assigns a *much larger* probability to \(y_w\) than to \(y_\ell\) (which will happen after a few updates), the gradient does **not** become smaller; it still tries to increase \(p(y_w)\) and decrease \(p(y_\ell)\) with exactly the same strength.

---

### Why this leads to degeneration

1. **Over‑penalising low‑probability dispreferred tokens**  
   Suppose after some training steps we have  
   \[
   \pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)=0.9,\qquad \pi_\theta(y_\ell\mid x)=10^{-5}.
   \]  
   The naïve gradient still subtracts \(\nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta (y_\ell\mid x)\).  
   Because \(\log \pi_\theta(y_\ell)\) is very negative, its gradient points toward *making that probability even smaller*. In practice this drives a large fraction of the probability mass away from all tokens that ever appeared as a dispreferred answer in any training pair.

2. **Shrinking the support of the distribution**  
   As many dispreferred completions are repeatedly pushed towards zero, the model’s categorical distribution collapses onto a tiny set of tokens that have never been penalised.  Empirically this appears as a **mode‑collapse**: sampling from the model yields repetitions of a handful of high‑probability continuations, and diversity (measured by entropy or distinct‑n) drops dramatically.

3. **Inter‑example interference**  
   Because each pair receives the same unscaled weight, examples where the model already gets the ordering right contribute just as much as those where it gets it wrong.  The optimisation therefore spends a lot of capacity on “already‑solved’’ examples, leaving insufficient gradient signal for hard cases.  In contrast, DPO’s weighting term  

   \[
   w(x,y_w,y_\ell)=\sigma\bigl(\hat r_\theta(x,y_\ell)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\bigr)
   \]

   (with \(\hat r_\theta=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)) is close to zero when the model orders the pair correctly, and close to one when it mis‑orders them.  Hence gradients are **concentrated on mistakes**, preventing wasteful pressure on already‑good pairs.

---

### A tiny toy illustration

Consider a vocabulary of three tokens, \(A,B,C\).  The reference policy (the SFT model) is uniform: \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(A)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(B)=\pi_{\text{ref}}(C)=1/3.\)  

We generate two preference pairs:

| Prompt | Preferred | Dispreferred |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| \(x_1\) | \(A\) | \(B\) |
| \(x_2\) | \(C\) | \(A\) |

Initialize \(\pi_{\theta}\) also uniform. After one step of naïve unlikelihood training with learning rate \(\eta=0.1\),

- For pair 1 we increase \(\log p(A)\) and decrease \(\log p(B)\).  
- For pair 2 we increase \(\log p(C)\) and decrease \(\log p(A)\).

The net update (summing over both pairs) yields  

\[
p(A)' = p(A)+0 = 1/3,\qquad
p(B)' = p(B)-0.1/3,\qquad
p(C)' = p(C)+0.1/3 .
\]

Thus token \(B\)’s probability is driven down while token \(C\)’s probability goes up, even though for prompt \(x_2\) token \(A\) is **not** an error—it was already larger than its competitor in pair 1. Repeating this process quickly pushes \(p(B)\rightarrow 0,\; p(A)\rightarrow 0,\; p(C)\rightarrow 1.\)

If we use DPO’s weighting, after the first step the model already ranks each pair correctly, so each weight \(w\simeq\sigma(0)=0.5.\) The subsequent gradients are halved; moreover, once a pair is correctly ordered (\(w<0.1\)), its contribution becomes negligible, so probabilities stop being driven to zero and the distribution stabilises around a balanced support.

---

### Empirical evidence reported in the paper

Table 7 in Appendix C lists generations from models fine‑tuned with naïve unlikelihood (various values of an auxiliary coefficient $\alpha$).  Even with a tiny $\alpha$ = 0.05, samples become repetitive (“the …”) and lose coherence—clear signs of collapsed probability mass. By contrast, DPO ($\alpha$ = 0, i.e. no extra unlikelihood term but with its importance weight) retains fluency and diversity while satisfying preferences.

The authors also plot KL–reward frontiers (Fig. 2).  Unlikelihood curves lie far above DPO: to reach a modest reward improvement they incur an enormous KL divergence from the reference policy, which is precisely what happens when many tokens are forced toward zero probability.

---

### Bottom line

The naïve objective treats every preference pair identically, relentlessly shrinking probabilities of dispreferred completions regardless of how already unlikely they are. This aggressive “unlikelihood’’ pressure eliminates mass from large portions of the vocabulary, causing mode collapse and exploding KL divergence. DPO avoids this by *weighting* each example with a sigmoid over the current implicit reward gap; only mis‑ordered pairs generate strong gradients, so probabilities are only nudged enough to correct mistakes while preserving overall distributional mass and diversity. This weighted gradient is therefore essential for stable optimisation of preferences without degeneration.

### What equivalence class of reward functions is represented by the DPO reparameterisation?
Question:
Theorem 1 claims that any reward function consistent with Bradley‑Terry can be written as \(r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{π(y|x)}{π_{ref}(y|x)}+c(x)\). I understand Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 about preference and policy invariance within an equivalence class, but I’m confused about what freedom remains after fixing \(π(y|x)\). Does DPO implicitly fix the additive term \(c(x)\) to zero, and if so, how does this affect identifiability? Could there be useful reward functions that cannot be captured because they require a non‑constant \(c(x)\)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DPO paper the authors show that, for any reward function that is *compatible* with a Bradley‑Terry (or more generally a Plackett‑Luce) preference model, we can rewrite the reward as  

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\;\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+c(x),
\tag{1}
$$

where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is the *optimal* policy for that reward and $c(x)$ is an arbitrary function of the context only.  
The term $c(x)$ does **not** affect either the preference probabilities
$$
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr)
$$
or the optimal policy (Lemma 2).  Hence all rewards that differ only by a context‑dependent constant belong to the same *equivalence class*.  

---

### What does DPO actually optimise?

DPO replaces the unknown reward by the *re‑parameterisation*

$$
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} ,
\tag{2}
$$

i.e. it **chooses the particular member of the equivalence class for which** $c(x)=0$.  
Why is this admissible?  

1. **The partition‑function argument** – Starting from the optimal policy formula  

   $$
   \pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
   \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
   $$

   taking logs gives (1) with  

   $$
   c(x)=\beta \log Z(x).
   $$

   If we *subtract* this constant from the reward we obtain exactly (2).  
   The subtraction does not change any pairwise differences $r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})$, so the induced Bradley‑Terry likelihood is unchanged.

2. **Identifiability** – Because only differences of rewards matter, the Bradley‑Terry model is under‑determined by one degree of freedom per context.  Conventional learning pipelines therefore impose an *identifiability constraint*, e.g. fixing the mean reward for a given prompt to zero or fixing one reference action’s reward to zero.  DPO’s choice $c(x)=0$ is precisely such a constraint: it forces the normalising constant $Z(x)$ to be one, which makes $\pi_{\theta}$ itself a proper probability distribution without any extra scaling.

3. **No loss of expressive power** – Theorem 1 proves that for *any* reward class consistent with Bradley‑Terry there exists a policy $\pi^{*}$ such that after applying the projection  

   $$
   f(r)=r-\beta\log Z_{r},
   \qquad Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y),
   $$

   we obtain a reward of the form (2).  Thus DPO can represent every admissible equivalence class; it simply picks the canonical representative whose additive term has been removed.

---

### Does fixing $c(x)=0$ hurt us?

No.  Because all members of an equivalence class lead to exactly the same preference likelihood and to exactly the same optimal policy, any downstream objective that depends only on those quantities—such as the KL‑constrained RLHF objective (Eq. 3 in the paper)—is unaffected by which particular representative we use.  Consequently, **there are no “useful” reward functions outside the class captured by DPO**; any useful reward can be written as a constant shift of a DPO‑compatible reward.

In practice this means:

* The learned implicit reward $\hat r_{\theta}$ is unique only up to an additive function of $x$.  When we inspect or visualise rewards we should remember that adding any $c(x)$ would not change behaviour.
* The optimisation problem becomes well‑posed because we have removed a degree of freedom that would otherwise make the maximum‑likelihood problem ill‑conditioned (the likelihood surface would be flat along directions that modify $c(x)$).
* When comparing DPO to methods that keep an explicit reward network, you may notice that DPO’s reward values are numerically smaller or centred around zero—this is just the consequence of having set $c(x)=0$.

---

### Summary

* The Bradley‑Terry preference model is invariant to adding any context‑only function $c(x)$ to a reward.
* Lemma 1 & 2 show that such additive terms do not affect preferences nor the KL‑constrained optimal policy.
* Theorem 1 demonstrates that **every** admissible reward class contains a member with $c(x)=0$; DPO explicitly picks this canonical member via the reparameterisation $\hat r_{\theta}= \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$.
* Fixing $c(x)=0$ therefore does **not** reduce expressivity or identifiability; it simply resolves an intrinsic under‑determination and yields a stable optimisation target.

So, DPO does implicitly set the additive term to zero, but this is harmless and indeed desirable because it removes an arbitrary degree of freedom while preserving all information needed to recover the optimal policy.

### How is the KL‑target hyperparameter (β) chosen in practice and how does it affect the reward–KL trade‑off?
Question:
In experiments β values such as 0.05, 0.1, 1, 5 are tried. Smaller β seems to enforce stronger KL regularisation while larger β allows more reward optimisation. Is there a principled way to set β (e.g., based on desired KL budget or on properties of the reference model), or is it purely empirical? Also, how does β interact with temperature at generation time?
Answer:
In the DPO formulation the scalar **β** is the only knob that determines how much the fine‑tuned policy is allowed to drift away from the reference (usually the SFT) model.  It appears in two places:

* in the **optimal‑policy equation**  

$$
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr),
$$

* and in the **training loss**  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-
\mathbb E_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}
\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!
\Bigl(\beta\!\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-
\beta\!\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}
{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\Bigr)
\Bigr].
$$

Because the KL‑penalty in the original RLHF objective (Eq. 3 of the paper) is multiplied by **β**, larger values of β diminish the penalty and therefore let the policy follow the reward more aggressively; smaller β tighten the KL constraint and keep the policy close to the reference distribution.

---

### How to pick β in practice

The paper does not prescribe a closed‑form rule; instead it suggests **tuning β so that the resulting policy attains a desired average KL‑divergence** from the reference model.  In experiments the authors sweep a small set of candidates ($β ∈ \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$) and plot the *reward–KL frontier* (Figure 2).  Each point on that frontier corresponds to a different β, and because DPO optimises exactly the same constrained objective as PPO, the frontier tells you directly how much reward you gain for a given KL budget.

A pragmatic way to use this is:

1. **Decide on a KL budget** that you are comfortable with (e.g., you want the fine‑tuned model not to diverge more than 6 nats per query from SFT).  
2. Run a short hyper‑parameter sweep over a handful of β values, compute the average KL on a validation set, and pick the β whose KL is closest to your budget.  
3. Keep that β for full training – no further tuning of learning rates or clipping parameters is usually needed (the authors report “almost no tuning”).

Because DPO’s loss is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy, this sweep is cheap: each candidate β requires only one additional fine‑tuning run of a few hundred gradient steps.

---

### Intuition behind small vs large β

* **β ≪ 1** – The term $\,\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\,$ in Eq. (7) becomes tiny, so the sigmoid inside the loss sees almost only the *difference* of those logits.  The gradient therefore heavily rewards any example where the model still prefers $y_{l}$ over $y_{w}$; consequently it pushes probability mass from dispreferred completions back onto the reference distribution.  The resulting policy changes very little and retains most of its original style and diversity.

* **β ≫ 1** – The log‑ratio is amplified, making it easier for the sigmoid to become saturated even when $\pi_{\theta}$ already deviates modestly from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  The loss then behaves more like an *unweighted* preference loss: it mainly tries to increase $\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})$ and decrease $\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})$ without caring much about staying close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  This yields higher reward scores but at the cost of larger KL and potentially more mode collapse.

---

### Interaction with generation temperature

Temperature $T$ controls how sharply probabilities are turned into tokens at inference time:
$$
p_T(y)=\frac{\exp(\log \pi_{\theta}(y)/T)}{\sum_{y'}\exp(\log \pi_{\theta}(y')/T)}.
$$

When you increase $T$, two effects interact with β:

1. **Effective KL scaling** – Raising $T$ flattens the distribution, which *reduces* the average KL between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  Thus a model trained with a relatively large β (permitting a big KL during training) may behave similarly to a model trained with a smaller β if you later sample with high temperature.

2. **Reward vs fluency trade‑off** – A high temperature injects randomness that can drown out subtle reward signals learned by DPO.  Empirically (Section 5.2) DPO remains robust across a range of temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance degrades noticeably when $T>0.5$.  This robustness stems from DPO’s explicit KL regularisation being baked into each gradient step; PPO must rely on an external value baseline that becomes noisy when sampling is too stochastic.

Consequently, when you plan to generate with a particular temperature, you can *compensate* by adjusting β: if you intend to use a low temperature (e.g., greedy or $T=0.2$) and care about staying close to SFT, pick a smaller β; if you will generate with $T=1.0$ and are willing to accept more divergence for higher reward, a larger β works better.

---

### Summary

* **β is not arbitrary** – it directly controls the average KL‑budget of the fine‑tuned policy.  
* In practice one selects β by **targeting a KL value**: run a short sweep over $\{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$, measure validation KL and pick the one closest to your budget.  
* Small β ⇒ strong KL regularisation (conservative updates); large β ⇒ aggressive reward maximisation (more drift).  
* Generation temperature rescales effective KL; therefore choose β consistent with your intended sampling temperature—low‑temperature generation pairs well with smaller β, high‑temperature generation tolerates larger β.

This procedure matches exactly what authors did in their experiments: they varied β, plotted reward–KL frontiers, and then chose a β that gave them their desired trade‑off without any further RL‑specific tuning.
