\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What is the intuition behind using group‑based advantage normalization in GSPO?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO compute \(\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}\). How does this ‘relative advantage’ differ from a vanilla advantage estimate using a value model, and why is it important for stability when the reward is defined at the sequence level?
Answer:
Both GRPO and GSPO replace the usual **value‑model‑based advantage** that appears in classic PPO with a *relative* advantage that is computed only from the rewards of the responses that belong to the same query‑wise group.  
Formally the two methods compute  

$$
\widehat A_i \;=\;
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j\in\{1\dots G\}} r(x,y_j)}
     {\operatorname{std}_{j\in\{1\dots G\}} r(x,y_j)} ,
$$

where $G$ is the number of sampled responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ for a single query $x$.  

### Why this differs from a vanilla advantage  

In the usual PPO setting the advantage of a token (or a whole trajectory) is

$$
A = r - V_\phi(x,\cdot),
$$

with $V_\phi$ a learned value network that tries to predict the *expected* reward of the current policy.  This estimate has two drawbacks when we are dealing with **sequence‑level rewards** such as those produced by a verifier:

1. **Bias from an imperfect value model** – training a separate value network that works well on long, diverse generations is hard; any systematic error directly contaminates the advantage signal and can lead to misguided policy updates.

2. **Mismatch of granularity** – the reward that we receive is attached to an entire response $y$, but the PPO objective still distributes an advantage to each token.  The token‑wise signal therefore has to be inferred indirectly from a scalar that actually refers to the whole sequence.

The *group‑based* normalisation sidesteps both problems.  Instead of trying to predict an absolute expected reward, we only look at how each sampled response compares **relative to its siblings** in the same batch. The mean subtraction removes any global shift (e.g., a change in overall difficulty of queries), while division by the standard deviation rescales the signal so that it has unit variance. In other words, $\widehat A_i$ tells us whether response $i$ is better or worse than “average” for that particular query, without ever having to estimate what “average” should be.

### Why this relative advantage is important for stability

Sequence‑level RL for language models typically works with **large groups** (often dozens of responses per query) and **long sequences** (hundreds of tokens).  In such a regime two sources of variance can easily blow up:

* **Reward variance across queries** – different prompts have very different difficulty levels; a raw reward might be 0.9 for an easy prompt and 0.2 for a hard one.  If we feed these raw numbers directly into PPO, the optimizer will try to push the policy toward producing high‑reward responses on easy prompts while ignoring hard prompts, producing an unstable learning signal.

* **High variance of token‑level importance ratios** – GRPO still uses token‑level likelihood ratios $w_{i,t}$.  For MoE models these ratios fluctuate dramatically because routing decisions change between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$.  The paper shows that this volatility makes many tokens get clipped, yet the remaining gradients are very noisy, which eventually causes catastrophic collapse.

The group‑based normalisation eliminates both issues:

1. **Centering per query** – subtracting the group mean forces the advantage to sum to zero within each query, guaranteeing that positive and negative updates balance locally.  This prevents any single query from dominating the gradient direction.

2. **Unit variance scaling** – dividing by the group standard deviation ensures that the magnitude of $\widehat A_i$ is comparable across groups, even when some queries are intrinsically harder.  Consequently, clipping thresholds can be set globally (the paper uses values on the order of $10^{-4}$) without needing per‑query tuning.

3. **Sequence‑level consistency** – because every token in a response receives the same $\widehat A_i$, the gradient contributed by a response is simply its log‑likelihood summed over tokens multiplied by a *single* scalar weight (the sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$).  All tokens are weighted equally, removing the token‑wise variance that plagued GRPO and leading to much cleaner gradient estimates (see Eq.\ (10) vs Eq.\ (11) in the paper).

When rewards are defined at the *sequence* level, this alignment between reward granularity and optimisation granularity makes the learning signal far less noisy, which in turn yields the dramatic stability gains reported for GSPO—including stable training of large MoE models without auxiliary tricks such as Routing Replay.

In summary, the group‑based advantage normalisation replaces an unreliable value‑model estimate with a simple, data‑driven relative ranking among responses sharing the same query.  By centring and scaling per group it neutralises query‑level reward heterogeneity and, together with sequence‑level importance weights, produces low‑variance gradients that keep training stable even when rewards are only available after an entire response has been generated.
