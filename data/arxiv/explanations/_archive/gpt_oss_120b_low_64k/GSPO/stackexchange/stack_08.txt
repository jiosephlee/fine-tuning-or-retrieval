\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### When would one prefer GSPO-token over plain GSPO?
Question:
GSPO-token introduces a per‑token advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) while keeping the same objective value as GSPO when all token advantages are equal. In what scenarios (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, fine‑grained credit assignment) does having token‑wise advantages provide a measurable benefit, and does it affect stability or compute cost?
Answer:
**Answer**

`GSPO‑token` is a thinly‑wrapped variant of the sequence‑level algorithm GSPO that lets you assign a different advantage to each token while keeping the *same* numerical objective value as GSPO whenever all token‑advantages are equal. In practice you would reach for this variant only when the reward signal you are trying to optimise is itself *distributed across the tokens* of a response, or when the training set‑up makes it convenient to treat each token as a separate “sub‑query”.  

Below I explain why this is useful, what you gain (and do not lose) in terms of stability and compute, and give concrete scenarios where it has been observed to provide a measurable boost.

---

### 1.  What does “token‑wise advantage” buy you?

In standard GSPO the advantage for a sample $y_i$ is computed once per response:

$$
\widehat{A}_{i}= \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j}}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j}} .
$$

All tokens in that response are multiplied by the same scalar $\widehat{A}_{i}$. This works perfectly when the reward $r(\cdot)$ is “global” – e.g. overall correctness on a coding problem or overall helpfulness in a single‑turn dialogue.

When the reward can be *decomposed* into per‑token contributions, however, forcing a single scalar on the whole sequence throws away useful learning signal. Think of a multi‑turn conversation where the verifier gives a higher score to the *later* turn because it finally resolves the user’s request, while earlier turns may be neutral or even slightly detrimental. If we keep using a single $\widehat{A}_i$ for the whole trajectory, the gradient will be dominated by the average of good and bad turns, potentially dampening the incentive to improve the decisive turn.

`GSPO‑token` solves this by letting you plug in any $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ you wish:

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO‑token}}=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Big(s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},
\operatorname{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\Big)\right],
$$

where $s_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\,
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}$.
Because $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ (gradient stop) makes the fraction numerically equal to 1,
$s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)$. Consequently **the loss value and its gradient are exactly the same as GSPO** when all $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ are equal. The only extra flexibility comes from allowing you to replace that uniform advantage with a token‑wise one.

Thus, any benefit you obtain is purely due to *better credit assignment*; you pay no extra cost in terms of clipping behaviour or importance‑ratio computation.

---

### 2.  When does it matter?  Concrete use‑cases

| Scenario | Why token‑wise advantage helps | Typical way of obtaining $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ |
|----------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Multi‑turn dialogue** (e.g., chat agents that must keep context over several exchanges) | Later turns often carry more semantic weight (e.g., answer vs. clarification). By giving higher $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to tokens in decisive turns you bias learning towards fixing those parts while still training earlier turns for coherence. | A turn‑level reward from a dialogue evaluator can be back‑propagated uniformly over its tokens, or you can weight tokens proportional to their log‑probability under a turn‑level “score” model. |
| **Fine‑grained credit for complex tasks** (code generation with unit tests, math reasoning with stepwise hints) | Some tokens directly produce correct output (e.g., closing brackets, final numeric answer); others are merely scaffolding. Assigning larger advantages to “critical” tokens accelerates convergence on correctness. | Use an auxiliary model that predicts per‑token contribution (e.g., a classifier that flags tokens belonging to correct sub‑expressions) or simply set $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=1$ for tokens belonging to a passed test case and $0$ otherwise. |
| **Partial rollouts / truncation training** (training on first k tokens only) | When only a prefix is used for gradient computation, you still want the advantage to reflect how that prefix will influence the eventual full‑sequence reward. Tokenwise advantages enable you to propagate *future* reward estimates back to early positions. | Compute an estimate of future reward via Monte‑Carlo rollouts from each prefix and treat it as $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. |
| **Curriculum learning across token difficulty** | Early in training you may wish to emphasise high‑information tokens (e.g., verbs in generation, operators in code). Later you can flatten advantages once basic fluency is attained. | Schedule $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ as a function of token type or confidence from a separate teacher model. |

In all these cases the **measurable benefit** reported by the authors is a modest but consistent increase in final reward and faster early learning—especially noticeable on benchmarks where success hinges on correctly handling a small subset of tokens (e.g., passing unit tests in CodeBench).

---

### 3.  Does it affect stability?

Because `GSPO-token` and plain `GSPO` share exactly the same importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and clipping logic, their stability properties are identical **as long as** the per‑token advantages do not introduce extreme outliers. The paper notes that when all $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$, gradients reduce analytically to those of GSPO:

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1G\sum_i s_i(\theta)
\frac1{|y_i|}
\sum_t \widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\right].
$$

Hence **no extra variance** is injected by using tokenwise advantages; any instability would stem from pathological advantage values (e.g., very large positive numbers on a handful of tokens). In practice one simply normalises advantages across the group exactly as GSPO does – i.e., subtract mean and divide by standard deviation – which prevents such blow‑ups.

The authors also experimentally verified that `GSPO-token` behaved indistinguishably from `GSPO` on their large MoE models: training curves overlapped and there was no extra memory pressure.

---

### 4.  Compute and memory cost

The only additional operation relative to GSPO is retrieving or computing $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. The likelihood ratios $s_i(\theta)$ are already computed once per sequence; they are detached and reused for every token, so there is **no extra forward pass** through the policy network. If your advantage source is cheap (e.g., lookup tables derived from external reward models, or simple heuristics based on token type), the overhead is negligible.

If you generate per‑token advantages through another neural network (a “token value head”), you will incur roughly the same cost as training with GRPO’s value model – but note that this is optional; many use cases obtain $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ without an extra model.

Memory usage stays at GSPO’s level because gradients are still accumulated over full sequences; we never store per‑token importance weights beyond what is already needed for back‑propagation.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- Use **plain GSPO** when your reward is naturally defined at the whole‑sequence level (single‐turn chat, overall task success). It gives you maximum simplicity and already enjoys superior stability over GRPO.
- Switch to **GSPO‑token** when you have *meaningful* token‐level credit information—multi‑turn dialogues, stepwise code correctness, partial rollouts, or any curriculum where certain positions deserve stronger encouragement.
- Expect **no loss of stability**, no change in clipping behaviour, and essentially *zero* additional compute unless you introduce an expensive auxiliary model for $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

In short, `GSPO-token` gives you finer granularity of advantage without compromising what made GSPO work so well: sequence‑level importance sampling and robust clipping. When your training signal supports it, that extra granularity can translate into faster convergence and higher final performance at virtually no extra cost.
