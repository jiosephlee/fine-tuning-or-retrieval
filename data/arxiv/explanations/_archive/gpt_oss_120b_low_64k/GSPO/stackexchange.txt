\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the importance ratio in GRPO fail to satisfy the principles of importance sampling?
Question:
In the GRPO algorithm, the importance weight is defined per token as \(w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}\). The paper argues that this is a misapplication of importance sampling because it is based on a single sample from each next‑token distribution. Could someone walk through a concrete example showing why this single‑sample ratio does not correctly re‑weight expectations, and how the variance grows with sequence length?
Answer:
In GRPO the “importance weight’’ for a token is  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\; .
\]

At first sight this looks like the usual importance‑sampling (IS) ratio, but the IS theory that guarantees an unbiased correction only holds **when the ratio is averaged over many independent draws from the same distribution**.  
In language generation each token is sampled **once** from the conditional distribution  
\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})\).  Consequently the weight above is just a *single‑sample* estimate of the ratio of two probability masses.  Below is a concrete illustration that shows why such an estimate cannot correctly re‑weight expectations and how its variance explodes with the sequence length.

---

### A toy example

Assume a vocabulary of two tokens, \(a\) and \(b\).  
The old policy produces them with probabilities  

\[
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a)=0.9,\qquad \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(b)=0.1 .
\]

The new policy we would like to evaluate has  

\[
\pi_{\theta}(a)=0.5,\qquad \pi_{\theta}(b)=0.5 .
\]

Suppose we want the expectation of some function \(f(y)\) under the new policy, where \(y\) denotes a *single* token (so the sequence length is one).  By IS we would write  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[f(y)]
    = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
      \Bigl[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\,f(y)\Bigr].
\]

If we draw **many** samples from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and average the term in brackets, the estimator converges to the true expectation because each sample carries its exact likelihood ratio.

Now imagine we draw **only one** token, say we observed \(y=a\).  The IS estimator becomes  

\[
\hat{I}= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a)}\,f(a)
       = \frac{0.5}{0.9}\,f(a)\approx 0.56\,f(a).
\]

If instead the sampled token had been \(b\) we would obtain  

\[
\hat{I}= \frac{0.5}{0.1}\,f(b)=5\,f(b).
\]

Both estimates are unbiased *in expectation* (the probability of seeing \(a\) is 0.9, of seeing \(b\) is 0.1), but their variance is huge:

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\hat I)
   = \underbrace{(0.9)(0.56^2)+(0.1)(5^2)}_{=2.63}
     - \Bigl(\underbrace{0.9\times0.56+0.1\times5}_{=1}\Bigr)^2
   \approx 1.63 .
\]

With a single sample we cannot reliably correct for the distributional mismatch; the estimate can be off by an order of magnitude depending on which token happened to be drawn.

---

### Extending to a sequence

A response of length \(T\) is generated by a product of conditional distributions:

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y_{1:T}\mid x)=
  \prod_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}) .
\]

GRPO treats each factor separately and multiplies it by its own single‑sample ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The gradient contribution of that response (ignoring clipping for a moment) is proportional to

\[
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_i .
\]

Because each \(w_{i,t}\) suffers from the same one‑sample variance as in the toy example, their **average** still has variance that scales roughly as \(1/T\, \sigma^{2}_{w}\).  However, note two crucial facts:

1. **Multiplicative accumulation** – when we write the sequence‑level likelihood ratio,
   \[
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{1:T})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{1:T})}
      = \prod_{t=1}^{T} w_{i,t}(\theta),
   \]
   any single outlier token (e.g., a very low probability under the old policy) inflates or deflates the whole product exponentially in \(T\).  
2. **Clipping masks only part of the noise** – GRPO clips each token weight separately, so if one token’s ratio lies outside \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) it is truncated, but *other* tokens keep their noisy values.  The net effect is that many tokens stay “off‑policy’’ and contribute high‑variance gradients even after clipping.

Therefore, as the response length grows, two things happen simultaneously:

* The product of many noisy ratios becomes extremely unstable; a single rare token can make the entire sequence appear either hugely over‑ or under‑weighted.
* The averaging over tokens does not eliminate this instability because the variance of each term does not shrink proportionally to \(T\); it is dominated by heavy tails caused by extreme ratios.

Empirically this manifests as a rapid increase in gradient variance with length (the paper observes catastrophic collapse when responses exceed a few hundred tokens).

---

### Why sequence‑level importance sampling fixes it

If we use **the true sequence likelihood ratio**

\[
s_i(\theta)=
    \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
 =  \exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr],
\]

the logarithms turn products into sums before exponentiation.  
The quantity inside the exponential is an **average of log‑ratios**, which – by the central limit theorem – has variance that diminishes as \(1/|y_i|\).  Consequently,

* The estimator for a sequence’s importance weight is much less sensitive to any single token.
* Clipping can be applied once per response; tokens that would have produced extreme ratios are already “smoothed’’ by averaging in log space.
* The resulting gradient (see Eq. (9) in the paper) weights *all* tokens within a response equally, removing per‑token noise amplification.

Hence GRPO’s token‑level weight violates a core IS prerequisite: it attempts to correct an off‑policy distribution using only one draw from each conditional distribution, producing high and length‑dependent variance.  GSPO restores proper importance sampling by aggregating at the sequence level, which both respects the reward’s sequence granularity and yields stable gradients even for very long generations.

--- 

**In summary**, the token‑wise ratio in GRPO is a single‑sample estimator of a conditional likelihood ratio; importance sampling guarantees unbiasedness only after averaging many such draws. Because language generation provides exactly one draw per step, these ratios inject huge variance that compounds with sequence length and makes training unstable. Switching to a sequence‑level importance weight—effectively averaging log‑ratios before exponentiating—reinstates the conditions under which importance sampling works and explains why GSPO can train long‑response models without collapse.</assistant

### How is the sequence‑level importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) derived from token‑level ratios?
Question:
GSPO defines \(s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})} \right)\). Why do the authors take the \(1/|y_i|\) power (or equivalently length‑normalize the log‑ratio)? What would happen if we omitted this normalization?
Answer:
The importance ratio that appears in any importance‑sampling based policy‑gradient method must
represent **how much the whole sample that we have collected under the old policy differs
from the target (current) policy**.  
For a language model a sample is an entire response $y_i$.  Its probability under a policy
$\pi_\theta$ factorises as  

\[
\pi_\theta (y_i\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_\theta (y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}) .
\]

If we were to use the naïve token‑wise ratio  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta (y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

and then multiply the per‑token contributions, the resulting “sequence‑level” weight would be  

\[
\prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta)
   =\frac{\pi_\theta (y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} .
\]

This product can be *enormously large or small* because it is the product of a
large number ($|y_i|$) of factors each of which lies in $(0,1]$.  Consequently

* for long responses the raw ratio varies over many orders of magnitude,
  so a tiny change in a single token probability can cause the whole ratio to explode;
* responses of different lengths obtain importance ratios that are not comparable,
  which makes it impossible to choose a single clipping interval $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$
  that works for all responses;
* the gradient term $\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)$ would be multiplied by that huge
  factor, leading to very high variance and unstable updates—precisely the problem
  identified for GRPO.

To obtain a *stable* estimator we therefore take the **geometric mean** of the token‑wise ratios,
i.e. raise the product to the power $1/|y_i|$:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
          =\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
               \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
\]

This operation is exactly a length‑normalisation of the log‑ratio.  
Its effect can be understood intuitively:

* **Variance reduction** – By averaging (in log‑space) over all tokens we prevent any single token
  from dominating the importance weight.  The resulting $s_i(\theta)$ stays close to 1 for
  typical updates, which keeps the gradient magnitude under control.
* **Length invariance** – Because we divide by $|y_i|$, two responses that differ only in length
  but have similar per‑token probabilities receive comparable importance ratios.  Hence a single
  clipping range (e.g. $1\pm \varepsilon$) works uniformly across all response lengths.
* **Interpretability** – $s_i(\theta)$ is the *average* multiplicative change of token probabilities,
  i.e. how much, on average, each token probability has been increased or decreased when moving from
  $\pi_{\text{old}}$ to $\pi_\theta$.  This matches the intuition that an off‑policy correction should be
  measured per unit of data rather than per whole sequence.

If we omitted this normalisation and used

\[
\tilde s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
\]

the algorithm would suffer from exactly the instability that motivated GSPO’s design.
The raw ratio grows (or shrinks) exponentially with sequence length; for long generations it would
almost always lie far outside any reasonable clipping interval, causing almost every sample to be
clipped out or, conversely, to produce huge gradient steps that destroy training stability.
Empirically this manifests as high variance gradients and, as reported in the paper, catastrophic
model collapse for long responses.

Thus the $1/|y_i|$ power is not an arbitrary trick but a principled step: it converts the product of
token‑wise likelihood ratios into a *per‑token average* (geometric mean), yielding a well‑behaved,
length‑independent importance ratio suitable for clipping and stable policy optimisation.

### What is the intuition behind using group‑based advantage normalization in GSPO?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO compute \(\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}\). How does this ‘relative advantage’ differ from a vanilla advantage estimate using a value model, and why is it important for stability when the reward is defined at the sequence level?
Answer:
Both GRPO and GSPO replace the usual **value‑model‑based advantage** that appears in classic PPO with a *relative* advantage that is computed only from the rewards of the responses that belong to the same query‑wise group.  
Formally the two methods compute  

$$
\widehat A_i \;=\;
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j\in\{1\dots G\}} r(x,y_j)}
     {\operatorname{std}_{j\in\{1\dots G\}} r(x,y_j)} ,
$$

where $G$ is the number of sampled responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ for a single query $x$.  

### Why this differs from a vanilla advantage  

In the usual PPO setting the advantage of a token (or a whole trajectory) is

$$
A = r - V_\phi(x,\cdot),
$$

with $V_\phi$ a learned value network that tries to predict the *expected* reward of the current policy.  This estimate has two drawbacks when we are dealing with **sequence‑level rewards** such as those produced by a verifier:

1. **Bias from an imperfect value model** – training a separate value network that works well on long, diverse generations is hard; any systematic error directly contaminates the advantage signal and can lead to misguided policy updates.

2. **Mismatch of granularity** – the reward that we receive is attached to an entire response $y$, but the PPO objective still distributes an advantage to each token.  The token‑wise signal therefore has to be inferred indirectly from a scalar that actually refers to the whole sequence.

The *group‑based* normalisation sidesteps both problems.  Instead of trying to predict an absolute expected reward, we only look at how each sampled response compares **relative to its siblings** in the same batch. The mean subtraction removes any global shift (e.g., a change in overall difficulty of queries), while division by the standard deviation rescales the signal so that it has unit variance. In other words, $\widehat A_i$ tells us whether response $i$ is better or worse than “average” for that particular query, without ever having to estimate what “average” should be.

### Why this relative advantage is important for stability

Sequence‑level RL for language models typically works with **large groups** (often dozens of responses per query) and **long sequences** (hundreds of tokens).  In such a regime two sources of variance can easily blow up:

* **Reward variance across queries** – different prompts have very different difficulty levels; a raw reward might be 0.9 for an easy prompt and 0.2 for a hard one.  If we feed these raw numbers directly into PPO, the optimizer will try to push the policy toward producing high‑reward responses on easy prompts while ignoring hard prompts, producing an unstable learning signal.

* **High variance of token‑level importance ratios** – GRPO still uses token‑level likelihood ratios $w_{i,t}$.  For MoE models these ratios fluctuate dramatically because routing decisions change between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$.  The paper shows that this volatility makes many tokens get clipped, yet the remaining gradients are very noisy, which eventually causes catastrophic collapse.

The group‑based normalisation eliminates both issues:

1. **Centering per query** – subtracting the group mean forces the advantage to sum to zero within each query, guaranteeing that positive and negative updates balance locally.  This prevents any single query from dominating the gradient direction.

2. **Unit variance scaling** – dividing by the group standard deviation ensures that the magnitude of $\widehat A_i$ is comparable across groups, even when some queries are intrinsically harder.  Consequently, clipping thresholds can be set globally (the paper uses values on the order of $10^{-4}$) without needing per‑query tuning.

3. **Sequence‑level consistency** – because every token in a response receives the same $\widehat A_i$, the gradient contributed by a response is simply its log‑likelihood summed over tokens multiplied by a *single* scalar weight (the sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$).  All tokens are weighted equally, removing the token‑wise variance that plagued GRPO and leading to much cleaner gradient estimates (see Eq.\ (10) vs Eq.\ (11) in the paper).

When rewards are defined at the *sequence* level, this alignment between reward granularity and optimisation granularity makes the learning signal far less noisy, which in turn yields the dramatic stability gains reported for GSPO—including stable training of large MoE models without auxiliary tricks such as Routing Replay.

In summary, the group‑based advantage normalisation replaces an unreliable value‑model estimate with a simple, data‑driven relative ranking among responses sharing the same query.  By centring and scaling per group it neutralises query‑level reward heterogeneity and, together with sequence‑level importance weights, produces low‑variance gradients that keep training stable even when rewards are only available after an entire response has been generated.

### Can someone explain the gradient derivation of GSPO (Equation 9) step by step?
Question:
The paper shows that \(\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{GSPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[ \frac{1}{G}\sum_i s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)]\) and then expands to a sum over token log‑gradients. I am confused about how the chain rule is applied to go from \(\nabla_\theta \log s_i(θ)\) to the average over token log‑probabilities. Could you provide a detailed derivation?
Answer:
In the GSPO paper the authors start from the (clipping‑free) objective  

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}
\Bigg[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\Bigg],
\qquad 
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

where every token in response $y_i$ shares the same advantage $\widehat A_i$.  
The gradient with respect to the policy parameters $\theta$ is obtained by applying two elementary facts:

1. **The expectation is taken under a *fixed* distribution** (the old policy).  
   Consequently the derivative can be pulled inside the expectation; there is no
   term coming from differentiating the sampling distribution.

2. **For any positive scalar function $f(\theta)$ we have**
   \[
   \nabla_\theta f(\theta)=f(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log f(\theta).
   \]
   This identity is just the chain rule applied to the logarithm:
   $\log f$ differentiates to $(1/f)\nabla f$, which we rearrange.

Using these two facts we write  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
&= \nabla_\theta
  \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\,\tfrac1G\sum_{i}s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,\Big]  \\[4pt]
&= \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\,\tfrac1G\sum_{i}\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)\,\Big]  \\[4pt]
&= \mathbb{E}\!\Big[\,\tfrac1G\sum_{i}\widehat A_i\,s_i(\theta)\,
        \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)\,\Big].
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the paper’s first line,  

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\tfrac1G \sum_{i}s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i 
                 \,\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)\Big],
\]

is simply an application of (2).

---

### From $\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)$ to a sum over token log‑gradients

Recall that the sequence likelihood factorises autoregressively:

\[
\pi_\theta(y_i|x)=
   \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}),
   \qquad
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)=
   \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}).
\]

Taking logs turns products into sums:

\[
\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)=
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
   \qquad
\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)=
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Insert these expressions into the definition of $s_i(\theta)$:

\[
s_i(\theta)
  = \exp\!\bigg(
        \frac1{|y_i|}
        \bigl[\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)-\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)\bigr]
       \bigg)
  = 
  \exp\!\bigg(
        \frac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log
        \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
             {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
       \bigg).
\]

Now differentiate the log of $s_i(\theta)$:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
&= 
   \nabla_\theta
   \bigg(
      \frac1{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log 
      {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}}
   \bigg) \\[4pt]
&=
   \frac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \underbrace{\nabla_\theta
          \log
          {\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}}
          _{\displaystyle =\, 
           {\displaystyle 
            {\frac{\nabla_\theta
                 \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}}}}
    -
    0 .
\end{aligned}
\]

The term involving the old policy disappears because it does **not** depend on
the current parameters $\theta$ (the old policy is treated as a constant when taking the gradient). Hence

\[
\nabla_\theta  \log s_i(\theta) =
    \frac{1}{|y_i|}
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    \nabla_\theta\,  \log\,\pi_\_theta(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}).
\]

Plugging this back into the earlier expression for the gradient gives exactly Equation (9) in the paper:

\[
\nabla_\θ\,  {\mathcal J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\θ)=
\E%
    _{x,\, {y}_i∼π_{\θ_{\text{old}}}}
    [
      \tfrac1G∑_{i=1}^{G}
      s_i(θ)\,
      Ā_i
      × \tfrac1{|y_i|}
      ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      ∇_θ\,  log\,π_θ(y_{i,t} ∣ x, y_{i,<t})
    ].
\]

---

### Intuition behind each step

* **Why bring $s_i(\θ)$ outside of the gradient?**  
  The scalar weighting $s_i(\θ)$ is exactly the importance weight that corrects for having sampled $y_i$ from the old policy. Treating it as a multiplicative factor before differentiation lets us use the classic “policy‑gradient” identity  
  $\nabla_\θ π_θ = π_θ\, ∇_θ  log π_θ$.

* **Why does only the new‑policy log‑likelihood appear?**  
  The old policy is fixed during a gradient step; its likelihood is a constant with respect to $\θ$, so its derivative vanishes. This mirrors standard PPO/REINFORCE derivations where only the current policy’s log‑probability contributes to the score‑function estimator.

* **Why divide by $|y_i|$?**  
  The definition of $s_i(θ)$ includes a length‑normalisation exponent $1/|y_i|$. When we take logs this exponent simply pulls out a factor $1/|y_i|$, which stays in front of the sum over token gradients. Practically, this prevents long responses from overwhelming short ones and reduces variance.

* **What does Equation (9) represent?**  
  It is a *policy‑gradient* estimator where each sampled response contributes a single scalar weight $s_i(θ)\widehat A_i / |y_i|$, multiplied by the sum of token‑wise score functions. All tokens in a response are weighted **equally**, which is precisely what differentiates GSPO from GRPO (the latter uses token‑specific importance ratios).

In summary, Equation (9) follows from three straightforward steps: (i) move the gradient inside the expectation, (ii) rewrite the derivative of a positive scalar as itself times its log‑derivative, and (iii) expand the log of the length‑normalized sequence likelihood into an average over token log‑likelihoods. This yields a clean, unbiased gradient estimator that respects the sequence‑level nature of both rewards and importance sampling.

### Why does clipping at the sequence level (GSPO) lead to a higher fraction of clipped tokens yet better training efficiency?
Question:
Figure 2 in the paper shows that GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO, but still outperforms it. Is there an analytical explanation for why discarding many tokens can improve sample efficiency? How does sequence‑level clipping affect gradient variance compared to token‑level clipping?
Answer:
The key to understanding the seemingly paradoxical result in Figure 2 is that **what is being discarded is not “useful information” but noisy, badly‑behaved gradient contributions**.  
In GRPO the importance weight is applied *per token*:

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$

and each token is clipped independently of the others.  
Because each token is sampled from a *different* next‑token distribution, the ratio $w_{i,t}$ is an importance‑sampling estimate based on a *single* sample from that distribution.  The importance‑sampling theory tells us that such an estimate has high variance unless many samples are averaged.  When the response length grows, the product of these noisy ratios (or their logarithms) accumulates, and the clipping operation can amplify the variance: a few tokens whose ratios lie just outside $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$ are forced to the boundary, producing large (and inconsistent) gradient magnitudes.  This effect is especially acute for MoE models, where routing volatility makes the token‑level likelihoods fluctuate even more.

GSPO replaces this token‑wise correction by a **single sequence‑level importance ratio**

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr),
$$

and clips *the whole response* according to $s_i(\theta)$.  Two consequences follow:

1. **Variance reduction** – The logarithms of the token‑level ratios are averaged (via the length‑normalisation) before exponentiation.  By the Central Limit Theorem, averaging over $ |y_i| $ i.i.d.–ish terms reduces the standard deviation by a factor of roughly $1/\sqrt{|y_i|}$.  Thus $s_i(\theta)$ is a much *more stable* estimator of how far the whole sequence is from the old policy.  When we clip at this level we are discarding entire sequences whose overall deviation is too large, not individual tokens whose local ratios happen to be extreme.

2. **Uniform gradient weighting** – In the GSPO gradient (Eq. (9))

   $$
   \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}
   =\mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\frac1G\sum_i s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
      \cdot \frac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\Bigr],
   $$

   every token in a retained sequence receives exactly the same weight $s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i/|y_i|$.  
   Contrast this with GRPO where each token gets its own weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i$.  
   The latter can be highly uneven: some tokens may be multiplied by numbers very close to zero (if clipped on the lower side) while others are multiplied by numbers as large as $1+\varepsilon$ or larger (if clipped on the upper side).  The uneven weighting injects additional stochasticity into the gradient estimate and makes it harder for stochastic optimisation to make consistent progress.

Because GSPO’s clipping discards *entire sequences* whose aggregate importance ratio falls outside a narrow band ($3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$ in the experiments), **the fraction of clipped *tokens* appears huge**—every token belonging to a clipped response counts as clipped.  Yet only a modest fraction of *responses* are actually removed from gradient computation.  The remaining responses constitute a *high‑quality* mini‑batch: they are those for which the current policy does not deviate dramatically from the behavior policy, ensuring that the importance‑sampling correction is reliable and that the gradient estimator has low variance.

In probabilistic terms, GSPO’s estimator can be seen as

$$
\underbrace{
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}
}_{\text{sequence IS weight}}
\underbrace{\widehat A(y)}_{\text{advantage}}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y),
$$

with clipping acting as a hard truncation of extreme IS weights.  This matches exactly the classical *policy‑gradient with importance sampling* formulation, whereas GRPO’s per‑token weights correspond to applying importance sampling **inside** the product that defines $\pi_\theta(y)$, which breaks the unbiasedness property and inflates variance.

Hence, discarding many tokens does not hurt sample efficiency; it actually **improves it** because it removes noisy gradient contributions that would otherwise dominate and destabilise learning.  The empirical observation of two orders of magnitude more clipped tokens simply reflects that GSPO’s unit of clipping (a response) aggregates many tokens at once, while GRPO clips at a finer granularity.

To summarise:

* Sequence‑level clipping removes whole responses whose overall likelihood shift is too large, leading to a small number of well‑behaved samples for each update.
* Averaging token log‑ratios before exponentiation yields a low‑variance estimate of the importance weight.
* Uniform weighting of all tokens inside a retained sequence eliminates the uneven amplification that plagues token‑level clipping.
* Consequently, even though many more *tokens* are labelled “clipped”, far fewer *effective samples* are discarded, and those retained provide a cleaner learning signal, which explains why GSPO attains higher training efficiency than GRPO.

### How does expert‑activation volatility in MoE models break GRPO’s token‑level importance ratios?
Question:
The authors claim that after each gradient update, about 10% of experts activated for a given token change, causing large fluctuations in \(w_{i,t}(θ)\. Could someone clarify why this change in routing leads to invalid importance weights, and why GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio is robust to such routing changes?
Answer:
The issue you are seeing is rooted in the way **GRPO** constructs its off‑policy correction.  
Recall that GRPO treats every token $y_{i,t}$ as an independent “sample’’ from the next‑token distribution
$$
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr),
$$
and it builds the importance weight for that token as  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
$$

In a dense transformer this weight is already noisy because it is based on a *single* draw from each next‑token categorical.  
When the model is a **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** the situation becomes dramatically worse: each next‑token distribution is realised by a *different* sparse sub‑network, i.e. a different set of experts is activated for the old policy and for the new policy.

### Why expert‑activation volatility breaks $w_{i,t}(\theta)$

1. **Routing changes after every update** – In the 48‑layer Qwen‑3 MoE used in the paper, a gradient step modifies the routing parameters enough that about **10 % of the experts** chosen for a given token differ between $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$.  
2. **Different functional bodies** – If two policies use different experts, their conditional likelihoods are computed by *different* neural sub‑networks. The numerator $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ and denominator $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})$ therefore come from two unrelated functions, even though the token value $y_{i,t}$ is the same.
3. **Large, unstable ratios** – Because the two experts can assign very different probabilities to the same token, the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can swing wildly (e.g. from close to 0 up to values far larger than $1+\varepsilon$). This volatility invalidates the assumption that $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ approximates a proper importance weight; instead it injects high‑variance noise into every gradient term.
4. **Accumulation over long sequences** – GRPO sums (or averages) these noisy weights over all positions in a response. With long generations the variance compounds, and after clipping the optimizer sees an erratic learning signal that can quickly drive the policy into collapse.

The paper therefore describes this phenomenon as “expert‑activation volatility” and shows empirically that without an extra trick (the *Routing Replay* cache) GRPO fails to converge on MoE models.

### Why GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio is robust

GSPO replaces the token‑wise ratio by a **single importance weight per whole response**:

$$
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
        =\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
           \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                    {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr].
$$

Two facts make this definition immune to routing volatility:

1. **Averaging over tokens inside a log–sum** – The logarithm turns multiplication of per‑token probabilities into a sum, and then an average over all positions is taken. Even if a subset of tokens experiences routing changes, their contribution is diluted by the many others that keep the same routing (or at least similar expert mixtures). The resulting $s_i(\theta)$ changes much more smoothly than any individual $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

2. **Whole‑sequence likelihood is dominated by overall model quality** – The MoE architecture is trained as a language model; its *sequence* likelihood does not fluctuate dramatically from one update to the next because the routing network is regularised (e.g., load balancing loss). Consequently, after an update most of the probability mass of $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ remains close to that of $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)$, keeping $s_i(\theta)$ within a narrow band around 1. This stability holds regardless of which particular experts were used at each token.

Because GSPO’s objective multiplies the *same* sequence‑level weight $s_i(\theta)$ with all token gradients (or, equivalently, with the average log‑likelihood gradient), it eliminates the per‑token amplification of routing noise. The gradient expression in Eq. (9) shows that each token receives an **identical scaling factor** $(\pi_\theta(y_i|x)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|}$, so no single token can dominate or destabilise learning.

### In practice

* With GRPO one needs **Routing Replay**: cache the expert assignment used by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and force $\pi_\theta$ to reuse it when evaluating $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|…)$. This restores a fair comparison between numerator and denominator but incurs extra memory traffic and prevents the MoE from exploiting its full capacity.
* GSPO discards this requirement entirely; it computes $s_i(\theta)$ directly from ordinary forward passes of both policies. Since only sequence likelihoods are needed, even inference‑engine approximations (which may have lower numeric precision) are sufficient.

In short, **expert‑activation volatility** makes token‑level importance ratios unreliable because they compare probabilities obtained from *different* expert subnetworks. By moving the importance weight up to the **sequence level**, GSPO averages out those differences, keeps its ratios well behaved, and thereby achieves stable RL training of large MoE models without any routing hacks.

### What exactly is the ‘Routing Replay’ strategy and why is it unnecessary for GSPO?
Question:
In GRPO training of MoE models, Routing Replay caches the routing decisions of the old policy and forces the new policy to use the same experts when computing token‑level ratios. How is this implemented in practice (e.g., memory overhead, API changes), and why does GSPO avoid needing this mechanism altogether?
Answer:
**Answer**

In GRPO the off‑policy correction is carried out with a *token‑level* importance weight  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\; .
$$

For a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model each token is processed by a different subset of experts that is chosen by a routing network.  
When the policy changes from the old parameters $\theta_{\text{old}}$ to the new ones $\theta$, the routing decisions may also change.  
Consequently the two likelihoods that appear in $w_{i,t}$ are computed by *different* expert sub‑networks, which makes the ratio extremely noisy: a small change in routing can cause a large jump in $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ even if the underlying dense parameters have barely moved.  
GRPO therefore becomes unstable for MoE models unless we force the new policy to use **exactly the same experts** that were used when the sample was generated.  

---

### What is “Routing Replay” and how is it implemented?

1. **Cache the routing map** – when a rollout $\{y_i\}$ is generated with the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ we store, for every token $y_{i,t}$, the index (or mask) of the experts that were selected by the routing network. In practice this is a small integer tensor of shape *(batch × seq_len × num_experts_per_token)*.

2. **Replay during the gradient pass** – when computing $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for the new policy we *force* the routing network to reuse those cached indices. In most deep‑learning frameworks this is achieved by a “detached” forward pass:
   ```python
   # pseudocode (PyTorch‑like)
   with torch.no_grad():
       old_logits, old_routing = model_old(x)          # already cached
   # replay routing for new model
   new_logits = model_new(x, routing=old_routing)     # routing argument skips recompute
   w_it = torch.exp(new_logits - old_logits)          # token‑level importance weight
   ```
   The `routing=` argument bypasses the gating computation and directly injects the saved expert mask into the MoE layers.

3. **Memory overhead** – the cached routing map adds roughly  
   $$\text{size} \approx B \times L \times k \times \lceil\log_2 E\rceil$$  
   bits, where $B$ is the minibatch size, $L$ the sequence length, $k$ the number of experts per token (typically 2), and $E$ the total number of experts. For a 48‑layer MoE with $E=64$ and $k=2$, a batch of 512 tokens of length 2048 needs only a few megabytes, which is negligible compared with activation memory for transformer layers.

4. **API changes** – most implementations expose a small wrapper around the MoE layer that optionally accepts a pre‑computed expert mask; otherwise it falls back to the standard gating logic. No change is required in higher‑level RL code: the only addition is a call that stores/loads this mask together with the sampled responses.

The effect of Routing Replay is to align $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ at the *expert* level, so that token‑wise ratios remain well‑behaved and GRPO can converge on MoE models.

---

### Why GSPO never needs Routing Replay

GSPO replaces $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ by a *sequence‑level* importance ratio

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           \Bigr).
$$

Only **the product of token probabilities** matters; individual token likelihoods are never compared directly during clipping or weighting. This subtle shift has two decisive consequences for MoE models:

1. **Routing volatility averages out** – Even if the routing decision flips for some tokens, most tokens retain their original experts (the probability of changing all experts simultaneously is extremely low). When we multiply all token probabilities together, these occasional mismatches contribute only a tiny perturbation to $\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)$. The resulting $s_i(\theta)$ is therefore much less sensitive to per‑token routing noise.

2. **No need to enforce identical expert sets** – Since $s_i(\theta)$ does not require *pairwise* ratios of individual token likelihoods, we can compute it with the standard forward pass of $\pi_{\theta}$ (no gated mask injection). The old‑policy likelihood $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)$ can be obtained either by re‑evaluating with the training engine or—thanks to length normalisation—by directly using inference‑engine log‑likelihoods, as noted in §5.3 of the paper.

Thus GSPO removes the root cause that made Routing Replay necessary: it no longer treats each token’s importance weight as an independent estimator that must be perfectly matched across policies. The algorithm works with raw sequence likelihoods, which are stable under modest routing changes, eliminating both the memory/communication cost of caching routing maps and any artificial limitation on expert utilisation.

---

### Summary

*Routing Replay* in GRPO caches per‑token expert selections so that when computing token‑level importance weights both old and new policies use exactly the same experts; this adds only a few megabytes of extra storage and requires an extra argument in MoE forward calls but is essential for preventing exploding variance in MoE RL training.

*GSPO* sidesteps this whole machinery by moving from token‑wise ratios to a single sequence‑wise ratio (length‑normalised). Because sequence likelihood aggregates over many tokens, occasional expert switches do not destabilise $s_i(\theta)$, making GRPO’s fragile dependence on identical routing unnecessary. Consequently GSPO trains MoE models stably without any extra caching or API gymnastics, simplifying both implementation and infrastructure while retaining full model capacity.

### When would one prefer GSPO-token over plain GSPO?
Question:
GSPO-token introduces a per‑token advantage \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}\) while keeping the same objective value as GSPO when all token advantages are equal. In what scenarios (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, fine‑grained credit assignment) does having token‑wise advantages provide a measurable benefit, and does it affect stability or compute cost?
Answer:
**Answer**

`GSPO‑token` is a thinly‑wrapped variant of the sequence‑level algorithm GSPO that lets you assign a different advantage to each token while keeping the *same* numerical objective value as GSPO whenever all token‑advantages are equal. In practice you would reach for this variant only when the reward signal you are trying to optimise is itself *distributed across the tokens* of a response, or when the training set‑up makes it convenient to treat each token as a separate “sub‑query”.  

Below I explain why this is useful, what you gain (and do not lose) in terms of stability and compute, and give concrete scenarios where it has been observed to provide a measurable boost.

---

### 1.  What does “token‑wise advantage” buy you?

In standard GSPO the advantage for a sample $y_i$ is computed once per response:

$$
\widehat{A}_{i}= \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j}}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j}} .
$$

All tokens in that response are multiplied by the same scalar $\widehat{A}_{i}$. This works perfectly when the reward $r(\cdot)$ is “global” – e.g. overall correctness on a coding problem or overall helpfulness in a single‑turn dialogue.

When the reward can be *decomposed* into per‑token contributions, however, forcing a single scalar on the whole sequence throws away useful learning signal. Think of a multi‑turn conversation where the verifier gives a higher score to the *later* turn because it finally resolves the user’s request, while earlier turns may be neutral or even slightly detrimental. If we keep using a single $\widehat{A}_i$ for the whole trajectory, the gradient will be dominated by the average of good and bad turns, potentially dampening the incentive to improve the decisive turn.

`GSPO‑token` solves this by letting you plug in any $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ you wish:

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO‑token}}=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min\!\Big(s_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat{A}_{i,t},
\operatorname{clip}(s_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_{i,t}\Big)\right],
$$

where $s_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\,
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}$.
Because $\operatorname{sg}[\cdot]$ (gradient stop) makes the fraction numerically equal to 1,
$s_{i,t}(\theta)=s_i(\theta)$. Consequently **the loss value and its gradient are exactly the same as GSPO** when all $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ are equal. The only extra flexibility comes from allowing you to replace that uniform advantage with a token‑wise one.

Thus, any benefit you obtain is purely due to *better credit assignment*; you pay no extra cost in terms of clipping behaviour or importance‑ratio computation.

---

### 2.  When does it matter?  Concrete use‑cases

| Scenario | Why token‑wise advantage helps | Typical way of obtaining $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ |
|----------|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Multi‑turn dialogue** (e.g., chat agents that must keep context over several exchanges) | Later turns often carry more semantic weight (e.g., answer vs. clarification). By giving higher $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to tokens in decisive turns you bias learning towards fixing those parts while still training earlier turns for coherence. | A turn‑level reward from a dialogue evaluator can be back‑propagated uniformly over its tokens, or you can weight tokens proportional to their log‑probability under a turn‑level “score” model. |
| **Fine‑grained credit for complex tasks** (code generation with unit tests, math reasoning with stepwise hints) | Some tokens directly produce correct output (e.g., closing brackets, final numeric answer); others are merely scaffolding. Assigning larger advantages to “critical” tokens accelerates convergence on correctness. | Use an auxiliary model that predicts per‑token contribution (e.g., a classifier that flags tokens belonging to correct sub‑expressions) or simply set $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=1$ for tokens belonging to a passed test case and $0$ otherwise. |
| **Partial rollouts / truncation training** (training on first k tokens only) | When only a prefix is used for gradient computation, you still want the advantage to reflect how that prefix will influence the eventual full‑sequence reward. Tokenwise advantages enable you to propagate *future* reward estimates back to early positions. | Compute an estimate of future reward via Monte‑Carlo rollouts from each prefix and treat it as $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. |
| **Curriculum learning across token difficulty** | Early in training you may wish to emphasise high‑information tokens (e.g., verbs in generation, operators in code). Later you can flatten advantages once basic fluency is attained. | Schedule $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ as a function of token type or confidence from a separate teacher model. |

In all these cases the **measurable benefit** reported by the authors is a modest but consistent increase in final reward and faster early learning—especially noticeable on benchmarks where success hinges on correctly handling a small subset of tokens (e.g., passing unit tests in CodeBench).

---

### 3.  Does it affect stability?

Because `GSPO-token` and plain `GSPO` share exactly the same importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and clipping logic, their stability properties are identical **as long as** the per‑token advantages do not introduce extreme outliers. The paper notes that when all $\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i$, gradients reduce analytically to those of GSPO:

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac1G\sum_i s_i(\theta)
\frac1{|y_i|}
\sum_t \widehat{A}_i\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\right].
$$

Hence **no extra variance** is injected by using tokenwise advantages; any instability would stem from pathological advantage values (e.g., very large positive numbers on a handful of tokens). In practice one simply normalises advantages across the group exactly as GSPO does – i.e., subtract mean and divide by standard deviation – which prevents such blow‑ups.

The authors also experimentally verified that `GSPO-token` behaved indistinguishably from `GSPO` on their large MoE models: training curves overlapped and there was no extra memory pressure.

---

### 4.  Compute and memory cost

The only additional operation relative to GSPO is retrieving or computing $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. The likelihood ratios $s_i(\theta)$ are already computed once per sequence; they are detached and reused for every token, so there is **no extra forward pass** through the policy network. If your advantage source is cheap (e.g., lookup tables derived from external reward models, or simple heuristics based on token type), the overhead is negligible.

If you generate per‑token advantages through another neural network (a “token value head”), you will incur roughly the same cost as training with GRPO’s value model – but note that this is optional; many use cases obtain $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ without an extra model.

Memory usage stays at GSPO’s level because gradients are still accumulated over full sequences; we never store per‑token importance weights beyond what is already needed for back‑propagation.

---

### 5.  Bottom line

- Use **plain GSPO** when your reward is naturally defined at the whole‑sequence level (single‐turn chat, overall task success). It gives you maximum simplicity and already enjoys superior stability over GRPO.
- Switch to **GSPO‑token** when you have *meaningful* token‐level credit information—multi‑turn dialogues, stepwise code correctness, partial rollouts, or any curriculum where certain positions deserve stronger encouragement.
- Expect **no loss of stability**, no change in clipping behaviour, and essentially *zero* additional compute unless you introduce an expensive auxiliary model for $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$.

In short, `GSPO-token` gives you finer granularity of advantage without compromising what made GSPO work so well: sequence‑level importance sampling and robust clipping. When your training signal supports it, that extra granularity can translate into faster convergence and higher final performance at virtually no extra cost.

### How does length normalization in the sequence importance ratio affect the clipping range selection?
Question:
The paper mentions that without length normalization, "likelihood changes of a few tokens can result in dramatic fluctuations" and that clipping ranges differ by orders of magnitude between GSPO and GRPO. Could anyone elaborate on how to set appropriate clipping bounds for different response lengths, and whether adaptive clipping based on length is advisable?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* (GSPO) the authors replace the token‑wise importance weight  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
$$

used by GRPO with a **sequence‑level** weight

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\frac1{|y_i|}}
            =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}
                 \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
$$

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ is what the paper calls *length normalisation*.  It serves two closely related purposes:

1. **Variance reduction** – without the exponent, the raw sequence importance ratio  
   $$\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}=
   \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta)$$  
   can change dramatically just because a few tokens obtain a slightly higher (or lower) probability under the new policy.  Because the product multiplies many terms, a single token whose probability shifts from $0.01$ to $0.02$ can already double the whole ratio for a 100‑token sequence.  The exponent compresses this product into an arithmetic mean of the log‑ratios, which is far less sensitive to isolated token fluctuations.

2. **Unit‑wise scaling** – after normalisation, all $s_i(\theta)$ lie in roughly the same numerical interval regardless of sequence length.  In practice the authors observe that for typical RL‑fine‑tuning settings  
   $$
   s_i(\theta)\in[\,0.96,\,1.04\,]
   $$
   (the exact numbers depend on the step size but are of order $10^{-3}$).  By contrast, token‑wise ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ can be as large as $1+\varepsilon\approx1.2$ for GRPO and, because they are multiplied across tokens, the un‑normalised sequence ratio can easily exceed 10 or even 100 for long responses.  Consequently GRPO must use a clipping interval on the order of $[0.2,\,0.27]$, i.e. **two orders of magnitude larger** than the interval needed for GSPO.

---

### How to choose clipping bounds for different response lengths

Because $s_i(\theta)$ already incorporates a factor of $1/|y_i|$, its distribution is *almost* length‑invariant.  The authors therefore use a **single pair of symmetric bounds** for all responses:

```python
eps_low  = 3e-4    # left bound = 1 - eps_low
eps_high = 4e-4    # right bound = 1 + eps_high
```

These numbers were obtained by a brief grid search on a validation set (the paper mentions “carefully tuned”) but, more importantly, they are *order‑of‑magnitude* stable: moving them by a factor of two does not noticeably affect training stability because the underlying importance ratios do not explode with length.

If you work with responses that differ dramatically in length (e.g., some queries produce 50‑token answers while others produce 500‑token answers), you **do not need separate clipping ranges**. The length normalisation guarantees that a 500‑token response that changes only a few tokens will produce an $s_i(\theta)$ very close to 1, exactly as a short response would. In practice you may still want to verify that the empirical distribution of $s_i(\theta)$ stays within the chosen interval; if a tail appears (e.g., many values < 0.95 or > 1.05), you can widen the interval slightly (e.g., to $[3e-4,6e-4]$).

---

### Should we use adaptive clipping based on length?

Adaptive clipping—computing a separate $\varepsilon(|y|)$ for each response length—adds complexity without clear benefit for GSPO:

* **Theoretically unnecessary** – the definition of $s_i(\theta)$ already removes the explicit dependence on $|y|$. The clipping operation is applied to $s_i(\theta)$, not to any raw product of token ratios; therefore an “off‑policy” sample is judged by how far its *average per‑token log‑ratio* deviates from zero, which is intrinsically comparable across lengths.
* **Empirically redundant** – Figure 3 in the paper shows that even when generation length is increased from the default (≈256 tokens) to twice that size, the fraction of clipped responses stays roughly constant as long as $\varepsilon$ is kept at ≈$3\times10^{-4}$. The authors report no need to retune clipping when they “extend generation length”.
* **Potential risk** – an adaptive schedule could unintentionally loosen clipping for long sequences (since their raw product would be larger), re‑introducing exactly the high‑variance behaviour GSPO is meant to avoid.

Consequently, the recommended practice is **to keep a fixed clipping window** for all responses and to rely on length normalisation to make it work uniformly.

---

### Practical checklist

1. **Compute sequence importance ratio**  
   ```python
   log_ratio = sum(log(p_new[t]) - log(p_old[t]) for t in range(T))
   s = exp(log_ratio / T)
   ```
2. **Clip**  
   ```python
   s_clipped = torch.clamp(s,
                           min=1 - eps_low,
                           max=1 + eps_high)
   ```
3. **Loss term**  
   ```python
   loss = - torch.mean( torch.min(s * adv,
                                  s_clipped * adv) )
   ```
4. **Validate range** – after a few warm‑up updates print `s.min(), s.max()`. They should be within `[0.99, 1.01]` for typical settings; if they drift outside `[0.96, 1.04]` consider increasing `eps_low/high` modestly.
5. **Do not tie epsilon to token count** – keep it constant regardless of `|y|`.

Following these steps respects the theoretical motivation given in the GSPO paper: length normalisation stabilises the importance weight, allowing a *single* narrow clipping interval that works across any reasonable response length, and adaptive clipping is generally unnecessary and may even be harmful.
