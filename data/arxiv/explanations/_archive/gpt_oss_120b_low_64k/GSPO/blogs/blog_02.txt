# Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO): A Simpler, More Stable RL Algorithm  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing large language models (LLMs) into high‑performing assistants. Yet, as models grow into billions‑of‑parameter territory—especially the sparsely‑gated Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) variants—training stability becomes a serious bottleneck. The previously popular **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** often collapses when applied to long generations or massive models, mainly because its token‑level importance weights inject huge variance into the gradient.

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** tackles this problem head‑on. By moving the importance ratio from the token level to the **sequence level**, normalizing by length, and using a group‑based advantage estimate, GSPO delivers a cleaner, more reliable learning signal. Below is a walk‑through of the core ideas, why they matter, and how they directly address GRPO’s instability.

---

## 1. From Token‑Level to Sequence‑Level Importance Ratios  

### The GRPO flaw  
In GRPO each token gets an importance weight  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

and this weight is used directly in the PPO‑style clipping objective.  
The problem is conceptual: importance sampling is meant to re‑weight **whole samples** drawn from an older distribution so that expectations under the new distribution are estimated correctly. Applying it token‑by‑token means we are correcting a **single draw** from each next‑token distribution – an unreliable estimate that quickly becomes noisy, especially for long responses where the errors accumulate.

### GSPO’s fix  
GSPO replaces the token ratio with a **sequence likelihood ratio**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
        =\exp\!\Bigg(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigg).
\]

* It measures **how much the whole response** produced by the old policy deviates from the new policy.  
* The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) performs **length normalization**, keeping \(s_i(\theta)\) within a comparable numerical range across responses of different lengths.

Because rewards are assigned at the sequence level (the verifier scores a complete answer), aligning the importance weight with that granularity eliminates the mismatch that caused GRPO’s high variance.

---

## 2. Length‑Normalized Clipping  

PPO and its descendants use clipping to keep off‑policy samples from dominating the gradient. In GRPO the clipping range \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) is applied to each token’s weight \(w_{i,t}\). With GSPO we clip **the entire response** using its normalized ratio \(s_i(\theta)\):

\[
\text{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr).
\]

Since \(s_i(\theta)\) already incorporates a divide‑by‑length operation, it varies much more gently than raw token ratios. Consequently we can use a **much tighter clipping interval** (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\) – \(4\times10^{-4}\)) while still preserving enough gradient signal. Empirically this leads to two orders of magnitude fewer clipped tokens compared with GRPO—yet training becomes *more* efficient because each retained gradient is far less noisy.

---

## 3. Group‑Based Advantage Estimation  

GRPO’s hallmark was to avoid a separate value model by computing a **relative advantage** among a group of responses generated for the same query:

\[
\widehat{A}_{i}
 = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G})}
        {\operatorname{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^{G})}.
\]

GSPO retains this advantageous design unchanged. All responses in a group share the same advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\), which is simply the z‑score of their reward within that batch. This keeps the algorithm lightweight—no extra value network is needed—while still providing a well‑behaved signal for policy updates.

---

## 4. Putting It All Together: The GSPO Objective  

The full GSPO loss (ignoring KL regularization for clarity) reads:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}}
\Bigg[
 \frac{1}{G}
 \sum_{i=1}^{G}
 \min\!\Bigl(
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i,\,
   \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat{A}_i
 \Bigr)
\Bigg].
}
\]

Key points:

* **Expectation over old policy rollouts** – we still sample \(\{y_i\}\) from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
* **Sequence ratio \(s_i(\theta)\)** provides a principled off‑policy correction aligned with the reward’s granularity.  
* **Clipping** guards against extreme off‑policy samples without discarding too many useful gradients.  
* **Group advantage** supplies a stable baseline without extra model overhead.

When all advantages in a group are equal, GSPO reduces exactly to its token‑level variant (**GSPO‑token**) where each token receives the same clipped weight; this variant can be handy for multi‑turn RL settings but does not change the underlying mathematics.

---

## 5. Why GSPO Beats GRPO in Practice  

| Issue | GRPO | GSPO |
|------|------|------|
| **Importance weight unit** | Token level → mismatched with sequence reward | Sequence level → perfectly aligned |
| **Variance source** | Per-token ratios amplify noise over long responses | Single normalized ratio per response keeps variance low |
| **Clipping effect** | Many tokens clipped; noisy gradients remain | Fewer clipped *responses*; gradients are clean |
| **MoE expert volatility** | Token ratios fluctuate when routing changes → need *Routing Replay* workaround | Sequence likelihood relatively stable despite routing changes → no extra tricks needed |
| **Infrastructure demands** | Must recompute token probabilities on training engine; precision mismatches cause issues | Can reuse inference engine likelihoods (sequence only), simplifying pipelines |

Empirical results on Qwen3‑30B models show:

* Stable training curves across thousands of updates where GRPO would collapse.  
* Faster reward convergence and better downstream benchmark scores (AIME’24, LiveCodeBench, Codeforces).  
* Elimination of the *Routing Replay* strategy for MoE models, saving memory and communication bandwidth.

---

## 6. A Glimpse at GSOP‑Token (Optional Variant)

For scenarios requiring finer control—e.g., multi‑turn dialogues where later turns deserve different bonuses—a token‑level variant is defined as:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)=
\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\operatorname{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]},
\]

where `sg` denotes “stop gradient”. Numerically \(s_{i,t}=s_i\), but we can now attach distinct advantages \(\widehat A_{i,t}\) per token while preserving the same stable gradient structure as GSPO.

---

## 7. Takeaways  

1. **Match units:** Importance sampling must correct at the same granularity at which rewards are assigned—sequences for LLM RL.  
2. **Normalize by length:** This keeps ratios comparable across variable‑length generations and permits tighter clipping ranges.  
3. **Group baselines work:** A simple z‑score over a batch of responses replaces heavyweight value networks without sacrificing performance.  
4. **Stability scales:** By eliminating token‐level noise, GSPO remains robust even for huge MoE models and long generation tasks, removing the need for ad hoc tricks like Routing Replay.  

The upshot is a reinforcement learning algorithm that is *simpler* (no value model, no routing hacks), *more stable* (greatly reduced variance), and *more efficient* (fewer wasted updates). As demonstrated on the latest Qwen3 series, GSPO unlocks further scaling of RL for LLMs—bringing us one step closer to truly intelligent language agents.
