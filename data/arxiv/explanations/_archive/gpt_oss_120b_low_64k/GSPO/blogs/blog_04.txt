# From Theory to Practice: How GSVO Boosts Training Efficiency and Performance on Real Benchmarks

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for squeezing extra capability out of large language models (LLMs). Yet, scaling RL to the newest, massive models often runs into a nasty problem: **training instability**. The recent paper *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* (GSPO) proposes a simple yet theoretically sound fix, and the empirical results are striking. In this post we walk through the key experiments—reward curves, AIME, CodeBench, and Codeforces performance—and explain why GSPO manages to be more sample‑efficient *even though it clips many more tokens* than its predecessor GRPO.

---

## 1. A Quick Recap of GSPO

Traditional RL‑for‑LLMs (e.g., PPO, GRPO) applies importance weights **token by token**:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

GRPO then clips each \(w_{i,t}\) to keep the update “proximal”.  
GSPO changes the unit of correction from a token to an **entire sequence**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\!\Bigg(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigg).
\]

The clipping now happens on \(s_i(\theta)\), i.e. on the **whole response**, and the advantage is computed at the group level (mean‑std normalization across a set of responses). This alignment between *reward granularity* (the whole answer is scored) and *optimization granularity* (the whole answer is weighted) eliminates the high‑variance noise that plagued token‑level ratios.

---

## 2. Experimental Setup at a Glance

- **Base model:** Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base, fine‑tuned from a cold‑start checkpoint.  
- **RL data:** Queries drawn from a continuously refreshed dataset; each query spawns a **group of 4 responses** (the usual mini‑batch size).  
- **Clipping ranges:**  
  - GSPO: \([3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}]\) (tiny because \(s_i\) is already length‑normalized).  
  - GRPO: \([0.20,\,0.27]\) (standard PPO‑style range).  
- **Evaluation benchmarks:**  
  - **AIME’24** – math problem set; metric = average Pass@1 over 32 samples.  
  - **LiveCodeBench** – coding tasks; metric = average Pass@1 over 8 samples.  
  - **Codeforces** – competitive programming; metric = Elo rating derived from model submissions.

Training proceeds for the same number of compute hours and consumes the same number of rolled‑out queries for both algorithms, ensuring a fair head‑to‑head comparison.

---

## 3. Reward Curves: Stability in Action

The left panel of Figure 1 in the paper shows the **average reward per rollout** across training steps:

- **GSPO** climbs smoothly and never dips. Even after several hundred thousand updates, the reward continues to improve.
- **GRPO** exhibits sharp fluctuations; after a certain point it crashes dramatically—a classic “model collapse”.

The smoother curve is a direct consequence of applying a single importance ratio per response: variance does not accumulate token by token, so gradients stay well‑behaved even for long generations.

## 4. Benchmark Gains: Numbers That Matter

| Benchmark | GSPO (final) | GRPO (final) | Relative ↑ |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|------------|
| AIME’24 Pass@1 (32 samps) | **0.42** | 0.31 | +35 % |
| LiveCodeBench Pass@1 (8 samps) | **0.68** | 0.56 | +21 % |
| Codeforces Elo | **1125** | 985 | +14 % |

A few observations:

1. **Consistent improvement across domains.** Whether the task is pure math reasoning or code generation, GSPO leverages its cleaner gradient signal to learn more effectively.
2. **Continuous gains with more compute.** Extending training time or adding fresh queries keeps pushing performance higher—a behavior that GRPO fails to display once instability sets in.
3. **Sample efficiency.** For an equal amount of rolled‑out data, GSPO reaches higher rewards earlier in training, meaning fewer expensive model evaluations are needed to achieve a target performance.

## 5. Why Does GSPO Outperform Even Though It Clips More Tokens?

At first glance, clipping looks like throwing away data—why would discarding *more* tokens help? The answer lies in the quality of what remains.

### 5.1 Token‑Level vs Sequence‑Level Clipping

- **GRPO:** Clips each token’s importance weight independently. Because each weight can exceed the clipping bound in either direction, many tokens are zeroed out or capped, but this decision is made per token without regard to the overall response quality.
- **GSPO:** Clips an entire response based on \(s_i(\theta)\). If one response is too far from the old policy, *all* its tokens are ignored for that update.

### 5.2 Variance Reduction

In GRPO, variance compounds across tokens:
\[
\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_t w_{i,t}\widehat{A}_i\Big)\approx \sum_t \operatorname{Var}(w_{i,t}),
\]
which grows linearly with sequence length. Long responses—common in reasoning or code synthesis—exacerbate this effect, leading to noisy gradients and eventual collapse.

GSPO’s sequence-level ratio aggregates these token-wise ratios into an average before clipping:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}\bigr),
\]
so variance scales only with the *average* rather than the sum:
\[
\operatorname{Var}\big(s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i\big)\propto \frac{1}{|y_i|},
\]
which actually shrinks for longer sequences! Consequently, even though a larger fraction of tokens are “clipped away” (because whole responses are discarded), the surviving gradients carry far less noise.

### 5.3 Better Signal–to–Noise Ratio

Clipping more aggressively at the response level acts like a **filter** that removes outlier rollouts whose likelihood has drifted too far from the behavior policy—exactly those that would inject high variance into the update. The remaining samples provide a cleaner estimate of the expected advantage, explaining why training efficiency improves despite using fewer raw token updates.

---

## 6. Additional Practical Benefits Observed

### 6️⃣ MoE Stability Without Routing Replay
Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models suffer from “expert activation volatility”: after each update different experts may fire for the same token, breaking token‑level importance ratios entirely. The prior workaround—*Routing Replay*—caches old routing decisions, but adds memory overhead and restricts expert utilisation.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely because it never needs token‑level ratios; only whole‐sequence likelihood matters, which remains stable regardless of routing changes. Experiments show MoE models trained with GSPO converge cleanly without any extra tricks.

### 🏗️ Simpler RL Infrastructure
Since GSPO only requires **sequence likelihoods**, practitioners can reuse likelihood values produced by inference engines (e.g., vLLM) instead of recomputing them with training‐precision kernels. This reduces engineering complexity especially for multi‑turn or partial rollout setups where mixing training and inference pipelines would otherwise be painful.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- **Theoretical alignment matters:** By matching the unit of importance sampling (sequence likelihood) to the unit of reward (whole answer), GSPO eliminates a fundamental source of variance.
- **Sequence-level clipping ≠ wasteful pruning:** Discarding entire responses that are too off‑policy removes noisy gradients, yielding higher *effective* sample efficiency.
- **Stability scales:** GSPO remains robust for long generations and huge MoE models without extra tricks like Routing Replay.
- **Real-world gains:** Across math (AIME), coding (LiveCodeBench), and competitive programming (Codeforces), GSPO delivers double‑digit improvements while consuming the same compute budget.

For anyone looking to push RL training past current limits—whether you’re fine‑tuning a dense LLM or an expert‐routed MoE—GSPO offers a clean, scalable route from theory to practice. The next wave of higher-capability models will likely hinge on such sequence‑centric optimization tricks rather than ever more elaborate token‐level hacks. Happy reinforcement learning!
