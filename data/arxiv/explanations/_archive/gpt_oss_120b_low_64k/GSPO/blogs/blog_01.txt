# Why Token‑Level Ratios Fail: The Hidden Instability in RL for Large Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing gigantic language models, turning a strong autoregressive generator into a problem‑solver that can handle mathematics, programming, or even complex multi‑turn conversations. The most popular RL recipe in this space—Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its “value‑free” cousin **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**—relies on **token‑level importance sampling ratios** to correct for the fact that we update the policy with data generated from an older version of the model.

At first glance this seems harmless: each token in a response gets a weight  
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]  
and the gradient is scaled by this ratio. In practice, however, this design choice is the root cause of the notorious instability that **catastrophically collapses** large models during RL training. Below we walk through why token‑level ratios are fundamentally mismatched with the reward signal, how they amplify variance, and why shifting to **sequence‑level** ratios—what the new **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm does—solves the problem.

---

## 1. The mismatch between reward and optimization unit

RL for language models typically assigns a single scalar reward \(r(x,y)\) to an entire query–response pair \((x,y)\). Whether the model writes a correct solution to an AIME problem or produces a syntactically valid program, the verifier looks at the **whole sequence** and outputs a score in \([0,1]\).

Yet GRPO still weights each **token** separately. The advantage \(\widehat{A}_i\) is computed per response (by normalizing rewards across a group of responses), but that identical advantage is multiplied by every token’s own importance weight \(w_{i,t}\). In effect we are forcing a token‑level correction on a reward that lives at the sequence level.

Fundamentally, importance sampling tells us that to estimate an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) using samples from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we must re‑weight **whole samples**, not individual components of those samples. Formally,
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] = 
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)\right],
\]
where \(z\) denotes a *complete* outcome (here: an entire response). Applying this principle at the token granularity violates the core assumption of importance sampling: each weight must correct for *the full* distributional mismatch.

---

## 2. High‑variance noise from single‑sample ratios

The ratio \(w_{i,t}\) is computed from *one* draw from the next‑token distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). Because we have only a single sample per token, the weight does **not** average out the stochasticity of that distribution. Consequently:

* The variance of \(w_{i,t}\) can be huge, especially when the old and new policies differ even slightly.
* The variance compounds over long sequences: summing noisy weights across dozens or hundreds of tokens inflates gradient noise dramatically.
* Clipping—intended to keep updates “proximal”—interacts badly with noisy weights: it sometimes clips too aggressively (throwing away useful signal) and sometimes not enough (letting extreme weights explode).

Empirically, training curves with GRPO show an ever‑increasing fraction of clipped tokens as sequence length grows. This noisy gradient signal eventually pushes the policy into regions where its likelihood collapses (the infamous *model collapse*), and once collapsed it rarely recovers even after checkpoint rollback or aggressive hyper‑parameter tuning.

---

## 3. The expert‑activation volatility twist (MoE models)

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures add another layer of instability. Because each token routes to a subset of experts, the **routing pattern** can change dramatically after just one gradient step. In GRPO this means that \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) may be computed by a completely different set of experts than \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\). The resulting token‑level importance ratio becomes meaningless—its numerator and denominator are evaluating *different* networks.

Researchers previously introduced **Routing Replay**, which caches old routing decisions and forces the new policy to reuse them when computing ratios. While effective, Routing Replay incurs extra memory cost and limits MoE capacity because it forces an artificial coupling between old and new activations.

---

## 4. The core insight: match unit of optimization to unit of reward

If rewards are defined per sequence, the natural importance weight should also be defined per sequence:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
\]

Key properties:

1. **Theoretical soundness** – It is exactly the importance sampling ratio for whole responses.
2. **Length normalization** – Raising to \(1/|y_i|\) keeps values comparable across different response lengths.
3. **Uniform token weighting** – All tokens inside a response share the same ratio, eliminating intra‑sequence variance.

GSPO plugs this sequence-level ratio into a PPO‑style clipped objective:
\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\Big(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\Big)
\Bigg].
\]

Because clipping now decides whether an *entire* response is too far off‑policy, far fewer gradients are discarded needlessly, yet those retained are far more reliable.

---

## 5. Empirical payoff: stability, efficiency, and simplicity

### Stability
Training curves with GSPO stay smooth even when scaling to 30B‑parameter MoE models; no catastrophic collapse is observed despite long generations (hundreds of tokens). The algorithm works out‑of‑the‑box—no Routing Replay or extra regularizers required.

### Efficiency
Even though GSPO clips *more* responses (the fraction of clipped items can be two orders of magnitude larger than GRPO’s clipped tokens), overall sample efficiency improves dramatically. This counterintuitive result shows that GRPO’s token-level gradients waste most of their signal in noise; GSPO’s clean sequence-level signal extracts far more learning per sample.

### Infrastructure simplification
Since GSPO only needs **sequence likelihoods**, it tolerates mismatches between training‐engine and inference‐engine precision. Practitioners can feed likelihoods directly from inference backends (e.g., vLLM) without recomputing them on heavy training kernels—a big win for distributed or partial rollout pipelines.

---

## 6. When do you still need token‑level granularity?

Some scenarios—like multi‑turn dialogues where you’d like to assign different advantages to early vs. late utterances—benefit from finer control. GSPO offers a straightforward extension called **GSPO‑token**, which retains sequence‑level importance scaling but lets you plug in per‑token advantages while keeping gradients mathematically identical when all token advantages are equal.

---

## 7. Takeaway for practitioners

| Issue | GRPO (token-level) | GSPO (sequence-level) |
|------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| Importance weight correctness | Violates importance sampling principle | Directly follows it |
| Gradient variance | Grows with sequence length | Controlled by length normalization |
| Sensitivity to MoE routing changes | Highly fragile → needs Routing Replay | Robust → no extra tricks |
| Clipping behavior | Many tokens clipped → noisy gradients | Fewer effective updates but higher quality |
| Engineering overhead | Extra memory/comms for routing replay; recompute old likelihoods | Simpler pipeline; can reuse inference likelihoods |

If you are training any sizeable language model—dense or MoE—with RLHF or similar reward signals, moving from token-level ratios to sequence-level ratios should be your first stability fix.

---

### Final thoughts

The hidden instability in large‑scale RL for language models isn’t just a “bug” in hyperparameter tuning; it stems from a conceptual mismatch—using token-level importance sampling while rewarding whole sequences. By realigning these units through **Group Sequence Policy Optimization**, researchers have turned a brittle training loop into a stable, efficient workhorse that powers state-of-the-art models like Qwen 3.

As RL continues to scale, algorithms that respect fundamental statistical principles while keeping implementation simple will be essential. GSPO demonstrates exactly that: solid theory → cleaner gradients → higher performance → easier engineering. If you’re hitting wall‑clock limits or inexplicable collapses during RL finetuning, give GSPO a try—the results speak for themselves.
