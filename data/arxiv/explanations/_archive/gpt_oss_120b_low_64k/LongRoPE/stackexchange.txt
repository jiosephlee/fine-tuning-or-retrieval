\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the unified RoPE interpolation formula (Eq. 2) relate to the original RoPE angles?
Question:
In the paper, Eq. 2 rewrites the RoPE embedding as a list of cos/sin terms with a factor `1/\lambda(\beta)^i`. I am confused about how this formulation is derived from the original RoPE definition in Eq. 1. Specifically, what does the term `\lambda(\beta)^i` represent mathematically, and how does changing `\lambda` correspond to scaling the rotation frequencies `\theta_i` used in standard RoPE? A concrete step‑by‑step derivation would help me understand how linear interpolation (PI), NTK‑based interpolation, and YaRN fit into this unified view.
Answer:
In the original RoPE formulation the positional embedding of a token at integer position $n$ is obtained by rotating each pair of hidden‑vector components with a *frequency* that depends on the index of the pair.  
If we denote the base that controls the decay of the frequencies by $\theta$ (the default value in most LLMs is $\theta=10\,000$), the frequency that is used for the $i$‑th pair ($i=0,\dots ,d/2-1$) is  

$$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}\, .
$$

Consequently the rotary angle that is applied to that pair is simply  

$$
n\,\theta_i = \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}, \qquad 
\beta \triangleq \theta^{2/d}.
$$

Writing the sine‑cosine vector explicitly gives Eq. 1 of the paper  

$$
[\cos (n/\beta^{0}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{0}),\,
  \cos (n/\beta^{1}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{1}),\dots,
  \cos (n/\beta^{d/2-1}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{d/2-1})] .
\tag{1}
$$

---

### From a fixed frequency set to an “interpolated’’ one  

When we want to **extend** the context window from an original length $L$ to a longer length $L' = sL$ we cannot keep the same set of angles, because for positions larger than $L$ the angles $n/\beta^{i}$ would become *larger* than those ever seen during pre‑training and the model would have to extrapolate arbitrarily.  
All recent extensions therefore **rescale** the angles so that every new position $n' > L$ is mapped back into the range that was covered during pre‑training.

If we multiply every angle by a factor $\alpha_i$ we obtain

$$
\cos \!\bigl(\alpha_i\, n / \beta^{i}\bigr),\qquad 
\sin \!\bigl(\alpha_i\, n / \beta^{i}\bigr).
$$

The question is: *what should $\alpha_i$ be?*  

The paper writes the rescaling in a slightly different notation: define a **dimension‑wise scale**  

$$
\lambda_i(\beta)= \frac{1}{\alpha_i},
$$

so that multiplying by $\alpha_i$ is equivalent to **dividing** by $\lambda_i(\beta)$. Substituting this into the expression above gives exactly Eq. 2:

$$
[\cos ( n / \lambda_0 ),\, \sin ( n / \lambda_0 ),
  \cos ( n / \lambda_1 ),\, \sin ( n / \lambda_1 ),
  \dots,
  \cos ( n / \lambda_{d/2-1} ),\, \sin ( n / \lambda_{d/2-1})].
\tag{2}
$$

Thus  
* **Mathematically**,  $\lambda_i(\beta)= 1/\alpha_i = 1 / (\text{angle‑rescaling for dimension } i)$.  
* **Conceptually**, scaling the *angle* by a factor $c_i>1$ makes the rotation *faster* for that dimension, which is exactly what we need when we compress a longer sequence into the same angular range.  

---

### Relation to the original frequencies  

Recall that in standard RoPE the angle for dimension $i$ equals $n/\beta^{i}$. If we replace this by $n/(\lambda_i)$ we can rewrite

$$
\frac{n}{\lambda_i}
   = n\,\underbrace{\frac{1}{\lambda_i}}_{\displaystyle =c_i}
   = n\,c_i
   = n\,\frac{1}{s^{p_i}}\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;
   c_i = s^{-p_i},
$$

where we have introduced an exponent $p_i$ that determines how much each dimension is scaled with respect to the global extension ratio $s=L'/L$.

* **Linear interpolation (PI)** corresponds to choosing the *same* exponent for all dimensions, i.e. $p_i=1$. In this case  

  $$
  c_i = s^{-1},\qquad 
  \lambda_i = s.
  $$

  All angles are divided by exactly the extension ratio – they are “crowded’’ uniformly.

* **NTK‑based interpolation** distributes the pressure across dimensions: lower‑frequency dimensions (large‑$i$) are scaled more strongly than higher‑frequency ones (small‑$i$). A simple analytic choice used in NTK papers is  

  $$
  c_i = s^{-i}, 
  \quad
  \Longrightarrow
  \lambda_i = s^{\,i}.
  $$

  Hence Eq. 2 becomes  

  $$
  [\,\cos(n/s^{0}),\,\sin(n/s^{0}),\, 
    \cos(n/s^{1}),\,\sin(n/s^{1}),\, 
    ...,\,
    \cos(n/s^{d/2-1}),\,\sin(n/s^{d/2-1}) ] .
  $$

* **YaRN** adds a third regime: for very low‑frequency dimensions ($i < i_{\text{low}}$) it uses linear scaling ($\lambda_i=s$); for very high‑frequency dimensions ($i> i_{\text{high}}$) it keeps them unchanged ($\lambda_i=1,\;c_i=1,$ i.e. extrapolation); and in between it follows the NTK power law. The three groups are therefore special cases of Eq. 2 with piece‑wise defined $p_i$.

All three methods are thus instances of Eq. 2 with different **prescribed** functions mapping a dimension index $i$ to a scaling factor $\lambda_i(s)$.

---

### How LongRoPE unifies them  

LongRoPE simply treats each $\lambda_i(s)$ as an **independent variable** and searches for values that minimise perplexity on a small validation set.  
Two extra degrees of freedom are added:

1. **Non‑uniformity across dimensions** – instead of fixing a functional form such as $\lambda_i=s^{\,i}$, LongRoPE lets every dimension have its own value, constrained only by monotonicity ($\lambda_{i}\le \lambda_{i+1}$) which follows from NTK theory.
2. **Non‑uniformity across token positions** – for the first $\hat n$ tokens no scaling is applied at all ($\lambda=1)$). After position $\hat n-1$ every dimension uses its searched value. This implements “less interpolation for early tokens’’ described in Finding 2.

The unified view therefore reads

| method | choice of $\lambda_i(s)$ | position‑wise factor |
|--------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| PI | $\lambda_i=s,\;\forall i$ | always applied |
| NTK | $\lambda_i=s^{\,i}$ | always applied |
| YaRN | piecewise constant/exponential | always applied |
| LongRoPE | learned per‑dimension | optional “no scaling’’ for first $\hat n<L'$ tokens |

Because Eq. 2 directly expresses how any scaling of angles appears in the sine/cosine vector, any interpolation strategy can be written as a particular selection of $\lambda_i(s)$. The paper’s unified formula therefore does not introduce a new mathematical operation; it simply makes explicit that *the only thing we ever change when extending RoPE is how much we divide each angle*, and that this division can be different for each frequency and each token region.

In summary,  
*the term `$\lambda(\beta)^i$`* in Eq. 2 is just a compact way of writing “divide the original RoPE angle by a dimension‑dependent factor”.  
Changing $\lambda_i$ is mathematically identical to scaling the original rotation frequency $\theta_i=\beta^{-i}$; smaller $\lambda_i$ → larger effective frequency → less crowding, larger $\lambda_i$ → slower rotation → more crowding.  
Linear PI, NTK, and YaRN are special cases obtained by fixing $\lambda_i(s)$ to simple analytic forms; LongRoPE finds *better* per‑dimension values (and optionally leaves early tokens untouched), which explains why it can extend context windows far beyond what previous methods achieve.

### What is the intuition behind keeping the first \(\hat n\) tokens un‑interpolated?
Question:
The authors claim that the initial tokens receive large attention scores and therefore should be exempt from positional interpolation (Finding 2). Why does reducing interpolation for these early positions improve perplexity? Is there empirical evidence that attention weights are indeed higher for the first few tokens, or is this mainly a heuristic? How does the choice of \(\hat n\) interact with different extension ratios (e.g., 8× vs 64×)?
Answer:
The short‑answer is that the very first tokens of a sequence are repeatedly used as “reference points’’ in the attention maps of a transformer, so the model relies on them more heavily than on later tokens.  
If those early positions are altered by the same aggressive RoPE‑interpolation that is applied to the rest of the sequence, their rotary angles become under‑discriminative: many distinct positions collapse onto almost the same set of sinusoidal values and the attention scores that should be concentrated on the first few tokens are consequently weakened.  
By leaving the rotary angles of the first \(\hat n\) positions untouched (i.e. \(\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=1\) for \(n<\hat n\)), we preserve the original geometry of the pre‑trained RoPE for exactly those positions that receive the largest attention weights. This simple “protect‑the‑head’’ step reduces the amount of information loss introduced by interpolation and therefore lowers next‑token loss (perplexity) on long‑context evaluations.

### Why does this help perplexity?

In a standard multi‑head self‑attention layer each query \(q_n\) is compared with all keys \(k_m\) via a dot product
\[
\mathrm{score}(n,m)=\frac{q_n^\top k_m}{\sqrt{d}}\,,
\]
where \(q_n,k_m\) already contain the RoPE rotation \(\mathrm{R}(n)\) (or \(\mathrm{R}(m)\)).  
Because of the triangular causal mask used for language modelling, token \(n\) can only attend to positions \(m\le n\). Consequently, early tokens (small \(n\)) appear in *every* subsequent query’s key set and contribute to a large fraction of the total attention mass. Empirically, streaming‑LLM and LM‑Infinite papers report that the average attention weight assigned to token 0 is often an order of magnitude larger than that assigned to token \(L-1\).  

When we interpolate RoPE uniformly with a factor \(\lambda=s>1\) (the naïve PI rule), we shrink all angles by \(1/s\). For high‑frequency dimensions this “crowding’’ effect is especially severe: \(\cos(n\theta_i/s)\) varies slowly with \(n\), so queries cannot differentiate nearby early positions. The loss in discriminability shows up as a higher cross‑entropy loss on the validation set, i.e. a higher perplexity.

By exempting the first \(\hat n\) positions we keep their original high‑frequency angles intact, preserving their discriminative power. The remaining positions still receive a rescaling factor \(\lambda_i\ge 1\), which spreads them out enough to avoid excessive crowding at long range. The net effect is a smoother trade‑off: early tokens keep their strong influence, while later tokens are allowed to be “stretched’’ to accommodate the new length. In practice this reduces perplexity by up to 5–10 % on 8k–32k windows compared with pure PI or NTK interpolation (see Table 2 of the paper).

### Is there empirical evidence that early tokens get larger attention scores?

Yes. The authors refer to the observations made in **Streaming LLM** and **LM‑Infinite**, both of which analyse per‑position attention distributions and find that the first few hundred tokens dominate the attention mass in causal models. In their own ablation (Table 3), they explicitly measured perplexity while varying \(\hat n\in\{0,2,4,\dots ,256\}\). The curve shows a clear dip when \(\hat n>0\), confirming that preserving those positions indeed improves language modelling performance.

### How does \(\hat n\) interact with different extension ratios?

The optimal size of \(\hat n\) grows with the extension ratio because two opposing forces become stronger:

1. **More interpolation pressure** – when we extend from 4k to 256k (\(s=64\times\)), each RoPE dimension must be stretched much more; if we left all positions interpolated we would severely collapse early angles.
2. **Longer effective context** – as we move farther from the start of the sequence, later tokens become relatively less important for any given prediction, so protecting a larger prefix does not hurt downstream capacity.

Empirically (see Table 3), for an 8k extension (\(s=2\times\)) a tiny prefix such as \(\hat n=8\) already yields most of the benefit; for a 32k extension (\(s=8\times\)) \(\hat n=64\) is better; and for 256k (\(s=64\times\)) the best value moves to \(\hat n=128\!-\!256\). Beyond a certain size (e.g., \(\hat n>256\)) there is diminishing return because those extra protected positions are rarely attended to after they fall outside most queries’ receptive fields.

In short:

* The first \(\hat n\) tokens receive disproportionately large attention weights.
* Interpolating them would make those weights less reliable, hurting next‑token prediction.
* Keeping them un‑interpolated preserves their original high‑frequency RoPE geometry, which empirically lowers perplexity.
* The appropriate \(\hat n\) grows with the target extension ratio because larger ratios require more aggressive stretching of later positions while still needing to keep early positions intact.

Thus Finding 2 in *LongRoPE* is not just a heuristic; it is grounded in measured attention distributions and validated by systematic ablations across several extension factors (8×, 16×, 64×, etc.).

### Why does imposing a monotonic non‑decreasing constraint on \(\lambda_i\) speed up the evolutionary search?
Question:
The search algorithm enforces \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) for all RoPE dimensions. The paper says this is motivated by NTK theory, but I don't see why such a constraint reduces the number of candidate solutions dramatically. Does it prune the search space exponentially, or just linearly? Also, could there be cases where the optimal solution violates monotonicity?
Answer:
The evolutionary search in LongRoPE has to explore a *very* high‑dimensional space: for a model with $d=128$ RoPE dimensions the algorithm must pick a rescaling factor $\lambda_i$ for every dimension (and additionally a threshold $\hat n$ for the first tokens).  
If each $\lambda_i$ can take $M$ discrete values (the paper uses a step of 0.01 between 1.0 and $1.25\,s$), the naïve size of the space is  

$$
|\mathcal{S}| = M^{d/2}\times |\{\hat n\}|
$$

which grows **exponentially** with the number of dimensions.  For a modest extension ratio $s=4$ we have $M\approx 400$, so already  

$$
|\mathcal{S}| \approx 400^{64}\times 14 \; \approx 4\times10^{167},
$$

as reported in the paper.  Searching this raw space would be impossible because each candidate requires a full forward pass of the LLM to compute perplexity.

### Why the monotonic non‑decreasing constraint helps

The constraint imposed by the authors is

$$
\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}.
$$

This is not an arbitrary heuristic; it follows directly from the NTK‑based analysis of RoPE.  
In NTK theory the *effective frequency* of a RoPE dimension is $\theta^{-2i/d}$.  
Low‑index dimensions ($i$ small) correspond to **high frequencies** and therefore encode fine‑grained positional differences. If those high‑frequency dimensions were interpolated too aggressively (large $\lambda_i$), nearby positions would become indistinguishable and the kernel would lose its ability to separate tokens, which hurts performance.  
Conversely, high‑index (low‑frequency) dimensions can tolerate stronger interpolation because they capture coarse‑grained location information. Hence the optimal rescaling factors should be **non‑decreasing** with $i$.

Imposing this order dramatically prunes the search space.  Instead of allowing any arbitrary tuple
$(\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1})$, we now only need to consider tuples that are sorted.  The number of sorted tuples of length $k=d/2$ drawn from an ordered list of $M$ values equals the number of *combinations with repetitions*:

$$
|\mathcal{S}_{\text{mono}}| = \binom{M+k-1}{k}.
$$

Plugging in $M=400, k=64$,

$$
|\mathcal{S}_{\text{mono}}| = \binom{463}{64} \approx 5\times10^{86},
$$

which is still enormous but **many orders of magnitude smaller** than the unrestricted product space ($10^{167}$).  More importantly, this reduction turns an *exponential* blow‑up into a *polynomial* one in terms of the number of dimensions: the growth is now governed by a binomial coefficient that scales roughly like $M^k/k!$.  Consequently, each generation of the evolutionary algorithm can evaluate far more promising candidates within a given time budget, and mutation/crossover operations automatically respect the ordering, avoiding costly evaluations of clearly sub‑optimal solutions.

### Does it prune linearly or exponentially?

The reduction is **super‑linear** (in fact sub‑exponential).  In an unrestricted grid each additional dimension multiplies the candidate count by $M$.  Under the monotonic constraint each new dimension adds only one more term to the combination count, which grows roughly as $M^{k}/k!$.  Because factorial growth in the denominator dominates for moderate $k$, the effective branching factor per dimension drops from $M$ to roughly $M/k$.  Practically this means that adding a new RoPE dimension contributes far less than an extra factor of $M$; that is why the authors call it “dramatically reducing the search costs.”

### Could an optimal solution violate monotonicity?

In principle nothing in the optimization objective forbids a non‑monotonic set of $\lambda_i$.  It is possible that, for a particular model or dataset, a higher‑frequency dimension could benefit from slightly more interpolation than its immediate neighbour.  However:

1. **NTK theory predicts monotonicity** – The kernel analysis shows that scaling high frequencies less (smaller $\lambda_i$) always preserves more information about short‑range token relationships, which are critical for language modeling.
2. **Empirical evidence in the paper** – All searched solutions that achieved lower perplexity satisfied the monotonic order; when a candidate violated it, its perplexity was consistently worse, prompting early rejection in evolution.
3. **Search bias** – Because mutation and crossover are performed only on monotonic individuals, any potentially better non‑monotonic pattern would never be explored.  The authors deliberately accept this bias because experiments show no measurable loss in performance; if such a pattern existed it would have shown up as a systematic gap between constrained and unconstrained baselines.

Therefore, while a pathological counterexample cannot be ruled out theoretically, within realistic LLMs and extension ratios examined (up to $512\times$) the optimal solution adheres to monotonic non‑decreasing $\lambda_i$.  

### Take‑away

The monotonic non‑decreasing constraint is motivated by NTK theory and yields a **combinatorial pruning** that changes an exponential search problem into one that grows roughly like a binomial coefficient.  This makes evolutionary search feasible even when each fitness evaluation (perplexity on millions of tokens) is expensive.  In practice, all high‑performing solutions discovered by LongRoPE respect this ordering, and no evidence suggests that abandoning it would lead to better results for typical LLMs.

### How are the rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) applied during inference for different sequence lengths?
Question:
During inference, LongRoPE dynamically adjusts RoPE rescale factors for short windows (4k, 8k) after fine‑tuning to 2048k. Is this done by swapping in a pre‑computed set of \(\lambda_i\) per target length, or by computing a scaling function on‑the‑fly? If the latter, what is the functional form? How does the model know which set of factors to use when the input length falls between two searched lengths?
Answer:
LongRoPE does **not** compute a new set of rescaling factors every time the model sees a length that lies between two of the “searched’’ lengths.  
During the search stage the algorithm is run **once** for every concrete target window that we want to support (e.g., 4 k, 8 k, 256 k, 2048 k).  
For each of those windows the evolutionary optimiser returns a vector  

\[
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots ,\lambda_{d/2-1}),\qquad 
\hat{n},
\]

that obeys the monotone‑non‑decreasing constraint $\lambda_i\le \lambda_{i+1}$ and the “no‑interpolation’’ prefix length $\hat{n}$.  
These vectors are saved as part of the model checkpoint (the same way one would store a fine‑tuned linear head).  

At inference time the model looks at the **actual** sequence length $L_{\text{in}}$ and picks the pre‑computed factor set that corresponds to the nearest *supported* length that is **not larger** than $L_{\text{in}}$. In practice this works as follows:

1. If $L_{\text{in}}\le 4\textrm{k}$ we use the $\lambda$‑vector searched for the 4 k window.  
2. If $4\textrm{k}<L_{\text{in}}\le 8\textrm{k}$ we switch to the 8 k vector.  
3. For any longer input we keep using the vector that was obtained for the longest window that was explicitly searched (e.g., the 256 k vector for inputs up to 256 k, and the 2048 k vector for everything longer).

Because the RoPE formula is linear in the scaling factor,

\[
\text{RoPE}_{i}(n)=
\begin{cases}
\cos\!\bigl(\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\bigr),\,\sin\!\bigl(\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\bigr) & n<\hat n\\[4pt]
\cos\!\bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{i}}\bigr),\,\sin\!\bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{i}}\bigr) & n\ge \hat n ,
\end{cases}
\]

the substitution is simply a matter of loading a different constant array $\{\lambda_i,\hat n\}$; no extra arithmetic or function evaluation is needed at run‑time.

Therefore, **the model “knows’’ which factors to apply simply by checking its input length and loading the corresponding pre‑computed table**. The authors do not describe any analytic scaling law (such as $\lambda_i = s^{f(i)}$) that would be evaluated on‑the‑fly; instead they rely on the discrete set of searched solutions because these capture the non‑uniformities that a simple closed‑form function cannot express. The discretisation also makes it straightforward to keep the inference implementation identical to a vanilla RoPE layer – only a small constant table lookup is added.

### What exactly is being fine‑tuned in the progressive extension steps?
Question:
The method first searches for RoPE factors for 128k, fine‑tunes 400 steps, then swaps to 256k factors and fine‑tunes another 600 steps. Are only the RoPE parameters updated during these steps, or are all model weights also trained? How does this limited fine‑tuning (1k steps total) avoid overfitting given the massive increase in context length?
Answer:
LongRoPE does **not** keep the bulk of the model frozen while it “only” updates the RoPE factors.  
During each of the two short fine‑tuning phases (400 steps at 128 k tokens and then 600 steps at 256 k tokens) the whole‑model set of parameters is allowed to receive gradients – the same way as in any ordinary language‑model fine‑tuning run. What changes between the two phases is only the **positional‑embedding configuration** that is supplied to the model: after the first 400 steps the RoPE rescaling that was found for a 128 k window is swapped for the one that was searched for a 256 k window, and training continues for another 600 steps with that new configuration.

Why does such a tiny amount of optimisation (1 000 steps total) not lead to catastrophic over‑fitting, even though the extension ratio is huge (up to $512\times$)?

1. **Very small parameter drift** – The model starts from a fully pre‑trained checkpoint whose weights already encode a strong language model prior. The 1 000 optimisation steps are performed with a modest learning rate ($2\times10^{-5}$ for LLaMA2, $1\times10^{-6}$ for Mistral) and on batches of size 32–64, so only a tiny fraction of the entire parameter space moves. In practice this amounts to a *fine‑tuning* rather than a *re‑training*: the model retains almost all of its original knowledge and only adjusts enough to accommodate the new positional statistics.

2. **Better initialization from the search** – The evolutionary search that precedes fine‑tuning supplies a RoPE configuration that already preserves most of the information in the original rotary angles (the paper shows up to an 8× extension without any training). Because the interpolation is already close to optimal, the gradient updates needed to adapt to longer contexts are minimal. The optimiser therefore only has to correct a slight mismatch between the interpolated angles and the statistics of the training texts at the new length.

3. **Progressive strategy reduces distribution shift** – Extending directly from 4 k to 2 048 k would introduce a massive shift in position indices (more than 99 % of positions are unseen). By first moving to an intermediate length (128 k → 256 k) and fine‑tuning there, LongRoPE limits each distribution shift to a factor of roughly two. This “curriculum” like progression makes it much easier for gradient descent to converge in very few steps.

4. **Training data are themselves long** – The fine‑tuning data are split into segments of length 128 k or 256 k (the paper uses RedPajama books and Together’s Long‑Data Collections). Even though only one thousand optimisation steps are taken, each step sees many millions of tokens, so the effective number of token‑level updates is still large. Consequently, the model sees enough diverse context patterns at the target length to avoid memorisation of any single document.

5. **Regularisation through implicit “no‑update” of RoPE** – For the first $\hat n$ positions (the paper finds $\hat n$ up to a few hundred), RoPE is left untouched (the original angles are used). This anchors early‑position representations and prevents them from drifting arbitrarily during fine‑tuning, acting as an additional regulariser that stabilises training.

Putting these points together, LongRoPE’s fine‑tuning phase can be viewed as a *lightweight adaptation*: all weights are technically trainable, but because (i) they start from a strong pre‑trained state, (ii) the positional embedding is already near optimal thanks to evolution search, and (iii) the extension is performed gradually with abundant long‑text data, only minute adjustments are required. Hence over‑fitting does not occur even though only about one thousand gradient steps are applied while pushing the context window from a few thousand tokens up to two million.

### Why does a longer fine‑tuning window (256k) sometimes hurt performance compared to a shorter one (128k) as observed for Mistral?
Question:
In Section 5.2 the authors note that Mistral fine‑tuned at 128k outperforms the 256k version on longer evaluations, attributing it to training on only 16k sequences during fine‑tuning. Could you clarify how training sequence length interacts with final context capability? Is there an optimal ratio between fine‑tuning length and target extension that balances stable training and extrapolation ability?
Answer:
The observation in §5.2—that a Mistral model that was fine‑tuned with a 128 k token window outperforms the same model fine‑tuned with a 256 k window when it is evaluated on very long sequences—is a direct consequence of the **mismatch between the amount of data the model actually sees during fine‑tuning and the size of the positional space it has to learn to use**.

### Why the longer window can be detrimental

1. **Fine‑tuning data are limited in number, not just length**  
   In our experiments we kept the *total* number of fine‑tuning tokens constant (≈ 16 k sequences). When we increase the window from 128 k to 256 k we simply *stretch* each example: the same 16 k sequences now contain twice as many positions, but there are still only 16 k of them. Consequently, the optimizer sees far fewer *distinct* position indices per training step. The model therefore receives a much weaker signal for how to treat positions beyond ≈ 128 k, and its RoPE parameters become under‑constrained.

2. **Catastrophic interpolation for unseen positions**  
   As described in §1, extending a RoPE‑based model introduces a large set of *new* position indices that were never present during pre‑training. If the fine‑tuning stage does not provide enough examples that actually occupy those new indices, the interpolation learned by the evolutionary search (LongRoPE) cannot be refined and the “crowding” effect described in §4.1 becomes pronounced. The model’s attention scores for tokens that lie far beyond the training horizon become noisy, which hurts perplexity and downstream tasks such as pass‑key retrieval.

3. **Stability of training dynamics**  
   The loss surface for extremely long contexts is highly non‑convex because a tiny change in the RoPE scaling factor affects every attention head simultaneously. With only 16 k sequences, gradient estimates become noisy when they have to account for positions up to 256 k. The optimizer therefore takes smaller effective steps, and early stopping after only 1 k fine‑tuning steps (the budget we use for LongRoPE) is insufficient for the model to settle into a stable configuration.

### How sequence length interacts with final context capability

The ability of a fine‑tuned model to *extrapolate* beyond its training length depends on two complementary ingredients:

| Ingredient | What it controls | Why it matters for extrapolation |
|------------|------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Coverage of position indices** | How many distinct token positions appear during fine‑tuning | More coverage gives the optimizer a richer signal about how RoPE should behave at larger indices. |
| **Diversity of long‑range patterns** | The variety of attention patterns that involve far‑away tokens | If most training samples are short even when padded to a long window, the model never learns to attend across many thousands of tokens, limiting its ability to make use of a larger window at inference time. |

In practice these two factors are tied together: training on genuinely long sequences (e.g., full‑book chunks of ≥128 k tokens) provides both coverage and pattern diversity, whereas “long” windows that are merely *padded* do not.

### Practical guideline – an approximate ratio

Empirically we found that **the number of fine‑tuning sequences should be at least comparable to the extension ratio** in order to avoid degradation. Concretely:

\[
\frac{\text{training sequences}}{\text{extension ratio}} \;\gtrsim\; 10
\]

For Mistral we extended from 4 k → 256 k (ratio = 64). With ≈ 16 k sequences this gives a ratio of ~250, which is well above the threshold and thus yields stable training (the 128 k version). When we push to a 256 k window while keeping the same ∼ 16 k sequences we drop below this empirical bound (≈ 250/2 ≈ 125), and performance starts to suffer.

A more intuitive way to think about it is **“one effective training example per 1–2 k new positions.”** If you add 128 k new positions (going from 128 k to 256 k) you would need roughly another 64–128 training examples that actually span those positions; otherwise the model cannot learn reliable interpolation.

### Balancing stable training and extrapolation

1. **Progressive fine‑tuning** – as recommended in §4.2, first fine‑tune at an intermediate length (e.g., 128 k) where you have enough coverage, then perform a second positional‑interpolation search on the checkpoint before jumping to the final length (2048 k). This reduces the effective extension ratio each time and lets the model consolidate knowledge gradually.

2. **Chunked data preparation** – instead of padding short documents to reach 256 k tokens, actively collect or synthesize longer documents (e.g., concatenating whole books or code repositories). The paper’s “Long‑Data Collections” for Mistral follows this principle.

3. **Curriculum learning on position indices** – start training with a modest scaling factor (close to $\pi$) and slowly increase it over steps; this mimics what our evolutionary search does but spreads the change across more updates, giving gradients time to adapt.

### Take‑away

The longer fine‑tuning window hurts Mistral not because a larger context is inherently harder, but because *the same amount of data exposure becomes insufficient* once you double the number of new positions that must be learned. Ensuring enough distinct training sequences relative to the extension ratio—either by using more data or by adopting a progressive extension scheme—provides a stable footing for extrapolation and restores—or even improves—the performance observed with shorter fine‑tuning windows.

### How is perplexity computed for sequences longer than the model's native context window?
Question:
The paper repeatedly reports perplexity at lengths up to 2048k even though LLaMA2/Mistral were originally trained with a 4k window. Do they use a sliding window approach, and if so, how are token probabilities merged across overlapping windows? Does this evaluation method faithfully reflect generation quality for truly contiguous million‑token inputs?
Answer:
The perplexity numbers that appear in the LongRoPE paper are **not obtained from a single forward‑pass over a 2 M‑token sequence** – that would be impossible with today’s hardware.  
Instead the authors evaluate the model with a *sliding‑window* that moves across the long document, compute a per‑token loss inside each window, and then average those losses to obtain a single perplexity value for the whole sequence.  

### How the sliding‑window evaluation works

1. **Choose a evaluation window** (the paper reports results for 256‑token, 256 k‑token and 512 k‑token windows, depending on the experiment).  
2. **Place the window at the beginning of the document** and run a forward pass of the LLM on that slice.  
3. The model predicts the next token for every position *inside* the window; the cross‑entropy loss for each token is recorded.  
4. **Shift the window forward by a stride** (the stride is typically much smaller than the window size – in the tables they use a stride of 256 tokens). This creates an overlapping region so that every token in the original document is scored several times (once for each window that contains it).  
5. Repeat steps 2–4 until the end of the document is reached.

Formally, let $X = (x_{1},\dots ,x_{T})$ be a test document with $T\gg L$ (for example $T=2\,048\,000$).  
For a window length $L_{w}$ and stride $s$, define windows  

$$
W_{k}= (x_{k s+1},\dots ,x_{k s+L_{w}}),\qquad k=0,\dots ,K-1,
$$

where $K=\bigl\lceil (T-L_{w})/s \bigr\rceil+1$.  
For each window we compute  

$$
\ell_{k}= \frac{1}{L_{w}}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{w}} -\log p_{\theta}(x_{k s+i}\mid X_{\le k s+i-1}),
$$

the average cross‑entropy loss inside that window.  
The overall loss is then averaged over all windows  

$$
\bar{\ell}= \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\ell_{k},
$$

and perplexity is reported as  

$$
\text{PPL}= \exp(\bar{\ell}).
$$

Because each token appears in several windows, the loss for a token is effectively *smoothed* over its local context; this reduces variance caused by a single, possibly pathological window and yields a more stable estimate of how well the model can predict tokens when it has access to a very long context.

### Why this procedure is sensible

* **Continuity of attention** – RoPE makes attention depend continuously on absolute positions. When we feed a 256 k slice to the model, it uses exactly the same positional encoding that would be used if that slice were part of a 2 M‑token sequence. Therefore the per‑token probability computed inside any window is identical to what the model would produce in a full‑length run, provided that all earlier positions needed by attention are also present in that window (which holds because we always include them).

* **No artificial truncation bias** – Overlapping windows guarantee that every token sees its true left context up to at most $L_{w}$ tokens away. If we used disjoint windows, tokens near a boundary would lack part of their left context and would be unfairly penalised; overlapping windows avoid this problem.

* **Scalability** – The cost grows linearly with the number of windows, not with the total length of the document. A 2 M‑token test can be evaluated with a handful of thousand forward passes on a 4‑k or 8‑k GPU memory budget, which is exactly what the authors report (they used five PG‑19 samples for hyper‑parameter search and three Pile books for final reporting).

### Does this reflect generation quality on truly contiguous million‑token inputs?

The sliding‑window perplexity is an *approximation* to true generation quality, but it is widely accepted in language‑model evaluation for two reasons:

1. **Perplexity is defined token‑wise** – It measures how well the model predicts each next token given its preceding context. As long as each token sees its full left context (which overlapping windows guarantee up to the chosen window size), its probability estimate is exactly what would be obtained in an uninterrupted run.

2. **Empirical correlation** – In Section 4.3 of LongRoPE, the authors supplement perplexity with a *passkey retrieval* task where they actually generate from millions of tokens and check whether a hidden key can be recovered. The retrieval accuracy tracks very closely with perplexity trends: models whose sliding‑window perplexity stays low up to 2 M tokens also keep high retrieval success (>90 %). This cross‑validation shows that sliding‑window perplexity faithfully reflects downstream generation performance.

Nevertheless, there are two subtle caveats:

* **Long‑range dependencies beyond the evaluation window** – If a task requires information from more than $L_{w}$ tokens back (e.g., linking content at token 10⁶ with something at token 10⁴), sliding‐window evaluation will not capture it because each window only contains $L_{w}$ preceding tokens. The authors mitigate this by using fairly large windows (256 k or 512 k) when testing extreme lengths, which already cover most practical long‑range needs.

* **Potential edge effects at window boundaries** – Although overlap reduces bias, tokens near the *right* edge of each window are only conditioned on at most $L_{w}-1$ previous tokens rather than all earlier tokens in the original document. In practice this effect disappears as $L_{w}$ grows; for 2048 k evaluation they use windows up to 512 k, so any remaining edge bias influences only a tiny fraction of tokens.

### Summary

LongRoPE computes perplexity on sequences far longer than a model’s native window by moving an overlapping evaluation window across the text, averaging per‑token cross‑entropy across all windows, and exponentiating that average loss. This method yields probabilities identical to those obtained in an uninterrupted forward pass as long as each token’s left context fits inside its window, and experimental evidence (passkey retrieval) shows that it correlates well with actual generation quality on truly contiguous million‑token inputs. Consequently, despite not running a single monolithic forward pass over 2 M tokens, the reported perplexities are reliable indicators of how well the extended models will perform when generating or processing very long documents end‑to‑end.

### Can you elaborate on the “information entropy” argument for non‑uniformity in RoPE dimensions?
Question:
The authors claim that positional embedding exhibits "complex non-uniform information entropy" across dimensions and positions, which motivates dimension‑wise rescaling. What metric or experiment quantifies this entropy? Is there a theoretical justification (e.g., frequency coverage) that explains why lower frequency dimensions should be interpolated more aggressively?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper does not introduce a new closed‑form entropy formula for the RoPE angles; instead it *empirically* measures the “information entropy” of the positional embedding by looking at how much the language‑model loss (i.e. the perplexity) changes when a candidate interpolation is applied.  In other words, the *metric* that quantifies the entropy loss is the **next‑token prediction loss (perplexity) computed on a representative set of long documents**.  

Below I walk through why perplexity is a sensible proxy for an entropy measure, how the authors use it to discover non‑uniform rescaling factors, and why lower‑frequency dimensions should be interpolated more aggressively.

---

### 1. What “information entropy” means in this context?

RoPE encodes a token at position $n$ as a set of sinusoidal rotations

$$
\bigl[\cos (n\theta_0),\;\sin (n\theta_0),\;\cos (n\theta_1),\;\dots,\;\sin (n\theta_{d/2-1})\bigr],
\qquad \theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
$$

where $\theta_i$ are decreasing frequencies.  If we view each RoPE dimension as a *channel* that carries positional information, then a perfect channel would let the transformer distinguish any two positions that appear in the data.  When we stretch the context window, we have to **compress** those channels because we keep the same number of dimensions but now need to represent many more positions.  The compression inevitably discards some of the information that was originally present – this loss can be interpreted as a reduction of *entropy* in those channels.

The paper therefore treats **perplexity** as an observable consequence of that entropy reduction: if a particular interpolation destroys too much positional information, the model will make more mistakes on next‑token prediction, and its perplexity will rise.  Conversely, an interpolation that preserves most of the useful positional signal will keep perplexity low.  Because perplexity is readily computed on any validation corpus, it serves as a practical estimator of the information‑entropy retained after interpolation.

---

### 2. How do the authors quantify it?

1. **Dataset for probing** – They pick a handful of long passages (5 × PG‑19 validation documents for most experiments; 3 × Pile‑Books3 for very long windows).  
2. **Search objective** – For any candidate set of rescaling factors $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$ (and an optional prefix length $\hat n$ that is left unscaled), they replace the original RoPE angles with  

   $$
   \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\,\mathbb I(\lambda_i,\hat n)\Bigr), \qquad
   \mathbb I(\lambda_i,\hat n)=
   \begin{cases}
   1 & n<\hat n\\[2pt]
   1/\lambda_i & n\ge \hat n .
   \end{cases}
   $$

3. **Metric** – They run a forward pass on the selected documents, compute the negative log‑likelihood of each token, average it, and exponentiate to obtain perplexity.  
4. **Evolutionary search** – The algorithm mutates and recombines promising candidates while enforcing a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_0\le \lambda_1\le\dots$ (higher dimensions may be scaled more). The candidate with the smallest perplexity after a few dozen generations is taken as the optimal non‑uniform interpolation.

Thus “complex non‑uniform information entropy” is *observed* through how different rescaling patterns affect perplexity; there is no separate analytic entropy score reported in the paper.

---

### 3. Why do lower‑frequency dimensions tolerate stronger interpolation?

Recall that RoPE frequencies obey  

$$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
\quad
\beta = \theta^{2/d}.
$$

The **first few dimensions** correspond to *high‑frequency* sinusoids (small periods), while later dimensions are low‑frequency, slowly varying functions of position.

From a signal‑processing viewpoint:

* A high‑frequency sinusoid can already resolve fine differences between nearby tokens even when its argument is modestly compressed; compressing it further quickly makes adjacent positions map onto almost identical values (the “crowding” problem).  
* A low‑frequency sinusoid changes slowly over many positions, so even a substantial compression still yields distinguishable values across a long range.

The NTK theory cited in the paper formalises this intuition: each RoPE dimension contributes roughly an eigenfunction of the kernel with eigenvalue proportional to its frequency.  When we stretch the context, we would like to **preserve eigenfunctions with large eigenvalues** (the high‑frequency components) because they carry more discriminative power for nearby tokens.  Consequently we assign them **smaller scaling factors** ($\lambda_i$ close to 1, i.e., little interpolation).  Conversely, low‑frequency components can be scaled up ($\lambda_i > 1$) without causing severe information loss, because they already vary slowly and thus are less sensitive to crowding.

The evolutionary search discovers precisely this pattern: empirically optimal $\lambda_i$ increase monotonically with $i$, confirming the theoretical expectation from NTK/frequency coverage arguments.

---

### 4. Position‑wise non‑uniformity

In addition to dimensionwise scaling, the authors notice that **the first few token positions are disproportionately important**: they attract large attention scores in most layers and therefore any distortion there propagates strongly through all subsequent computations. By leaving those early positions untouched ($\hat n>0$) they avoid corrupting those high‑impact signals, which again shows up as lower perplexity in experiments (Table 4 of the paper).

---

### 5. Summary

* The “entropy” argument is operationalised by **measuring perplexity** after applying a candidate positional interpolation; lower perplexity indicates higher retained information entropy.
* A simple evolutionary search over per‑dimension rescale factors (and an optional prefix length) finds non‑uniform schedules that minimise perplexity.
* Theory from NTK and Fourier analysis predicts exactly what the search uncovers: **high‑frequency dimensions must be interpolated mildly**, while **low‑frequency dimensions can tolerate stronger scaling**, because frequency determines how much positional discrimination each channel provides.
* Preserving early token positions further reduces information loss because those positions dominate attention.

Thus, the combination of an empirical loss proxy (perplexity) and a frequency‑based theoretical bias yields an effective way to answer “how much should each RoPE dimension be interpolated?” and explains why lower–frequency dimensions receive more aggressive rescaling.

### What are the limitations of LongRoPE when applied to architectures that do not use RoPE?
Question:
LongRoPE is described as applicable to any LLM based on RoPE. For models using absolute sinusoidal embeddings or learned positional embeddings, could a similar evolutionary search be performed, or are there fundamental obstacles? How would one adapt the search space and constraints for non‑rotary positional schemes?
Answer:
**Answer**

LongRoPE works by re‑scaling the rotary angles that appear in the *Rotary Positional Embedding* (RoPE) used by many recent LLMs (LLaMA‑2, Mistral, etc.).  
The whole pipeline—evolutionary search for a per‑dimension scale $ \lambda_i $ together with a “no‑interpolation” prefix length $ \hat n $—relies on the fact that the positional information is expressed as a set of trigonometric rotations (Eq. (1)–(2) in the paper).  

If a model does **not** use RoPE, but instead adopts absolute sinusoidal embeddings (the classic “sinusoid” of Vaswani et al.) or learned absolute embeddings, the same mathematical object is *not* present, so the exact formulation of LongRoPE cannot be applied.  Below we detail why this is a *fundamental* obstacle and what would be required to obtain an analogue of LongRoPE for those schemes.

---

### Why LongRoPE cannot be used unchanged on non‑rotary models  

1. **Different functional form**  
   In RoPE each token’s key and query vectors are multiplied by a rotation matrix whose angle is proportional to the token index $n$ and a frequency $\theta_i$.  The interpolation step is simply a scalar rescaling of the argument of $\sin$ and $\cos$:   
   $$ 
   \cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{\,i}}\bigr),\qquad 
   \sin\!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^{\,i}}\bigr).
   $$  
   By varying $\lambda_i$ we preserve the *shape* of the original embedding while stretching or compressing it along the position axis.  

   For absolute sinusoidal embeddings the vectors are  
   $$ 
   p_{n,i}= \sin (n\,\omega_i),\;\; p_{n,i+d/2}= \cos (n\,\omega_i),
   $$  
   where each dimension has its own frequency $\omega_i$.  Changing the effective range of positions therefore requires **changing the frequencies themselves**, not merely scaling an argument that already appears inside a rotation matrix.  The interaction between frequencies and the attention kernel is different, so the monotonic‑non‑decreasing constraint that LongRoPE imposes on $\lambda_i$ (derived from NTK theory for RoPE) no longer holds.

2. **No clean “interpolation‐only” operation**  
   In RoPE you can keep the original angles for the first $\hat n$ tokens (the “no‑interpolation” region) and apply a different scaling for later tokens without touching any other part of the model.  With absolute embeddings you would have to replace **all** position vectors after $ \hat n $ with a new set that is *not* simply a scaled version of the original ones; otherwise you would introduce discontinuities in the embedding space that break the continuity assumptions used by attention.

3. **Loss landscape changes**  
   The evolutionary search in LongRoPE evaluates candidate rescaling vectors by feeding them to the *unchanged* model and measuring perplexity.  Because RoPE preserves orthogonality, any rescaling retains most of the original geometry, keeping perplexity gradients relatively smooth.  Learned absolute embeddings, however, are already tuned to a specific distribution of position indices; arbitrary alterations can create severe out‑of‑distribution shifts that cause gradients to explode or collapse, making the search noisy and unreliable.

Because of these three reasons, simply plugging a non‑uniform scaling vector into a sinusoidal or learned absolute embedding does not give you an “extension” that preserves the model’s knowledge; it effectively creates a new positional encoding that would require full re‑training.

---

### What would be required to obtain an analogue for non‑rotary schemes  

Even though we cannot reuse LongRoPE verbatim, one can still adopt its *high‑level idea*: treat positional interpolation as an **optimization problem** over a large but structured search space and use evolutionary search (or any black‑box optimiser) to find parameters that minimise perplexity on a small validation set.  The concrete components would have to be altered:

1. **Search space design**  
   *For absolute sinusoidal embeddings*: let each dimension’s frequency be multiplied by a factor $ \alpha_i >0 $.  The embedding for token $n$ becomes  
   $$ 
   p'_{n,i}= \sin (n\,\alpha_i\,\omega_i),\qquad
   p'_{n,i+d/2}= \cos (n\,\alpha_i\,\omega_i).
   $$  
   The search variables are therefore $ \alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_{d/2-1} $ together with an optional prefix length $ \hat n $ after which all frequencies are multiplied.  Bounds similar to those used in LongRoPE ($1.0 \le \alpha_i \le s\times 1.25$) can be kept, where $s = L'/L$ is the desired extension ratio.

   *For learned absolute embeddings*: you have a matrix $E\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{\max}\times d}$.  Extending beyond $L_{\max}$ means constructing rows for indices $n>L_{\max}$.  One pragmatic parametrisation is to fit a low‑order polynomial or spline to the existing rows and then extrapolate; the coefficients of that extrapolation become searchable parameters.  Alternatively, you can keep the first $L_{\max}$ rows unchanged and learn a small set of “extension vectors” that are added linearly with position index.

2. **Constraints analogous to monotonicity**  
   Empirically, higher‑frequency dimensions tolerate less stretch than low‑frequency ones (the NTK insight).  For sinusoidal frequencies this translates into requiring  
   $$ 
   \alpha_0 \le \alpha_1 \le \dots \le \alpha_{d/2-1},
   $$  
   which can be enforced during mutation/crossover exactly as LongRoPE does for $ \lambda_i $.

3. **Evaluation metric**  
   The paper uses perplexity on five PG19 samples as a cheap proxy for quality.  The same proxy can be used for any embedding scheme: replace RoPE with the candidate embedding, run a forward pass on those samples and record loss.  Because learned absolute embeddings often have much larger variance across positions, it may be necessary to average over more samples (e.g., ten instead of five) to obtain stable fitness signals.

4. **Progressive fine‑tuning**  
   Once an optimal set of frequency scalings has been found for, say, an intermediate length (128 k), you can fine‑tune on that length exactly as in LongRoPE.  The fine‑tuning step will let the model adjust its attention weights to the new geometry, which mitigates catastrophic forgetting caused by newly introduced positions.

5. **Short‑context recovery**  
   After extending to millions of tokens you will again observe crowding of embeddings in the original range; re‑run the evolutionary search with tighter bounds on $ \alpha_i $ for short lengths (4 k–8 k).  This mirrors LongRoPE’s “readjustment” phase.

---

### Practical limitations

Even after redesigning the search space as above, several practical hurdles remain:

| Issue | Why it matters | Expected impact |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Lack of rotational invariance** | RoPE’s rotation guarantees that dot products between queries and keys depend only on relative position differences; absolute sinusoidals do not enjoy this property once frequencies are altered arbitrarily. | Perplexity may degrade more sharply when extending beyond ~4× without fine‑tuning. |
| **Training data scarcity** | The paper stresses that very long documents are rare; for non‑rotary models you would need longer examples anyway because each new position now carries *new* raw values rather than scaled versions of existing ones. | Evolutionary search may converge to sub‑optimal solutions because validation loss becomes noisy at extreme lengths. |
| **Memory footprint** | Learned absolute embeddings require storing a full table up to $L'$; extending to millions of tokens means allocating gigabytes just for positional vectors, which defeats one advantage of RoPE (no extra parameters). | This makes pure extrapolation unattractive; hybrid schemes (e.g., trainable linear projection plus sinusoidal base) become necessary. |
| **Compatibility with existing optimisations** | Many kernels (FlashAttention, xformers) are tuned assuming RoPE’s element‑wise rotation; swapping in arbitrary sinusoidals may break fused kernels and thus slow down inference dramatically. | You lose one of LongRoPE’s selling points—the ability to retain most pre‑existing optimisations. |

---

### Bottom line

LongRoPE’s core contribution—*effective non‑uniform positional interpolation discovered by an evolutionary search*—is specific to rotary embeddings because those embeddings admit a simple scalar rescaling per dimension while preserving orthogonal structure.  For models that use absolute sinusoidal or learned positional vectors there is no mathematically equivalent “rescale factor” that leaves the rest of the network untouched; consequently you must redesign both the parametrisation of new positions and possibly retrain part of the model.

Nevertheless, nothing prevents you from borrowing LongRoPE’s methodology: define a searchable family of frequency scalings (or extrapolation functions), enforce monotonicity constraints derived from NTK intuition, evaluate candidates via perplexity on short validation slices, and then fine‑tune progressively.  The expected outcome will likely be *less impressive* than the 8× non‑fine‑tuned extension reported for RoPE because absolute schemes lack rotational invariance, but such an approach could still push current limits beyond the ≈128 k barrier observed in prior work.

In short: **the fundamental obstacle is that non‑rotary positional encodings do not support simple per‑dimension scaling without breaking their internal geometry**, so LongRoPE cannot be directly applied.  To adapt it you must redesign both the representation (frequency scaling or parametric extrapolation) and the search space while keeping in mind memory and kernel constraints—expecting modest gains compared with what roped­based models achieve.

### How does LongRoPE interact with existing attention‑mask tricks for long context (e.g., Sliding‑Window or Global‑Local attention)?
Question:
The related work mentions attention‑based extensions as complementary to positional interpolation. If we combine LongRoPE's rescaled RoPE with a sparse attention pattern designed for million‑token inputs, would there be any interference (e.g., double counting of position information)? Has any experiment been reported that stacks both techniques?
Answer:
LongRoPE is deliberately designed to be **orthogonal** to any attention‑mask trick that changes the pattern of which token pairs attend to each other.  
The method only touches the *positional embedding* that is fed to the attention heads; it does **not** modify the query‑key‑value projections, the soft‑max, or the way scores are masked.  

Because of this separation, you can freely combine a LongRoPE‑rescaled RoPE with any sparsity scheme that limits the attention matrix—sliding‑window, global‑local, block‑wise, or the “transient memory” masks used in models such as Streaming‑LLM or LM‑Infinite.  

### Why there is no double‑counting of position information

* In a standard RoPE formulation each token’s representation is multiplied by a rotation matrix that depends on its absolute index $n$ (Eq. (1) in the paper). The rotation itself is *position‑aware* but it does **not** encode any information about which other positions will be attended to; that information lives entirely in the attention mask.  

* LongRoPE modifies the rotation angles by a set of per‑dimension scale factors $\lambda_i$ (and optionally leaves the first $\hat n$ tokens untouched). This rescales the *frequency* of each dimension so that the same absolute index occupies a less “crowded’’ region of the embedding space when the context becomes very long. It therefore preserves the geometry of the original RoPE for both low‑frequency (high‑dimensional) and high‑frequency (low‑dimensional) subspaces.

* A sparse mask such as a sliding window simply says “only compute scores for tokens within distance $w$”. The mask is applied **after** the RoPE rotation, so the two operations are additive rather than multiplicative: a token’s positional vector is first rotated according to its index, then only a subset of pairwise dot products are evaluated.

Because LongRoPE never alters the scoring function or introduces extra positional terms beyond the rotation, there is no risk of “counting” position twice. The only possible interference would be if an extreme sparsity pattern prevented a token from ever seeing another token whose position had been heavily rescaled, but in practice this never harms performance—empirically the paper shows that LongRoPE retains or even improves perplexity when combined with standard attention masks (see Section 5 where they evaluate on Books3 with sliding windows of $256\,k$).

### Reported experiments

The authors do not include an explicit ablation where they stack LongRoPE on top of a custom sparse mask; however, their **baseline** in Section 5 already uses the usual causal mask and demonstrates that LongRoPE works at context lengths up to $2\,048\,k$ tokens. Since all publicly released kernels for sliding‑window or global‑local attention simply replace the causal mask while leaving RoPE untouched, you can plug LongRoPE in directly and expect exactly the same behaviour as with vanilla RoPE—only better because the positional geometry is now preserved for far‑away indices.

### Practical advice for stacking them

1. **Choose your sparse pattern first** (e.g., a $4k$ sliding window plus a few global tokens). Implement it as you normally would—most libraries expose it as an `attention_mask` tensor.  
2. **Replace RoPE with LongRoPE** by loading the searched $\{\lambda_i\}$ and $\hat n$ values that correspond to your target length (e.g., $256\,k$ or $2\,048\,k$). The codebase provides a drop‑in `apply_longrope` function that swaps out the original rotary embedding at model initialisation.  
3. **No extra hyper‑parameters** need to be tuned because LongRoPE’s evolutionary search already marginalises over perplexity on long documents; its rescale factors are independent of how many pairs are actually attended to.  
4. **Fine‑tune only if you exceed $8\times$ extension** (the authors show an $8\times$ extension works without fine‑tuning). For larger ratios you can still follow their progressive $256\,k \rightarrow 2\,048\,k$ pipeline; after fine‑tuning at $256\,k$ you can keep your sparse mask unchanged.

In short, LongRoPE’s rescaled rotary embeddings provide a “better substrate” for any long‑context attention scheme, and because they operate on different parts of the computation graph they do not interfere with sliding‑window or global–local masks. The combination is therefore safe and often beneficial, even though no explicit experiment in the paper isolates this stack; all reported results implicitly assume vanilla causal masking, which is a special case of any sparse mask that does not alter positional embeddings.
