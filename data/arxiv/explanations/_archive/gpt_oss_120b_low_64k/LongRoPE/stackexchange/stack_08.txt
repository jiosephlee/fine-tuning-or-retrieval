\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Can you elaborate on the “information entropy” argument for non‑uniformity in RoPE dimensions?
Question:
The authors claim that positional embedding exhibits "complex non-uniform information entropy" across dimensions and positions, which motivates dimension‑wise rescaling. What metric or experiment quantifies this entropy? Is there a theoretical justification (e.g., frequency coverage) that explains why lower frequency dimensions should be interpolated more aggressively?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper does not introduce a new closed‑form entropy formula for the RoPE angles; instead it *empirically* measures the “information entropy” of the positional embedding by looking at how much the language‑model loss (i.e. the perplexity) changes when a candidate interpolation is applied.  In other words, the *metric* that quantifies the entropy loss is the **next‑token prediction loss (perplexity) computed on a representative set of long documents**.  

Below I walk through why perplexity is a sensible proxy for an entropy measure, how the authors use it to discover non‑uniform rescaling factors, and why lower‑frequency dimensions should be interpolated more aggressively.

---

### 1. What “information entropy” means in this context?

RoPE encodes a token at position $n$ as a set of sinusoidal rotations

$$
\bigl[\cos (n\theta_0),\;\sin (n\theta_0),\;\cos (n\theta_1),\;\dots,\;\sin (n\theta_{d/2-1})\bigr],
\qquad \theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
$$

where $\theta_i$ are decreasing frequencies.  If we view each RoPE dimension as a *channel* that carries positional information, then a perfect channel would let the transformer distinguish any two positions that appear in the data.  When we stretch the context window, we have to **compress** those channels because we keep the same number of dimensions but now need to represent many more positions.  The compression inevitably discards some of the information that was originally present – this loss can be interpreted as a reduction of *entropy* in those channels.

The paper therefore treats **perplexity** as an observable consequence of that entropy reduction: if a particular interpolation destroys too much positional information, the model will make more mistakes on next‑token prediction, and its perplexity will rise.  Conversely, an interpolation that preserves most of the useful positional signal will keep perplexity low.  Because perplexity is readily computed on any validation corpus, it serves as a practical estimator of the information‑entropy retained after interpolation.

---

### 2. How do the authors quantify it?

1. **Dataset for probing** – They pick a handful of long passages (5 × PG‑19 validation documents for most experiments; 3 × Pile‑Books3 for very long windows).  
2. **Search objective** – For any candidate set of rescaling factors $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$ (and an optional prefix length $\hat n$ that is left unscaled), they replace the original RoPE angles with  

   $$
   \cos\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\,\mathbb I(\lambda_i,\hat n)\Bigr), \qquad
   \mathbb I(\lambda_i,\hat n)=
   \begin{cases}
   1 & n<\hat n\\[2pt]
   1/\lambda_i & n\ge \hat n .
   \end{cases}
   $$

3. **Metric** – They run a forward pass on the selected documents, compute the negative log‑likelihood of each token, average it, and exponentiate to obtain perplexity.  
4. **Evolutionary search** – The algorithm mutates and recombines promising candidates while enforcing a monotonicity constraint $\lambda_0\le \lambda_1\le\dots$ (higher dimensions may be scaled more). The candidate with the smallest perplexity after a few dozen generations is taken as the optimal non‑uniform interpolation.

Thus “complex non‑uniform information entropy” is *observed* through how different rescaling patterns affect perplexity; there is no separate analytic entropy score reported in the paper.

---

### 3. Why do lower‑frequency dimensions tolerate stronger interpolation?

Recall that RoPE frequencies obey  

$$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
\quad
\beta = \theta^{2/d}.
$$

The **first few dimensions** correspond to *high‑frequency* sinusoids (small periods), while later dimensions are low‑frequency, slowly varying functions of position.

From a signal‑processing viewpoint:

* A high‑frequency sinusoid can already resolve fine differences between nearby tokens even when its argument is modestly compressed; compressing it further quickly makes adjacent positions map onto almost identical values (the “crowding” problem).  
* A low‑frequency sinusoid changes slowly over many positions, so even a substantial compression still yields distinguishable values across a long range.

The NTK theory cited in the paper formalises this intuition: each RoPE dimension contributes roughly an eigenfunction of the kernel with eigenvalue proportional to its frequency.  When we stretch the context, we would like to **preserve eigenfunctions with large eigenvalues** (the high‑frequency components) because they carry more discriminative power for nearby tokens.  Consequently we assign them **smaller scaling factors** ($\lambda_i$ close to 1, i.e., little interpolation).  Conversely, low‑frequency components can be scaled up ($\lambda_i > 1$) without causing severe information loss, because they already vary slowly and thus are less sensitive to crowding.

The evolutionary search discovers precisely this pattern: empirically optimal $\lambda_i$ increase monotonically with $i$, confirming the theoretical expectation from NTK/frequency coverage arguments.

---

### 4. Position‑wise non‑uniformity

In addition to dimensionwise scaling, the authors notice that **the first few token positions are disproportionately important**: they attract large attention scores in most layers and therefore any distortion there propagates strongly through all subsequent computations. By leaving those early positions untouched ($\hat n>0$) they avoid corrupting those high‑impact signals, which again shows up as lower perplexity in experiments (Table 4 of the paper).

---

### 5. Summary

* The “entropy” argument is operationalised by **measuring perplexity** after applying a candidate positional interpolation; lower perplexity indicates higher retained information entropy.
* A simple evolutionary search over per‑dimension rescale factors (and an optional prefix length) finds non‑uniform schedules that minimise perplexity.
* Theory from NTK and Fourier analysis predicts exactly what the search uncovers: **high‑frequency dimensions must be interpolated mildly**, while **low‑frequency dimensions can tolerate stronger scaling**, because frequency determines how much positional discrimination each channel provides.
* Preserving early token positions further reduces information loss because those positions dominate attention.

Thus, the combination of an empirical loss proxy (perplexity) and a frequency‑based theoretical bias yields an effective way to answer “how much should each RoPE dimension be interpolated?” and explains why lower–frequency dimensions receive more aggressive rescaling.
