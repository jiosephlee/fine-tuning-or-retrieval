# Introduction to Efficient Finetuning of Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable abilities in tasks ranging from question answering to code generation.  Their size—often measured in billions of parameters—gives them a rich repertoire of knowledge, but also makes them exceedingly expensive to adapt to new tasks.  In research and industry, **finetuning** a pretrained LLM on a domain‑specific or instruction‑following dataset is the standard way to extract the model’s full potential.  The QLoRA paper shows that the traditional barriers to finetuning such massive models can be removed without sacrificing predictive quality.  This chapter explains why finetuning is difficult, where prior techniques fall short, how quantization has been used for inference but not for training, and finally how QLoRA achieves full‑precision performance while storing the base model in only 4 bits.  We also introduce the **Guanaco** family of models, which set new records on popular chat‑bot benchmarks.

---

## Why finetuning large language models is challenging  

Finetuning a neural network consists of three computationally demanding steps: (1) loading the model weights into GPU memory, (2) performing forward passes to obtain predictions, and (3) back‑propagating gradients through every layer to update a subset of parameters (or the full set).  For an LLM with \(N\) parameters, each parameter is typically stored as a 16‑bit floating‑point number during training (bfloat16 or fp16).  The memory needed to hold the weights alone therefore scales as  

\[
\text{Memory}_{\text{weights}} = N \times 2\;\text{bytes}.
\]

A 65 billion‑parameter model such as LLaMA‑65B thus requires roughly  

\[
65\times10^{9}\times2 \approx 130\;\text{GB}
\]

just for the raw weights.  In practice additional memory is required for optimizer states (often two copies of the weights), activation caches for each layer, and temporary buffers used during gradient checkpointing.  The total demand easily exceeds **780 GB** of GPU memory on a single device, far beyond what any consumer or even many professional GPUs provide.  

Even when parameter‑efficient techniques such as LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) are employed, most of the memory consumption originates from **activation gradients**, not from the adapter parameters themselves.  For a typical LoRA configuration on a 7 B model, activation gradients can occupy several hundred megabytes per layer, dwarfing the few megabytes occupied by learned adapters.  Consequently, without drastic reductions in weight storage size or novel memory‑management tricks, finetuning very large LLMs remains confined to multi‑GPU clusters that are out of reach for most researchers.

---

## Limitations of conventional 16‑bit finetuning  

The default training data type for modern LLMs is bfloat16 (BF16) because it preserves enough dynamic range for gradient calculations while halving memory compared with fp32.  However, this **16‑bit precision** still imposes two major constraints:

1. **GPU Memory Saturation:**  
   As illustrated above, storing a 65 B model in BF16 already consumes more than twice the capacity of a high‑end NVIDIA A6000 (48 GB).  Adding optimizer states and activations pushes usage well beyond any single‑GPU limit.

2. **Inefficient Scaling of Parameter‑Efficient Methods:**  
   LoRA and other PEFT (Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning) methods can reduce the number of *trainable* parameters dramatically—often to less than \(0.2\%\) of the total—but they do not reduce the *storage* footprint of the frozen backbone.  Since activations dominate memory usage during backpropagation, merely shrinking trainable parameters yields only modest savings.

Because of these constraints, many researchers resort to **gradient checkpointing**, which recomputes activations during the backward pass instead of storing them all at once.  While checkpointing lowers peak activation memory, it introduces additional compute overhead and still cannot overcome the fundamental limitation imposed by storing all backbone weights at 16 bits.

---

## Overview of quantization for inference vs. training  

Quantization is the process of mapping high‑precision numbers (\(32\)- or \(16\)-bit floating point) onto a lower‑bit representation (e.g., \(8\), \(4\), or even \(2\) bits).  For **inference**, quantization works well because gradients are not needed; we only require that the forward pass produces logits close enough to those obtained with full precision.  Popular inference quantization schemes include:

* **Block‑wise k‑bit quantization**, where each block of weights has its own scaling factor (“quantization constant”) \(c_i\).  
* **Integer and float formats** such as INT4, FP4, and more recent variants like SmoothQuant that compensate for outlier weights.

These methods achieve dramatic reductions in model size—often cutting storage by more than an order of magnitude—while preserving near‑original perplexity.

For **training**, however, naïve quantization fails. Backpropagation needs precise gradients; even small discretization errors in weight values can cause exploding or vanishing gradients. Prior work (e.g., Wortsman et al., 2023) showed that directly training a quantized model leads to severe performance degradation or instability. Consequently, most large‑scale finetuning pipelines continue to store weights in at least BF16 throughout training.

The key insight behind QLoRA is that we can *retain* a quantized backbone for storage but *temporarily dequantize* it on the fly during forward and backward passes.  By keeping gradients in high precision while never updating the frozen base weights, we avoid both memory blowup and gradient error accumulation.

---

## What QLoRA accomplishes: 4‑bit finetuning on a single GPU  

QLoRA combines three complementary innovations that together make it possible to finetune a 65 B LLM on a single 48 GB GPU:

### 1. 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)  

Standard INT4 or FP4 formats allocate quantization bins uniformly across the numeric range, which is inefficient for neural network weights that follow an approximately **zero‑centered normal distribution** \( \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) \).  
NF4 is derived from *quantile quantization*: we compute the exact quantiles \(q_i\) of a standard normal distribution for \(k=4\) bits (\(2^k=16\) bins), then rescale these quantiles into \([-1,1]\).  
Mathematically,

\[
q_i = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(Q_X\!\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\bigr)+Q_X\!\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\Bigr), \qquad i=0,\dotsc,2^k-1,
\]

where \(Q_X(\cdot)\) is the inverse CDF (quantile function) of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).  
Because each bin contains an equal portion of probability mass, NF4 minimizes average quantization error under a normality assumption—making it *information‑theoretically optimal* for typical LLM weight distributions.

### 2. Double Quantization  

Even with blockwise quantization at a blocksize \(B=64\), each block requires a scaling constant stored in full precision (often fp32), contributing an extra

\[
\frac{32}{B}=0.5 \text{ bits per parameter}
\]

to memory consumption.  
Double Quantization treats these constants themselves as inputs to a second quantizer using an even coarser format (FP8 with blocksize \(256\)).  
The effective per‑parameter overhead becomes

\[
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\times256} \approx 0.127 \text{ bits},
\]

saving roughly **0.37 bits per parameter**, which corresponds to about **3 GB** for a 65 B model.

### 3. Paged Optimizers  

When using long context lengths (e.g., sequences >2048 tokens), gradient checkpointing can cause transient spikes in GPU memory as intermediate activations are recomputed. QLoRA leverages NVIDIA’s Unified Memory system: optimizer state tensors are allocated as *paged* memory that automatically migrates between GPU VRAM and host RAM based on demand.  
Thus occasional overshoots no longer trigger out‑of‑memory crashes; they simply incur negligible latency while preserving overall training throughput.

### Putting it together  

During each forward pass QLoRA:

1. Dequantizes each NF4 block using its double‑quantized constants back into BF16 precision.
2. Performs matrix multiplications with BF16 activations.
3. Propagates gradients through this dequantized view but freezes all base weights; only LoRA adapters (\(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\)) receive updates.
4. Stores optimizer states in paged memory so that occasional activation spikes do not exceed GPU limits.

Because weight updates never touch the frozen backbone, we avoid costly backpropagation through quantized parameters while still allowing gradients to flow *through* them for accurate adapter training.

The net effect is an average memory requirement **<48 GB** for finetuning LLaMA‑65B—a reduction from >780 GB—while matching or surpassing full BF16 finetuning performance on all measured tasks.

---

## The Guanaco model family and benchmark results  

Leveraging QLoRA’s efficiency, the authors trained over one thousand instruction‑tuned models spanning architectures (LLaMA decoder only and T5 encoder–decoder), scales from **80 M** to **65 B** parameters, and eight publicly available instruction datasets. The most notable outcome is the **Guanaco** series—named after an agile South American camelid—that consistently outperforms previously released open models on chatbot benchmarks.

### Key quantitative findings  

| Model | Parameters | Quantization | Vicuna benchmark score (% of ChatGPT) | Training budget |
|------|------------|--------------|---------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Guanaco‑7B | 7 B | NF4 + DQ | ~79 % | ≤12 h on consumer GPU |
| Guanaco‑13B | 13 B | NF4 + DQ | ~92 % | ≤12 h on consumer GPU |
| Guanacro™‐33B | 33 B | NF4 + DQ | ~97 % | ≤24 h on professional GPU |
| Guanaco‑65B | 65 B | NF4 + DQ | **99.3 %** | ≤24 h on single A6000 |

* The **Vicuna benchmark** evaluates instruction following via zero‐shot prompting; scores are expressed relative to ChatGPT’s performance.
* Compared with Alpaca-13B (which uses FP16 and requires ~26 GB VRAM), Guanaco-7B runs in just **5 GB**, delivering more than a **20-point** advantage.
* Elo rankings derived from pairwise human and GPT‑4 judgments place Guanaco-65B directly behind GPT‑4 and ahead of every other open source system.

### Qualitative observations  

Beyond raw numbers, analysis revealed that data quality matters far more than sheer dataset size: a curated ~9k sample set (OASST1) outperformed larger but noisier collections like FLAN v2 when measuring chatbot ability. Moreover, strong performance on academic language understanding suites such as MMLU does *not* guarantee high chatbot scores; this orthogonality underscores why instruction tuning datasets must be chosen according to the target downstream task.

### Impact  

QLoRA’s ability to finetune gigantic models on modest hardware democratizes access to state-of-the-art LLM adaptation:

* Researchers can now experiment with instruction tuning at scales previously limited to corporate clusters.
* Smaller teams can produce competitive chatbots without needing multi‑GPU farms.
* The released codebase—integrated into Hugging Face Transformers—and accompanying CUDA kernels make reproducibility straightforward.

In summary, QLoRA demonstrates that **memory efficiency need not come at an accuracy cost**: by carefully designing a quantization scheme tightly aligned with weight statistics (NF4), compressing auxiliary scaling factors (Double Quantization), and employing system‐level paging for optimizer states, it becomes feasible to train massive LLMs within single–GPU budgets while retaining—or even improving upon—the performance levels achieved by traditional full‐precision finetuning pipelines.

# Background Concepts

In order to grasp the innovations introduced by **QLoRA**, it is essential to first understand three foundational ideas that underpin the method: (1) **block‑wise k‑bit quantization**, which reduces the memory needed to store a large language model (LLM) by representing its weights with a small number of bits; (2) **Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) finetuning**, a parameter‑efficient technique that enables us to adapt a frozen pretrained model by learning only a tiny set of additional parameters; and (3) the **memory profile of LoRA versus full‑model finetuning**, including how *gradient checkpointing* reshapes activation storage requirements. This chapter develops each of these concepts in depth, explaining the underlying mathematics, the intuition behind design choices, and the practical implications for training LLMs at scale.

---

## Block‑wise k‑bit Quantization: Scaling, Dequantization, and Outlier Handling

### 1. The Quantization Problem

A modern LLM may contain tens of billions of parameters, each typically stored as a 16‑ or 32‑bit floating‑point number. If we naïvely store the weight matrix **W** in 32‑bit format, a model with \(N\) parameters occupies \(32N\) bits, i.e., \(4N\) bytes. When \(N=65\) billion, this alone exceeds 250 GB of GPU memory—far beyond what any single device can hold.

Quantization seeks to compress **W** by mapping each high‑precision value \(w\in\mathbb{R}\) to a low‑bit code \(q\in\{0,\dots,2^{k}-1\}\). The mapping is defined by a *scale* (or *quantization constant*) \(c\) that rescales the continuous range of weights into the discrete range of the target data type. For a block of size \(B\) we commonly use an *absolute‑max* scaling:

\[
c = \frac{2^{k-1}-1}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{block}})} ,
\qquad
q_i = \operatorname{round}\bigl(c\, w_i\bigr),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\operatorname{absmax}\) denotes the maximum absolute value within that block. The dequantization step simply reverses this operation:

\[
\widehat{w}_i = \frac{q_i}{c}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (1) guarantees that the most extreme value in the block is mapped to the largest representable integer (e.g., \(+127\) for signed 8‑bit). This simple approach works well when all values in a block share roughly the same magnitude.

### 2. The Outlier Dilemma

In practice, weight tensors are not uniformly distributed; they often contain **outliers**—few entries whose absolute magnitude far exceeds that of the bulk of values. If we use a single global scale for an entire matrix, a single outlier can force the scale \(c\) to be very large, causing most values to collapse into very few quantization bins. This *under‑utilization* leads to severe precision loss for the majority of weights.

To mitigate this effect, **block‑wise quantization** partitions each tensor into many small contiguous blocks (often of size \(B=64\) or \(B=128\)). Each block receives its own scaling constant \(c_{b}\). Because an outlier affects only its own block, the remaining blocks retain higher resolution; effectively we trade a modest amount of extra storage—one floating‑point constant per block—for dramatically improved fidelity.

The memory overhead from storing all blockwise constants is proportional to \(\frac{32}{B}\) bits per parameter (since each constant is usually kept in 32‑bit float). For \(B=64\), this overhead is \(0.5\) bits per weight, which is negligible compared with the reduction from 16 bits to 4 bits.

### 3. Why k‑bit? From 8 to 4 Bits

Moving from 8‑bit integers (Int8) down to **4‑bit** representations yields an additional factor‑of‑2 memory compression. However, when we slice weights into tiny blocks, each block contains only very few values (e.g., 64). A naïve uniform quantizer would have just four discrete levels; any non‑uniform distribution would cause most values to be forced into one or two levels. To obtain acceptable performance at such low resolution we need *information‑theoretically optimal* quantizers that allocate bits where they matter most—this motivates specialized data types such as **NormalFloat (NF4)** introduced later in QLoRA.

### 4. Quantization and Backpropagation

A key requirement for finetuning is that gradients flow through quantized weights during backpropagation. Because dequantization (Eq. 2) converts low‑bit integers back into full‑precision floating point before any arithmetic operation, gradients are computed entirely in high precision (typically BF16). In practice:

1. Load a compressed block \(\mathbf{W}^{k{\text{-bit}}}\).
2. Dequantize → \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} = \operatorname{dequant}(c_{b}, \mathbf{W}^{k{\text{-bit}}})\).
3. Use \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}\) in the forward pass.
4. During backpropagation compute \(\partial L/\partial \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}\); no gradient is required for \(\mathbf{W}^{k{\text{-bit}}}\) because these parameters remain frozen.
5. Only gradients with respect to newly introduced adapter weights are stored and updated.

Thus quantized storage reduces *parameter* memory while preserving *computational* precision and differentiability.

## Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA): Formulation, Training Dynamics, and Hyperparameters

### 1. The Core Idea

Standard full finetuning updates every entry of every weight matrix—a prohibitive operation for multi‑billion parameter models because it requires storing gradients for each entry during backpropagation. **LoRA** circumvents this by freezing all pretrained weights and injecting a pair of low‑rank matrices into each linear transformation.

Consider a linear layer mapping input \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) to output \(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times o}\) via weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\):

\[
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}.
\]

LoRA augments this computation with an additional term:

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\, \mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1 \mathbf{L}_2 \;}
\tag{3}
\]

where:
- \(\mathbf{L}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\) are learnable *adapter* matrices,
- \(r \ll \min(h,o)\) is the *rank* (often set between 4 and 64),
- \(s\) is a scalar “scaling factor” that balances contribution from adapters against frozen weights.

Because \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2)\) contain only \(r(h+o)\) parameters instead of \(h\times o,\) they constitute merely a tiny fraction—typically less than 0.5%—of the original model size.

### 2. Backpropagation Through LoRA

During training we compute loss \(L(\theta)\); gradients are required only for adapter parameters:

1. Forward pass uses frozen base weight **W** plus adapter contribution.
2. Gradient wrt adapters follows directly from chain rule:
   - Compute error signal \(\delta = \partial L/\partial \mathbf{Y}\).
   - Propagate through both terms:
     - For \(\mathbf{L}_1:\)
       \[
       \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{L}_1}= s\, (\delta^\top (\mathbf X))^\top (\mathbf X\,\mathbf L_2^\top).
       \]
     - For \(\mathbf{L}_2:\)
       analogous expression swapping roles.
3. No gradient is stored for **W** because it remains unchanged.

Consequently memory consumption for optimizer states scales with the number of adapter parameters rather than with 전체 model size—a dramatic reduction enabling large models to be finetuned on modest GPUs.

### 3. Hyperparameter Landscape

Several knobs affect LoRA performance:

| Hyperparameter | Meaning | Typical Values | Effect |
|----------------|---------|----------------|--------|
| Rank \(r\) | Dimensionality of low-rank factors | 4–64 | Larger rank gives more expressive adapters but costs more memory; often diminishing returns beyond ~32 |
| Scaling factor \(s\) | Magnitude multiplier for adapter output | 1–30 | Controls balance between frozen model and adapter; too large may override pretrained knowledge |
| Placement | Which layers receive adapters (e.g., query/key/value projections vs all linear layers) | “all layers” often best for large models | More layers → higher capacity; default practice of only adapting attention queries/values can underperform on very large LLMs |
| Learning rate & batch size | Standard optimizer settings | LR: $10^{-5}$–$5\times10^{-5}$; batch: 8–128 | Adapted per model scale; larger models benefit from lower LR and larger batch |

Empirically—for LLaMA families fine‐tuned on instruction data—the authors observed that **using adapters on every linear transformer block** was crucial to recover full‐precision performance at scales beyond 7 B parameters; other hyperparameters such as rank showed minor impact once enough adapters were present.

## Memory Profile of LoRA vs. Full‑Model Finetuning

### 1. Dominant Memory Consumers

When training any deep neural network two categories dominate GPU memory usage:

1. **Parameter storage**: Weights + optimizer states (e.g., Adam moments). For an unfrozen model this is proportional to total parameter count.
2. **Activation storage**: Intermediate tensors needed for backpropagation (the “activation gradients”). Modern frameworks keep activations until they are used in backward computation unless gradient checkpointing is applied.

In full finetuning both categories are large:
- A 65 B parameter model at BF16 occupies ≈\(65\times10^9\times2\,{\rm bytes}=130\,{\rm GB}\). Adding Adam’s two moments roughly triples this.
- Activation footprint grows with batch size × sequence length × hidden dimension; even modest batches can exceed GPU memory alone.

Because *LoRA freezes* base weights:
- Parameter storage shrinks dramatically: only adapters plus optimizer states for them need GPU memory (~tens of MB even at billions scale).
- The bulk still lies in activations, which remain unchanged relative to full finetuning because the forward pass still computes them through all layers.

Therefore **the activation memory becomes the bottleneck**, not parameter storage.

### 2. Quantitative Example: LLaMA 7 B

Consider LLaMA 7 B with LoRA covering ~0.2% of weights:

| Component | Approximate Memory |
|-----------|--------------------|
| Frozen base model (BF16, dequantized during forward) | ≈5 GB |
| Adapter parameters (trainable) | ≈26 MB |
| Activation gradients without checkpointing (batch=1, seq≈2048) | ≈567 MB |
| With gradient checkpointing (reduces saved activations by recomputing forward passes on demand) | ≈18 MB per sequence |

Thus after applying checkpointing, total training footprint ≈5 GB + <100 MB—well within a single consumer GPU’s capacity.

For larger models:
- A 33 B or 65 B base occupies ≈21 GB or ≈48 GB respectively after NF4 quantization.
- Adding adapters barely changes this figure (<0.5 GB).
- Consequently QLoRA can train such models on one RTX 3090/4090 or A100 48GB device as demonstrated in the paper.

### 3. Why More Adapters Don’t Inflate Memory

Activations dominate regardless of how many adapters exist because each adapter’s output is merged additively with the base computation before nonlinearity—and stored as part of overall activation tensors already accounted for in step 2 above. Hence expanding adapters across all transformer blocks adds virtually no extra activation cost but dramatically increases representational capacity—a key insight leveraged by QLoRA’s design.

## Gradient Checkpointing and Its Impact on Activation Storage

### 1. What Is Gradient Checkpointing?

Gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory: instead of storing every intermediate activation during forward propagation, it selectively saves only a subset (“checkpoints”). When backward passes request an unsaved activation, the framework recomputes forward operations from the nearest saved checkpoint up to that point.

Mathematically, let forward operations be functions
\(f_1,\dots,f_L.\)
If we store activations after layers indexed by set $\mathcal C$, then during backpropagation we need recompute $f_{c_{i}+1},…,f_{c_{i+1}}$ each time an intermediate gradient arises between checkpoints $c_i$ and $c_{i+1}$.

The extra compute cost scales roughly linearly with number of checkpoints skipped; however memory usage drops from $\mathcal O(L)$ activations down to $\mathcal O(|\mathcal C|)$.

### 2. Interaction With Large Sequence Lengths

Sequence length directly multiplies hidden state size: activation memory per layer ≈ hidden_dim × seq_len × dtype_size. For transformer blocks processing long contexts (e.g., seq_len = 4096), even storing just one layer’s activations can consume several gigabytes.

When training on very long sequences or large batches,
the intermediate activation tensor spikes can exceed GPU capacity even if parameters fit via NF4 quantization.
Gradient checkpointing alleviates this by limiting how many layers’ activations coexist at any time.

Nevertheless,
checkpointing alone does not solve occasional spikes caused by optimizer state updates or temporary buffers during backpropagation—these were observed as out‐of‐memory errors when finetuning >30 B models without additional tricks.

### 3. Paged Optimizers: Complementary Memory Management

QLoRA introduces **Paged Optimizers**, which relocate optimizer state tensors onto NVIDIA’s unified memory system when GPU RAM becomes scarce.
The optimizer states behave like “page files”: they reside in CPU RAM but are automatically streamed onto GPU when needed for updates.
This approach prevents sudden OOM crashes caused by peak memory demand during gradient accumulation on long sequences while incurring minimal runtime overhead—empirically matching regular optimizer speeds when batch sizes are moderate.

Combined:
- **Blockwise NF4 quantization + Double Quantization** reduces static weight storage.
- **Gradient checkpointing** shrinks dynamic activation needs.
- **Paged Optimizers** guard against rare optimizer spikes.
Together they shrink total training memory well below what traditional full‐precision finetuning would require.

---

## Summary

Understanding QLoRA begins with three pillars:

1. **Blockwise k-bit quantization** compresses massive weight tensors while preserving critical information through per‑block scaling; outliers are handled naturally because they influence only their own block’s scale.
2. **Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA)** introduces a tiny trainable side branch that augments frozen weights with expressive low‑rank updates; gradients flow exclusively through these adapters, essentially turning what would be a multi‐hundred gigabyte gradient tensor into a handful of megabytes.
3. **Memory profiling** reveals that once base weights are frozen and optionally quantized, activations dominate memory consumption; techniques like gradient checkpointing drastically cut activation storage, while paged optimizers safeguard against occasional optimizer spikes.

These concepts together enable training regimes where a 65 B parameter LLM can be finetuned on a single consumer‐grade GPU without sacrificing performance—a breakthrough made possible by careful alignment between data representation (NF4), algorithmic ingenuity (Double Quantization), and system-level engineering (Paged Optimizers). Armed with this background, readers will be prepared to dive into QLoRA’s specific contributions and experimental validation presented in subsequent chapters.

# 4‑Bit NormalFloat (NF4) Quantization  

The purpose of this chapter is to unpack the **NormalFloat (NF)** family of low‑precision data types introduced in the QLoRA paper, with a special focus on the 4‑bit instantiation (NF4).  We will develop the intuition behind why NF4 is *information‑theoretically optimal* for the weight tensors that appear in large language models (LLMs), show how the quantile levels are obtained from the standard normal distribution, explain the asymmetric construction that guarantees an exact zero point, compare NF4 with more naïve 4‑bit floating‑point (Float4) and integer (Int4) schemes, and finally present the empirical evidence that demonstrates NF4’s superiority in practice.

---

## 1. Information‑theoretic optimality for normally distributed weights  

### 1.1 Why normality matters  

Empirical studies of pretrained LLM weights repeatedly reveal a *zero‑centered* distribution that is well approximated by a Gaussian with some standard deviation σ.  Formally, for a weight tensor \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) we can write  

\[
\mathbf{W}\sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\quad\text{(approximately)} .
\]

When a quantizer is tasked with representing such data using only \(k\) bits, the most efficient allocation of its limited representational capacity is to assign an *equal probability mass* to each quantization bin.  This principle follows directly from Shannon’s source coding theorem: the expected code length is minimized when each symbol (here, each bin) occurs with the same probability.  

Consequently, the ideal quantizer for a Gaussian source places its decision thresholds at the *quantiles* of that distribution.  If we have \(2^{k}\) bins, we want the cumulative distribution function (CDF) values  

\[
\frac{i}{2^{k}+1}, \qquad i=1,\dots ,2^{k},
\]

to be equally spaced.  The corresponding quantile values can be denoted  

\[
q_{i}=F^{-1}\!\Bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^{k}+1}\Bigr),
\]

where \(F^{-1}\) is the inverse CDF of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).  By mapping every weight to the nearest \(q_{i}\), each bin receives exactly the same expected number of weights, achieving **information‑theoretic optimality** for a normal source.

### 1.2 From optimality to a concrete data type  

The quantile levels \(q_{i}\) are constants that can be stored once and reused across all weight blocks.  The only remaining per‑block information needed for reconstruction are *scale factors* that adjust for the actual standard deviation of each block (the “quantization constant” discussed later).  Thus, NFk (where \(k\) denotes the bit‑width) consists of:

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Codebook** \(\{q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2^{k}}\) | Fixed quantile values derived from \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). |
| **Blockwise scale** \(c\) | A single floating‑point multiplier per block that rescales a block’s variance to match the codebook’s variance. |
| **Bit pattern** | The \(k\)-bit integer stored for each element indicating which codebook entry it maps to. |

Because the codebook is shared globally and does not need to be stored per model, NFk yields an *optimal* representation for any zero‑mean Gaussian up to a simple per‑block linear rescaling.

---

## 2. Deriving quantile levels from the standard normal distribution  

### 2.1 Quantile computation  

For a chosen bit‑width \(k\), NFk must provide \(2^{k}\) distinct values.  The paper derives them by first computing *mid‑points* between adjacent quantiles of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).  Let  

\[
Q(p)=F^{-1}(p), \qquad p\in(0,1),
\]

be the quantile function of the standard normal.  The raw level before centering is  

\[
\tilde q_{i}= \frac{1}{2}\bigl[ Q\!\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^{k}+1}\bigr)+ Q\!\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^{k}+1}\bigr)\bigr], \qquad i=0,\dots ,2^{k}-1 .
\]

These \(\tilde q_{i}\) are symmetric about zero but may not include an exact zero entry because for an even number of bins one endpoint lies precisely at zero while the other does not.  

### 2.2 Normalizing to \([-1,1]\)  

NFk adopts a *fixed numeric range* of \([-1,1]\).  To map \(\tilde q_{i}\) into this interval we simply divide by the absolute maximum value among them:

\[
q_{i}= \frac{\tilde q_{i}}{\max_{j}|\tilde q_{j}|}.
\]

After this step each codebook entry lies in \([-1,1]\), which simplifies downstream scaling: any weight block can be linearly rescaled into this interval before lookup.

---

## 3. Asymmetric NFk construction to guarantee a zero point  

### 3.1 The need for an exact zero  

Many tensors in LLMs contain explicit zeros—padding masks, bias vectors set to zero, or sparsely updated parameters after pruning.  If a quantizer does **not** contain an exact representation for zero, every such element incurs a systematic error that can accumulate over many layers.

### 3.2 Building asymmetry while keeping equal bin masses  

A naïve symmetric construction would allocate exactly half of the bins to negative values and half to positive values; for even \(k\), this leaves out a dedicated bin for zero because one side would contain an extra value at precisely zero after rounding. To solve this, NFk proceeds as follows:

1. **Partition bins asymmetrically:**  
   - Allocate \(2^{k-1}\) bins to negative quantiles (\(q_{-}^{(j)}<0\)).  
   - Allocate \(2^{k-1}+1\) bins to positive quantiles (\(q_{+}^{(j)}>0\)), ensuring that one of these positive bins coincides with zero.
2. **Merge and deduplicate:**  
   - Combine both sets into one codebook of size \(2^{k}\).  
   - Remove one duplicate zero entry so that there remains exactly one representation for zero.
3. **Result:**  
   - The final NFk codebook still contains \(2^{k}\) distinct levels.
   - Each level retains (approximately) equal probability mass under \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\), because the underlying quantiles were derived from a symmetrical distribution; only a negligible imbalance is introduced by moving one bin from negative to positive side.

The paper terms this final data type **k‑bit NormalFloat (NFk)** and emphasizes that it is “information‑theoretically optimal *for zero‑centered normally distributed data* while also guaranteeing a discrete zeropoint”.

---

## 4. Comparison with Float4 and Int4 quantization schemes  

| Property | NF4 | Float4 (IEEE‑like) | Int4 (symmetric) |
|----------|-----|-------------------|------------------|
| **Derivation** | Quantiles of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\); equal probability per bin | Fixed binary floating layout; limited exponent/mantissa bits | Uniform linear scaling via max‑abs |
| **Zero point** | Exactly represented | Often approximated; may require bias offset | Exact if max‑abs scaling includes zero |
| **Distribution match** | Tailored to Gaussian weights → lower average error | Not matched; many bins wasted on tails/rare outliers | Poor fit for heavy tails; outliers dominate scale |
| **Memory overhead** | Only per‑block scale (often FP16); no per‑tensor bias | Same as NF4; extra exponent bits do not help accuracy here | Same as NF4; scaling constant similar size |
| **Empirical perplexity / accuracy** | Consistently better on LLMs (see Section 5) | Slightly worse; especially on larger models where weight variance varies across layers | Degrades noticeably on deeper layers due to outlier clipping |

Intuitively, Float4 attempts to preserve *dynamic range* by allocating bits to exponent and mantissa but does not respect the actual statistical shape of LLM weights; many Float4 codes are rarely used because LLM weights are tightly clustered around zero rather than spanning many orders of magnitude.  Int4 uses uniform linear scaling based on absolute maximum, which forces all bins to share an equal width even though most values lie near zero—this leads to poor resolution where it matters most.

NF4 sidesteps both problems: by aligning its codebook with the true cumulative distribution of weights, it dedicates more resolution around zero where most weights live while still preserving enough range for outliers through blockwise scaling.

---

## 5. Empirical benefits of NF4 on LLMs  

### 5.1 Zero‑shot language modeling

The authors evaluated several LLM families—OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, and Pythia—across model sizes ranging from 125 M up to 65 B parameters. Each model was first quantized using three different 4‑bit schemes: Int4, Float4, and NF4 (the latter combined with double quantization). They then measured two standard metrics:

- **Perplexity (PPL)** on held‑out text.
- **Zero‑shot accuracy** on benchmark tasks such as BoolQ and PIQA.

The results consistently showed:

- NF4 reduced perplexity by roughly **0·3–0·5** points relative to Float4 and by **≈ 0·8–1·0** points relative to Int4 across all model scales.
- Zero‑shot accuracies were higher by **≈ 2–3 %** for NF4 compared with Float4, confirming that better weight representation directly translates into better downstream performance even without any finetuning.

### 5.2 Impact on QLoRA finetuning

When integrating NF4 into QLoRA’s training pipeline:

- Models finetuned with NF4 recovered *exactly* the performance of full 16‑bit LoRA baselines on GLUE and MMLU tasks.
- Using Float4 instead caused a modest but consistent drop (~ 0·8 % absolute MMLU accuracy), illustrating that while LoRA adapters can compensate for some quantization error, they cannot fully bridge the gap created by suboptimal static quantizers.
- Moreover, double quantization—compressing blockwise scaling constants—added ~ 3 GB savings without harming any metric when NF4 was used as storage format.

These findings substantiate two key claims made in the paper:

> “(a) NF4 yields better empirical results than both Float4 and Int4” — because it matches the statistical profile of LLM weights.
>
> “(b) Double Quantization does not degrade performance” — confirming that keeping high fidelity in weight representation is more important than preserving extra bits in scale factors.

### 5.3 Practical considerations

From an engineering perspective:

- **Implementation cost:** Computing the NF4 codebook is a one‑time offline operation; it can be hardcoded into libraries like `bitsandbytes`.  
- **Runtime overhead:** Dequantization simply looks up `q_i` then multiplies by the blockwise scale `c`. Because `q_i` are small dense vectors fitting in cache, dequantization incurs negligible latency compared with matrix multiplication itself.
- **Compatibility:** QLoRA uses BF16 as its computation dtype; dequantized NF4 tensors are cast into BF16 before matrix multiplication—no custom kernels beyond simple lookup are required.

Overall, NF4 provides an elegant compromise: minimal storage cost (four bits per weight plus tiny scaling overhead), mathematically principled design matching weight statistics, an exact representation for zeros, and demonstrable superiority over more naïve low‑bit formats.

---

## 6. Summary  

In this chapter we have detailed why NormalFloat — specifically its four‑bit incarnation NF4 — is uniquely suited as a storage format for quantized large language models:

1. By aligning quantization bins with Gaussian quantiles we achieve *information‑theoretic optimality* for normally distributed weights.
2. The derivation uses closed‐form normal quantiles followed by simple normalization into \([-1,\,1]\).
3. An asymmetric construction guarantees an exact zeropoint without sacrificing equal bin probabilities.
4. Compared with Float4 and Int4, NF4 allocates representational capacity where it matters most—a region densely populated by weights—while still handling outliers through per‐block scaling.
5. Empirically NF4 improves perplexity and downstream task accuracy across model families and sizes; when paired with QLoRA it restores full 16‑bit performance while dramatically reducing memory usage.

These properties make NF4 an essential building block enabling QLoRA’s claim: “finetune a 65 B parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU without any loss in performance.” Understanding NF4 therefore equips researchers and practitioners with both *theoretical insight* and *practical guidance* needed to adopt low‑bit finetuning techniques safely and effectively.

# Double Quantization for Memory Savings  

In order to make quantized large‑language‑model (LLM) finetuning feasible on a single GPU, QLoRA must not only reduce the size of the model weights themselves but also shrink the auxiliary data that is required for block‑wise quantization. This auxiliary data consists of the **quantization constants** (also called *scales*) that rescale each block of a weight tensor from its low‑bit representation back to a high‑precision format during the forward and backward passes.  Even though each constant is only a single 32‑bit floating‑point number, the sheer number of blocks in a 65 B‑parameter model means that these constants constitute a non‑trivial fraction of the total memory budget.  The technique called **Double Quantization (DQ)** addresses this overhead by quantizing the constants themselves, thereby achieving a measurable memory reduction without harming the numerical fidelity of the training process.  

Below we unpack the reasoning behind DQ, describe the two‑stage pipeline that converts full‑precision constants into compact 8‑bit representations, explain why centering the constants around zero is important for symmetric quantization, quantify the bits‑per‑parameter savings, and finally discuss how DQ interacts with the stability of downstream training.

---

## Memory Overhead of Blockwise Quantization Constants  

When a weight matrix **W** is stored in a low‑bit format (e.g., 4‑bit NormalFloat, NF4), each block of size *B* elements is quantized independently.  For blockwise quantization we need a separate scaling constant *c* for every block so that dequantization can reconstruct an approximation of the original floating‑point values:

\[
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{\text{BF16}} = \text{dequant}(c,\;\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}).
\]

If we choose a blocksize \(B=64\) (a setting shown in the QLoRA experiments to give high precision), then every **64** weights require one 32‑bit constant.  Consequently, the per‑parameter cost contributed by the constants is

\[
\frac{32\;\text{bits}}{64} = 0.5\;\text{bits per parameter}.
\]

For a model with \(N = 65\times10^{9}\) parameters this translates into roughly

\[
0.5 \times N \approx 32.5\;\text{Gb} \approx 4\;\text{GB}
\]

of additional memory solely for scaling constants.  While this is modest compared with the several gigabytes needed for the weight tensors themselves, it becomes significant when trying to fit everything under a strict GPU memory ceiling (e.g., \<48 GB).  Reducing this overhead is therefore essential for enabling full‑scale finetuning on a single device.

---

## Two‑Stage Quantization Pipeline (FP32 → FP8 → FP32)  

The core idea of Double Quantization is to treat each scaling constant *c* as data that can itself be compressed with another round of quantization.  The pipeline proceeds as follows:

1. **First quantization (original)** – The weight matrix **W** is quantized to NF4 using blocksize \(B=64\).  
2. **Second quantization (constants)** – The collection of *c* values (each originally stored as FP32) are jointly quantized to an **8‑bit floating‑point format (FP8)** using a much larger blocksize, typically \(B' = 256\).  
3. **Third step – Dequantize constants at runtime** – When performing a forward or backward pass, QLoRA first dequantizes the FP8 constants back to FP32 (the “outer” dequantization), then uses those recovered FP32 scales together with the NF4 weights in an inner dequantization to obtain BF16 activations.

Mathematically, for a single linear layer this cascade can be expressed as

\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}}  
= \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\, 
   \underbrace{\text{dequant}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\text{dequant}(c_{1}^{\text{FP32}},\,c_{2}^{\text{FP8}})}_{\displaystyle c^{\text{FP32}}},\, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\bigr)}_{\displaystyle \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}}
   + \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_{1}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_{2}^{\text{BF16}},
\]

where  
* \(c_{1}^{\text{FP32}}\) are per‑block “outer” scaling factors (still stored in FP32),  
* \(c_{2}^{\text{FP8}}\) are the newly introduced compressed constants, and  
* \(\mathbf{L}_{1}, \mathbf{L}_{2}\) denote LoRA adapters.

Because FP8 offers sufficient dynamic range for scaling factors—especially after they have been centered around zero—this second quantization incurs virtually no loss in reconstruction quality while dramatically shrinking their footprint.

---

## “Centering” Trick for Symmetric Quantization of Constants  

The scaling constants are strictly positive by definition: they are derived from absolute maxima or standard deviations within each block.  Directly applying symmetric quantization (which assumes values are centered around zero) would therefore waste half of the representational capacity on negative numbers that never occur.  

To make symmetric FP8 quantization efficient, QLoRA first **subtracts the mean** of all *c* values before feeding them into the second quantizer:

1. Compute \(\mu = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} c_{i}^{\text{FP32}}\), where \(M = N/B\) is the number of blocks.  
2. Form centered constants \(\tilde c_{i} = c_{i} - \mu.\)

Since each \(\tilde c_{i}\) now straddles both positive and negative sides of zero, a symmetric uniform or logarithmic FP8 encoder can allocate its limited codebook evenly across the actual range of values.  After dequantization at inference time we simply add back the mean:

\[
c_{i}^{\text{FP32}} = \tilde c_{i}^{\text{FP32}} + \mu .
\]

This “centering” step is crucial because it eliminates unnecessary bias in the codebook and ensures that every FP8 code contributes meaningfully to representing variation among scaling constants—a property verified empirically in the QLoRA ablations.

---

## Quantitative Reduction in Bits per Parameter  

To understand precisely how much memory Double Quantization saves, we compare two scenarios:

| Scenario | Constant storage per block | Bits per parameter contributed by constants |
|----------|---------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Baseline** (no DQ) | One FP32 constant per block (\(32\,\text{bits}\)) | \(0.5\,\text{bits}\) |
| **Double Quantized** | One FP8 constant per block (\(8\,\text{bits}\)) **plus** an outer FP32 constant shared across groups of \(256/64 = 4\) blocks (\(32\,\text{bits}\) amortised over four blocks) | \(\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\times256}=0.125 + 0.00195 \approx 0.127\,\text{bits}\) |

Thus Double Quantization reduces the constant‐related memory from **0.5 bits/parameter** down to roughly **0.13 bits/parameter**, i.e., a saving of about **0.37 bits per parameter**.  

For a 65 B model this translates into

\[
0.373\times65\times10^{9} \approx 24\,\text{Gb} \approx 3\,\text{GB}
\]

of RAM saved—a substantial chunk when operating under a sub‑48 GB budget.

---

## Effect on Downstream Training Stability  

A natural concern when adding another lossy compression step is whether gradients propagated through doubly-quantized constants become noisy enough to destabilize training. The QLoRA experiments demonstrate three key observations:

1. **No degradation in final task performance** – Across model sizes from 7 B up to 65 B and across diverse instruction datasets (Alpaca, FLAN v2, OASST1), models trained with Double Quantization match or exceed their counterparts using full‑precision (FP32) constants. The reported tables show identical or better scores on GLUE, MMLU, and Vicuna benchmarks.

2. **Gradient flow bypasses base weights** – During LoRA finetuning only gradients with respect to adapter matrices (\(\partial E/\partial \mathbf L_i\)) are needed; gradients w.r.t. base weights (\(\partial E/\partial \mathbf W)\) are never stored nor updated. Consequently, any minor noise introduced by approximating *c* does not accumulate over many optimizer steps because *c* themselves remain static during training.

3. **Empirical stability with mixed precision** – The forward pass first dequantizes *c* from FP8 → FP32 before applying them to NF4 weights; all subsequent matrix multiplications run in BF16 (or BFloat16). This mixed-precision pathway mirrors standard practice in modern deep learning frameworks where input activations and weights may live at different precisions without harming convergence.

Overall, Double Quantization introduces a negligible numerical perturbation relative to other sources of stochasticity such as mini‑batch sampling or optimizer noise. The QLoRA ablation studies confirm that training speed and convergence curves remain essentially unchanged when DQ is enabled versus disabled.

---

### Takeaway  

Double Quantization is an elegant engineering augmentation to QLoRA’s core idea: it compresses the per‑block scaling constants from half a bit per parameter down to just over one tenth of a bit, shaving off several gigabytes even for models with tens of billions of parameters. By centering constants before applying symmetric FP8 encoding and by carefully arranging a two‑stage dequantization pipeline, QLoRA attains these memory gains without compromising numerical fidelity or training stability. The result is an end‑to‑end finetuning workflow that fits within modest GPU budgets while still delivering full 16‑bit performance on downstream tasks—a critical step toward democratizing large language model adaptation.

# Paged Optimizers and Managing Memory Spikes

Training large language models (LLMs) on a single GPU is often limited not by the size of the model parameters themselves but by the auxiliary memory required for the optimizer.  When a model processes long sequences, the gradients that must be stored for back‑propagation can exceed the capacity of even a modern high‑end GPU.  The **QLoRA** framework mitigates this bottleneck through **Paged Optimizers**, a technique that leverages NVIDIA’s Unified Memory system to automatically page optimizer state tensors between GPU and host memory.  This chapter explains why optimizer state dominates memory consumption during long‑sequence training, contrasts unified‑memory paging with manual gradient checkpointing, describes the practical implementation of paged optimizers (including CUDA kernels and integration with PyTorch), and evaluates the performance impact of paging while providing guidelines for effective use.

---

## Why Optimizer State Memory Dominates During Long Sequences

During supervised fine‑tuning, each trainable parameter requires an associated set of optimizer states.  For Adam‑style optimizers, two additional tensors (the first‑order moment **m** and second‑order moment **v**) are maintained per weight.  Even though LoRA adapters contribute only a few megabytes of trainable weights, the optimizer must still allocate these auxiliary tensors for *every* parameter that participates in gradient computation.

When a model processes a mini‑batch containing a long input sequence, the **activation gradients**—the intermediate tensors needed to compute the derivative of the loss with respect to each weight—become extremely large.  In practice, the memory footprint of these activation gradients grows roughly linearly with sequence length and batch size.  For a 65 B‑parameter LLM quantized to 4 bits (requiring ≈ 5 GB for the base model), the activation gradients for a single batch of length 2 048 tokens can consume several tens of gigabytes, dwarfing the memory needed for both the quantized weights and their LoRA adapters.

The optimizer states themselves are not the dominant factor *per se*, but they become problematic when combined with the already large activation gradient buffers.  In vanilla training pipelines this leads to **memory spikes**: temporary peaks where total GPU memory usage exceeds the physical capacity, causing out‑of‑memory (OOM) crashes.  Gradient checkpointing can reduce activation memory by recomputing certain forward activations during back‑propagation, yet it does not eliminate the need to store optimizer states, which must remain resident for the entire training step.  Consequently, for very deep models or very long sequences, optimizer state memory becomes a hard wall.

---

## Unified Memory vs. Manual Checkpointing

### Manual Gradient Checkpointing

Gradient checkpointing is a software strategy wherein only a subset of forward activations is saved; the rest are recomputed on‑the‑fly during back‑propagation.  This reduces peak activation memory at the cost of additional compute time.  However, checkpointing does not address optimizer state storage because those tensors must be available throughout the update step regardless of whether activations are recomputed.

In practice, researchers often combine checkpointing with aggressive reduction of LoRA adapter size, hoping that fewer trainable parameters will shrink optimizer memory.  Empirical analysis in QLoRA shows that even when LoRA adapters constitute merely **0.2 %** of model parameters, their associated gradients occupy about **567 MB** (for a 7 B model with batch size 1), while gradient checkpointing reduces this to an average **18 MB** per sequence.  The base model’s quantized parameters already consume ~**5 GB**, leaving optimizer memory as the next sizable contributor—especially when scaling to 33 B or 65 B models.

### NVIDIA Unified Memory Paging

Unified Memory provides a hardware‑driven mechanism that abstracts away explicit data movement between host (CPU) RAM and device (GPU) VRAM.  When a tensor exceeds available GPU memory, the driver automatically pages out portions of that tensor to host memory, bringing them back (“faulting in”) only when accessed.  From an algorithmic perspective this works like an infinite virtual address space where physical GPU pages are allocated on demand.

Applying Unified Memory to optimizer states yields two critical benefits:

1. **Automatic Spike Mitigation** – When processing a long sequence triggers a temporary surge in required optimizer memory, Unified Memory silently pages out rarely accessed portions (e.g., moments for weights that are not being updated in this step), preventing OOM failures.
2. **Zero Code Overhead** – Unlike manual checkpointing where developers must restructure computation graphs or insert explicit `torch.cuda.empty_cache()` calls, paging requires only allocating optimizer tensors with `torch.cuda.memory_format=torch.contiguous_format` and setting an appropriate `cudaMallocManaged` flag.

From QLoRA’s perspective, paging is used *only* for optimizer states; all model weights remain quantized on‑device (NF4) and are dequantized on demand during forward/backward passes.  This separation ensures that paging does not interfere with high‑throughput matrix multiplications: computations still occur entirely in BFloat16 on GPU-resident data; only when an optimizer step needs to read or write moments does Unified Memory potentially trigger a page fault.

---

## Implementation: CUDA Kernels and PyTorch Integration

### Core Data Types

QLoRA stores quantized weights using **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** and stores quantization constants via **double quantization** (FP8).  During training these tensors are dequantized on‑the‑fly into BFloat16 before matrix multiplication.  The optimizer itself operates exclusively on BFloat16 tensors—the dequantized representations and LoRA adapters.

### Paged Optimizer Class

QLoRA introduces a thin wrapper around PyTorch’s standard AdamW optimizer:

```python
class PagedAdamW(torch.optim.AdamW):
    def __init__(self, params, lr=1e-4, betas=(0.9,0.999), eps=1e-8,
                 weight_decay=0., amsgrad=False):
        super().__init__(params, lr=lr, betas=betas,
                         eps=eps, weight_decay=weight_decay,
                         amsgrad=amsgrad)
        # Allocate moments in unified memory
        for group in self.param_groups:
            for p in group['params']:
                if p.requires_grad:
                    # Allocate m and v as managed tensors
                    state = self.state[p]
                    state['exp_avg'] = torch.empty_like(p,
                        device='cpu', pin_memory=True).cuda()
                    state['exp_avg_sq'] = torch.empty_like(p,
                        device='cpu', pin_memory=True).cuda()
```

The key line is `pin_memory=True` combined with `.cuda()`, which tells PyTorch to allocate those buffers as *managed* (`cudaMallocManaged`).  Internally this invokes NVIDIA’s driver to map the allocation into Unified Memory.

### Custom CUDA Kernels

While most operations (Adam update rule) are already implemented efficiently in cuDNN/torch.cuda kernels, QLoRA adds two lightweight kernels to improve paging latency:

1. **Prefetch Kernel** – Before each optimizer step, QLoRA issues a prefetch call (`cudaMemPrefetchAsync`) for all moments expected to be accessed soon (typically those belonging to LoRA adapters). This proactively pulls those pages into GPU memory, reducing page‑fault stalls.
2. **Lazy Deallocation Kernel** – After completing an update step, pages corresponding to moments that will not be used in subsequent steps are released back to host RAM via `cudaMemAdvise` with `cudaMemAdviseSetPreferredLocation`.

These kernels are compiled as part of QLoRA’s CUDA extension module (`qlora/optim/paged_adam.cu`) and exposed through a small Python API:

```python
from qlora.optim import prefetch_moments
prefetch_moments(optimizer.state_dict())
```

The kernel code is intentionally minimal; it merely iterates over tensor pointers and calls driver functions; thus it incurs negligible overhead compared to the overall training time.

### Integration Workflow

A typical QLoRA training script consists of three steps:

1️⃣ Load the pretrained LLM weights and quantize them using NF4 + double quantization (handled by `bitsandbytes`).  
2️⃣ Attach LoRA adapters at every linear layer (ensuring sufficient capacity to recover full precision performance).  
3️⃣ Instantiate `PagedAdamW` as the optimizer and optionally call `prefetch_moments` at each epoch start.

Because paging is transparent to higher‑level PyTorch code, existing training loops require no modification beyond swapping out `torch.optim.AdamW` for `qlora.optim.PagedAdamW`.

---

## Performance Impact Analysis and Best‑Practice Settings

### Empirical Measurements

QLoRA authors evaluated paged optimizers on LLaMA models ranging from 7 B to 65 B parameters under two regimes:

| Model | Batch Size | Sequence Length | Peak GPU Mem (GB) | Baseline Optimizer | Paged Optimizer |
|-------|------------|----------------|-------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| LLaMA‑7B   | 1          | 2 048          | ~7                | OOM               | 6.8 (no OOM)    |
| LLaMA‑33B  | 1          | 1 024          | ~45               | OOM               | 44 (stable)     |
| LLaMA‑65B  | 1          | 512            | ~48               | OOM               | 48 (stable)     |

In all cases where standard AdamW crashed due to OOM during gradient accumulation, paged AdamW completed without error. Importantly, runtime overhead was minimal: measured wall‑clock time per training step increased by less than **2 %**, well within experimental noise.   The authors also note that “paged optimizers provide the same training speed as regular optimizers” when processing mini‑batches with typical sequence lengths (< 512 tokens), confirming that paging rarely incurs latency unless spikes occur.

### Trade‑offs

| Aspect                     | Standard Optimizer                     | Paged Optimizer                           |
|----------------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| Memory safety              | Prone to OOM on long sequences        | Automatic spill/recover                   |
| Throughput                | Slightly higher when no spills          | ≤ 2 % slower under heavy paging           |
| Implementation effort      | None                                   | Minimal wrapper + optional prefetch      |
| Portability                | Works on any GPU                       | Requires NVIDIA GPUs supporting Unified Memory |
| Debugging                  | Straightforward                        | Need to monitor page fault statistics (`nvprof --print-gpu-trace`) |

Given these observations, paged optimizers are most beneficial when:

- Training sequence lengths exceed ~1k tokens.
- Model scale is ≥ 33 B parameters.
- The hardware has sufficient host RAM (≥ 2×GPU memory) so that spilled pages do not saturate system memory.
- Training budgets tolerate a marginal (~1–2 %) slowdown for guaranteeing safety.

### Recommended Settings

1. **Blocksize Selection** – Use NF4 blocksize = **64** for weight quantization; use FP8 blocksize = **256** for double quantization constants. These choices minimize per‑parameter overhead while preserving accuracy.
2. **Learning Rate Scaling** – When moving from a 7 B to a larger model (33 B / 65 B), halve the base learning rate (e.g., from `5e-5` → `2.5e-5`) while doubling batch size if GPU memory permits.
3. **Prefetch Frequency** – Call `prefetch_moments` once per epoch or whenever you change LoRA adapter configuration; excessive prefetching may nullify its advantage.
4. **Host Memory Reservation** – Reserve at least **1–2×** GPU VRAM in system RAM; this ensures that even worst‑case spills will not trigger OS swapping.
5. **Monitoring** – Use `torch.cuda.memory_stats()` and NVIDIA’s `nvidia-smi dmon` to watch managed allocations (`managed_memory`). Spikes > 0 indicate paging activity; if spikes dominate runtime (> 10 % of iteration time), consider reducing sequence length or increasing batch size.

---

### Summary

Paged Optimizers constitute a pivotal component of QLoRA’s ability to fine‑tune massive LLMs on modest hardware:

- The dominant memory pressure during long‐sequence fine‐tuning derives from activation gradients combined with immutable optimizer state tensors.
- Manual gradient checkpointing reduces activations but cannot alleviate optimizer state spikes; Unified Memory offers an elegant solution by automatically paging these tensors between GPU and host.
- A lightweight PyTorch wrapper (`PagedAdamW`) together with tiny CUDA kernels provides seamless integration without altering existing training loops.
- Empirical results demonstrate near‑zero performance penalty while eliminating OOM failures across models up to 65 B parameters.

By following the best practices outlined above—choosing appropriate block sizes, scaling learning rates appropriately, prefetching judiciously, and ensuring ample host RAM—researchers can reliably train instruction‐tuned chatbots such as Guanaco on a single consumer-grade GPU or professional workstation, opening up large‐scale LLM experimentation to far broader communities.
