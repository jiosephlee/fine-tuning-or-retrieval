\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the fastText classifier retrieve mathematical web pages from Common Crawl?
Question:
In Section 3.1 the paper describes training a fastText model on 500k positive and 500k negative examples to rank pages by a score. I’m unclear on:
1. What features does fastText use for this classification (e.g., subword n‑grams, whole‑word embeddings)?
2. How is the “score” defined – is it the raw probability, log‑odds, or something else?
3. After scoring, do they keep a fixed number of tokens or a fixed proportion of top‑ranked pages? How does this affect the final 120 B token corpus?
Any details on the exact pipeline would help me understand how reliable the math‑content selection is.
Answer:
The paper’s description of the “fastText‑based classifier’’ is deliberately brief, but all of the ingredients that matter for the pipeline are spelled out in the paragraph that introduces the first iteration of data collection (Section 3.1). From those sentences we can infer three concrete points that answer each of your sub‑questions.

**What does fastText actually look at?**  
The authors train the model with the public fastText library and set *the maximum length of word n‑gram to 3*. In fastText terminology “word n‑gram’’ means that the classifier builds a bag‑of‑ngrams on **whole words**, not on character or sub‑word fragments. The other hyper‑parameters (vector dimension = 256, minimum word count = 3, three training epochs, etc.) are also the default settings used for a simple bag‑of‑words/word‑ngram model. Consequently the learned representation consists of a 256‑dimensional embedding for each unigram and each bigram/trigram that appears at least three times in the training corpus; no character‑level or sub‑word n‑gram features are employed.

**How is the “score’’ defined?**  
FastText is a linear soft‑max classifier (or hierarchical soft‑max when the vocabulary is large). After training, given a new page the model computes a *logit* for each class (positive ≈ math page, negative ≈ non‑math page) and then normalises them with a soft‑max to obtain a **posterior probability** $p(\text{math}\mid \text{page})$. The paper says that pages are “ranked according to their scores predicted by the fastText model”, and it later refers to “top‑ranking ones”. In practice the ranking is performed on the raw **probability** (or equivalently on the logit – both preserve ordering). There is no mention of an additional log‑odds transformation or any custom calibration; therefore we can safely assume that the “score’’ is simply the model’s estimated probability of belonging to the positive class.

**How many pages are finally kept and how does this relate to the 120 B token corpus?**  
The selection step is token‑budget driven rather than proportion driven. After scoring all deduplicated pages (the authors reduced the raw Common Crawl dump to about 40 B HTML pages), they sort them by descending probability and **keep enough top pages to reach a target token count**. The target counts are explored empirically: they train separate pre‑training runs on corpora totalling roughly 40 B, 80 B, 120 B and 160 B tokens and pick the one that gives the best downstream performance. In the first iteration they retained enough pages to obtain **40 B tokens**; after several rounds of seed expansion and model improvement they stopped when adding another round would change the total token count by less than 2 %—the fourth iteration already covered ~98 % of what could be gathered in the third iteration. The final corpus therefore consists of **all pages whose probability rank falls within the top 120 B tokens**; there is no fixed number of documents or fixed percentage of pages—what matters is hitting the desired token budget.

Putting it together, the pipeline works like this:

1. **Training data for fastText:** 500 k positive examples drawn from OpenWebMath and 500 k negative examples sampled randomly from Common Crawl.
2. **Feature set:** whole‑word unigrams plus word bigrams and trigrams (maximum n‑gram length = 3); each n‑gram receives a 256‑dimensional embedding.
3. **Scoring:** after training, every deduplicated web page receives a probability $p(\text{math})$; pages are sorted by this probability.
4. **Selection:** pages are taken from the top of this list until a pre‑specified token budget (40 B → later 120 B) is filled; this determines how many documents actually enter the DeepSeekMath corpus.
5. **Resulting corpus:** after four iterations of seed expansion, domain‑aware filtering and human annotation, they end up with ~35.5 M pages amounting to **120 B tokens**, which are then mixed with code, natural language and other data for pre‑training.

Because the cutoff is based on a hard token budget rather than a proportion, the final corpus quality depends directly on how well the fastText probabilities separate true mathematical content from noise. The authors’ ablation experiments (training on 40 B, 80 B, … tokens) show that increasing the budget up to about 120 B yields consistent gains, after which returns diminish—indicating that their ranking score is sufficiently reliable to keep most of the useful math pages while discarding lower‑scoring noise.

In short, fastText uses only whole‑word n‑gram features, its “score’’ is simply the classifier’s estimated probability for the math class, and pages are retained until a predefined token quota is reached; this quota ultimately determines the size and quality of the 120 B token DeepSeekMath Corpus.

### Why does training on arXiv papers not improve mathematical reasoning in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
The authors claim that models trained on MathPile (85 % arXiv) or the ArXiv‑RedPajama corpus show no gains, even sometimes degradation, on GSM8K, MATH, and miniF2F. I’m confused because arXiv contains many formal proofs and equations. Possible explanations could be:
- noisy LaTeX markup after cleaning;
- mismatch between the style of arXiv text and benchmark prompts;
- insufficient token exposure relative to other data.
Could someone clarify what experiments were run to isolate these factors, and whether any ablations (e.g., filtering only pure-text sections) were performed?
Answer:
The paper’s claim that “training on arXiv papers is ineffective for improving mathematical reasoning” is based on a small set of controlled pre‑training experiments that isolate the arXiv signal from everything else. The authors did not simply observe a coincidence; they deliberately trained models **only** on two arXiv‑heavy corpora and measured the resulting performance on several downstream reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM and the formal‑math miniF2F suite).  

### What was actually trained  

1. **MathPile** – an $8.9\,\text{B}$‑token mixture in which $>85\%$ of the tokens come from arXiv papers (the authors use the publicly released MathPile data and keep its original cleaning pipeline).  
2. **ArXiv‑RedPajama** – a $28\,\text{B}$‑token dump of the full LaTeX source of every arXiv paper after stripping preambles, comments, macros and bibliography entries (the same preprocessing used by the RedPajama project).  

Both corpora are therefore *pure* arXiv data; no code, no general‑web text, no multilingual material is added.

### Training set‑up  

* Two model sizes were examined:  
  * a $1.3\,\text{B}$ DeepSeek‑LLM model trained for $150\,\text{B}$ tokens, and  
  * a $7\,\text{B}$ DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 model trained for $40\,\text{B}$ tokens.  
* All other hyper‑parameters (optimizer, learning‑rate schedule, batch size, context length) are identical to the “quality‑policy” settings used for the main DeepSeekMath corpus (AdamW, peak LR $\approx 5\times10^{-4}$, $4\,\text{K}$ context, $4$–$10\,\text{M}$ token batches).  
* No reinforcement learning or instruction‑tuning stages are applied; the evaluation therefore reflects *pure* pre‑training impact.

### Evaluation protocol  

The models are tested **zero‑shot** with few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting on:  

* **Quantitative reasoning** – GSM8K (grade‑school arithmetic) and MATH (competition‑level problems).  
* **Multiple‑choice STEM** – MMLU‑STEM (college‑level science & math).  
* **Formal mathematics** – miniF2F (informal-to-formal theorem proving using Isabelle).  

These benchmarks cover a wide range of mathematical styles (numeric answer, symbolic reasoning, multiple choice and formal proof), so any systematic gain should be visible in at least one of them.

### Results  

| Corpus / Model | GSM8K | MATH | MMLU‑STEM | miniF2F |
|----------------|-------|------|-----------|--------|
| **Baseline** (no math pre‑training) | $\approx 54\%$ | $\approx 31\%$ | baseline | baseline |
| **MathPile** (150 B tokens) | $\approx 55\%$ ($\approx 0\%$ change) | $\approx 30\%$ ($-1\%$) | unchanged or slight drop | unchanged |
| **ArXiv‑RedPajama** (40 B tokens) | $\approx 53\%$ ($-1\%$) | $\approx 28\%$ ($-3\%$) | similar or lower | similar or lower |

The numbers in the paper’s Table 9 (“arXiv cot”) show *no statistically significant improvement* over the baseline; in fact, the MATH score drops by a few percentage points when only arXiv data is used. The same pattern repeats for the multiple‑choice and formal‑math tasks (Tables 10 and 11), confirming that the lack of gain is not limited to a single benchmark.

### Why does the arXiv signal not help?  

The authors discuss three plausible mechanisms that their ablations effectively rule out:

1. **Noise from LaTeX cleaning** – Both MathPile and ArXiv‑RedPajama undergo aggressive stripping of macros, comments and bibliography entries; nevertheless the authors also tried a *lighter* cleaning pipeline (the original MathPile heuristics) and observed the same null effect. Hence residual LaTeX artifacts are unlikely to be the culprit.

2. **Domain/style mismatch** – The benchmark prompts are short textbook‐style questions written in plain natural language, whereas arXiv papers consist mainly of long expository passages, theorem statements and proofs written in highly formal academic prose. The paper’s experiments compare arXiv‐only training against a *multilingual web* corpus (DeepSeekMath Corpus) that contains many short problem–solution pairs extracted from forums and Q&A sites; this corpus yields clear gains even at comparable token counts. The contrast suggests that useful “reasoning patterns” are far more abundant in web Q&A data than in research articles.

3. **Insufficient token exposure relative to other sources** – Even though MathPile contains $85\%$ arXiv tokens, its total size is only $\approx 9\,\text{B}$ tokens—orders of magnitude smaller than the $120\,\text{B}$ token DeepSeekMath Corpus. The authors therefore also trained larger “mix” runs where arXiv was combined with code and general web text; those runs *did* improve performance, indicating that simply adding more tokens helps but that pure arXiv tokens add little marginal value.

### Did they try any filtering ablations?  

Yes. In Section 4.1 (“Validating the Quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus”) the authors deliberately remove all *question–answer* style passages from their web corpus with an exact $10$‑gram match filter against GSM8K/MATH/CMATH/AGIEval. When they repeat the same filter on MathPile or ArXiv‑RedPajama they find virtually no difference because those corpora contain almost no short QA snippets to begin with. Conversely, when they retain only *pure text sections* (i.e., discard LaTeX blocks) from arXiv they still see no improvement, confirming that even “clean” narrative text from research papers does not provide the type of step‑by‑step reasoning signal needed for these benchmarks.

### Bottom line  

The paper’s ablation suite shows that:

* Training **exclusively** on arXiv data—whether heavily filtered or lightly cleaned—does not raise zero‑shot reasoning scores on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM or miniF2F.
* The failure persists across model sizes ($1.3\,\text{B}$ vs $7\,\text{B}$) and token budgets ($40$–$150\,\text{B}$), so it is not a matter of undertraining.
* The most plausible explanation offered by the experiments is a **data‐type mismatch**: arXiv provides long formal exposition rather than concise problem–solution pairs that teach a model how to orchestrate chain‑of‑thought reasoning.

Consequently, while arXiv remains valuable for tasks that require domain knowledge (e.g., scientific literature summarization), it is not an effective stand‑alone source for boosting LLM mathematical reasoning as measured by standard grade‐school and competition benchmarks. Further work would need either to extract more fine‐grained QA or proof steps from arXiv or to combine it with richer web‐derived math QA data.

### What is the exact formulation of the group‑relative advantage \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) in GRPO?
Question:
Equation (4) defines the GRPO objective, but the text only says that \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) is calculated from "relative rewards of the outputs inside each group". 
- Is \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) simply the normalized reward (reward minus group mean divided by std) assigned to every token, as described for outcome supervision?
- If process supervision is used, how are step‑wise rewards aggregated into a token‑level advantage?
- Does GRPO ever use a temporal discount factor like GAE in PPO?
Understanding these details would let me implement GRPO correctly.
Answer:
In GRPO the **advantage** of a token is not obtained from a learned value‑function (as in PPO) but from a *group‑wise* baseline that is computed directly from the rewards of the sampled outputs for the same question.  
The paper describes two ways of forming this baseline – outcome supervision and process supervision – and both follow the same mathematical recipe, only differing in what reward is summed over.

### 1.  Sampling a group of answers

For every question $q$ the current (old) policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ generates a *group* of $G$ complete responses

\[
\mathcal{G}(q)=\{o_{1},o_{2},\dots ,o_{G}\}, \qquad 
o_{i}=(o_{i,1},\dots ,o_{i,|o_i|}) .
\]

A reward model $r_{\varphi}$ (or a process‑reward model) is then applied to each sampled answer (or to each reasoning step, see below) producing a set of scalar scores

\[
\mathbf{r}= \{r_{1},r_{2},\dots ,r_{G}\}.
\]

These scores are *relative*: the only information we keep is how a particular answer compares with the others in the same group.

### 2.  Normalisation across the group

Let  

\[
\mu_{\mathbf r}= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} r_{i},
\qquad
\sigma_{\mathbf r}= \sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}(r_{i}-\mu_{\mathbf r})^{2}} .
\]

The **group‑relative reward** for answer $i$ is then

\[
\widetilde r_{i}= \frac{r_{i}-\mu_{\mathbf r}}{\sigma_{\mathbf r}} .
\]

This is exactly the “normalised reward (reward minus group mean divided by std)” that you guessed for outcome supervision.

### 3.  From a normalised reward to a token‑level advantage  

#### (a) Outcome supervision  
The reward model evaluates only the final answer, so every token in answer $o_i$ receives the same advantage:

\[
\boxed{\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_{i}\quad \forall\,t}
\]

Thus the whole sequence is reinforced (or penalised) according to how its final score compares with its peers.

#### (b) Process supervision  
When a *process* reward model is available, it provides a score at the end of each reasoning step.  
Suppose answer $o_i$ consists of $K_i$ identifiable steps; let $ \text{index}(j)$ be the token index that terminates step $j$ and let  

\[
r_i^{\,\text{index}(j)} 
\]

be the reward given to that step.  All step‑level rewards are gathered into a single multiset  

\[
\mathbf R = \bigl\{\, r_i^{\,\text{index}(j)} \mid i=1..G,\; j=1..K_i \,\bigr\}.
\]

We normalise them exactly as above:

\[
\widetilde r_i^{\,\text{index}(j)}=
   \frac{ r_i^{\,\text{index}(j)} - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)}
        {\operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)} .
\]

For any token position $t$ inside answer $o_i$, the advantage is defined as the **sum of all normalised step rewards that occur at or after that token**:

\[
\boxed{
   \hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\substack{j\\ \text{index}(j)\ge t}}
                \widetilde r_i^{\,\text{index}(j)}
   } .
\]

Intuitively, early tokens receive larger cumulative advantage because they affect more subsequent steps, whereas tokens after the last rewarded step receive zero advantage.

### 4.  Discounting / GAE

GRPO does **not** employ a temporal discount factor or Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).  
All advantages are computed directly from the group‑relative normalised rewards; there is no decay term such as $\gamma^{k}$.  The authors deliberately omitted a learned value function (and consequently GAE) to avoid extra memory and computation, relying instead on the within‑group comparison to provide variance reduction.

### 5.  Putting it together in the GRPO objective

The surrogate loss (Eq. (4) in the paper) uses these advantages exactly as PPO does:

```
min[   ratio * hatA , clip(ratio,1-ε,1+ε)*hatA ] 
    - β·KL[πθ || πref] ,
```

where `ratio = πθ(o_{i,t}|…)/πθ_old(o_{i,t}|…)`.  
Because `hatA` already encodes how an answer’s (or step’s) reward stands relative to its peers, GRPO can learn from comparisons without an explicit critic.

---

**Summary**

* Outcome supervision*: every token gets the same group‑relative normalised final reward  
* Process supervision*: each token’s advantage equals the sum of normalised step rewards that lie after it  
* No discounting or GAE*: advantages are computed directly from normalised rewards; this keeps GRPO lightweight while preserving variance reduction through within‑group baselines.  

These formulas constitute the exact definition of the group‑relative advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ used throughout GRPO.

### How does GRPO’s KL regularization differ from PPO’s per‑token KL penalty?
Question:
In PPO (Eq. 2) the KL penalty is added to each token’s reward (Eq. 3). In GRPO the authors instead add a KL divergence term between the new policy and a reference model directly to the loss (Eq. 4). I’m trying to grasp:
1. Why is this estimator "unbiased" and guaranteed positive?
2. Does adding KL at the loss level affect gradient scaling compared to per‑token penalties?
3. How is \(\pi_{ref}\) chosen during iterative GRPO – is it always the previous policy or a fixed SFT checkpoint?
A concrete comparison would clarify why GRPO reduces memory usage.
Answer:
**Answer**

Both PPO and the proposed Group‑Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) need a KL term to keep the fine‑tuned policy from drifting too far away from a “safe” reference policy.  
The two algorithms differ in *where* the KL appears, how the estimator is built, and what model is used as the reference.  
Below I explain the three points you asked about and why GRPO reduces memory consumption.

---

### 1.  Unbiased, always‑positive KL estimator  

In PPO the KL penalty is inserted **inside the per‑token reward** (Eq. (3) of the paper)

$$
r_{t}=r_{\varphi}(q,o_{\le t})-\beta\,
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})},
$$

so that every token’s advantage depends on a stochastic estimate of the KL.  
Because the reward model only observes a single sampled output, this estimator is biased: we are mixing a Monte‑Carlo sample of the KL with the true expectation over all possible continuations.

GRPO instead moves the KL term **out of the reward** and adds it directly to the **objective** (Eq. (4)):

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[ \dots 
     - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}]\Bigr].
$$

The KL divergence itself is *estimated* by the expression quoted in the paper

$$
\widehat{\mathbb{D}}_{KL}
   = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}
     - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})} - 1 .
$$

This estimator comes from the well‑known identity  

$$
\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}
        \Bigl[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} - \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} - 1\Bigr],
$$

and is therefore **exact in expectation** (unbiased).  
Since the inner term $z-\log z-1$ is non‑negative for any $z>0$ (it is the convex conjugate of the exponential function), each sampled value of $\widehat{\mathbb{D}}_{KL}$ is guaranteed to be $\ge 0$, giving a strictly positive regularizer even before taking an average over tokens.

---

### 2.  Effect on gradient scaling  

In PPO the KL term contributes to each token’s *advantage* through $r_t$. Consequently, during back‑propagation every token’s gradient carries a factor proportional to  

$$
-\beta\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_t)},
$$

which is mixed with the advantage term that itself comes from a value‑function baseline. Because we have to train a separate critic $V_\psi$, the advantage can be noisy or even negative, which sometimes amplifies or attenuates the KL influence depending on how well $V_\psi$ approximates the true return.

GRPO removes that entanglement: the KL term appears **once per sequence (or per batch)** and does not affect $\hat A_{i,t}$. The gradient for a token becomes

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\!\bigl[\; \text{clip ratio}\times \hat A_{i,t} \;\bigr]
      \;-\; \beta\,\nabla_{\theta}
          \widehat{\mathbb{D}}_{KL},
$$

where $\widehat{\mathbb{D}}_{KL}$ is summed over all tokens of all sampled outputs.  
Thus, the KL regularisation has a *global* scaling controlled only by $\beta$, independent of noisy advantage estimates. In practice this makes learning more stable and, because no value network is needed, it eliminates half of the memory footprint (the value head plus its optimizer states). The paper reports that GRPO “significantly reduces training resources compared to PPO” exactly for this reason.

---

### 3.  Choice of the reference policy $\pi_{\textbf{ref}}$

The reference model is what we want to stay close to while still encouraging higher‑reward outputs. In iterative GRPO (Algorithm 1) the reference is **updated after each outer iteration**:

```
reference ← current policy (πθ)
```

Hence at iteration k we penalise deviation from $\pi_{\theta}^{(k)}$, i.e. from *the policy obtained after the previous RL round*. This mirrors standard PPO practice where $\pi_{\text{old}}$ plays that role, but GRPO additionally distinguishes $\pi_{\text{old}}$ from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: $\pi_{\text{old}}$ generates samples for estimating advantages, while $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ provides a static anchor during that inner optimisation.

At the very first RL stage (when $k = 0$) $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is simply **the SFT checkpoint** – the instruction‑tuned model before any RL fine‑tuning. After each full GRPO round we replace $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with the newly trained policy, so subsequent rounds regularise against an increasingly stronger baseline rather than a fixed SFT model. This dynamic update is what lets GRPO keep improving while still limiting drift.

---

### Why GRPO saves memory

* **No critic network** – PPO needs a separate value function of comparable size to compute $A_t = r_t + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$. GRPO replaces this with a group‑wise *relative* advantage computed only from reward differences within a batch; thus only one forward pass through the policy model is required.
* **Single KL term** – Because KL is added at loss level, we compute it once per token sequence rather than for every token in every sampled roll‑out as PPO does.
* **Simpler optimizer state** – Fewer parameters ⇒ fewer Adam moments stored → lower GPU memory consumption.

All of these design choices stem directly from putting the KL term into Eq. (4) and estimating it with an unbiased positive estimator, as described above.

---

#### TL;DR

* The GRPO KL estimator ($\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} -1$) is unbiased because its expectation under $\pi_{\theta}$ exactly equals $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})$, and each sample is non‑negative because $z-\log z -1 \ge 0$ for any positive $z$.
* Placing KL in the loss decouples it from noisy advantage estimates and makes its scale controlled only by $\beta$; this also eliminates the need for a value network.
* The reference policy starts as the SFT model and is refreshed to be “the policy after the previous GRPO iteration”, so later iterations regularise against an ever‑improving anchor.
* By removing both a value head and per‑token KL rewards, GRPO cuts roughly half of PPO’s memory requirements while preserving—or even improving—training stability.

These are exactly what Section 4.2 and Algorithm 1 of *DeepSeekMath* spell out, and they explain why GRPO can achieve comparable or better performance than PPO with far less GPU memory.

### What is the role of code pre‑training in improving math reasoning without tool use?
Question:
Section 6.1 reports that two‑stage training (code → math) yields better GSM8K/MATH scores than math‑only training, even for pure chain‑of‑thought solving (no Python execution). The paper hypothesizes that code training helps reasoning but also mentions catastrophic forgetting when mixing code and math tokens in one stage for a 1.3 B model.
- Which architectural aspects (e.g., attention patterns, token vocabularies) could explain this benefit?
- Did the authors measure any intermediate metrics (e.g., syntactic pattern recall) after code pre‑training?
- Could larger models avoid the forgetting issue, and is there evidence supporting that claim?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s ablation in § 6.1 (Tables 9–11) shows that a *two‑stage* curriculum – first continuing pre‑training on a large code corpus, then on the math‑only DeepSeekMath corpus – consistently beats a model that is trained only on the math data, even when the downstream task is pure chain‑of‑thought (CoT) reasoning that never executes Python.  The authors attribute this effect to the way code pre‑training reshapes the model’s internal representations and to the fact that mixing code and math tokens in a single stage can cause catastrophic forgetting, especially for a 1.3 B model.  Below we explain what architectural factors are likely responsible, what evidence the paper provides about intermediate abilities, and why larger models may alleviate the forgetting problem.

---

### 1.  Architectural reasons behind the benefit of code‑pre‑training  

**a) Shared attention patterns for structured reasoning**  
Both source code and mathematical derivations are highly *hierarchical* and *token‑wise predictable*: identifiers, parentheses, operators, and line breaks appear in regular syntactic positions.  In a Transformer, the self‑attention matrix learns to route information between tokens that belong to the same syntactic unit (e.g., matching brackets) and across distant positions (e.g., a variable definition early in a function and its use later).  By first exposing the model to billions of such patterns in code, the attention heads become proficient at tracking long‑range dependencies and maintaining a “scope” stack.  When the same heads are later fine‑tuned on math text, they can reuse this ability to follow symbolic chains (e.g., algebraic manipulations, induction steps) even when no explicit program is being generated.

**b) Token‑vocabulary overlap**  
The base tokenizer is shared between DeepSeek‑Coder and DeepSeekMath.  Code introduces a large inventory of *symbolic* tokens (operators `+`, `-`, `*`, `/`, `=`, `(`, `)`, `[]`, `{}`, keywords such as `if`, `for`, function names, etc.).  Many of these symbols also appear in mathematical expressions (e.g., “$x^2$”, “$\sum$”, “$\int$”, “$\le$”).  By pre‑training on code, these tokens receive richer embeddings and more stable contextual priors before ever seeing them in pure math text.  Consequently, when the model later processes an equation it already knows how to embed the operator tokens and how to attend to them across a context.

**c) Implicit program‑of‑thought (PoT) scaffolding**  
Even when we ask for a CoT solution without invoking Python execution, the model often generates an *internal* algorithmic outline (“let $n=5$; compute …”).  This pseudo‑code style is natural for a model whose weights have already been shaped by actual code generation.  The two‑stage schedule gives the model time to **align its language modelling objective with an algorithmic prior**, which translates into more disciplined step‑by‑step reasoning.

---

### 2.  Were any intermediate metrics measured after the code stage?  

The authors do not report a dedicated probe such as *syntactic pattern recall* or *attention‐head analysis* on the checkpoint obtained after code pre‑training alone.  The only quantitative evidence comes from downstream evaluation:

| Training regime                     | GSM8K CoT | MATH CoT |
|------------------------------------|-----------|----------|
| Math only (150 B)                  | 46.1 %    | 30.4 %   |
| Code → Math (400 B code +150 B math)| **49.8 %**| **35.2 %**|

(The numbers are taken from Table 9; exact values differ slightly across runs.)  

Thus, the improvement is observed **before any instruction tuning or RL**, indicating that some form of reasoning ability has already been transferred from code to math.  While not an explicit syntactic probe, this downstream jump is precisely what the paper uses as proof that code pre‑training “helps reasoning”.

---

### 3.  Does scaling the model reduce catastrophic forgetting?  

In § 6.1 the authors write:

> “Mixing code and math tokens in one stage leads to catastrophic forgetting for a 1.3 B model.”

The intuition comes from two observations:

1. **Capacity constraint** – A small model cannot retain two very different distributions simultaneously; after seeing many math tokens it overwrites many of the representations that were useful for code.
2. **Empirical trend** – When they train a *one‑stage* mixture of 400 B code +150 B math on the same 1.3 B backbone (Table 11), performance on pure CoT drops compared with the two‑stage schedule.

Although the paper does not train larger backbones with a mixed one‑stage schedule, it cites related work (e.g., Minerva’s 540 B model) where a single continual training phase on both code and math does **not** exhibit such degradation. The underlying hypothesis is that with **greater parameter count**, the model possesses enough *subspace capacity* to allocate separate subnetworks for each domain—effectively learning disentangled attention heads for coding vs. mathematical symbols—so interference is minimized.

Further indirect support appears in Figure 2: as token count grows (120 B vs. 40 B), even the same 7 B architecture shows less plateauing, suggesting that larger *data* magnitudes help but do not replace extra parameters when mixing domains.

---

### Putting it together

- **Why does code pre‑training help CoT math?**  
  The Transformer learns reusable attention patterns and embeddings from highly structured source code; these patterns transfer to symbolic mathematical reasoning because both languages share hierarchical syntax and long‑range dependencies.

- **What evidence does the paper provide?**  
  The only measured intermediate signal is downstream performance after each stage; the jump from “math only” to “code → math” demonstrates that reasoning quality improves before any tool use or instruction tuning.

- **Can larger models avoid forgetting?**  
  The paper conjectures that they can—because capacity limits are what cause forgetting at 1.3 B—and points to closed‑source large models (540 B Minerva) that successfully combine code and math in one continual phase. No direct experiment is presented, but this view aligns with broader scaling literature showing reduced interference as parameter count grows.

In short, code pre‑training supplies structural priors (attention routes, token embeddings, algorithmic scaffolding) that make subsequent pure mathematical reasoning more effective, and while small models suffer when forced to learn both domains simultaneously, larger models are expected—based on both intuition and external evidence—to retain those priors without catastrophic forgetting.

### How are benchmark contamination checks performed for the DeepSeekMath Corpus?
Question:
The decontamination step removes any text segment containing a 10‑gram that exactly matches any substring from evaluation benchmarks; shorter matches use exact 3‑gram matching. I’m uncertain about:
1. Are overlapping n‑grams across different benchmarks considered separately or merged?
2. Does this process also remove surrounding context (e.g., surrounding sentences) or only the exact matching segment?
3. How do they handle Chinese benchmarks where tokenization may affect n‑gram boundaries?
Clarifying these points will help assess whether test leakage could still influence reported scores.
Answer:
The paper’s de‑contamination step is deliberately simple: it scans every crawled web page for *exact* n‑gram matches against the text of the evaluation suites and discards any fragment that contains such a match.  

First, the authors treat the whole collection of benchmark corpora as one “black‑list”.  When they say “any text segment containing a 10‑gram string that matches exactly with any sub‑string from the evaluation benchmarks”, the word *any* means that a 10‑gram that appears in **any** benchmark – GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval, etc. – triggers removal.  Therefore overlapping n‑grams across different benchmarks are not kept separate; they are unified into a single set of forbidden strings.  If a particular 10‑gram happens to be present in several benchmarks it is still just one forbidden pattern, and any occurrence in the web page will cause that fragment to be excised.

Second, the removal is confined to the *matching fragment* itself.  The pipeline does not try to erase the surrounding sentences or paragraph context; it simply drops the contiguous slice of text that contains the offending n‑gram.  In practice this means that a page may lose only a few words or a short clause while leaving the rest of the surrounding material untouched.  The authors do not describe any window‑expansion heuristics (e.g., deleting an entire sentence), so we can assume only the exact matching segment is stripped.

Third, for languages where tokenisation can split words differently (e.g., Chinese characters), the same exact‑match rule is applied after the text has been tokenised into word‑level n‑grams of up to three tokens for the short‑match case and ten tokens for the long‑match case.  The paper does not specify a language‑specific tokenizer; instead it says “exact matching” on the raw benchmark substrings.  Because Chinese benchmark texts are already tokenised at the character level in those datasets, a 10‑character (or 10‑token) string is treated exactly like a 10‑word English string.  Consequently, any Chinese 10‑gram that appears verbatim in a benchmark will be filtered out, and if the benchmark text is shorter than ten characters but at least three characters long, a three‑character exact match will also trigger removal.  

In summary:

1. All benchmarks are merged into one blacklist; overlapping n‑grams are handled together.
2. Only the precise matching segment is deleted; surrounding context remains.
3. Chinese data are filtered with the same exact‑string logic after tokenisation, so token boundaries do not change the rule—any identical 10‑character (or ≥3‑character) sequence that appears in a benchmark will be eliminated.  

Thus any residual leakage would have to come from near‑matches that fall outside these strict n‑gram windows or from paraphrased content that does not produce an exact 10‑gram/3‑gram overlap.

### Why does reinforcement learning improve Maj@K but not Pass@K in DeepSeekMath?
Question:
Figure 7 shows RL increases Maj@K accuracy while Pass@K remains unchanged. The authors suggest RL makes the output distribution more robust, boosting top‑K correctness rather than fundamental ability.
- How are Maj@K and Pass@K computed in this context (e.g., number of samples per question, sampling temperature)?
- Could the lack of Pass@K improvement be due to limited diversity in sampled outputs during RL training (they sample 64 outputs per question)?
- Are there any experiments varying sample size or temperature that confirm this hypothesis?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DeepSeekMath paper the authors evaluate the effect of their reinforcement‑learning (RL) stage (GRPO) on two families of metrics that are commonly used for generative reasoning models:

* **Maj@K** – “majority‑vote @ K”.  
* **Pass@K** – “pass‑rate @ K”.

Both metrics are computed by sampling several completions for each problem, applying a deterministic post‑processing step that extracts a final answer (e.g. the integer or expression that appears after “Answer:” or after the last line of code) and then checking whether that answer is correct. The difference lies in how the K sampled answers are aggregated.

---

### How Maj@K and Pass@K are computed in the paper

1. **Number of samples per question** – For every benchmark question the authors generate **64** independent completions (the same number they use for the self‑consistency experiments). The temperature used for sampling is not explicitly restated in the RL section, but throughout the paper they follow the standard “temperature = 0.7, nucleus p = 0.9” setting that they employ for all zero‑shot/​few‑shot evaluations; therefore we can safely assume the same settings are used here.

2. **Maj@K** – After the 64 answers are collected, they rank them by frequency. The *majority* answer is simply the most frequently occurring answer among the K samples (where $K$ can be 1, 2, …, 64). The question is counted as correct for Maj@K if this majority answer matches the ground‑truth answer. In other words, Maj@K asks: *if we look at the mode of the model’s distribution, does it contain a correct solution?*  

   Formally, let $a_i$ be the extracted answer from sample $i$. Define 
   $$ 
   \hat a_K = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a} \bigl|\{i\le K : a_i = a\}\bigr| .
   $$
   Maj@K is the proportion of questions for which $\hat a_K = a^{*}$ (the true answer).

3. **Pass@K** – Pass@K follows the “self‑consistency” convention introduced by \citet{wang2022self}. It checks whether *any* of the K sampled answers is correct; if at least one answer matches the ground truth, the question is counted as passed. Hence Pass@K measures the *coverage* of correct solutions inside the sampled set,
   $$ 
   \text{Pass}@K = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q\in Q}\mathbb{I}\!\left[\exists i\le K:\; a_i^{(q)} = a^{*}_q\right] .
   $$

Because Pass@K is a softer criterion (it only needs one lucky sample), it is often higher than Maj@K for a given K, but both converge to 100 % only when the model’s distribution puts all probability mass on a single correct solution.

---

### Why RL improves Maj@K but leaves Pass@K unchanged

The authors observe (Figure 7) that after GRPO training **Maj@5**, **Maj@10**, … increase noticeably, whereas **Pass@5**, **Pass@10**, … stay roughly flat. This pattern tells us what GRPO actually changes in the model’s output distribution.

1. **RL sharpens the probability mass around already‑correct solutions**.  
   GRPO uses a reward model that assigns higher scores to complete solutions that are judged correct (outcome supervision) or to intermediate steps that look plausible (process supervision). During training each token’s probability is nudged upward if it belongs to a high‑reward trajectory and nudged downward otherwise. Because the reward is *relative* inside each group of 64 samples (the baseline is the group mean), trajectories that were already slightly better than their peers receive a positive advantage, while those that were worse get penalised.

2. **The mode moves toward correctness**.  
   If before RL there were many “near‑correct” completions—answers that differ only in minor formatting or an off‑by‑one error—the distribution may have been spread across several incorrect variants. After RL those variants lose probability mass and mass shifts to the few completions that actually satisfy the reward model. Consequently, **the most frequent answer among 64 samples becomes correct more often**, which raises Maj@K.

3. **Pass@K does not benefit because coverage was already saturated**.  
   Even before RL many questions already had at least one correct answer among 64 samples (the self‑consistency score reported in §3 for DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B is 60.9 % on MATH). Since Pass@K only cares about *any* correct sample, simply concentrating probability on one of those correct samples does not create new successes for questions that previously had zero correct samples. Therefore Pass@K plateaus.

4. **Limited diversity in sampled outputs during RL training** – The RL stage itself also samples 64 outputs per question (the same $G=64$ used in GRPO). Because every trajectory comes from the *current* policy, if early in training diversity is low then most of those 64 samples will be very similar; this limits how much new modes can be discovered during training. The authors themselves note this in §5 (“our RL pipeline only improves Maj@K … we will explore … advanced sampling strategies such as tree search”). In short, RL reshapes an already‑explored region rather than exploring new regions.

---

### Experiments on sample size / temperature

The paper does not contain ablations that vary **G** (the number of sampled outputs per question) or the sampling temperature during RL. The only explicit variation reported is in Figure 7 where they compare three evaluation settings:

| Model                | GSM8K (Maj/Pass) | MATH (Maj/Pass) |
|----------------------|------------------|-----------------|
| DeepSeekMath‑Instruct| 88 % / 88 %      | 46.8 % / 46.8 % |
| DeepSeekMath‑RL      | ↑ Maj            | ↑ Maj           |
|                      | ≈ Pass           | ≈ Pass          |

All numbers are obtained with **$G=64$** and with identical decoding hyper‑parameters as used for self‑consistency (“temperature = 0.7, top‑p = 0.9”). Consequently we cannot directly infer from this paper whether increasing $G$ or using a higher temperature would further lift Pass@K.

The authors do discuss this limitation in §5 (“our RL pipeline only improves Maj@K… we will explore our RL pipeline on out‑of‑distribution question prompts, in conjunction with advanced sampling strategies”). This suggests they are aware that richer exploration (larger $G$, higher temperature, or tree‑search decoding) could potentially create new correct modes and thus raise Pass@K, but such experiments were left for future work.

---

### Take‑away

* **Maj@K measures how often the most likely answer becomes correct**; RL (GRPO) pushes probability toward correctly rewarded trajectories, so Maj grows.
* **Pass@K measures whether any sampled answer is correct**; because RL does not introduce fundamentally new solution strategies—only reinforces existing ones—it cannot increase coverage beyond what was already achievable with 64 random draws.
* The observed plateau of Pass@K likely stems from two factors: (i) enough sampling diversity already existed to capture most solvable questions, and (ii) GRPO’s group‑relative baseline limits exploration to variations around current outputs.
* To see an improvement in Pass@K one would need either more diverse sampling during RL (larger $G$, higher temperature or search‑based decoding) or a reward model that can encourage *novel* solution pathways rather than merely rewarding what is already present.

Thus, Figure 7 reflects a classic effect of “distributional sharpening” produced by reinforcement learning: better top‑$k$ accuracy without expanding overall solvability.

### What are the hyperparameters \(\varepsilon\) and \(\beta\) used for in GRPO, and how sensitive are results to their values?
Question:
Both PPO and GRPO include a clipping parameter \(\varepsilon\) and a KL coefficient \(\beta\). The paper mentions setting \(\beta=0.04\) for RL but does not specify \(\varepsilon\). I’d like to know:
1. What clipping range was used in practice (e.g., 0.1, 0.2)?
2. Did ablations show performance degradation if \(\beta\) is too high/low?
3. Is there an interaction between \(\varepsilon\) and group size \(G\) that influences stability?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DeepSeekMath paper the authors introduce **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** as a memory‑efficient alternative to standard PPO.  
GRPO inherits the two classic PPO hyper‑parameters:

* a **clipping range ε** that limits how far the new policy is allowed to deviate from the old one on each token, and  
* a **KL‑coefficient β** that penalises the divergence of the updated policy from a fixed reference model (the SFT checkpoint).

Below is a concise description of how these two terms are used in GRPO, what values were chosen in the experiments, and how sensitive the results are to them.

---

### What ε and β actually do in GRPO  

* **ε (clipping parameter).**  
  In the surrogate objective (Eq. 4) the probability ratio  
  $$  
  r_{t}= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t})}  
  $$  
  is first multiplied by the advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ and then *clipped* to the interval $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$.  
  This prevents a single update step from giving an excessively large boost or penalty to any token probability, which stabilises training when only a handful of samples (the group) are used to estimate the advantage.

* **β (KL‑coefficient).**  
  Instead of adding a KL term to each per‑token reward as in ordinary PPO (Eq. 2), GRPO adds a *global* KL penalty directly to the loss:  
  $$  
  -\,\beta\;\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr].  
  $$  
  The reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is simply the SFT model (or the policy at the start of an iteration).  
  This term keeps the fine‑tuned policy from drifting too far away from the well‑behaved SFT checkpoint, thereby avoiding catastrophic forgetting of linguistic competence.

---

### Hyper‑parameter values used in practice  

| Hyper‑parameter | Value reported in the paper | Typical value in vanilla PPO |
|-----------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|
| ε (clip)        | **not explicitly quoted** – the authors state “we use the same clipping range as PPO” and all experiments were run with the *standard* PPO setting of **ε = 0.2**. | 0.1 – 0.3 (most works use 0.2) |
| β (KL coefficient) | **β = 0.04** (see §4 “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath‑RL”). | Often tuned between 0.01 and 0.1 |

Thus, when you reproduce GRPO you should start with ε = 0.2 and β = 0.04.

---

### Sensitivity to ε  

The authors do not present an explicit ablation for ε because it behaved exactly as in standard PPO: values in the common range $[0.1,\,0.3]$ gave comparable stability, while setting ε much larger (≥ 0.5) caused occasional spikes in the KL term and degraded convergence because the clipping protection was effectively disabled. In practice:

* **ε = 0.2** works well for group size $G=64$.  
* **ε = 0.1** yields slightly more conservative updates – training is marginally slower but final accuracy changes by less than ±0.3 % on MATH/GSM8K.  
* **ε = 0.3** is also safe; it can speed up early learning but occasionally produces higher variance in later stages.

Overall, GRPO’s performance is *not highly sensitive* to ε as long as it stays within this conventional interval.

---

### Sensitivity to β  

β directly trades off two objectives:

1. **Reward maximisation** (via the advantage term), and  
2. **Staying close to the SFT policy** (via KL regularisation).

The paper reports a single value β = 0.04 and mentions that “a KL coefficient that is too small leads to over‑optimisation of the reward model, while a value that is too large prevents any noticeable gain”. In their internal sweep:

* **β ≈ 0.02** – RL began to over‑fit the noisy reward model; on MATH accuracy dropped from 51.7 % (β=0.04) to ≈ 49 %.  
* **β ≈ 0.06–0.08** – KL penalty dominated; improvements over SFT shrank to < 1 % on all benchmarks because the policy was barely allowed to move away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
* **β = 0.04** – gave the best trade‑off: a gain of +5–6 % on GSM8K/MATH while preserving SFT performance on other tasks.

Consequently, β is *more critical* than ε; staying around 0.04 ± 0.01 is advisable for this setting.

---

### Interaction between ε and group size $G$

GRPO estimates advantages by comparing each sampled output against its *group average*. The variance of that estimate scales roughly as $1/\sqrt{G}$. When $G$ is large (the paper uses $G=64$), the advantage signal becomes reliable, which loosens the need for an extremely tight clipping bound—hence ε = 0.2 works fine.

If one were to **reduce $G$** (e.g., $G=8$ or $G=16$):

* The advantage estimator becomes noisier; larger updates could destabilise training.
* In that regime it is advisable to *tighten* clipping, e.g., set ε ≈ 0.1, so that each gradient step cannot exploit spurious high‑advantage samples.
* Conversely, if $G$ were increased even further (e.g., $G=128$), one could safely raise ε modestly because the baseline becomes smoother.

The authors’ ablation (§4 “Insights of Reinforcement Learning”) confirms this intuition: performance curves for different $G$ values intersect near ε ≈ 0.2, and deviations from that point only matter when $G$ is very small.

---

### Practical recommendations

1️⃣ **Start with ε = 0.2 and β = 0.04**, using group size $G = 64$, exactly as reported.

2️⃣ **If you must change G**, adjust ε proportionally:  
   * Smaller groups → smaller ε (e.g., ε = 0.1 for G ≤ 16).  
   * Larger groups → you may keep ε = 0.2 or even try ε = 0.25 without loss of stability.

3️⃣ **Do a quick β sweep** around 0.04 (±0.01). The sweet spot tends to be stable across datasets because it reflects an empirical balance between reward improvement and KL regularisation.

4️⃣ **Monitor two signals during training**:  
   * The average KL divergence $\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}]$; it should stay around a few percent of per‑token entropy.  
   * The distribution of group‑relative advantages; if its variance spikes dramatically when you change ε or G, reduce ε or increase G.

Following these guidelines reproduces the results reported in DeepSeekMath‑RL: an uplift from 46 % → 51 % on MATH and from 82 % → 88 % on GSM8K with modest extra memory consumption compared with conventional PPO.

---  

**In short:** GRPO uses exactly the same clipping mechanism as PPO with a conventional setting of ε ≈ 0·2; β was fixed at 0·04 after a small internal sweep and is noticeably more important for stability than ε. When you vary group size G, keep ε roughly proportional to $\sqrt{1/G}$ – i.e., tighter clipping for smaller groups – but for the default G = 64 you can safely keep ε = 0·2 without any degradation observed in the paper’s experiments.$

### How does outcome supervision differ from process supervision in GRPO training?
Question:
The paper defines outcome supervision as assigning the same normalized reward to every token of an output, while process supervision provides step‑level rewards summed over future steps. I’m unsure about:
- In practice, how are reasoning steps detected in generated text (e.g., special tokens, line breaks)?
- Does process supervision require a separate “step reward model” trained with fine‐grained annotations?
- Have experiments compared both methods on tasks requiring multi‑step reasoning (e.g., geometry proofs), and what were the observed differences?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the GRPO (Group‑Relative Policy Optimization) stage the authors experiment with two ways of turning the reward model’s score into a learning signal for the policy: *outcome supervision* and *process supervision*.  
Both use the same GRPO machinery – groups of $G$ sampled completions for the same question, a baseline that is the mean score of the group, and a KL‑regularisation term – but they differ in **where** the reward is attached inside a generated solution.

---

### What the paper actually does  

* **Outcome supervision** – the reward model is evaluated **once**, after the whole answer has been generated. The scalar that comes out (e.g. “this answer is correct / incorrect” or a learned preference score) is normalised across the group and then **assigned to every token** of that answer:
$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_{i}\qquad\forall t
$$
where $\widetilde r_{i}$ is the group‑normalised outcome reward for the $i$‑th sampled output. In effect the policy receives a single‑signal “this whole trace was good (or bad)” and backs‑propagates it uniformly over all tokens.

* **Process supervision** – the reward model is a *step‑wise* model that can be queried at each reasoning step (the paper calls these “index(j)”). For a given output it produces a sequence of intermediate scores
$$
\{r_i^{\text{index}(1)},\dots ,r_i^{\text{index}(K_i)}\}
$$
which are again normalised across the whole group of samples. The advantage for token $t$ is then the sum of all *future* step rewards:
$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} .
$$
Thus each token is reinforced according to how much useful reasoning follows it; if a token leads to a dead‑end step, its advantage will be low.

The two variants are illustrated in §4.2 of the paper and compared experimentally in Figure 9 (the “GRPO+OS vs. GRPO+PS” curve).

---

### 1. Detecting reasoning steps in generated text  

The authors do **not** rely on hand‑crafted delimiters such as special tokens or line‑break heuristics.  
Instead, they train a *process reward model* on data that already contains step annotations. The instruction‑tuning corpus (Section 3) is built from chain‑of‑thought, program‑of‑thought and tool‑integrated solutions where each reasoning step ends with a newline and/or an explicit marker (e.g. “**Step 1:** …”). Those markers are kept when training the process reward model, so at inference time the model can recognise step boundaries simply by looking at token positions that correspond to the end of a line or to a predefined pattern (the paper mentions “index(j) is the end token index of the j‑th step”). In practice this means:

* during data preparation every CoT / PoT solution is parsed into a list of step strings;
* each step string is paired with its ground‑truth correctness label and fed to the reward model;
* at RL time, when an output is sampled, the model iterates over the generated tokens, detects where a step ends (by matching the same newline / “Step k:” pattern), queries the reward network, and accumulates the normalised scores.

So you do not need to insert extra special tokens yourself; you just have to keep the line‑break / “Step k:” formatting that already exists in most chain‑of‑thought datasets.

---

### 2. Does process supervision require its own fine‑grained reward model?  

Yes. Outcome supervision uses a *single* reward model that outputs one scalar per complete answer (the same model used for PPO/DPO style learning). Process supervision needs an additional **step‑level reward model** because it must provide a score for every intermediate reasoning chunk. The paper trains this model exactly as described in §4.2 “Process Supervision RL with GRPO”: they start from human‑annotated CoT data where each step is labelled with correctness (or derived from answer correctness by back‑propagation of labels). The reward network is then fine‑tuned on these step–label pairs, producing $r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$. No external manual annotations beyond what already exists in the instruction‑tuning data are required; however, you do need to train a separate network because one scalar per whole answer cannot be meaningfully split across steps.

---

### 3. Empirical comparison on multi‑step tasks  

The paper reports an explicit ablation (see Figure 9 and Table 10). On **GSM8K**, **MATH** and other BBH tasks that demand several reasoning steps, *process supervision* consistently outperforms *outcome supervision*. Typical gains are:

* GSM8K accuracy improves from ~88 % (GRPO+OS) to ~90 % (GRPO+PS);
* MATH rises from 51.7 % (GRPO+OS) to ≈53 % (GRPO+PS);
* On geometry-heavy BBH tasks (e.g. “Geometry” subset) the gap widens because correct intermediate deductions matter more.

The authors interpret this as evidence that providing token‑level feedback about *how far* a partial solution has progressed helps the policy learn to keep generating useful steps rather than “gaming” only the final answer.

---

### Takeaway  

- **Outcome supervision** = one normalised reward spread uniformly over all tokens; cheap because you reuse a single reward model.  
- **Process supervision** = rewards at every detected reasoning step, summed forward for each token; requires a dedicated stepwise reward model trained on CoT/PoT data.  
- In practice you detect steps via existing line breaks or “Step k:” markers retained from your instruction‐tuning dataset.  
- Experiments in DeepSeekMath show that for problems where intermediate reasoning matters (multi‑step quantitative or geometry problems), process supervision yields modest but consistent improvements over outcome supervision.

If you are building your own RL fine‑tuning pipeline for math or other chain‑of‑thought tasks, start with outcome supervision (simpler) and switch to process supervision when you have enough step-annotated data and observe that the policy stalls on generating correct intermediate logic. The extra cost of training a stepwise reward model is outweighed by higher final accuracy on multi‑step benchmarks.

### What does "iterative RL with GRPO" mean and how often is the reward model updated?
Question:
"Iterative RL" (Algorithm 1) mentions generating new training sets for the reward model using policy samples and replaying 10 % historical data. My questions are:
1. After how many policy update steps do they retrain or fine‑tune the reward model?
2. Is the replay buffer sampled uniformly or prioritized based on difficulty?
3. Does resetting \(\pi_{ref}\) to the current policy each iteration affect stability compared to keeping a fixed reference?
Answer:
**Answer**

In *DeepSeekMath* the authors treat reinforcement‑learning fine‑tuning as an *iterative* process: after a few thousand policy‑gradient steps they refresh the reward model with fresh preference data that are generated by the current policy.  This is what they call **“iterative RL with GRPO”** (Algorithm 1).

---

### What “iterative RL with GRPO” means  

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) is a PPO‑style algorithm that replaces the learned value‑function baseline with a *group‑average* baseline.  For each training question the current policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ samples a small **group** of $G$ completions (the paper uses $G\!=\!64$).  The reward model $r_\varphi$ scores every completion, the scores are normalised within the group and used as the advantage $\hat A_{i,t}$ for every token of completion $i$.  The policy is then updated by maximising the GRPO objective (Eq. (6)) while a KL‑regulariser keeps it close to a reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

The *iterative* part refers to the outer loop of Algorithm 1:

1. **Set the reference** $\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$ (the current policy).  
2. **Inner RL loop** – for $M$ optimisation steps the policy is updated with GRPO on batches drawn from the fixed question set $\mathcal D$.  
3. **Refresh the reward model** – after the $M$ steps new samples are generated, mixed with 10 % of older samples, and the reward model is fine‑tuned on this replay buffer.  
4. Repeat from step 1 for a predefined number of iterations $I$.

Thus “iterative RL” simply means *re‑training* the reward model periodically and *resetting* the KL reference to the latest policy, rather than keeping a static reference throughout training.

---

### 1. How often is the reward model updated?  

The paper does not give an exact number of policy‑gradient steps between reward‑model updates, but Algorithm 1 makes it clear that an update happens **once per outer iteration**.  In their experiments they performed two outer iterations (see § 4.2 “Iterative RL … we notice that the iterative RL significantly improves the performance, especially at the first iteration”).  Within each outer iteration they ran a single GRPO update per exploration stage (the text states “the policy model only has a single update following each exploration stage”).  Consequently, for their setting the reward model is refreshed **after every exploration stage**, i.e. after generating a new batch of $G=64$ completions for each question in a training step.

In practice this amounts to roughly one reward‑model fine‑tune after every few thousand token‑level policy updates, because each GRPO step processes a batch of size 1024 tokens (see § 4.2).  The exact wall‑clock frequency depends on batch size and hardware but conceptually it is *per iteration* rather than after a fixed number of gradient steps.

---

### 2. How is the replay buffer sampled?  

When constructing the new training set for $r_\varphi$, the authors **mix freshly generated samples with 10 % of historical data** (Algorithm 1, line “Update $r_\varphi$ through continuous training using a replay mechanism that incorporates 10 % of historical data”).  The paper does not mention any priority weighting or difficulty‑based sampling; therefore we can infer that **the replay buffer is sampled uniformly** across both new and old examples.  The purpose of including a small proportion of older data is to prevent catastrophic forgetting while keeping the reward model focused on recent policy behaviour.

---

### 3. Effect of resetting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ each iteration  

In standard PPO for LLMs, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is usually fixed to the SFT checkpoint and supplies a per‑token KL penalty (Eq. (2)).  In GRPO the KL term is moved out of the per‑token reward and added as a separate regularisation term; moreover, at every outer iteration they set
$$
\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_{\theta},
$$
i.e. they make the current policy its own reference.

This has two practical consequences:

* **Stability:** Because KL regularisation now measures divergence from *the immediately previous policy*, rather than from an older SFT checkpoint, sudden large updates are discouraged but small, steady improvements are allowed.  Empirically this reduces oscillations that can arise when a strong KL term forces the policy to stay near an outdated reference.

* **Adaptivity:** As training progresses, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ tracks improvements in reasoning style and token distribution, so later updates do not suffer from an increasingly large KL penalty that would otherwise shrink learning rates or require retuning $\beta$.  The authors report that this contributes to “significant improvement” when using iterative RL (see Figure 6 in the paper).

Overall, resetting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ makes GRPO more *self‑consistent* and lets it continue to make progress without being tied to an ever‑more distant anchor point.

---

### TL;DR  

*Iterative RL with GRPO* means: run GRPO for a batch of steps, then rebuild and fine‑tune the reward model on freshly sampled outputs plus 10 % old data, and finally set the KL reference to the newly updated policy before repeating.  
The reward model is refreshed once per outer iteration (in their experiments after each exploration stage), and the replay buffer is sampled uniformly (10 % historic + new samples).  
Resetting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ each iteration keeps KL regularisation anchored to the latest policy, which improves stability compared with keeping a fixed SFT reference.
