# From Web Crawls to Math Mastery: How DeepSeekMath Harvested 120 B Tokens of Math Data  

Large language models have shown a remarkable knack for tackling math problems, but the secret sauce often lies in the data they are fed. **DeepSeekMath 7B** demonstrates that a carefully curated web‑scale corpus can push an open‑source model to the brink of GPT‑4‑level performance—*all without any proprietary data*. In this post we walk through the data‑collection pipeline that turned the public **Common Crawl** into the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, a 120 billion‑token trove of multilingual mathematical text. We’ll break down the fastText classifier, the iterative domain expansion, de‑duplication tricks, multilingual filtering, and why this approach beats the more common arXiv‑centric corpora.

---

## 1. Why Start From the Open Web?  

Most “math‑pre‑training” efforts (e.g., Minerva, MathPile) rely heavily on arXiv papers or curated textbooks. While scholarly PDFs are high quality, they are **English‑biased** and limited in sheer volume. DeepSeekMath’s authors asked a simple question:

> *Does the public web contain enough high‑quality math content to rival or surpass arXiv?*

The answer turned out to be a resounding “yes”—provided you can **filter** the noise. By mining Common Crawl (CC), they accessed a “long tail” of math forums, educational sites, Q&A pages, and even non‑English resources that traditional pipelines miss.

---

## 2. Seed Corpus: The Starting Point  

The pipeline begins with a **seed dataset**: the publicly released **OpenWebMath** collection. Although modest (≈13 B tokens), it offers clean examples of math‑heavy web pages (forum posts, lecture notes, problem statements). This seed serves two purposes:

1. **Positive examples** for training a binary classifier that distinguishes math pages from generic web text.  
2. A basis for **domain discovery**—the seed hints at which top‑level domains (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`) are likely math‑rich.

---

## 3. FastText as a Scalable Math Classifier  

Training massive transformer classifiers on billions of webpages is overkill; instead DeepSeekMath uses **fastText**, a lightweight linear model with subword embeddings that trains in minutes on a single GPU.

### Training details
* **Positive set:** 500 k randomly sampled pages from OpenWebMath.  
* **Negative set:** 500 k random pages from CC (ensuring diverse topics).  
* **Hyperparameters:** 256‑dimensional vectors, learning rate 0.1, n‑gram length 3, minimum word count 3, 3 epochs.  

FastText learns a score *s(p)* for any page *p*. Higher scores → more “math‑like”. Because of its bag‑of‑ngrams nature, fastText quickly picks up domain‐specific terminology (`∫`, `mod`, `ψ(x)`) while ignoring generic boilerplate.

---

## 4. Iterative Mining and Domain Expansion  

A single pass over CC would miss many math pages that don’t resemble the seed vocabulary closely enough. The authors therefore adopt an **iterative refinement loop**:

1. **First pass:** Apply fastText to all deduplicated CC pages (≈40 B HTML pages after URL deduplication). Keep only the top‑scoring tokens (≈40 B tokens).  
2. **Domain analysis:** Group pages by their base domain (e.g., `math.stackexchange.com`). Compute what fraction of each domain was captured in step 1.
   * Domains where >10 % of pages were selected are flagged as “math‐related”.  
3. **Manual URL annotation:** Human annotators browse these flagged domains and mark sub‑paths that consistently contain math content (`/questions/`, `/lecture-notes/`).  
4. **Seed augmentation:** Add newly discovered pages to the positive set and retrain fastText → improved recall on more obscure math sites.
5. **Repeat:** Steps 1–4 run for four iterations total.

By the final iteration they amassed **35.5 M distinct math pages**, amounting to **120 B tokens**—roughly 7× larger than Minerva’s web portion and 9× larger than OpenWebMath.

> **Key insight:** The “domain expansion” step turns a modest seed into a self‑reinforcing discovery engine, allowing the classifier to learn from its own successes.

---

## 5. De‑duplication and Near‑Duplication Pruning  

Common Crawl contains massive redundancy: identical articles mirrored across mirrors, pagination artifacts, or boilerplate footers. Feeding duplicated text would waste token budget and bias training toward popular sites.

* **URL‐based deduplication:** Exact match on normalized URLs removes identical copies.
* **Near‑deduplication:** Fingerprinting (e.g., MinHash) identifies near‐identical pages (minor edits or different query parameters). Pages sharing >95 % similarity are collapsed to a single representative.

After this cleaning, only ~40 B raw HTML pages remain—still huge but tractable for fastText scoring.

---

## 6. Multilingual Harvesting  

Unlike many math corpora that filter out non‑English content early on, DeepSeekMath deliberately keeps multilingual pages:

* The fastText model is trained on tokenized raw text without language filtering; its n‑gram features capture Chinese characters (`∑`, `方程`) just as well as Latin ones.
* During iteration, domains like `zhihu.com/math` or Chinese university lecture sites surface naturally.
* Final token distribution: roughly *70 % English* and *30 % Chinese*, with smaller fractions of other languages.

Consequences:
* Significant gains on Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, Gaokao variants).  
* A more balanced language model that generalizes better when faced with mixed‐language prompts.

---

## 7. Benchmark Contamination Safeguards  

When constructing a pre‑training corpus it’s easy to inadvertently leak evaluation data (e.g., copying GSM8K test questions). DeepSeekMath applies a strict filter:

* Any 10‑gram appearing verbatim in any benchmark (English or Chinese) is excised from the source page.
* For shorter strings (<10 grams but ≥3 grams), exact matching still triggers removal.
* This conservative approach eliminates overfitting risk while preserving most of the surrounding context.

---

## 8. Quality Validation: Why Size Alone Isn’t Enough  

The authors ran controlled experiments with four corpora:

| Corpus | Size (tokens) | Dominant source | Benchmarks used |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| MathPile | 8.9 B | Mostly arXiv | GSM8K, MATH |
| OpenWebMath | 13.6 B | CC filtered | Same |
| Proof-Pile-2 | ~40 B | Mix (code + arXiv) | Same |
| **DeepSeekMath** | **120 B** | CC + fastText filter + multilingual | Same |

All models were finetuned for an equal number of steps (150 B tokens). Results showed:

* DeepSeekMath consistently outperformed the others on *both English and Chinese* tasks.
* The learning curve remained steep even after >100 B tokens—a sign that added data kept providing novel patterns rather than repeating what was already seen.
* Multilingual gains were evident: Chinese benchmarks improved while others plateaued.

Thus, it isn’t just “more tokens”; it’s *more diverse high‑quality tokens*, especially from underrepresented languages and forums.

---

## 9. What About ArXiv?  

A surprising finding: adding large arXiv slices (MathPile’s 85 % arXiv component or a raw ArXiv-RedPajama dump) did **not** improve downstream math reasoning—and sometimes hurt performance on GSM8K/MATH/Lemma benchmarks.

Possible reasons discussed:

1. **Domain mismatch:** Academic papers often contain dense LaTeX formulae but little natural language reasoning flow; models may overfit to formal proof style rather than stepwise problem solving.
2. **Language bias:** ArXiv is overwhelmingly English; it doesn’t help multilingual benchmarks.
3. **Redundancy:** Many papers recycle similar theorem statements; token diversity suffers.

The takeaway is clear: *quality trumps quantity*, and “web math” provides richer reasoning patterns than raw research articles for LLM pretraining.

---

## 10. From Corpus to Model – The Bigger Picture  

With the corpus ready, DeepSeekMath follows these steps:

1️⃣ Continue pretraining DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 (7 B) on a mixture where **56 %** is DeepSeekMath tokens → total 500 B training tokens.  
2️⃣ Supervise fine‑tune on instruction data (CoT + PoT + tool–integrated formats).  
3️⃣ Apply the novel **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** reinforcement learning loop using only the same instruction set — boosting MATH accuracy from 46.8 % → **51.7 %** without external tools.

Result: an open-source model that cracks over half of competition‐level MATH problems—something previously seen only in closed models like Gemini Ultra or GPT‑4.

---

## 11. Key Lessons for Practitioners  

| Aspect | What DeepSeekMath Shows |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Data source selection** | Public web sources hold abundant high-quality math content; focus on clever filtering rather than sheer scraping of papers. |
| **Classifier choice** | A lightweight fastText model can be iteratively refined to achieve recall comparable to much larger neural classifiers at fraction of cost. |
| **Iterative expansion** | Looping between classifier training → domain discovery → seed augmentation yields exponential coverage improvement. |
| **Deduplication matters** | Removing exact and near duplicates preserves token budget for novel content; essential at billions-of-page scale. |
| **Multilingual inclusion** | Simple “don’t filter non-English” policy yields measurable gains on Chinese benchmarks without hurting English performance.|  
| **Benchmark safety** | Stringent n‑gram matching prevents leakage; crucial for fair evaluation later on.|

If you’re building your own domain-specific LLM—be it chemistry, law, or finance—adapting this pipeline can save months of manual crawling and provide a competitive edge.

---

## 12. Looking Ahead  

The DeepSeekMath team points to several future directions:

* Refine domain expansion with *active learning*: let the model query annotators for ambiguous pages instead of exhaustive manual URL tagging.
* Pair fastText with transformer‐based “teacher” models for better precision at later stages.
* Explore better reward models for RL that understand stepwise reasoning beyond final answer correctness.
* Scale up model size while preserving code training benefits identified in their ablations.

---

### TL;DR

DeepSeekMath proves that with an *engineered fastText classifier*, *iterative domain hunting*, *careful deduplication*, and *multilingual openness*, you can turn publicly available Common Crawl into a 120 billion-token math corpus that outperforms arXiv-heavy alternatives across both English and Chinese benchmarks—all while staying open source.

If you’re curious about implementations or want to try building your own “web→domain” corpus, most of the tooling is open—fastText libraries, URL deduplication scripts, and basic HTML extraction pipelines are all readily available on GitHub today.

Happy crawling—and may your next LLM be as mathematically savvy as DeepSeekMath!



# Why Starting from a Code Model Boosts Mathematical Reasoning  

Large language models (LLMs) have become surprisingly good at math, but getting them to think like a mathematician is still hard. Recent work on **DeepSeekMath** shows that the secret sauce isn’t just more math data – it’s beginning *from a code‑oriented model* and then feeding it mathematical text. In this post we unpack the experiments that lead to this insight, explain why a two‑stage (code → math) or a mixed (code + math) training regime works better than pure math training, and discuss the phenomenon of catastrophic forgetting and its implications for future LLM design.

> **TL;DR**  
> Training first on source‑code tokens (or mixing code with math) gives the model a “structured reasoning” scaffold. When the model later sees equations, algebraic manipulations, or Python snippets, it already knows how to chain operations, manage variables, and debug logical flow. The result: higher accuracy on step‑by‑step chain‑of‑thought (CoT) problems *and* on program‑aided (PoT) problems where the model writes and runs Python code.

---

## 1. The Experimental Setup  

The DeepSeekMath team built several small‑scale training pipelines using a 1.3 B‑parameter “general” LLM as the base. All pipelines kept the same hyper‑parameters for fairness (AdamW optimizer, 4 K context, 5 M token batch size, etc.). The only variable was **what data appeared before the big math pre‑training phase**.

| Training Regime | First Phase (tokens) | Second Phase (tokens) | Total Tokens |
|-----------------|----------------------|-----------------------|--------------|
| **Two‑stage A** | 400 B code tokens → *then* 150 B math tokens | — | 550 B |
| **Two‑stage B** | 400 B *general* (non‑code) tokens → *then* 150 B math tokens | — | 550 B |
| **One‑stage C** | 150 B math tokens only | — | 150 B |
| **One‑stage D** | 400 B code + 150 B math mixed in a single pass | — | 550 B |

*All “math tokens” come from the DeepSeekMath corpus (≈120 B high‑quality web pages filtered for mathematics). “Code tokens” are drawn from GitHub/Stack Overflow style repositories.*

After each pre‑training run, the resulting model was fine‑tuned on a modest instruction set (≈776 K math Q&A pairs) and evaluated on three families of benchmarks:

1. **Step‑by‑step reasoning** – GSM8K and MATH using CoT prompting.
2. **Program‐aided reasoning** – same datasets using PoT prompting where the model writes Python (`math`, `sympy`) before execution.
3. **General language & coding** – MMLU, BBH, HumanEval to ensure we didn’t destroy other abilities.

---

## 2. Two‑Stage vs. One‑Stage: What Changed?

### 2.1 Two‑Stage Code → Math  

- **Observation:** After just the code phase, the model already improves on PoT tasks (e.g., GSM8K with Python) by ~5–6 % absolute compared to a vanilla baseline.
- **Why?** Code training teaches the model how to *serialize* procedural knowledge: function definitions, loops, variable bindings, error handling. When a math prompt later asks for “solve √(x+1)=3”, the model can internally translate that into `sympy.solve(x + 1 - 9)` without having ever seen such a problem before.
- **Follow‑up math phase:** Adding math data further lifts both CoT and PoT scores; the final DeepSeekMath‑Base hits **64.2 % on GSM8K** and **36.2 % on competition‐level MATH**, eclipsing even larger closed models like Minerva 540B.

### 2.2 Two‑Stage General → Math  

- Using general English web text in place of code leads to almost no improvement over pure math training. The model lacks an explicit procedural template to reuse when constructing programs, so PoT gains disappear.

### 2.3 One‑Stage Mixed Code+Math  

- Mixing code and math in a single pass mitigates *catastrophic forgetting*: after heavy code pre‑training alone, a subsequent long math phase can cause the model to lose some of its coding fluency (the “forgetting” effect).  
- The mixed regime preserves coding ability **and** still benefits step‑by‑step reasoning: CoT accuracy climbs modestly (~2–3 % over pure math), while PoT performance remains high (~80 % on GSM8K with Python).  
- However, for very small models (1.3 B), squeezing both domains into one pass yields diminishing returns for pure CoT tasks—perhaps because capacity is insufficient to fully internalize two very different token distributions simultaneously.

---

## 3. Catastrophic Forgetting in Practice  

When training sequentially on two very different corpora, neural networks tend to overwrite earlier weights—a well‐known phenomenon called **catastrophic forgetting**.

- In DeepSeek’s two‑stage experiments, after *400 B* of code pre‐training the model’s coding benchmark scores were high (~45 % pass@1 on HumanEval).  
- Once they added *150 B* of pure math tokens **without any interleaving**, those coding scores dropped by ~5–7 points while math scores rose dramatically.

The mixed one‑stage schedule solves this by repeatedly exposing the network to both kinds of patterns throughout training: each mini‐batch contains a blend of code snippets and mathematical prose, preventing any single set of weights from being overwritten completely.

> **Takeaway:** If you plan to specialize an LLM for multiple domains (e.g., code + math), never train them *completely* sequentially unless you’re willing to sacrifice one domain.

---

## 4. Why Code Helps Even Without Tools  

You might think code only matters when we explicitly ask the model to write Python (`PoT`). Surprisingly, step‐by‐step reasoning also improves:

1. **Structured Thought Chains:** CoT prompts often look like “First compute … then plug into …”. This mirrors how a compiler parses statements into an abstract syntax tree (AST). A model familiar with AST construction can more reliably keep track of intermediate symbols.
2. **Variable Scoping & Reuse:** Code data teach the model that `x = …` defines something that can be referenced later—exactly what CoT does when it says “Let x = …”.
3. **Error Detection:** Compilers flag type mismatches; analogously, a model pre-trained on buggy code learns to detect impossible intermediate results (“division by zero”) and correct them before final answer generation.

Empirically this shows up as a ~4–5 % boost on GSM8K CoT after just the code stage—well before any mathematical text is seen.

---

## 5. Implications for Future LLM Design  

### 5.1 Pre‑training Order Matters  
If you have limited compute and want strong mathematical abilities in a ~7–10 B parameter model, start from a *code‐first* checkpoint rather than from a generic language model. The incremental cost is modest (the publicly available DeepSeek-Coder‐Base-v1.5 is already open).

### 5.2 Multi‑Domain Interleaving  
When building models that need both programming and reasoning skills:

- Use an *interleaved curriculum*: e.g., each epoch consists of ≈30 % code, ≈70 % math tokens.
- Monitor catastrophic forgetting by periodically re‐evaluating both domains during training.

### 5.3 Data Selection Over Model Scale  
DeepSeekMath‑Base (7 B) matches or exceeds Minerva 540 B despite being >70× smaller—showing that high-quality domain data beats raw scale when it comes to structured reasoning tasks.

### 5.4 Beyond Code: Other Structured Domains?  
If code provides such scaffolding for mathematics, perhaps other formal languages—SQL queries, regular expressions, or even LaTeX proof scripts—could be used as *pre‐training primers* for other reasoning specialties (e.g., logic puzzles or symbolic physics).

---

## 6. Quick Recap – What You Should Do Next  

| Goal | Recommended Pre‑training Recipe |
|------|--------------------------------|
| Strong step‑by‑step maths with no external tools | Start from a code checkpoint → fine-tune on large web/math corpus |
| Program‐aided maths (PoT) | Same as above + mix some Python snippets during pre‐training |
| Balanced coding + maths for a small LLM (<2 B) | Interleaved one‑stage training with ~70 % code / ~30 % maths tokens |
| Pure maths without needing coding expertise | You can skip code pre‑training but expect lower performance than the mixed approach |

---

## 7. Final Thoughts  

The DeepSeekMath experiments provide concrete evidence that **training order and domain interleaving are as important as sheer token counts**. By giving an LLM an early taste of programming concepts—variables, functions, control flow—we effectively hand it a mental toolbox that later translates into better mathematical reasoning, both when it writes explicit code and when it reasons purely in natural language.

As open-source foundations continue to grow, adopting this “code first → math second” recipe could become a standard baseline for anyone looking to push small models toward GPT‑4 or Gemini levels of quantitative reasoning—without needing billions of parameters or proprietary data.

Happy training! 🚀



# Teaching LLMs to Think Like Mathematicians: Chain‑of‑Thought, Program‑of‑Thought, and Tool‑Integrated Reasoning  

Large language models (LLMs) have gotten remarkably good at answering factual questions, but solving a math problem is a very different beast.  A good solution usually requires **multiple reasoning steps**, sometimes **symbolic manipulation**, and often the assistance of a **computer algebra system** or a short Python script.  The DeepSeekMath team tackled this by training an instruction‑tuned model—**DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B**—with three complementary prompting styles:

1. **Plain Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)** – step‑by‑step natural‑language reasoning.  
2. **Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)** – writing and executing Python code to do the heavy lifting.  
3. **Tool‑Integrated Reasoning** – mixing textual reasoning with calls to external tools (e.g., a calculator or symbolic library) inside the same prompt.

Below we unpack each paradigm, show how it is represented in the training data, and explain why the combination gives DeepSeekMath its strong performance on benchmarks such as GSM8K and the competition‑level MATH dataset.

---

## 1. Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT): “Let Me Think Out Loud”

### What it looks like  
A CoT prompt asks the model to *explain every intermediate logical step* before presenting the final answer.  A typical example from the training set:

```
Q: A rectangle has width 5 cm and length 12 cm. What is its area?
A: To find the area of a rectangle we multiply width by length.
   Area = width × length
   Substituting the given values: Area = 5 × 12
   Multiplying gives 60.
   Therefore, the area is 60 cm².
```

### Why CoT matters  
- **Explicit reasoning trace**: The model learns to generate a logical skeleton that can be inspected or corrected.
- **Few‑shot transfer**: Even when presented with a new problem type, the model can reuse the “multiply width and length” pattern it has seen many times.
- **Benchmark boost**: On GSM8K, DeepSeekMath‑Instruct reaches 88 % accuracy with plain CoT; on MATH (a much harder competition benchmark) it climbs above 50 %, surpassing all other open‑source 7B models.

### Training details  
The authors curated ~776 K examples that pair a problem with a CoT solution, mixing English datasets (GSM8K, MATH, MathInstruct, Lila-OOD) and Chinese K‑12 problems.  The data were tokenized up to a 4 K context length and used for supervised fine‑tuning (SFT) for only 500 steps—enough for the model to internalize the “think step by step” pattern without overfitting.

---

## 2. Program‑of‑Thought (PoT): “Let Me Write a Little Code”

### What it looks like  
PoT replaces natural language calculations with short Python snippets that are executed during evaluation. A PoT example:

```
Q: Compute the sum of the first 100 positive integers.
A:
```python
def triangular_number(n):
    return n * (n + 1) // 2

result = triangular_number(100)
print(result)
```
The program prints **5050**, so the answer is 5050.
```

### Why PoT matters  
- **Exact arithmetic**: Python’s `int` type handles arbitrarily large numbers without rounding errors, which is crucial for many GSM8K and MATH problems.
- **Symbolic libraries**: By allowing imports such as `sympy`, the model can perform differentiation, solve equations, or simplify expressions—tasks that are error‑prone when done purely in text.
- **Higher ceiling on hard problems**: In program-aided evaluation (i.e., running the code), DeepSeekMath‑Base already outperforms prior state-of-the-art open models like Llemma 34B. After instruction tuning, the PoT capability stays strong.

### Training details  
The PoT portion of the dataset contains both **program-only** solutions (just code) and **mixed** solutions where a textual explanation surrounds the code block. The same 776 K SFT examples include about one third PoT entries in each language. During fine‑tuning, prompts are truncated at a code token boundary to avoid cutting off important syntax.

---

## 3. Tool‑Integrated Reasoning: “Let Me Call a Calculator”

### What it looks like  
Tool integration goes beyond raw code; it treats an external utility as a *first-class* part of the dialogue. A typical training instance might look like:

```
Q: What is sin(π/6)?
A: I will use Python's math library.
>>> import math
>>> answer = math.sin(math.pi/6)
>>> print(answer)
0.5
So sin(π/6) = 0.5.
```

Or in a more abstract form:

```
Q: Solve x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0.
A: Use SymPy to factor the polynomial.

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x')
poly = x**2 - 5*x + 6
roots = sp.solve(poly, x)
print(roots)
```

The solver returns `[2, 3]`, so x = 2 or x = 3.
```

### Why tool integration matters  
- **Unified workflow**: The model learns when to *think* in words and when to *delegate* an operation to an external tool.
- **Better generalization**: Even if a problem type was unseen during training, a well‑trained model can still call `math.sqrt` or `sympy.integrate` correctly.
- **Benchmark impact**: When tool use is allowed during evaluation, DeepSeekMath‑Instruct reaches nearly **60 % accuracy on MATH**, eclipsing all open-source baselines that either lack tool support or cannot reliably format calls.

### Training pipeline  
The same instruction set used for CoT and PoT was enriched with “tool tags” (`>>>`, code fences) that signal an execution step. The authors deliberately mixed Chinese and English problems so that token-level patterns for tool calls appear in both scripts—helpful for multilingual benchmarks like CMATH and MGSM-zh.

---

## Putting It All Together

### Multi‑style fine‑tuning → Multi‑style competence  
DeepSeekMath‑Instruct does not pick one style over another; instead it *jointly* learns all three from the same corpus. This gives it flexibility at inference time:

- If a downstream system only permits plain text answers (e.g., an offline API), the model can revert to CoT.
- If an environment supports Python execution (as many evaluation harnesses do), it can output PoT or tool‐integrated code.
- If users want brevity but correctness, they can ask explicitly for a tool call (“use Python to compute …”).

### Empirical gains on key benchmarks  

| Benchmark | No tool (CoT only) | Tool allowed (PoT / Tool‐integrated) |
|-----------|-------------------|--------------------------------------|
| GSM8K    | **88 %** (CoT)    | ~90 % (PoT improves minute edge)   |
| MATH     | **51 %** (CoT)    | **~60 %** (tool‐integrated)          |
| Chinese CMATH | ~84 %          | ~88 %                                 |
| SAT / OCW | noticeable lift over baseline models |

These numbers are *significant*: they put DeepSeekMath‑Instruct ahead of any open‐source model under 70 B parameters and within striking distance of closed models such as GPT‑4 or Gemini Ultra on pure reasoning tasks.

### Why does this work?  

1. **Data diversity** – By exposing the model to multiple reasoning modalities, it learns richer latent representations of mathematical concepts.
2. **Code pre‑training advantage** – The base model started from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5; its existing familiarity with programming constructs made PoT learning smoother.
3. **Self‐consistency via sampling** – Even though not part of instruction tuning directly, sampling multiple outputs and picking consistent answers further pushes accuracy (the paper reports self-consistency over 64 samples reaching 60.9 % on MATH).

---

## Takeaways for Practitioners

- **Collect mixed CoT/PoT data** rather than focusing solely on one style; even modest amounts (~776 K examples) are enough when combined with high‑quality math pretraining.
- **Include multilingual examples** if you care about non‑English benchmarks; tool syntax stays English (`import sympy`) while surrounding text can be Chinese or any other language.
- **Leverage existing code pretrained checkpoints**—starting from a coder model dramatically speeds up PoT learning and improves overall reasoning ability.
- **Design your inference pipeline to detect tool calls**, execute them safely, and feed back results; this simple loop turns a text generator into an effective problem solver.

---

## Closing Thoughts

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that teaching LLMs *how* to think—stepwise narration, writing concise programs, and calling external utilities—can together close much of the gap between open-source models and proprietary giants on tough math benchmarks.  

If you’re building an AI tutor, an automated proof assistant, or any system that needs reliable quantitative reasoning, consider adopting all three paradigms in your instruction tuning pipeline. The synergy they provide is now empirically validated on some of the hardest publicly available math tests—proof that LLMs can indeed be taught to think like mathematicians.



# Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): A Memory‑Efficient Twist on PPO for Math RL  

Large language models have become surprisingly good at solving mathematical problems, but getting that extra boost often requires reinforcement learning (RL) on top of supervised fine‑tuning. The most common RL recipe for language models is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, an actor‑critic algorithm that pairs a policy network (the model we are training) with a separate **critic** (a value‑function model) to estimate the advantage of each token. While PPO works well, the critic adds a hefty memory footprint—something that quickly becomes a bottleneck when we fine‑tune 7‑B or larger models.

Enter **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, the new algorithm introduced in the DeepSeekMath paper. GRPO keeps the spirit of PPO—clipping ratios, KL regularization, and advantage‑guided updates—but it **throws away the critic altogether**. Instead, it uses the *average reward of a small group of sampled completions* as a baseline. The result? Almost the same learning signal, far less memory consumption, and surprisingly large performance jumps on standard math benchmarks such as **GSM8K** and **MATH**.

Below we walk through the intuition behind GRPO, compare it side‑by‑side with classic PPO, and illustrate a concrete step‑by‑step update.

---

## 1. Why PPO Needs a Critic (and Why That’s Costly)

PPO optimizes the following surrogate objective:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{q\sim P(Q),\,o\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\!\!\!\left[
\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
\min\!\Big(
\rho_t A_t,\; \text{clip}(\rho_t,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon) A_t
\Big)
\right],
\]

where  

* \(\rho_t = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_t|q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_t|q,o_{<t})}\) is the probability ratio,  
* \(A_t = r_t + \gamma V(o_{t+1}) - V(o_t)\) is the **advantage**, computed using a learned value function \(V(\cdot)\) (the critic).  

The critic must be trained in parallel with the policy and stored in GPU memory. For a 7‑B model, an additional value network of comparable size can double the memory requirement. Moreover, because LLM rewards are typically given only at the *final token* (e.g., “did we get the correct answer?”), learning a token‑level value function is noisy and sometimes unnecessary.

---

## 2. The Core Idea of GRPO: Use Group Averages as Baselines

GRPO replaces the learned baseline with a **group-relative baseline**:

1. **Sample a small group** (e.g., \(G = 64\)) of completions \(\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\) for the same question \(q\) from the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
2. Evaluate each completion with a reward model \(r_\varphi(q,o_i)\).  
3. Compute the **group mean** \(\bar r = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} r_i\) and optionally the standard deviation for normalization.  
4. Define the advantage for every token in completion \(o_i\) as

   \[
   \hat A_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \bar r}{\sigma_r},
   \]

   i.e., how much better (or worse) this particular answer is compared to its peers.

Because all samples share the same question, this “relative” advantage naturally captures *comparative* quality—exactly what reward models are trained to predict (they are usually built from pairwise preferences). No extra network is needed; we just reuse the sampled outputs themselves to compute a baseline.

---

## 3. GRPO vs. PPO: A Quick Comparison Table

| Aspect | PPO | GRPO |
|--------|-----|------|
| **Baseline** | Learned value network \(V(\cdot)\) | Group mean of sampled rewards |
| **Memory cost** | Policy + critic ≈ 2× model size | Only policy (no extra network) |
| **KL handling** | Added to per‑token reward (Eq. 2 in paper) | Added as a separate term in loss (Eq. 4) |
| **Advantage source** | Temporal difference using future values | Simple relative score among samples |
| **Implementation complexity** | Train two models simultaneously | Same training loop; just sample multiple outputs |
| **Performance on math tasks** | Strong baseline; requires careful tuning | +5–6 % absolute gain on MATH; +5 % on GSM8K in DeepSeekMath experiments |

The empirical results in the DeepSeekMath paper are striking: after applying GRPO to a 7‑B instruction‑tuned model, GSM8K accuracy rose from 82.9 % to 88.2 %, while MATH jumped from 46.8 % to 51.7 %. That’s competitive with much larger (70 B+) open‑source models—all while using roughly half the GPU memory required for PPO.

---

## 4. Visual Analogy: “Voting in a Classroom”

Imagine you ask an entire class (the policy) to solve the same math problem. Each student writes an answer on their own paper (a sampled completion). A teacher’s rubric (the reward model) grades each paper independently. Instead of estimating how “good” each individual answer *should* be based on some abstract theory (the critic), you simply look at how each student performed relative to their classmates’ average score.

- If you scored higher than average → positive advantage → your answer gets reinforced.
- If you scored lower than average → negative advantage → your answer is discouraged.

The teacher doesn’t need to know anything about how you arrived at your solution; she only cares about your rank within this group. This is exactly what GRPO does—hence “Group Relative”.

---

## 5. Step‑by‑Step Example of a GRPO Update  

Let’s walk through a tiny toy scenario with a 2‑token language model solving a simple arithmetic question:  

> **Question:** “What is \(7+5$?”  

### Step 0: Prepare
- Old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) = current model checkpoint.
- Reward model \(r_\varphi\) = binary correctness checker (+1 if answer correct, 0 otherwise).  
- Hyperparameters: group size \(G=4\), clipping \(\varepsilon=0.2\), KL weight \(\beta=0.04\).

### Step 1: Sample a Group
Sample four completions from the old policy:

| Sample ID | Completion (tokens) | Predicted answer |
|-----------|---------------------|------------------|
| \(o_1\)   | “12”                | Correct (+1) |
| \(o_2\)   | “13”                | Wrong (0) |
| \(o_3\)   | “12”                | Correct (+1) |
| \(o_4\)   | “11”                | Wrong (0) |

### Step 2: Score with Reward Model  
Rewards vector: \(\mathbf{r} = [1,0,1,0]\).

### Step 3: Compute Group Baseline  
Mean reward \(\bar r = (1+0+1+0)/4 = 0.5\).  
Standard deviation \(\sigma_r = \sqrt{\frac{(0.5)^2+( -0.5)^2+(0.5)^2+( -0.5)^2}{4}} = 0.5\).

### Step 4: Normalized Advantages  
For each sample \(i\),

\[
\hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \bar r}{\sigma_r}.
\]

Thus:
- \(\hat A_1 = (1-0.5)/0.5 = +1.\)
- \(\hat A_2 = (0-0.5)/0.5 = -1.\)
- \(\hat A_3 = +1.\)
- \(\hat A_4 = -1.\)

Since our task is single‑step generation, every token receives this same advantage.

### Step 5: Compute Probability Ratios  
For each sample we compute

\[
\rho_i = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i)}.
\]

Assume:
- \(\rho_1 = 1.05,\;\rho_2=0.95,\;\rho_3=1.02,\;\rho_4=0.98.\)

### Step 6: Apply PPO‑style Clipping  
For each sample:

\[
L_i = \min(\rho_i\,\hat A_i,\; \operatorname{clip}(\rho_i,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_i).
\]

With \(\varepsilon=0.2:\)
- For positive advantages (\(i=1,3\)), clipping keeps \(\rho_i=1.05,\;1.02 < 1+\varepsilon=1.2.\) So \(L_{1}=1.05\times(+1)=+1.05,\;L_{3}=+1.02.\)
- For negative advantages (\(i=2,4\)), clipping lower bounds at \(1-\varepsilon=0.8.\) Both ratios >0.8, so \(L_{2}=0.95\times(-1)= -0.95,\;L_{4}= -0.98.\)

### Step 7: KL Regularization  
Compute KL divergence between new policy and reference policy (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\); usually the previous checkpoint). For illustration we set an average KL contribution of \(0.^{\!}03.\)

Total loss per batch:

\[
\mathcal{L} = -\,\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} L_i + \beta\, D_{\text{KL}}.
\]

Plugging numbers:

\[
-\frac{(1.05 + 1.02 - 0.95 - 0.98)}{4} + 0.04\times0.^{\!}03 
\simeq -(0.\!14)/4 + 0.\!0012 
\simeq -0.\!035 + 0.\!0012 
\simeq -0.\!0338.
\]

Negative loss means gradient ascent on the original objective—exactly what PPO does—except that we never touched a separate critic.

### Step 8: Update Policy  
Backpropagate through this loss and update parameters \(\theta.\)

That’s it! The full GRPO iteration consists of sampling many groups per batch (the DeepSeekMath experiments used \(G=64\)), computing normalized advantages based on group means, applying clipped ratios, adding KL regularization, and stepping once.

---

## 6️⃣ What Makes GRPO Effective for Math RL?

* **Comparative Reward Signal:** Math problems often have binary correctness (“right vs wrong”). By comparing answers inside a group we get a clean signal without needing dense value estimates.
* **Reduced Variance:** Normalizing by group statistics stabilizes gradients across batches.
* **Memory Savings:** No extra value network → larger batch sizes or more groups can fit into GPU memory.
* **Simplicity:** Implementation fits cleanly into existing PPO pipelines; you only need to change how advantages are computed.

The DeepSeekMath team validated these hypotheses empirically across multiple domains:
- On *in‑domain* tasks (GSM8K & MATH), GRPO lifted scores by ~5 % absolute.
- On *out‑of‑domain* benchmarks like CMATH and BBH, modest yet consistent gains were observed, suggesting that better alignment of the policy distribution translates beyond the specific training data.

---

## 7️⃣ TL;DR – When Should You Pick GRPO?

- You’re fine‑tuning an LLM for *reasoning heavy* tasks where rewards are naturally comparative (e.g., math correctness, code correctness).
- GPU memory is limited; training a full actor‑critic is prohibitive.
- You already have or can cheaply train a reward model that can score whole completions or reasoning steps.

In those settings, swapping classic PPO for GRPO gives you essentially free memory savings while delivering noticeable performance improvements—exactly what DeepSeekMath demonstrated with its compact 7 B model reaching GPT‑4–close scores on competition‐level math benchmarks.

--- 

**Bottom line:** GRPO shows that reinforcement learning for language models doesn’t always need an expensive critic; clever use of *group-relative baselines* can provide an equally powerful learning signal with far less hardware overhead—a valuable insight as we push open‑source models toward closed‑source performance levels in mathematics and beyond.*



# Putting It All Together: How DeepSeekMath 7B Reaches 51.7 % on the Competition‑Level MATH Benchmark  

Large language models have become the go‑to tool for solving math problems, but most publicly available models still lag far behind the closed‑source giants such as GPT‑4 or Gemini‑Ultra. The new **DeepSeekMath 7B** narrows that gap dramatically, hitting **51.7 %** on the competition‑level **MATH** benchmark—without any external toolkits, chain‑of‑thought voting, or ensemble tricks. In this post we walk through the full training “recipe” that made it possible, compare the result with other open‑source and proprietary models, and explain what the reported **self‑consistency over 64 samples (60.9 %)** means for everyday users.

---

## 1. From Code to Math: The Base Model  

DeepSeekMath starts from **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 (7 B)**, a model already fine‑tuned on a massive code corpus. Why begin with code?  

* **Structured reasoning:** Coding data teaches the model to follow precise logical steps and handle symbolic manipulation—skills that transfer directly to mathematics.  
* **Empirical boost:** Experiments in the paper show that a code‑first pre‑training stage improves both *tool‑free* and *tool‑assisted* math performance, even at the modest 7 B scale.  

The base model inherits all the coding abilities of DeepSeek‑Coder, which later helps when we ask it to write Python for program‑of‑thought (PoT) prompts.

---

## 2. Massive Math Pre‑Training (500 B Tokens)

### 2.1 The DeepSeekMath Corpus  

The heart of the improvement is a curated **120 B token math corpus** scraped from Common Crawl. The pipeline works as follows:

1. **Seed set:** OpenWebMath provides a high‑quality seed of ~500 k positive examples.
2. **FastText classifier:** Trained on the seed vs. a balanced set of random web pages; scores each page for “math‐likeness.”
3. **Iterative mining:** Pages with top scores are added to the seed; the classifier is retrained; this repeats four times, ending with **35.5 M web pages** (≈120 B tokens).  
4. **Multilingual coverage:** Over half of the tokens are English; a substantial portion is Chinese, which later yields gains on Chinese math benchmarks.
5. **Decontamination:** Any n‑gram that matches benchmark data (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, etc.) is stripped to avoid leakage.

The final corpus is *seven times larger* than Minerva’s math web pages and *nine times larger* than OpenWebMath, while maintaining higher average quality (validated by downstream few‑shot evaluations).

### 2.2 Training Details  

* **Token mix:** 56 % DeepSeekMath Corpus, 4 % AlgebraicStack (math code), 10 % arXiv papers, 20 % GitHub code, 10 % general natural language.
* **Schedule:** Same AdamW hyperparameters as DeepSeek LLMs; peak LR = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴; batch size = 10 M tokens; context length = 4K.
* **Duration:** 500 B tokens total (≈4 epochs over the math corpus).  

The result—**DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B**—already outperforms many larger closed models on pure reasoning benchmarks:

| Benchmark | DeepSeekMath‑Base | Minerva 540B | Mistral 7B |
|-----------|-------------------|--------------|------------|
| GSM8K (grade school) | 64.2 % | 63.x % | ~45 % |
| MATH (competition) | 36.2 % | 35.x % | ~22 % |

So even before any instruction tuning, a modest 7 B model reaches parity with a 540 B closed system.

---

## 3. Instruction Tuning: CoT + PoT + Tool‑Integrated Reasoning  

Next comes **DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B**, trained on a curated set of ~776 K examples that cover three solution styles:

| Style | Data source | What it teaches |
|-------|------------|-----------------|
| Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT) | GSM8K, MATH annotations + MathInstruct + Lila-OOD | Stepwise natural language reasoning |
| Program‑of‑Thought (PoT) | Same sources but solutions expressed as Python scripts using `math` / `sympy` | How to offload heavy arithmetic to code |
| Tool‐Integrated Reasoning (ToRA) | Custom tool prompts where the model calls an external calculator or evaluator | Seamless switching between text and tool usage |

Training uses a simple cross‑entropy loss for 500 steps (batch size 256, LR 5e−5). No RL yet—just supervised learning that teaches *how* to format answers.

### Performance Jump

| Benchmark | DeepSeekMath‑Base | DeepSeekMath‑Instruct |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------------|
| GSM8K (CoT) | 64.2 % | **88.2 %** |
| MATH (CoT) | 36.2 % | **46.8 %** |
| MATH (tool‐enabled) | – | **≈60 %** |

Compared to other open‐source giants (e.g., WizardMath 7B, InternLM2‑Math 20B), DeepSeekMath‑Instruct already beats them despite being only half the size of many competitors.

---

## 4. Reinforcement Learning with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

### 4.1 Why RL?  

Instruction tuning improves *how* to answer but does not directly optimize for the *correctness* metric used in benchmarks. Reinforcement learning lets us push the policy toward higher reward scores derived from a learned reward model.

### 4.2 GRPO vs. Classic PPO  

Standard PPO couples a policy with a separate value network (“critic”), which is memory hungry—problematic for multi‐GPU training of LLMs. GRPO cuts this overhead by:

1. **Sampling a group** of \(G\) outputs per question from the *old* policy.
2. Using the *average* reward of that group as the baseline instead of a learned value function.
3. Computing tokenwise advantages based on *relative* rewards within the group.
4. Applying a KL penalty directly between current and reference policies rather than embedding KL into each token reward.

The net effect is an RL algorithm that needs **no extra critic model**, thus saving GPU memory while still benefiting from advantage estimation.

### 4.3 Training Set & Settings  

* Only the CoT-style instruction data (`GSM8K` + `MATH`) → ~144 K questions.
* For each question sample **64** completions (the “self-consistency” set).
* Reward model trained on preference data derived from correct/incorrect answer judgments.
* Learning rates: reward model 2e−5; policy model 1e−6; KL coefficient = 0.04.

### 4.4 Results – The Final Score  

After one round of GRPO:

| Metric | Before GRPO (Instruct) | After GRPO (RL) |
|--------|------------------------|-----------------|
| GSM8K (CoT) | 88.2 % | **88.2 %** (unchanged – already saturated) |
| MATH (CoT) | 46.8 % | **51.7 %** |
| MATH self‑consistency (64 samples) | – | **60.9 %** |

The leap from 46.8 % → 51.7 % on MATH puts DeepSeekMath 7B *ahead of every other open source model up to at least 70 B*, and only behind closed systems like GPT‑4 or Gemini Ultra.

## 6. Self‑Consistency Over 64 Samples: What Does It Mean for You?

Self-consistency is a simple inference trick introduced by Wei et al.: generate multiple completions for the same question, then pick the answer that appears most often among them.

* In DeepSeekMath’s experiments they produced **64** answers per MATH problem.
* The majority vote raised accuracy from **51.7 % → 60.9 %**, i.e., nearly a ten point jump simply by sampling more.

For end users this translates into:

1️⃣ **Higher reliability** when you can afford extra compute—run several generations and take a majority vote or run a lightweight consistency scorer.

2️⃣ **No architectural changes needed**; it works out-of-the-box with any fine-tuned model.

3️⃣ A practical tradeoff: generating many samples costs more GPU time but yields an accuracy boost comparable to adding another RL fine-tuning pass.

## 7. Takeaways & Future Directions  

- **Data matters more than scale:** A carefully filtered web crawl can give a small model capabilities rivaling multi‐hundred–billion parameter closed models.
- **Code first → math later:** Starting from a code LLM gives an immediate reasoning edge.
- **GRPO makes RL affordable:** By dropping the critic network you can run PPO‐style RL on commodity hardware without sacrificing performance.
- **Self-consistency is cheap performance gain:** Sampling many outputs and voting can add ~9 points on hard benchmarks.
- **Room to grow:** Geometry and theorem proving still lag behind closed models; multilingual data selection could be refined further; larger reward models or better process supervision might push Maj@K even higher.

DeepSeekMath demonstrates that with clever data engineering and an efficient RL algorithm, even a modestly sized open-source LLM can break through long-standing performance ceilings in mathematical reasoning.
