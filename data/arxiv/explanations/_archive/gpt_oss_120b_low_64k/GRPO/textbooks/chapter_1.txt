\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs) have transformed many facets of artificial intelligence, yet enabling them to solve mathematical problems remains a particularly demanding goal.  Mathematics is not merely a collection of facts; it requires the manipulation of symbols, the execution of multi‑step logical chains, and often the integration of external tools such as symbolic algebra systems.  In recent years, research has demonstrated that LLMs can make substantial progress on quantitative reasoning benchmarks, but the gap between publicly available models and proprietary systems such as GPT‑4 or Gemini‑Ultra is still large.  This chapter provides a detailed overview of why mathematical reasoning is difficult for LLMs, surveys the benchmark ecosystem that quantifies progress, contrasts closed‑source and open‑source efforts, and finally describes the overarching objectives of the DeepSeekMath project as presented in the accompanying research paper.

## Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Hard for LLMs  

Mathematical problem solving differs from ordinary language generation in several fundamental ways.  First, mathematics is highly **structured**: a correct solution must obey algebraic identities, logical deductions, and domain‑specific conventions (e.g., proper use of quantifiers).  Small deviations—such as an off‑by‑one arithmetic error—render an entire answer invalid, whereas a language model generating narrative text can tolerate minor inconsistencies without catastrophic failure.  

Second, mathematics requires **deep multi‑step reasoning**.  A typical grade‑school problem may demand a series of intermediate calculations, each dependent on the previous result.  The model must therefore retain precise intermediate numeric values across many tokens and correctly propagate them forward.  Standard auto‑regressive decoding does not guarantee this level of fidelity; token‑level probabilities are optimized for fluency rather than exact symbolic consistency.

Third, many problems benefit from **external tool use** (e.g., calling Python’s `sympy` library for symbolic integration).  Incorporating tool calls demands that the model not only generate syntactically correct code but also understand how to invoke the tool, interpret its output, and incorporate that output back into a natural‑language solution.  Achieving this “program‑of‑thought” capability stretches the typical next‑token prediction paradigm.

Finally, **data scarcity and contamination** pose practical obstacles.  High‑quality math content on the web is intermingled with unrelated text; mining useful statements without overwhelming noise requires careful filtering.  Moreover, public benchmarks (e.g., MATH) must be excluded from training data to avoid inadvertent memorization that would inflate scores without truly improving reasoning ability.

These challenges collectively explain why early LLMs performed poorly on mathematical tasks despite strong performance on general language understanding.

## Benchmark Landscape: MATH, GSM8K, CMATH, and Others  

To evaluate progress on mathematical reasoning, researchers have curated several benchmark suites that differ in difficulty, format, and language coverage.

* **MATH** – A competition‑level dataset containing roughly 12 000 problems drawn from high school to undergraduate mathematics competitions.  Problems are presented in natural language and require full step‑by‑step solutions; accuracy is measured by exact match of the final answer after self‑consistency or chain‑of‑thought prompting.

* **GSM8K** – A grade‑school level dataset with 8 500 arithmetic word problems.  Unlike MATH, GSM8K focuses on elementary quantitative reasoning but still expects a coherent derivation rather than a single numeric guess.

* **CMATH** – The Chinese counterpart to GSM8K/MATH; it comprises K‑12 problems written in Mandarin across diverse topics such as linear equations and geometry.  CMATH enables evaluation of multilingual reasoning abilities.

* **Other multilingual or specialized suites** – The paper also references Chinese benchmarks such as MGSM‑zh, Gaokao‑MathCloze, and Gaokao‑MathQA (both derived from Chinese college‑entrance examinations), as well as broader reasoning collections like MMLU‑STEM (multiple‐choice STEM questions) and BBH (hard reasoning tasks).

Performance on these benchmarks is typically reported as **accuracy** (percentage of correctly solved items) for self‑contained solutions or **Pass@K / Maj@K** when multiple sampled outputs are considered.  The authors emphasize that achieving above 50 % on competition‐level MATH is a significant milestone for open models; prior open‐source systems hovered well below this threshold.

## Closed‑Source vs. Open‑Source Models  

Proprietary systems such as **GPT‑4** and **Gemini‑Ultra** have set the state‐of‐the‐art bar by attaining roughly 70–80 % accuracy on MATH when equipped with tool use and chain‐of‐thought prompting.  Their training pipelines typically involve massive token counts (trillions) drawn from curated internet corpora plus extensive reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).  However, their internal weights remain inaccessible to the research community.

In contrast, **open‐source models** are freely downloadable and amenable to modification.  Prior to DeepSeekMath, notable open baselines included:

* **Mistral 7B**, a general-purpose decoder trained primarily on natural language.
* **Llemma 34B**, which underwent mathematical pretraining on Proof‑Pile‑2.
* **Minerva 540B**, a closed model built on PaLM but limited public release.
* Various instruction‑tuned models (e.g., InternLM2-Math 20B, WizardMath series) that apply supervised fine‐tuning or PPO reinforcement learning on math data.

The open landscape suffered from two main deficiencies: (1) insufficient scale or quality of math‐focused pretraining data, leading to poor performance on MATH (<40 % for most 7 B–34 B models), and (2) limited reinforcement learning pipelines tailored to mathematics due to memory constraints of classic PPO methods.

The DeepSeekMath work seeks to close this gap by constructing a massive high‑quality math corpus (“DeepSeekMath Corpus”) and introducing an efficient RL algorithm called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** that reduces memory overhead while still boosting reasoning ability.

## Goals of the DeepSeekMath Project  

The DeepSeekMath initiative pursues three tightly coupled objectives:

1. **Scalable Math Pretraining** – By mining *120 B* math tokens from Common Crawl using an iterative fastText classifier pipeline (seeded with OpenWebMath), the authors assemble a multilingual corpus that is roughly seven times larger than Minerva’s web component and nine times larger than OpenWebMath alone.  Experiments demonstrate that training a modest *7 B* model (“DeepSeekMath‑Base”) on this corpus yields performance comparable to Minerva 540 B on GSM8K and MATH, confirming that data quality can outweigh raw parameter count for mathematical reasoning.

2. **Instruction Tuning with Chain‑of‑Thought and Program‑of‑Thought Data** – After pretraining, DeepSeekMath-Base undergoes supervised fine–tuning (“DeepSeekMath-Instruct”) on *776 K* high‑quality instruction examples spanning English and Chinese problems solved via plain CoT, programmatic PoT (Python + `sympy`/`math` libraries), and tool‐integrated formats.  This step brings the model’s performance up to parity with much larger open models (e.g., 70 B instruction‐tuned baselines).

3. **Efficient Reinforcement Learning via GRPO** – Standard PPO requires an additional value network acting as a baseline—a costly component when scaling to billions of parameters.  GRPO replaces this critic with a simple groupwise average reward baseline: for each question it samples *G* responses from the current policy, computes reward scores using a learned reward model, then normalizes each response’s advantage relative to its peer group.  The method directly penalizes KL divergence between the updated policy and a frozen reference model instead of embedding KL into per-token rewards.  Empirically, GRPO raises DeepSeekMath-Instruct’s accuracy from *46.8 % → 51.7 %* on MATH and improves out‑of‑domain tasks such as CMATH (*84.6 % → 88.8 %*) while consuming dramatically less GPU memory than PPO.

Collectively these goals aim to deliver an open-source LLM whose mathematical capabilities rival closed alternatives while remaining reproducible for the community.  By publishing data collection details, training hyperparameters, and algorithmic innovations like GRPO, the authors provide a concrete blueprint for future work in mathematically capable language modeling.
