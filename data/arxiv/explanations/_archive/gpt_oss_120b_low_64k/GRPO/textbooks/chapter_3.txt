\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: Large‑Scale Math Pre‑Training Corpus Construction
================================================

The remarkable performance of DeepSeekMath 7B on challenging mathematical benchmarks is rooted in a carefully engineered pre‑training corpus containing roughly 120 billion math‑related tokens harvested from the public Common Crawl archive.  This chapter presents a complete, step‑by‑step description of how that corpus—referred to as the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**—was built, why each design decision matters, and how the resulting data enables strong mathematical reasoning in a relatively small 7 B‑parameter model.  All procedures described below follow the pipeline documented in the DeepSeekMath paper; no external information is introduced.

---

### Seed Corpus Selection (OpenWebMath)

The construction process begins with a **seed corpus**, a high‑quality collection of web pages that are already known to contain mathematical content.  The authors chose **OpenWebMath** as this seed because it is a publicly released set of math‑focused webpages extracted from Common Crawl and manually curated for relevance.  OpenWebMath provides a reliable source of positive examples: each document contains well‑structured explanations, problem statements, or derivations that are representative of the language and notation the final model should learn.

Using OpenWebMath as a seed serves two purposes. First, it supplies concrete training data for a supervised classifier that will later retrieve many more pages from the massive Common Crawl dump. Second, it guarantees that the classifier’s initial notion of “mathematical text” aligns with human judgments, avoiding drift toward unrelated technical domains.

---

### FastText Classifier Training and Iterative Refinement

#### First‑Round Training

With the seed corpus in hand, the authors trained a **fastText** text classifier—a shallow, linear model augmented with n‑gram features that scales efficiently to hundreds of millions of examples.  The training set comprised:

* **Positive examples** – 500 000 randomly sampled documents from OpenWebMath.
* **Negative examples** – 500 000 web pages randomly drawn from the broader Common Crawl archive (outside the seed).

FastText was configured with a 256‑dimensional word embedding space, a learning rate of 0.1, n‑gram length up to three words, a minimum word occurrence threshold of three, and three training epochs.  These hyper‑parameters balance expressive power (capturing multi‑word mathematical phrases like “partial derivative”) with speed and memory efficiency.

The trained model yields for any unseen page a **score** indicating how likely the page is math‑related.  The authors then applied this classifier to a deduplicated subset of Common Crawl containing roughly **40 billion HTML pages** (after URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication).  All pages were ranked by their fastText scores; only the top‑scoring pages were retained for further inspection.

#### Iterative Improvement

The first classifier inevitably missed many valuable pages because its positive set was limited to the original OpenWebMath distribution.  To broaden coverage, the authors performed **four rounds of iterative refinement**, each consisting of:

1. **Domain analysis** – The entire Common Crawl corpus was partitioned into *domains* (all pages sharing the same base URL). For each domain they computed the fraction of pages already selected in the previous round.
2. **Domain selection** – Domains where more than 10 % of pages had been selected were labeled as “math‐related”. Typical examples include `mathoverflow.net` and other academic forums.
3. **Manual URL annotation** – Within each selected domain, annotators inspected URL patterns (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/*`) to identify sub‑paths that reliably host mathematical content. These URLs were added to an expanded positive set.
4. **Classifier retraining** – The new positive URLs were merged with previously collected math pages, forming an enriched seed corpus for re‑training fastText with the same hyper‑parameters.

Each iteration amplified recall: with more diverse positives, fastText learned additional lexical cues (e.g., language‐specific symbols or code snippets) that were absent initially. After four iterations, **35.5 million distinct math web pages**—corresponding to roughly **120 billion tokens**—had been gathered.  The process halted after the fourth round because only ~2 % of new pages were added compared with the third iteration, indicating diminishing returns.

---

### Domain‑Level Analysis and Manual URL Annotation

A pivotal insight behind this pipeline is that large web archives contain *clusters* of mathematically rich content at the domain level. By measuring collection percentages per domain after each iteration, the authors could automatically flag promising sites without exhaustive crawling.

Once a domain was flagged, humans examined its URL structure to locate subdirectories where mathematics consistently appears (for example, question threads, lecture notes, or problem archives). This manual step is lightweight because only a few representative URLs per domain need labeling; fastText then generalizes from them to all pages under those paths.

This approach yields two crucial benefits:

* **Scalability** – Instead of labeling millions of pages individually, annotators only need to tag a few hundred domains.
* **Precision** – Manual curation eliminates false positives such as news sites that happen to contain occasional numbers but are not mathematically oriented.

---

### Deduplication, Token Statistics, and Multilingual Coverage

Before feeding collected pages to the language model trainer, several cleaning stages ensure data quality and accurate token accounting.

1. **URL‑based deduplication** – Identical URLs appearing multiple times across different Common Crawl snapshots were collapsed.
2. **Near‑deduplication** – Content similarity heuristics (e.g., shingling) removed near duplicates such as mirrored copies across mirrors or minor format changes.
3. **Token counting** – After filtering, tokenizers identical to those used for model pre‑training counted tokens; this produced precise estimates:  
   * 120 billion tokens total  
   * Approximately 56 % coming directly from the DeepSeekMath Corpus during subsequent model training (the remainder includes code from GitHub, algebraic code from AlgebraicStack, arXiv excerpts, and general natural language data).

Because fastText operates on raw text irrespective of language, the pipeline naturally harvested both English and Chinese webpages—the two most common languages observed among math sites in Common Crawl.  Consequently, downstream evaluations show consistent improvements on Chinese benchmarks (e.g., CMATH) alongside English ones.

---

### Benchmark Decontamination Methods

A critical concern when constructing a pre‑training corpus for evaluation is *contamination*: inadvertent inclusion of test or benchmark material can artificially inflate scores. The DeepSeekMath authors implemented stringent decontamination:

* For every benchmark (English: GSM8K, MATH; Chinese: CMATH, AGIEval), they extracted every contiguous *10‑gram* fragment occurring in any test instance.
* Any web page containing an exact match to any such 10‑gram was discarded entirely.
* For shorter benchmark fragments (3–9 grams), exact matching was also applied; if a page contained such short sequences it was removed as well.

This exhaustive n‑gram matching guarantees that no direct copy of evaluation questions or solutions survives in the training set, preserving the integrity of subsequent benchmark results.

---

### Why This Corpus Matters

The final DeepSeekMath Corpus distinguishes itself along three dimensions:

1. **Scale** – At 120 B tokens it is roughly seven times larger than the math web portion used by Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath alone.
2. **Quality** – Iterative classifier refinement plus manual domain annotation yields high precision; empirical pre‑training experiments show superior downstream performance compared with smaller corpora such as MathPile or Proof‑Pile‑2 even when those are trained for an equivalent number of steps.
3. **Multilinguality** – By not restricting collection to English sources, the corpus enriches Chinese mathematical reasoning ability—a benefit reflected in benchmark gains on CMATH and Gaokao‐based tests.

When DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B continued training on this corpus (alongside code and general text), it matched or exceeded far larger closed models (e.g., Minerva 540B) on quantitative reasoning tasks despite having only seven billion parameters.  The experiments demonstrated that *data quality and relevance can outweigh sheer model size*, especially for structured reasoning domains such as mathematics.

In summary, constructing a massive yet clean mathematical pre‑training corpus requires:

1. Starting from a reliable seed (OpenWebMath).  
2. Training an efficient fastText classifier on balanced positive/negative examples.  
3. Iteratively expanding positive examples via domain‐level statistics and minimal manual URL labeling.  
4. Rigorously deduplicating and decontaminating against benchmark leakage.  

These steps together produce a high‑quality multilingual dataset that empowers open‑source language models to achieve state‑of‑the‑art performance on challenging mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
