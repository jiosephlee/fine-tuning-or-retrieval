\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# From Web Crawls to Math Mastery: How DeepSeekMath Harvested 120 B Tokens of Math Data  

Large language models have shown a remarkable knack for tackling math problems, but the secret sauce often lies in the data they are fed. **DeepSeekMath 7B** demonstrates that a carefully curated web‑scale corpus can push an open‑source model to the brink of GPT‑4‑level performance—*all without any proprietary data*. In this post we walk through the data‑collection pipeline that turned the public **Common Crawl** into the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, a 120 billion‑token trove of multilingual mathematical text. We’ll break down the fastText classifier, the iterative domain expansion, de‑duplication tricks, multilingual filtering, and why this approach beats the more common arXiv‑centric corpora.

---

## 1. Why Start From the Open Web?  

Most “math‑pre‑training” efforts (e.g., Minerva, MathPile) rely heavily on arXiv papers or curated textbooks. While scholarly PDFs are high quality, they are **English‑biased** and limited in sheer volume. DeepSeekMath’s authors asked a simple question:

> *Does the public web contain enough high‑quality math content to rival or surpass arXiv?*

The answer turned out to be a resounding “yes”—provided you can **filter** the noise. By mining Common Crawl (CC), they accessed a “long tail” of math forums, educational sites, Q&A pages, and even non‑English resources that traditional pipelines miss.

---

## 2. Seed Corpus: The Starting Point  

The pipeline begins with a **seed dataset**: the publicly released **OpenWebMath** collection. Although modest (≈13 B tokens), it offers clean examples of math‑heavy web pages (forum posts, lecture notes, problem statements). This seed serves two purposes:

1. **Positive examples** for training a binary classifier that distinguishes math pages from generic web text.  
2. A basis for **domain discovery**—the seed hints at which top‑level domains (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`) are likely math‑rich.

---

## 3. FastText as a Scalable Math Classifier  

Training massive transformer classifiers on billions of webpages is overkill; instead DeepSeekMath uses **fastText**, a lightweight linear model with subword embeddings that trains in minutes on a single GPU.

### Training details
* **Positive set:** 500 k randomly sampled pages from OpenWebMath.  
* **Negative set:** 500 k random pages from CC (ensuring diverse topics).  
* **Hyperparameters:** 256‑dimensional vectors, learning rate 0.1, n‑gram length 3, minimum word count 3, 3 epochs.  

FastText learns a score *s(p)* for any page *p*. Higher scores → more “math‑like”. Because of its bag‑of‑ngrams nature, fastText quickly picks up domain‐specific terminology (`∫`, `mod`, `ψ(x)`) while ignoring generic boilerplate.

---

## 4. Iterative Mining and Domain Expansion  

A single pass over CC would miss many math pages that don’t resemble the seed vocabulary closely enough. The authors therefore adopt an **iterative refinement loop**:

1. **First pass:** Apply fastText to all deduplicated CC pages (≈40 B HTML pages after URL deduplication). Keep only the top‑scoring tokens (≈40 B tokens).  
2. **Domain analysis:** Group pages by their base domain (e.g., `math.stackexchange.com`). Compute what fraction of each domain was captured in step 1.
   * Domains where >10 % of pages were selected are flagged as “math‐related”.  
3. **Manual URL annotation:** Human annotators browse these flagged domains and mark sub‑paths that consistently contain math content (`/questions/`, `/lecture-notes/`).  
4. **Seed augmentation:** Add newly discovered pages to the positive set and retrain fastText → improved recall on more obscure math sites.
5. **Repeat:** Steps 1–4 run for four iterations total.

By the final iteration they amassed **35.5 M distinct math pages**, amounting to **120 B tokens**—roughly 7× larger than Minerva’s web portion and 9× larger than OpenWebMath.

> **Key insight:** The “domain expansion” step turns a modest seed into a self‑reinforcing discovery engine, allowing the classifier to learn from its own successes.

---

## 5. De‑duplication and Near‑Duplication Pruning  

Common Crawl contains massive redundancy: identical articles mirrored across mirrors, pagination artifacts, or boilerplate footers. Feeding duplicated text would waste token budget and bias training toward popular sites.

* **URL‐based deduplication:** Exact match on normalized URLs removes identical copies.
* **Near‑deduplication:** Fingerprinting (e.g., MinHash) identifies near‐identical pages (minor edits or different query parameters). Pages sharing >95 % similarity are collapsed to a single representative.

After this cleaning, only ~40 B raw HTML pages remain—still huge but tractable for fastText scoring.

---

## 6. Multilingual Harvesting  

Unlike many math corpora that filter out non‑English content early on, DeepSeekMath deliberately keeps multilingual pages:

* The fastText model is trained on tokenized raw text without language filtering; its n‑gram features capture Chinese characters (`∑`, `方程`) just as well as Latin ones.
* During iteration, domains like `zhihu.com/math` or Chinese university lecture sites surface naturally.
* Final token distribution: roughly *70 % English* and *30 % Chinese*, with smaller fractions of other languages.

Consequences:
* Significant gains on Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, Gaokao variants).  
* A more balanced language model that generalizes better when faced with mixed‐language prompts.

---

## 7. Benchmark Contamination Safeguards  

When constructing a pre‑training corpus it’s easy to inadvertently leak evaluation data (e.g., copying GSM8K test questions). DeepSeekMath applies a strict filter:

* Any 10‑gram appearing verbatim in any benchmark (English or Chinese) is excised from the source page.
* For shorter strings (<10 grams but ≥3 grams), exact matching still triggers removal.
* This conservative approach eliminates overfitting risk while preserving most of the surrounding context.

---

## 8. Quality Validation: Why Size Alone Isn’t Enough  

The authors ran controlled experiments with four corpora:

| Corpus | Size (tokens) | Dominant source | Benchmarks used |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| MathPile | 8.9 B | Mostly arXiv | GSM8K, MATH |
| OpenWebMath | 13.6 B | CC filtered | Same |
| Proof-Pile-2 | ~40 B | Mix (code + arXiv) | Same |
| **DeepSeekMath** | **120 B** | CC + fastText filter + multilingual | Same |

All models were finetuned for an equal number of steps (150 B tokens). Results showed:

* DeepSeekMath consistently outperformed the others on *both English and Chinese* tasks.
* The learning curve remained steep even after >100 B tokens—a sign that added data kept providing novel patterns rather than repeating what was already seen.
* Multilingual gains were evident: Chinese benchmarks improved while others plateaued.

Thus, it isn’t just “more tokens”; it’s *more diverse high‑quality tokens*, especially from underrepresented languages and forums.

---

## 9. What About ArXiv?  

A surprising finding: adding large arXiv slices (MathPile’s 85 % arXiv component or a raw ArXiv-RedPajama dump) did **not** improve downstream math reasoning—and sometimes hurt performance on GSM8K/MATH/Lemma benchmarks.

Possible reasons discussed:

1. **Domain mismatch:** Academic papers often contain dense LaTeX formulae but little natural language reasoning flow; models may overfit to formal proof style rather than stepwise problem solving.
2. **Language bias:** ArXiv is overwhelmingly English; it doesn’t help multilingual benchmarks.
3. **Redundancy:** Many papers recycle similar theorem statements; token diversity suffers.

The takeaway is clear: *quality trumps quantity*, and “web math” provides richer reasoning patterns than raw research articles for LLM pretraining.

---

## 10. From Corpus to Model – The Bigger Picture  

With the corpus ready, DeepSeekMath follows these steps:

1️⃣ Continue pretraining DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 (7 B) on a mixture where **56 %** is DeepSeekMath tokens → total 500 B training tokens.  
2️⃣ Supervise fine‑tune on instruction data (CoT + PoT + tool–integrated formats).  
3️⃣ Apply the novel **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** reinforcement learning loop using only the same instruction set — boosting MATH accuracy from 46.8 % → **51.7 %** without external tools.

Result: an open-source model that cracks over half of competition‐level MATH problems—something previously seen only in closed models like Gemini Ultra or GPT‑4.

---

## 11. Key Lessons for Practitioners  

| Aspect | What DeepSeekMath Shows |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Data source selection** | Public web sources hold abundant high-quality math content; focus on clever filtering rather than sheer scraping of papers. |
| **Classifier choice** | A lightweight fastText model can be iteratively refined to achieve recall comparable to much larger neural classifiers at fraction of cost. |
| **Iterative expansion** | Looping between classifier training → domain discovery → seed augmentation yields exponential coverage improvement. |
| **Deduplication matters** | Removing exact and near duplicates preserves token budget for novel content; essential at billions-of-page scale. |
| **Multilingual inclusion** | Simple “don’t filter non-English” policy yields measurable gains on Chinese benchmarks without hurting English performance.|  
| **Benchmark safety** | Stringent n‑gram matching prevents leakage; crucial for fair evaluation later on.|

If you’re building your own domain-specific LLM—be it chemistry, law, or finance—adapting this pipeline can save months of manual crawling and provide a competitive edge.

---

## 12. Looking Ahead  

The DeepSeekMath team points to several future directions:

* Refine domain expansion with *active learning*: let the model query annotators for ambiguous pages instead of exhaustive manual URL tagging.
* Pair fastText with transformer‐based “teacher” models for better precision at later stages.
* Explore better reward models for RL that understand stepwise reasoning beyond final answer correctness.
* Scale up model size while preserving code training benefits identified in their ablations.

---

### TL;DR

DeepSeekMath proves that with an *engineered fastText classifier*, *iterative domain hunting*, *careful deduplication*, and *multilingual openness*, you can turn publicly available Common Crawl into a 120 billion-token math corpus that outperforms arXiv-heavy alternatives across both English and Chinese benchmarks—all while staying open source.

If you’re curious about implementations or want to try building your own “web→domain” corpus, most of the tooling is open—fastText libraries, URL deduplication scripts, and basic HTML extraction pipelines are all readily available on GitHub today.

Happy crawling—and may your next LLM be as mathematically savvy as DeepSeekMath!
