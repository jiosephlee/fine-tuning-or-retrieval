\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 5: # Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) Architecture  

In this chapter we present a complete and self‑contained exposition of **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)**, the parameter‑efficient extension of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).  We begin by recalling the OFT formulation and then describe how BOFT replaces the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with a *butterfly‑factorized* orthogonal matrix.  The discussion proceeds through the exact mathematical construction of the BOFT transformation matrix \( \mathbf{R}(m,b) \), its special cases (block‑diagonal OFT and full OFT), the role of the two hyper‑parameters \((m,b)\) in controlling expressivity versus parameter count, and finally the practical engineering choices of **identity initialization** and a **multiplicative dropout** scheme that make BOFT both stable in training and robust to over‑fitting.

---

## 1. From OFT to BOFT: Why Factorize an Orthogonal Matrix?  

OFT adapts a pretrained linear layer  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
\]

by left‑multiplying it with a learned orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\).  The forward pass for an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) becomes  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}.
\tag{1}
\]

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_d\)), pairwise angles among the rows/columns of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) are preserved, which empirically yields better generalization than additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA.  

The obstacle is that a dense orthogonal matrix needs \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameters, far exceeding what is affordable for large foundation models.  OFT mitigates this by imposing a **block‑diagonal** structure

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}), \qquad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, \;
br=d,
\tag{2}
\]

which reduces the trainable count to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\).  Unfortunately, block‑diagonal matrices fragment the transformation: each block acts on a disjoint subset of neuron dimensions, thereby limiting expressivity and preventing approximation of many classic linear transforms.

BOFT resolves this trade‑off by **factorizing** a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of *sparse* orthogonal matrices following a butterfly topology—a pattern known from the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).  The factorization yields dense connectivity between input and output dimensions while requiring only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) trainable parameters.

---

## 2. Full BOFT Formulation  

### 2.1 Butterfly Factors  

Let \(k\ge 2\) be an even integer (typically a power of two).  A **butterfly factor** is defined as  

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\tag{3}
\]

where each \(\mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}\) supplies the diagonal entries of its four sub‑blocks.  The pattern contains exactly \(k\) non‑zero entries (one per row), which is the minimal number that can still permit an invertible (and thus potentially orthogonal) map.

### 2.2 Recursive Butterfly Matrix  

Assume the hidden dimension \(d=2^{N}\).  The **butterfly matrix** \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) is built recursively:

1. Partition the \(d\times d\) matrix into four quadrants of size \(d/2\times d/2\).
2. Apply a butterfly factor to each quadrant block; simultaneously recurse on each sub‑quadrant.

Formally,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf B(d)
&= 
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
   \begin{bmatrix}
      \mathbf B_{1}({d}/{2}) & \mathbf 0\\
      \mathbf 0                & \mathbf B_{2}({d}/{2})
   \end{bmatrix}\\[4pt]
&= 
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,{d}/{2})\, 
   \cdots 
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2),
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

where each **butterfly component**  

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
      \operatorname{diag}\bigl(
           \underbrace{\mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\,\dots,\,
           \mathbf B^{F}_{d/k}(k)}_{d/k~{\rm blocks}}
      \bigr)
      \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\tag{5}
\]

is block‑diagonal with block size \(k\).  The recursion depth is \(\log_{2} d = N\); at each level the block size halves, producing a cascade of sparse matrices whose non‑zero pattern exactly matches the classic FFT butterfly graph.

### 2.3 Block‑Butterfly Extension  

To increase flexibility we replace scalar diagonals in (3) with small dense blocks of size \(b\times b,\; b=2,4,\dotsc\.  Define  

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)
    = 
    \operatorname{diag}\bigl(
       \underbrace{\widetilde{\mathbf B}^{F}_{1}(k),\, 
                    \dots,\,
                    \widetilde{\mathbf B}^{F}_{d/k}(k)}_{d/k~{\rm blocks}}
   \bigr),
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\mathbf B}^{F}_{i}(k)
        = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
            {\rm diag}(\!\!\underbrace{\!D_{i1}}_{\!b\times b})
            &
            {\rm diag}(\!\!\underbrace{\!D_{i2}}_{\!b\times b})
            \\[4pt]
            {\rm diag}(\!\!\underbrace{\!D_{i3}}_{\!b\times b})
            &
            {\rm diag}(\!\!\underbrace{\!D_{i4}}_{\!b\times b})
        \end{bmatrix},
        % where each D_{ij} ∈ ℝ^{b×b}.
        % Actually each Dij is itself a b×b matrix placed on the diagonal.
        % To keep notation simple we just write as above.
        % The key idea: each entry becomes a b×b block.
        % So each butterfly component now contains (k/b)×(k/b) blocks.
        .
        .
        .
        }

Each block \(D_{ij}\in\rho(b)\) will itself be constrained to be orthogonal, guaranteeing that every component
\( \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)\) remains an orthogonal matrix.

### 2.4 The BOFT Transformation Matrix  

With all components orthogonal, BOFT defines its overall orthogonal finetuning matrix as

> **Definition (BOFT Orthogonal Matrix).**  
> Let \(m=\log_{2}(d/b)+1\, (\ge 1)\).  
> For each level \(i=1,\dots,m\), pick block size  
> \(k_i = 2^{\,m-i+1}\).  
> Set  
>   \( \displaystyle
>       \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} 
>          = 
>          \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}\bigl(d,k_i\bigr).
>  	$
> Then

\[
\boxed{
   \mathbf R(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
    	\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} ,
}
\qquad
    (\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{\!\top}
    = 
    (\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{-1}.
    }
    \tag{6}
%
The forward pass for a linear layer thus becomes

\[
   \boxed{
       \mathbf z
       = 
       (\,\underbrace{\;\bigl[\;\prod_{i=1}^{m}
               		\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\,]\,}_{%
               		=\;\displaystyle {\bf R}(m,b)}
               		\, {\bf W}^{0}\; )^{T}{bf x}}
       .
   }
   \tag{7}
∎
%
Equation (7) is precisely OFT’s Eq (1), except that now   
\( {\bf R}= {\bf R}(m,b)\) has been expressed as a product of only  
\( m = O(\log d)\) sparse factors, each with only \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries.

## 3. Special Cases: Recovering Block‑Diagonal OFT and Full OFT  

BOFT subsumes earlier OFT variants simply by fixing the hyper‑parameters:

| Setting | Resulting Transformation |
|---------|---------------------------|
| **\(m=1,\; b=d** | A single component equals a dense orthogonal matrix; BOFT reduces to **full OFT** (no sparsity). |
| **\(m=1,\; b< d** | Only one block‑butterfly component with block size \(b.\) Its overall sparsity pattern becomes exactly a *block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrix as in Equation (2); thus **block‑diagonal OFT** is recovered. |
| **\(m=\log_2 d,\; b=2** | The classic FFT butterfly topology with only scalar diagonals; this achieves maximal parameter reduction (\(O(d\,log d)\)) while still yielding a dense overall orthogonal transform. |

Consequently, practitioners can navigate continuously from highly constrained block updates to fully expressive dense orthogonal updates simply by varying \(m,b.\)

---

## 4. Hyper‑Parameters \( (m,b) : Expressivity vs Efficiency  

- **Number of factors \(m.\)** Each additional factor multiplies another sparse orthogonal matrix into the product. Adding factors increases depth of information mixing across dimensions; empirically this improves *expressivity*—the ability to approximate arbitrary orthogonal matrices—while raising parameter count linearly:  
  \( |\theta| = m\times(b-1)d/2.\)

- **Block size \(b.\)** Inside each butterfly factor, diagonal entries are replaced by \(b\times b\) submatrices. Larger \(b\) expands the local transformation power at each level but also inflates trainable parameters proportionally to \(b-1.\)

A useful rule of thumb:

| Desired Parameter Budget | Suggested Pair \( (m,b)\) |
|--------------------------|---------------------------|
| Tight budget (\(<10 d$) | Small blocks (\(b=2,4,\dotsc)\) with modest depth (\(m≈3–5).\)) |
| Moderate budget (\(~ d\,log d$) | Choose \(b=2,\; m=\lceil log_2 (d/b)\rceil.\) |
| Maximal expressivity | Set \(m=1,\; b=d.\)** |

Empirically (see Section 7 in the original paper), configurations such as BOFT\( (9,2)\)—nine factors with scalar diagonals—already outperform block‑diagonal OFT with substantially fewer parameters.

## 5. Identity Initialization via Zero Skew‑Symmetric Matrices  

A finetuning routine should start from *exactly* the pretrained model to avoid sudden performance drops. In OFT this is achieved by initializing $\mathbf R$ as identity $\mathbf I_d$. In BOFT we guarantee that each sparse factor starts as identity:

1. Represent any orthogonal matrix via the **Cayley transform**:
   \( 
      {\bf Q}= ({\bf I}+{\bf S})({\\bf I}-{\bf S})^{-1},
      ~~ {\bf S}= - {\bf S}^\top,
      ~~ {\bf S}\inℝ^{p\times p},
    )
   where $p$ equals either $k$ (scalar case) or $kb$ (block case).

2. Initialise all skew‑symmetric generators ${\bf S}$ to zero matrices:
   ${\bf S}=0$ $\Longrightarrow$ ${\bf Q}= {\bf I}.$

Since every component $\tilde{{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is built from independent Cayley transforms on its blocks, setting all skew‑symmetrics to zero yields 

```
for i = 1 … m:
    tilde_B^{b}_{(d,i)} ← I_d .
```

Consequently,

```
R(m,b) = I_d .
```

Thus during early training steps every downstream layer behaves exactly like its frozen predecessor, satisfying the “small perturbation” principle common in finetuning literature.

## 6. Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT  

Standard dropout zeroes out individual activations or weight entries at random—a procedure well suited for additive updates such as LoRA but incompatible with multiplicative left–multiplication by an orthogonal matrix: completely zeroing out rows would break orthogonality. BOFT therefore adopts a *structured* dropout that respects the group structure.

### 6.1 Dropout Mechanism  

Given $\displaystyle {\bf R}= {\bf R}(m,b)=
         \prod_{i=1}^{m}{B}_i$, define two dropout rates:

* $p_1$: fraction of entire butterfly components ${B}_i$ to be *skipped*.
* $p_2$: fraction of diagonal blocks *within* each selected component to be replaced by identity submatrices.

The algorithm per training iteration:

```
For i = 1 … m:
    With probability p_1:
        replace B_i ← I_d                 # skip whole factor
    else:
        For each diagonal b×b block J inside B_i:
            With probability p_2:
                replace J ← I_b          # identity on that block
```

Because each affected replacement is itself an exact orthogonal submatrix,  
the product remains perfectly orthogonal after dropout—no re‐normalisation required.

### 6.2 Intuition and Benefits  

- *Regularisation*: Randomly disabling parts of the butterfly network prevents any single component from overfitting, analogous to ensemble averaging across many shallow factorizations.
- *Preservation of Orthogonality*: Since we never set arbitrary entries to zero but only substitute whole subblocks with identities, we never break $\tilde{{B}}^{\,b}_i{}^\top\, tilde{{B}}^{\,b}_i=\I,$ guaranteeing stability.
- *Computational Simplicity*: Implementation merely toggles flags on precomputed masks; there is no need for costly re‐orthogonalisation steps during forward passes.

---

## 7. Putting It All Together: Forward Pass Summary  

For any linear layer (including attention projections or feed‑forward MLP layers), BOFT proceeds as follows:

1. **Precompute** all butterfly components $\tilde{{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ given $(m,b)$.
2. **Initialize** them via Cayley transforms with zero skew–symmetrics → identity.
3. At every training step:
   - Apply multiplicative dropout according to $(p_1,p_2)$.
   - Multiply them in order to obtain $\displaystyle {\bf R}= {\bf R}(m,b)$.
   - Perform modified forward computation:
     $\displaystyle {\bf z}=({\\bf R}{\\bf W}^0)^{{\\rm T}} {\\bf x}$.
4. Backpropagate gradients only into the trainable parameters governing each Cayley generator (the non‑zero entries inside $\tilde{{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$).

Because after finetuning we can *collapse* $\displaystyle {\bf R}$ into a single dense orthogonal matrix and merge it with ${\\bf W}^0$, inference incurs no additional latency compared with standard full finetuning.

---

### Summary  

BOFT replaces the naive dense orthogonal transformation of OFT by a **butterfly‐factorized** product of sparse orthogonal matrices.  
The core construction—Equation (6)—offers three crucial advantages:

* **Parameter Efficiency:** only $\frac12(b-1)d m = O(d\,log d)$ trainable scalars instead of $O(d^2)$.
* **Expressivity Control:** hyper‑parameters $(m,b)$ smoothly interpolate between block‑diagonal OFT ($m=1$ small $b$), full OFT ($m=1$, $b=d$), and highly sparse yet expressive FFT‐like factorizations ($m=\log d$, $b=2$).
* **Training Stability:** identity initialization via zero skew symmetrics guarantees exact start from the pretrained model; multiplicative dropout offers regularisation without breaking orthogonality.

The BOFT architecture thus furnishes a principled, mathematically grounded finetuning tool that retains OFT’s angle‐preserving benefits while dramatically shrinking its memory footprint—making it practical for adapting today’s massive vision transformers, language models, and diffusion generators to new tasks.
