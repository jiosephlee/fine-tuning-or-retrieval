# The Maths Behind BOFT: Expressivity, Spectral Guarantees, and Inductive Bias  

## Introduction  

Fine‑tuning massive foundation models without blowing up the parameter budget is a hot topic.  Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT) ​\[[qiu2023controlling]\] took a principled step by **multiplying a learnable orthogonal matrix** onto each frozen linear layer, thereby preserving the pair‑wise angles between neurons.  The downside?  A dense orthogonal matrix costs $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ trainable values for a $d$‑dimensional layer, which quickly becomes prohibitive.  

Enter **Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning (BOFT)**.  By factorising the orthogonal matrix into a product of *butterfly*‑structured sparse orthogonal factors, BOFT reduces the trainable count to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ while still being able to generate dense transformations.  This post walks through the key theoretical insights that make BOFT work:  

1. **Expressivity** – why a butterfly factorisation is strictly more powerful than the block‑diagonal OFT used previously.  
2. **Spectral guarantees** – how orthogonal updates preserve singular values and why that matters for stability.  
3. **Metric‑learning view** – interpreting the orthogonal matrix as a bilinear similarity transform.  
4. **Inductive bias from butterfly topology** – how the sparsity pattern encodes useful priors that improve generalisation.

All of the results below are grounded in the BOFT paper; no extra assumptions are introduced.

---

## 1. Expressivity: From Block‑Diagonal to Butterfly  

### What “expressivity’’ means here  

For a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ we ask: *Can a given parameterisation represent every orthogonal matrix (the full $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ degrees of freedom), or at least a useful subset?*  In OFT the orthogonal matrix is forced to be block‑diagonal:

\[
\mathbf{R}_{\text{OFT}} = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}\bigr), \qquad \mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d .
\]

Each block $\mathbf{R}_i$ can be any $b\times b$ orthogonal matrix, but there is **no interaction across blocks**.  Consequently, any transformation that mixes dimensions belonging to different blocks cannot be expressed.

### Butterfly factorisation  

A *butterfly factor* of size $k$ (with $k$ even) is

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}.
\]

By stacking $\log_{2} d$ such factors with appropriately chosen block sizes we obtain the *butterfly matrix*  

\[
\mathbf{R}_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)},
\]

where each $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors of size $2^{i}$ (or their $b\times b$ extensions).  
If we choose $m=\log_{2} d$ and $b=2$, the product yields a *dense* orthogonal matrix while using only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ free parameters (each $2\times 2$ block contributes one rotation angle).

### Why BOFT is strictly more expressive  

**Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT).**  
*For any block size $b$, BOFT can represent all matrices that block‑diagonal OFT can, and additionally many matrices that mix dimensions across blocks.*  

*Sketch of proof.*  
- Any block‑diagonal OFT matrix can be recovered by setting all butterfly components except the first one to identity; the first component then collapses to a block‑diagonal matrix with exactly the same blocks as OFT.  
- Conversely, butterfly components introduce *permutations* between dimensions at each level (the classic “swap’’ pattern of FFT butterflies). After $\log_2 d$ levels every source dimension has been routed to every target dimension via at least one path in the induced graph. Hence the resulting product can have non‑zero entries in positions that are forced to zero in any block‑diagonal structure.

Empirically (Figure 3 in the paper) BOFT with modest depth ($m=6$, $b=4$) achieves far lower approximation error when trying to reconstruct a random dense orthogonal matrix than OFT with far more parameters.

### A hierarchy that captures the whole orthogonal group  

If we interleave *forward* and *transpose* butterfly products as  

\[
\mathbf R^{G}= \mathbf B_{d-1,1}\,\mathbf B_{d-1,2}^{\!\top}\,\dots\, \mathbf B_{1,1}\,\mathbf B_{1,2}^{\!\top},
\]

the resulting composition spans the full orthogonal group (the “kaleidoscope hierarchy’’).  In practice we stop far earlier because full expressivity is unnecessary—and often harmful—for fine‑tuning.

## 2. Spectral Norm Preservation  

### Orthogonal updates keep singular values intact  

Let $\mathbf W^{0}= \mathbf U\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top}$ be the SVD of a pretrained linear layer.  OFT and BOFT replace it by

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}
   = \bigl(\mathbf R\,\mathbf U\bigr)\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top}.
\]

Since $\mathbf R$ and $\mathbf U$ are both orthogonal, their product is again orthogonal, leaving $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ untouched. Consequently

\[
\sigma_{\max}(\widetilde{\mathbf W}) = \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W^{0}),
\]

i.e., **the spectral norm is exactly preserved** regardless of how we optimise $\mathbf R$.  

### Why this matters  

- **Training stability:** Gradient explosion/vanishing often correlates with large spectral norms; keeping them fixed avoids sudden spikes during optimisation.  
- **Generalisation:** Recent work on spectral regularisation shows that bounding the spectral norm improves out‑of‑distribution robustness; here we get an exact bound for free.  
- **Compatibility with downstream tasks:** Many downstream heads (e.g., classification layers) assume the magnitude of features stays within a known range; orthogonal finetuning respects that assumption.

In contrast, additive low‑rank methods such as LoRA can increase or decrease singular values depending on the learned low‑rank matrices, requiring explicit regularisation.

## 3. Orthogonal Finetuning as Bilinear Metric Learning  

The forward pass after BOFT on an input vector $\mathbf x$ reads

\[
z_i = \bigl(\mathbf w_i^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}\,\mathbf R\,\mathbf x,
\]

where $\mathbf w_i^{0}$ denotes column $i$ of $\mathbf W^{0}$. This is precisely a **bilinear similarity** between a learned query $\mathbf w_i^{0}$ and an input transformed by $\mathbf R$.  

### Connection to metric learning  

If we view $\mathbf R$ as a learnable linear map defining a Mahalanobis distance

\[
d_{\mathbf R}(\phi,\psi)^2 = (\phi-\psi)^{\!\top}\,\bigl(\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\! \mathbf R\bigr)\,(\phi-\psi),
\]

orthogonality forces $\mathbf R^{\!\top}\!\! \mathbf R = \mathbf I$, so distances are measured in an *isotropic* space but with an additional rotation/reflection applied to one side only. In other words, BOFT learns *which linear combinations of pretrained features are most useful* while preserving their geometry—exactly what metric learning seeks to do without altering overall scale.

This viewpoint helps explain why BOFT often enjoys better transfer performance than pure additive updates: it re‑weights relationships between features rather than injecting new directions that may overfit.

## 4. Inductive Bias from Butterfly Structure  

### Structured sparsity ≈ prior knowledge  

The butterfly topology originates from fast Fourier transforms: each factor exchanges information between two halves of the vector while keeping intra‑half connections local. This design embodies two desirable properties for fine‑tuning:

| Property | What butterfly gives | Why it helps |
|----------|----------------------|--------------|
| **Dense connectivity with few edges** | $\mathcal O(d \log d)$ non‑zero entries suffice to reach every output from every input | Guarantees expressivity without blowing up parameters |
| **Recoverability of classic transforms** | By fixing specific diagonal values we obtain DFT, Hadamard, DCT etc. | Implicitly encourages transforms that have proven utility in signal processing and vision |
| **Permutation invariance across layers** | Each factor can be seen as a permutation followed by local rotations | Encourages smooth mixing of information rather than abrupt blockwise jumps |

Because these patterns are hardcoded into the parameterisation, training cannot stray into “wild’’ parts of the orthogonal group that would overfit small downstream datasets.

### Empirical signs of better generalisation  

In image classification (ViT finetuning) and language modelling (LLM instruction tuning) experiments from the paper, BOFT consistently outperforms LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT under identical parameter budgets. The authors attribute this gain largely to the *inductive bias* introduced by butterflies: they act like a built‑in regulariser that keeps updates close to well‑behaved linear transforms while still allowing enough flexibility to adapt.

---

## 5. Putting It All Together  

BOFT’s mathematical foundations explain its empirical success:

1. **Expressivity** – The layered butterfly product creates dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal O(d \log d)$ free parameters, strictly superseding block‑diagonal OFT.
2. **Spectral preservation** – Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix leaves all singular values unchanged, yielding stable training dynamics.
3. **Metric learning interpretation** – The model learns a bilinear similarity under an isotropic metric, reusing pretrained feature geometry.
4. **Inductive bias** – Butterfly sparsity encodes useful signal-processing priors, guiding optimisation toward transformations that generalise well.

In practice this means you can take a giant foundation model—say GPT‑4 or Stable Diffusion—freeze most weights, learn only a few megabytes of butterfly parameters per layer, and still achieve state‑of‑the‑art downstream performance without extra inference latency.

---

## Takeaway Checklist for Practitioners  

- When parameter budget is tight but you need strong transfer ability → consider **BOFT** instead of LoRA or vanilla OFT.  
- Choose depth $m=\log_2(d/b)$ and small block size $b$ (e.g., $b=2$ or $4$) for maximal compression; larger $b$ reduces depth but increases parameters linearly.  
- Initialise all butterfly components as identity (Cayley transform with zero skew‐symmetric part) for zero deviation at start.  
- If overfitting remains a concern, apply **multiplicative dropout**: randomly replace whole butterfly components or individual $2b\times 2b$ blocks by identity during training.

BOFT demonstrates how marrying classic algorithmic ideas (the FFT butterfly) with modern deep learning theory yields both *theoretically elegant* and *practically powerful* fine‑tuning tools. As model sizes keep growing, such parameter-efficient strategies will become indispensable for researchers and engineers alike.
