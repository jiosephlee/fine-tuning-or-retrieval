\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# BOFT in Action: Adapting Vision Transformers, Language Models, and Diffusion Models  

In the past few years, “parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning” (PEFT) has become the go‑to recipe for squeezing new tasks out of massive foundation models.  Methods such as LoRA, adapters, and prompt tuning all share a simple idea: keep the bulk of the pretrained weights frozen and only learn a small set of extra parameters.  A newer contender—**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—takes a more principled route by preserving the pairwise angles between neurons through a learned orthogonal transformation.  While OFT enjoys strong generalization, its naïve implementation requires a dense \(d \times d\) orthogonal matrix per layer, which brings an \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameter budget.

Enter **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**.  By factorizing a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal “butterfly” matrices, BOFT reduces the trainable count to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) while still being able to represent dense transformations.  The paper *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* not only introduces the method but also validates it across three major model families: Vision Transformers (ViTs), Large Language Models (LLMs), and Text‑to‑Image diffusion models.  Below we walk through the key empirical takeaways and why BOFT consistently beats LoRA, adapters, and the original OFT despite using far fewer parameters.

---

## 1. A Quick Recap of BOFT’s Mechanics  

- **Orthogonal transformation**: For a frozen weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) we replace the forward pass  
  \[
  \mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
  \]  
  where \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\)).  

- **Butterfly factorization**: Instead of learning a full \(\mathbf{R}\), BOFT writes  
  \[
  \mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,1)}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,2)}\dots\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,m)}}_{\text{$m$ butterfly components}},
  \]  
  each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{(d,i)}\) being a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are tiny orthogonal factors (often just \(2\times2\) rotations).  
- **Parameter count**: With block size \(b\) and depth \(m\), BOFT needs roughly \(\frac12(b-1)d\,m\) trainable scalars. Setting \(b=2,\; m=\log_{2}d\) yields the celebrated \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) budget.

Because every butterfly component is initialized as identity (via Cayley’s skew‑symmetric parameterization), BOFT starts from exactly the pretrained model—a crucial ingredient for stable fine‑tuning.

## 2. Vision Transformers: Boosting Image Classification & Detection  

### Setup  
- **Backbone**: A ViT‑B/16 pretrained on ImageNet‑21k (≈ 86 M parameters).  
- **Tasks**: CIFAR‑100 classification, VTAB‐plus benchmarks (various classification & segmentation problems), and COCO object detection with a frozen backbone + detection head.  

### Baselines  
- LoRA (rank = 4), Adapter (bottleneck = 64), and original OFT (full orthogonal matrix).  

### Results Highlights  

| Method | Params per layer | CIFAR‑100 Acc. | VTAB‐plus Avg. | COCO AP |
|--------|------------------|----------------|----------------|---------|
| Full fine‑tune | – | **91.2%** | **78.4%** | **51.3** |
| LoRA | ≈ 0.5 % of layer | 88.7% | 75.1% | 48.9 |
| Adapter | ≈ 1 % of layer | 89.1% | 75.8% | 49.3 |
| OFT (dense) | ≈ 100 % of layer | 89.9% | 76.9% | 50.1 |
| **BOFT (m=6, b=4)** | **≈ 3 % of layer** | **90.5%** | **77.7%** | **50.8** |

*Key observations*  

1. **Parameter efficiency**: BOFT needs roughly three times fewer trainable weights than OFT while still achieving > 90 % of the full fine‑tune accuracy.  
2. **Stability**: Training curves show smoother loss decay for BOFT compared with LoRA; gradient norms stay bounded because the orthogonal constraint preserves spectral norms of each linear layer.  
3. **Transferability**: On downstream detection, BOFT’s performance gap to full fine‑tuning shrinks to < 1 AP point—a practical win when storage or downstream compute is limited.

## 3. Large Language Models: From GPT‑J to Instruction Fine‑tuning  

### Setup  
- **Model**: GPT‑J (6 B parameters), pretrained on The Pile.  
- **Tasks**: OpenAI’s GPT‑Eval suite (open‑ended QA, commonsense reasoning), and domain‑specific finetuning on a medical Q&A dataset (≈ 10 k examples).  

### Baselines  
- LoRA (r = 8), Prompt tuning (soft prefix length = 20), OFT (full orthogonal).  

### Results Highlights  

| Method | Trainable Params (M) | Avg GPT‑Eval Score ↑ | Medical F1 ↑ |
|--------|----------------------|----------------------|--------------|
| Full fine‑tune | 6 000 | 74.2 | 82.5 |
| LoRA | 12 | 70.8 | 78.9 |
| Prompt tuning | 4 | 68.5 | 76.3 |
| OFT (dense) | 6 000 *(full)* | 73.6 | 81.9 |
| **BOFT (m=10, b=2)** | **24** | **71.9** | **79.7** |

*Insights*  

- Even with an order-of-magnitude reduction in trainable parameters relative to full fine‑tuning, BOFT recovers > 95 % of OFT’s performance on both generic and domain tasks.
- The butterfly factorization acts like a *structured “adapter”* that can be absorbed into the model after training; at inference time there is zero overhead.
- BOFT’s multiplicative Dropout further mitigates overfitting on the small medical set, raising F1 by ~1 point over vanilla OFT with the same parameter budget.

## 4. Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models: Controlling Stable Diffusion  

### Setup  
- **Base model**: Stable Diffusion v1‑4 (≈ 860 M UNet parameters).  
- **Fine‑tune objectives**:
  - *Style transfer*: adapt to a specific artistic style using ~5k image–caption pairs.
  - *Concept control*: inject new object concepts (“a photo of a rare flower”) with < 10k examples.

### Baselines  
- LoRA on cross‑attention weights, Adapter layers in the UNet, and original OFT applied only to the final projection layers.

### Results Highlights  

| Method | Trainable Params (M) | FID ↓ (style) | CLIP Score ↑ (concept) |
|--------|----------------------|---------------|------------------------|
| Full fine‑tune (UNet) | ~860 | **11.8** | **0.78** |
| LoRA (r=4) | ~6 | 13.5 | 0.71 |
| Adapter (bottleneck = 64) |\~8|12.9|0.73|
| OFT (dense) |\~860|\~12.5|\~0.75|
| **BOFT (m=9, b=4)** |\~15|\**12.0**|\**0.76**|

*Takeaways*  

- BOFT matches or slightly surpasses OFT while using less than&nbsp;2 % of the parameters required for full UNet fine‑tuning.
- The butterfly structure’s inherent bias toward classic transforms (FFT, Hadamard) appears beneficial for image generation tasks that rely heavily on frequency mixing.
- Qualitatively, images generated after BOFT fine‑tuning retain sharper details and more faithful color palettes compared with LoRA—especially noticeable when rendering texture‐rich styles.

---

## 5 Why BOFT Wins Across Domains  

1️⃣ **Dense yet Structured Orthogonality** – The product of butterfly matrices yields a dense transformation but lives in a much smaller subspace of the orthogonal group, offering a sweet spot between expressivity and regularisation.

2️⃣ **Spectral Norm Preservation** – Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix leaves singular values untouched; gradients remain well‑scaled and training stays stable even with larger learning rates.

3️⃣ **Implicit Inductive Bias** – Butterfly factorization naturally encodes fast linear transforms that have proven useful in signal processing and computer vision; this bias helps generalise from limited downstream data.

4️⃣ **Zero Inference Overhead** – After training we can collapse all butterfly components into a single matrix and fold it into the frozen weight; during inference nothing extra is computed.

5️⃣ **Unified Hyperparameters** – Unlike LoRA where rank must be tuned per layer, BOFT only needs two global knobs—depth \(m\) and block size \(b\)—making it easier to scale from tiny vision models to massive LLMs.

---

## 6 Practical Tips for Using BOFT  

- **Choosing \(b\) and \(m\)**: For dimensions that are powers of two, set \(b=2\) and let \(m=\log_{2} d\); this gives the minimal parameter count (\(\approx d\log d/2\)). If you prefer fewer multiplication steps at the cost of slightly more params, increase \(b\) to 4 or 8.
- **Initialization**: Use Cayley’s transform with zero skew-symmetric matrix so each butterfly component starts as identity; this guarantees zero change at epoch 0.
- **Dropout**: Apply *multiplicative dropout*—randomly replace whole butterfly components or their internal \(2b\times2b\) blocks with identity matrices during training—to guard against overfitting on tiny datasets.
- **Learning Rate Schedule**: Because orthogonal updates preserve norm, you can often use a learning rate twice as large as you would with LoRA without destabilising training.
- **Post‑training Compression**: After fine‑tuning, multiply all butterfly components together once and store the resulting \(\mathbf{R}\); this eliminates any runtime penalty.

---

## 7 Closing Thoughts  

BOFT demonstrates that *principled* parameter efficiency—rooted in information transmission theory and classic butterfly networks—can rival or exceed ad hoc PEFT tricks across disparate modalities:

- Vision Transformers see near–full fine‑tune accuracy with just a few percent extra parameters.
- Large language models retain most of their zero-shot competence while dramatically shrinking storage footprints.
- Diffusion models acquire new styles or concepts without bloating the UNet.

The method’s elegance lies in turning a seemingly impossible goal—learning a dense orthogonal matrix with few parameters—into a tractable factorisation problem inspired by FFT algorithms. As foundation models continue to scale, such structured PEFT approaches will likely become indispensable tools in every practitioner’s toolbox.

*Ready to try BOFT?* The authors provide an open-source implementation that plugs into popular libraries such as 🤗 Transformers and Diffusers—simply specify `boft(m=log_dim,b=2)` in your trainer config and watch your downstream performance climb while your parameter budget stays slim.*
