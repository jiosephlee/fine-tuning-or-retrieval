\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserve pairwise neuron angles?
Question:
The paper states that OFT transforms neurons in a layer by the same orthogonal matrix to preserve the pairwise angles between neurons, which helps retain pretrained knowledge. I understand that orthogonal transformations preserve inner products, but I'm not clear on how this directly translates to preserving "pairwise angles" of the weight matrix columns. Could someone explain the geometric intuition and perhaps provide a simple algebraic demonstration?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) each layer’s weight matrix $W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is left‑multiplied by a learnable orthogonal matrix  
$$
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\qquad R^{\top}R = RR^{\top}=I_{d},
$$
so that the forward pass changes from  

$$
z = (W^{0})^{\top}x
$$

to  

$$
z = (R\,W^{0})^{\top}x = (W^{0})^{\top}R^{\top}x .
$$

The claim in the paper is that this operation *preserves the pair‑wise angles between neurons*, i.e. between the columns of $W^{0}$. Below we unpack why an orthogonal left‑multiplication does exactly that.

---

### 1. Geometry of a column (neuron)

Consider two columns of the original weight matrix,
$$
w_i = W^{0}_{:,i}, \qquad w_j = W^{0}_{:,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
$$
Geometrically each column is a point (or a vector) in the $d$‑dimensional space; it is the “direction” that a neuron looks for in its input.

The **angle** $\theta_{ij}$ between them is defined by the inner product:
$$
\cos \theta_{ij}
   = \frac{\langle w_i , w_j\rangle}{\|w_i\|\,\|w_j\|}
   = \frac{w_i^{\top} w_j}{\|w_i\|\;\|w_j\|}.
\tag{1}
$$
Thus, preserving an angle is equivalent to preserving the *cosine similarity* of every pair of columns.

---

### 2. What an orthogonal matrix does

An orthogonal matrix represents a rotation (or a rotation followed by a reflection) of the whole space. Formally, for any vector $v\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$,

* **Lengths are preserved:** $\|Rv\| = \|v\|$.
* **Inner products are preserved:** $\langle Rv, Ru\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle.$

Both properties follow directly from $R^{\top}R = I_d$:
$$
(Rv)^{\top}(Ru)= v^{\top}R^{\top}Ru = v^{\top}u .
\tag{2}
$$

Because an orthogonal map is *linear* and preserves inner products, it maps every vector into another vector with exactly the same length and the same mutual angles as before – it merely changes the coordinate frame.

---

### 3. Applying the orthogonal matrix to all columns simultaneously

When we replace $W^{0}$ by $RW^{0}$, each column is transformed in exactly the same way:
$$
(RW^{0})_{:,i}= R w_i , \qquad
(RW^{0})_{:,j}= R w_j .
$$

Now compute the cosine similarity of the transformed columns:
$$
\cos \widetilde{\theta}_{ij}
   = \frac{(Rw_i)^{\top}(Rw_j)}{\|Rw_i\|\;\|Rw_j\|}
   = \frac{w_i^{\top}R^{\top}Rw_j}{\|w_i\|\;\|w_j|}
   = \frac{w_i^{\top} w_j}{\|w_i\|\;\|w_j|}
   = \cos \theta_{ij}.
\tag{3}
$$

The numerator uses (2) to cancel out $R^{\top}R=I_d,$ and the denominators are unchanged because orthogonal maps preserve norms. Consequently every pairwise angle stays exactly the same after applying $R.$

In matrix notation this can be seen as
$$
(RW^{0})^{T}(RW^{0}) = (W^{0})^{T}R^{T}RW^{0}= (W^{0})^{T}W^{0},
$$
so the Gram matrix of column inner products is invariant. Since angles are completely determined by this Gram matrix, they are invariant as well.

---

### 4. Intuitive picture

Imagine you have a set of arrows drawn on a piece of paper representing neurons. An orthogonal transformation is like picking up the paper and rotating it (or flipping it). The relative directions between any two arrows do not change – they still point at the same angle with respect to each other. Because OFT uses *the same* rotation for **all** columns of a layer, every neuron “moves together” as a rigid body, preserving their configuration.

If instead we applied different rotations to different columns, angles could change; this is why OFT restricts itself to a *single* shared orthogonal matrix per layer.

---

### 5. Why this matters for finetuning

The pretrained model has learned useful relational structure among its neurons: similar neurons tend to have small angles, dissimilar ones large angles, etc. By keeping those angles unchanged, OFT guarantees that any downstream task can only modify *how* these pre‑existing directions are combined with the input (through $R^\top x.$) while retaining the geometric information encoded during pretraining. This explains why OFT often shows better stability and generalisation compared with unconstrained finetuning or additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA.

---

**Summary**

An orthogonal matrix preserves inner products and norms; consequently it leaves all pairwise cosine similarities – and therefore all angles – between the columns of a weight matrix unchanged. In OFT every neuron (column) is transformed by exactly the same orthogonal matrix, so their geometric configuration is rigidly rotated but not distorted, which is precisely what the paper refers to when it says “pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved.”

### How is the Cayley transform used to parameterize orthogonal matrices in BOFT?
Question:
Both OFT and BOFT enforce orthogonality via the Cayley transform, i.e., \(R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^{-1}\) with a skew‑symmetric \(Q\). I am confused about (1) why this guarantees \(R\) is orthogonal, (2) how gradients are back‑propagated through the inverse, and (3) whether any numerical stability issues arise when \(I - Q\) is close to singular. Could anyone walk through the derivation and practical considerations?
Answer:
The Cayley transform is the core “orthogonal‑ness guarantee’’ that both OFT and BOFT inherit from the earlier orthogonal‑finetuning work [Qiu et al., 2023].  
Below we walk through why the map  

\[
R(Q)= (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},\qquad Q^\top=-Q,
\]

produces an orthogonal matrix, how the autograd chain‑rule deals with the matrix inverse, and what practical tricks the authors use to keep the computation stable when \(I-Q\) becomes ill‑conditioned.

---

### 1.  Why does the Cayley transform produce an orthogonal matrix?

Take a skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q\) (\(Q^\top=-Q\)).  Consider the product  

\[
R^\top R
 = \bigl((I-Q)^{-\top}(I+Q)^\top\bigr)\bigl((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\bigr).
\]

Because \(Q^\top =-Q\),

\[
(I+Q)^\top = I+Q^\top = I-Q,
\qquad
(I-Q)^{-\top}= \bigl((I-Q)^\top\bigr)^{-1}= (I+Q)^{-1}.
\]

Hence  

\[
R^\top R = (I+Q)^{-1}(I-Q)(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}= I .
\]

So \(R^\top R = I\), i.e. \(R\) is orthogonal.  The derivation uses only the algebraic fact that a skew‑symmetric matrix satisfies \(Q^\top=-Q\); no other property of \(Q\) is required, which is why any choice of trainable parameters that are forced to be skew‑symmetric yields a valid orthogonal transformation.

The paper enforces skew‑symmetry by parameterising the independent entries of \(Q\) (the strictly upper‑triangular part) as free variables and setting  

\[
Q = \tilde Q - \tilde Q^{\!\top},
\]

where \(\tilde Q\) contains the learnable scalars.  This guarantees that during optimisation the constraint is never violated.

---

### 2.  Back‑propagation through the inverse

Automatic differentiators (PyTorch, JAX, etc.) expose a derivative for the matrix inverse:

\[
\frac{\partial\, \operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}B)}{\partial A}
   = -A^{-\!\top} B^{\!\top} A^{-\!\top},
\]

or, in more convenient tensor notation,

\[
d(A^{-1}) = -A^{-1}\,(dA)\,A^{-1}.
\]

Applying this to \(R(Q)\),

\[
R(Q)= (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
\]
the differential is

\[
dR = dQ\, (I-Q)^{-1}
     + (I+Q)\, d[(I-Q)^{-1}]
   = dQ\, (I-Q)^{-1}
     - (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} dQ (I-Q)^{-1}.
\]

Collecting terms,

\[
dR = \bigl[I - R(Q)\bigr]\, (I-Q)^{-1} \, dQ .
\]

During back‑propagation the framework automatically computes this Jacobian by chaining the derivative of the inverse with the standard product rule, so there is no need for a hand‑crafted gradient.  In practice the authors simply write

```python
R = (I + Q) @ torch.inverse(I - Q)
```

and let autograd handle the rest.  Because each factor in BOFT is a *small* orthogonal block (2×2 or \(b\times b\)), the involved inverses are cheap and numerically well behaved.

---

### 3.  Numerical stability when \(I-Q\) ≈ singular

The inverse exists as long as \(-1\) is not an eigenvalue of \(Q\).  For a skew‑symmetric matrix all eigenvalues are purely imaginary (\(\lambda = i\, \mu,\; \mu \in \mathbb{R}\)), therefore \(-1\) can never be an eigenvalue of \(Q\).  Consequently \(I-Q\) is *always* nonsingular in exact arithmetic.

In finite‑precision arithmetic two issues can arise:

* **Nearly singular denominator** – if an eigenvalue of \(Q\) is close to \(i\,(-1)\), then some diagonal entry of \(I-Q\) gets close to zero and the condition number blows up.  
* **Overflow/underflow** – large entries in \((I-Q)^{-1}\) can amplify rounding errors.

The paper mitigates these risks in three ways that are common in Cayley‑based parameterisations:

1. **Zero‑initialisation of the skew part**  
   At training start \(Q=0$, so \(R=I.\)
   Because optimisation proceeds with small learning rates, \(\|Q\|_F\) stays modest for many iterations, keeping all eigenvalues far from \(-i.\)

2. **Clipping of the raw parameters**  
   The authors bound the magnitude of each independent entry of \(\tilde Q.\) Practically they apply `torch.clamp` or `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_` after each update so that \(\|Q\|_2 < c< 1.\)

3. **Using a stable linear solve instead of explicit inversion**  
   In code they compute
   ```python
   R = torch.linalg.solve(I - Q, I + Q)
   ```
   which solves \((I-Q)x = (I+Q)\).  Solvers based on LU decomposition are more robust than forming \((I-Q)^{-1}\) explicitly and they share the same gradient implementation in PyTorch.

Empirically, BOFT’s training curves reported in §5 show no sign of divergence due to instability; indeed BOFT enjoys *better* training stability than LoRA because orthogonal updates preserve spectral norms.

---

### Putting it together for BOFT

Each butterfly component \(\tilde B^{b}_{(d,i)}\) is built from many independent \(2b\times2b\) blocks.  
For every block we form a skew‑symmetric matrix,
apply the Cayley transform to obtain an orthogonal block,
assemble them into a block‑diagonal matrix,
and finally multiply all components:

```
R = I
for i in range(m):
    Q_i = make_skew_symmetric(params_i)
    B_i = solve(I - Q_i, I + Q_i)      # ← stable Cayley
    R   = B_i @ R
```

Because every block is orthogonal, their product remains orthogonal.
The gradients flow through each `solve` call using the inverse derivative shown above; with modest block size (\(b=2$ or $4$ in most experiments), both forward and backward passes are cheap (\(\mathcal{O}(d b)\)) while still yielding a dense orthogonal matrix after multiplication.

In summary:

* The Cayley formula maps any skew‑symmetric matrix to an orthogonal one; this algebraic guarantee underpins both OFT and BOFT.
* Autograd handles the matrix inverse via the standard identity \(dA^{-1}=-A^{-1}(dA)A^{-1}\); no special manual gradients are required.
* Numerical pathologies are avoided because skew‑symmetry prevents true singularities, and practical implementations use small initialisation, parameter clipping, and linear solves rather than explicit inverses.

These design choices make BOFT both mathematically sound and practically stable for large‑scale finetuning of vision transformers, language models and diffusion models as demonstrated in the paper.

### What exactly is a "butterfly factor" \(B^F(k)\) and how does it relate to the classic FFT butterfly diagram?
Question:
Equation (2) defines a butterfly factor as a block matrix with four diagonal blocks built from vectors \(d_1, d_2, d_3, d_4\). I am familiar with the FFT butterfly where each stage mixes pairs of elements using a 2×2 rotation. How should I interpret the diagonal blocks in \(B^F(k)\)? Are they essentially independent 2×2 rotations applied to disjoint groups of coordinates, or is there a more subtle coupling? A concrete example for \(k=4\) would help.
Answer:
The matrix that the authors call a **butterfly factor** is just a compact way of writing a very sparse orthogonal building‑block that appears in every stage of the Cooley‑Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT).  
Recall the elementary “FFT‑butterfly’’ that mixes two signal samples  

\[
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\ x_2
\end{bmatrix}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1 & c_2\\
c_3 & c_4
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1\\ x_2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 
\begin{bmatrix}
c_1 & c_2\\
c_3 & c_4
\end{bmatrix}\in {\rm O}(2).
\]

If we want to apply the same 2‑point mixing to many *disjoint* pairs of coordinates in a vector of length \(k\), we place many copies of the 2×2 matrix along the diagonal of a \(k\times k\) matrix. The authors generalise this idea by allowing each copy to have *different* diagonal scalings on its four entries.  

Formally, for an even integer \(k=2s\) they define  

\[
B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(d_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 
d_{i}\in \mathbb R^{s}.
\]

The four vectors \(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4}\) each contain \(s=k/2\) numbers.  The block‑matrix can be visualised as a collection of \(s\) independent 2×2 blocks

\[
B^{F}(k)=
\operatorname{blkdiag}\Bigl(
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
(d_{1})_{j} & (d_{2})_{j}\\[2pt]
(d_{3})_{j} & (d_{4})_{j}
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{the }j^{\text{th}}\text{ butterfly}},\,j=1,\dots ,s
\Bigr).
\]

Thus each row‑pair \((j,\, j+s)\) of the full matrix interacts only with the corresponding column‑pair \((j,\, j+s)\); all other entries are zero.  When the four scalars in a block satisfy the orthogonality constraints (for instance, the block can be written as a rotation matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}\cos \theta_j & -\sin \theta_j\\ \sin \theta_j & \cos \theta_j\end{bmatrix}\)), the whole factor \(B^{F}(k)\) is orthogonal because it is a block‑diagonal composition of orthogonal blocks.

---

### Concrete example for \(k=4\)

Take \(k=4\) so that \(s=k/2=2\).  Choose two scalar pairs for each block:

*Block 1* (indices 1 and 3):  
\(d_{1}^{(1)} = a,\; d_{2}^{(1)} = b,\; d_{3}^{(1)} = c,\; d_{4}^{(1)} = d.\)

*Block 2* (indices 2 and 4):  
\(d_{1}^{(2)} = e,\; d_{2}^{(2)} = f,\; d_{3}^{(2)} = g,\; d_{4}^{(2)} = h.\)

Collecting them into vectors,  
\(d_1=[a,e]^{\top},\, d_2=[b,f]^{\top},\, d_3=[c,g]^{\top},\, d_4=[d,h]^{\top}.\)

Then

\[
B^{F}(4)=
\begin{bmatrix}
a & 0 & b & 0\\[2pt]
0 & e & 0 & f\\[2pt]
c & 0 & d & 0\\[2pt]
0 & g & 0 & h
\end{bmatrix}
=
\operatorname{blkdiag}\!\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
a&b\\c&d
\end{bmatrix},
~
\begin{bmatrix}
e&f\\g&h
\end{bmatrix}
\right).
\]

If we set each 2×2 block to be a rotation,
\(a=\cos \theta_1,\; b=-\sin \theta_1,\; c=\sin \theta_1,\; d=\cos \theta_1,\)
and similarly for the second block with angle \(\theta_2,\) then  

\(B^{F}(4)= {\rm blkdiag}\bigl(R(\theta_1),R(\theta_2)\bigr)\)

is an orthogonal matrix that applies two independent rotations, one on coordinates (1,3) and one on coordinates (2,4).  

These two rotations are **exactly** what you see in the traditional FFT diagram: each stage “splits’’ the vector into pairs, mixes each pair by a 2×2 rotation (often multiplied by a complex twiddle factor), and then passes the result to the next stage. The only difference is that in the paper the authors keep all four diagonal vectors as independent trainable parameters, allowing any orthogonal 2×2 block rather than a fixed complex exponential.

---

### Relation to the FFT butterfly diagram

In the radix‑2 Cooley‑Tukey FFT an input of length \(k=2^L\) is processed by \(L=\log_2 k\) stages.  
At stage \(l\) every element is paired with another element at distance \(2^{l-1}\); the pairing pattern is exactly what the permutation of diagonal blocks in successive butterfly components implements.  

If we write the whole FFT as a product of sparse matrices,
\(F = B_L B_{L-1}\dots B_1,\)
each factor \(B_\ell\) has exactly the structure of \(B^{F}(k)\) (or a permuted version thereof): it consists of \(k/2\) independent 2×2 rotations placed on disjoint coordinate pairs. Multiplying these factors propagates information across all coordinates, because after \(L=\log_2 k\) stages any input entry has been mixed with every other entry – this is precisely the “dense connectivity’’ property that the paper’s information‑transmission view requires.

Consequently, **a butterfly factor \(B^{F}(k)\) is not a single global rotation**; it is a collection of independent 2×2 rotations applied to disjoint pairs. When several such factors are stacked (with appropriate permutations between them), the overall product becomes dense and can represent any orthogonal matrix up to approximation error, while each individual factor remains extremely cheap to store (\(\mathcal O(k)\) parameters instead of \(\mathcal O(k^2)\)).

So, to answer your question:  
*the diagonal blocks in \(B^{F}(k)\) are indeed independent 2× 2 orthogonal transformations acting on separate coordinate pairs.* The coupling across all coordinates emerges only after several butterfly factors are multiplied together, exactly as in the classic FFT butterfly diagram.

### Why does the information‑transmission view require at least \(d\) non‑zero edges per sparse factor?
Question:
The paper argues that each sparse matrix \(B_i\) must have at least \(d\) edges to be full‑rank, which is a prerequisite for orthogonality. However, I don't see why full‑rank forces exactly \(d\) non‑zeros rather than, say, \(d+1\) or more. Could someone clarify the relationship between sparsity pattern, rank, and the orthogonality constraint in this graph interpretation?
Answer:
In the information‑transmission picture each factor \(\mathbf B_i\) is interpreted as a bipartite graph that connects the \(d\) nodes on level \(i\) (the “source” side) to the \(d\) nodes on level \(i+1\) (the “target’’ side).  
A factor can be used as a **bijection** – i.e. every source node must be matched to exactly one target node – only when it is **full‑rank**. Full‑rank is a necessary condition for orthogonality because an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf B_i\) satisfies \(\mathbf B_i^{\!\top}\mathbf B_i=\mathbf I_d\); consequently \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf B_i)=d\).

Now ask: how many edges (non‑zero entries) are needed for a \(d\times d\) matrix to be full‑rank?  
If a column (or a row) were all zeros, the rank would drop below \(d\). More generally, any set of columns that together contain fewer than \(d\) non‑zero positions cannot span \(\mathbb R^{d}\). The *minimum* number of non‑zeros that guarantees the possibility of full rank is therefore exactly \(d\): we may place a single non‑zero in each column (and, by symmetry, in each row). Such a pattern is a **perfect matching** in the bipartite graph and corresponds to a permutation matrix (or, after multiplying each selected entry by \(\pm 1\), to an orthogonal matrix with only one non‑zero per row and column).  

If we added more than \(d\) edges we would still be able to obtain a full‑rank matrix, but those extra edges would be *redundant* from the point of view of rank – they increase the number of trainable parameters without improving the basic feasibility of orthogonality. The goal of the paper’s “minimum free edges’’ desideratum is precisely to avoid this redundancy: keep the graph as sparse as possible while still allowing each factor to be orthogonal.

Hence the statement in §3:

> “For each matrix \(\mathbf B_i\) to be full‑rank (a necessary condition of orthogonality), we need to have \(d\) edges to form a bijection between all the nodes …”

is an expression of the combinatorial fact that a bipartite graph admitting a perfect matching must contain at least \(d\) edges, and that a perfect matching is sufficient for a \(d\times d\) matrix to be orthogonal (by assigning \(\pm 1\) or any unit‑norm scalar to the matched entries).  

In summary, full rank forces **at least** \(d\) non‑zero entries per sparse factor because fewer than \(d\) edges cannot give a bijective mapping between the two sides of the graph; having exactly \(d\) edges yields the sparsest possible pattern that still permits orthogonality. Adding extra edges (\(d+1,\ d+2,\dots\)) is allowed but unnecessarily increases parameter count, which contradicts the information‑transmission objective of using as few trainable parameters as possible.

### How does BOFT achieve dense connectivity with only \(O(d \log d)\) trainable parameters?
Question:
The authors claim that a product of \(\log d\) sparse orthogonal matrices each with \(O(d)\) non‑zeros yields a dense orthogonal matrix, analogous to the butterfly network in FFT. I would like a step‑by‑step illustration (perhaps for \(d=8\)) showing how information from any source node reaches any destination node after the sequence of butterfly layers, and how many trainable parameters are actually needed per layer.
Answer:
In BOFT a dense orthogonal matrix $R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is not stored directly.  
Instead it is written as a product of $\log _2 d$ very sparse orthogonal factors

$$
R=\tilde B_{(d,2)}^{(1)}\;\tilde B_{(d,4)}^{(2)}\;\tilde B_{(d,8)}^{(3)}\;\cdots ,
\qquad d=2^{N},\; N=\log _2 d .
$$

Each factor $\tilde B_{(d,k)}$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are **butterfly factors**
$B^{F}(k)$ of size $k\times k$.  A butterfly factor contains only
$k$ non‑zero entries – one entry in each row and each column – and can be
parameterised by a single $(k/2)$-dimensional orthogonal matrix (or,
equivalently, by a Cayley‑transformed skew‑symmetric matrix).  
Consequently every factor contributes only $\frac{k}{2}\,d/k = \frac{d}{2}$
trainable scalars.

Below we illustrate the whole construction for the smallest non‑trivial
size $d=8\;(N=3)$.  The three factors are

* **Level 1** – block size $k_1=2$ :   $\tilde B_{(8,2)}=
  \operatorname{diag}\big(B^{F}_1(2),B^{F}_2(2),B^{F}_3(2),B^{F}_4(2)\big)$;
* **Level 2** – block size $k_2=4$ :   $\tilde B_{(8,4)}=
  \operatorname{diag}\big(B^{F}_1(4),B^{F}_2(4)\big)$;
* **Level 3** – block size $k_3=8$ :   $\tilde B_{(8,8)} = B^{F}_1(8)$.

---

### 1.  Information flow on the $8\times 4$ grid  

Think of the multiplication $R = \tilde B_{(8,8)}\,\tilde B_{(8,4)}\,\tilde B_{(8,2)}$
as a layered graph (Fig. 1 in the paper).  
The graph has four vertical levels:  

```
level 0 (inputs)   :  x1 … x8
level 1 (after  B2):  y1 … y8
level 2 (after  B4):  z1 … z8
level 3 (after  B8):  w1 … w8   (= output)
```

An edge exists from node $j$ at level $\ell-1$ to node $i$ at level
$\ell$ iff the corresponding entry of the factor $\tilde B_{\ell}$ is non‑zero.
Because each factor is a permutation of a set of disjoint $2\times2$
butterflies, every node has exactly one outgoing and one incoming edge at
each level.

* **Level 1 ($\tilde B_{(8,2)}$)** pairs the eight indices as
  $(1,2),(3,4),(5,6),(7,8)$.  
  Within each pair the two values are mixed by a $2\times2$ orthogonal block,
  e.g.  

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
    c_1 & -s_1\\
    s_1 & \phantom{-}c_1
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad c_1^2+s_1^2=1 .
$$

* **Level 2 ($\tilde B_{(8,4)}$)** groups the indices into blocks of four:
  $(1,2,3,4)$ and $(5,6,7,8)$.  
  Inside each block the two “butterfly” pairs from level 1 are crossed:
  edges go from (1↔3) and (2↔4). In matrix form this is

$$
B^{F}(4)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
      d_{11}&0&d_{12}&0\\[0.3ex]
      0&d_{21}&0&d_{22}\\[0.3ex]
      d_{31}&0&d_{32}&0\\[0.3ex]
      0&d_{41}&0&d_{42}
    \end{bmatrix},
$$

where each column contains exactly one non‑zero entry; the four columns
together implement an orthogonal permutation of the four inputs.

* **Level 3 ($\tilde B_{(8,8)}$)** finally mixes the two four‑element groups.
  Its non‑zero pattern connects node $i$ with node $i+4$ for all $i=1,\dots ,4$
  (and symmetrically for $i=5,\dots ,8$). This is again a single
  butterfly factor of size eight.

After three layers every original input node can reach every output node by
following a unique path that picks one edge at each level. For example,
the path from input $x_1$ to output $w_6$ is:

```
x1 ─► y2 (level‑1 butterfly)
     ─► z5 (level‑2 crossing)
     ─► w6 (level‑3 mixing)
```

Because each level is a perfect matching between the two halves of the
current vector, the composition implements a *complete bipartite*
connectivity: the product matrix $R$ is dense (all eight rows have non‑zero
entries in all eight columns).

---

### 2. How many trainable parameters?

A single butterfly factor of size $k$ contains exactly $k/2$ independent
orthogonal degrees of freedom:

* each $k/2\times k/2$ diagonal vector $\mathbf d_i$ in (3) can be written as
  cosine/sine pairs that obey orthogonality,
* after Cayley transformation a skew‑symmetric matrix of size $k/2\times k/2$
  supplies precisely those $(k/2)(k/2-1)/2 = O(k^2)$ parameters,
* however in BOFT we *freeze* all entries that must be $\pm1$ to satisfy the
  row/column permutation constraint; only the *rotational* part remains.
The paper counts an “effective” trainable scalar for each non‑trivial edge,
which for a factor with block size $k$ equals

$$
\frac{k}{2}\times \frac{d}{k}= \frac{d}{2}.
$$

Thus **every layer costs exactly $\tfrac{d}{2}$ parameters**, independent of
the block size. For $d=8$,

* Level 1 ($k=2$) : $8/2 = 4$ scalars,
* Level 2 ($k=4$) : another $4$,
* Level 3 ($k=8$) : another $4$.

The whole orthogonal matrix therefore uses  
$m\,d/2 = (\log _{2} d)\times d/2 = 3\times4 =12$ trainable numbers.
In big‑O notation this is

$$
O(d\,\log d),
$$

as claimed in the paper. By contrast a completely dense orthogonal matrix would need
$d(d-1)/2 = O(d^{\,2})$ free parameters.

---

### 3. Summary

For an eight‑dimensional layer BOFT builds three sparse orthogonal matrices,
each formed by independent $2\times 2$, $4\times4$, and $8\times8$
butterfly factors. The three layers together realise a dense orthogonal transform:
every input coordinate can travel to every output coordinate via exactly one path
through the three “butterfly” graphs. Because each factor contributes only
$d/2 =4$ trainable scalars, the total number of learnable parameters scales as
$O(d\,\log d)$. The same construction works for any power‑of‑two dimension,
explaining how BOFT achieves dense connectivity with only logarithmically many
more parameters than a naïve block‑diagonal OFT.

### What is the difference between BOFT($m$, $b$) and the original OFT when $m=1$?
Question:
The paper says BOFT($1$, $b$) reduces to block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$, while BOFT($1$, $d$) equals full OFT. I'm trying to reconcile this with the definition of butterfly components. Does $m=1$ simply mean we have a single block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are themselves orthogonal matrices of size $b$? How does this differ from the block‑diagonal structure used in OFT’s original formulation?
Answer:
In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that is applied to a frozen weight matrix is built as the product of **\(m\) butterfly components**  

\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
\]

where each component \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks have size \(b\).  
The parameter \(b\) therefore determines how many rows/columns are coupled inside a single block, while \(m\) tells us how many such block‑diagonal factors are multiplied together.

---

### What happens when \(m=1\)?

If we set \(m=1\) we have only **one** factor:

\[
\mathbf R(1,b)=\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)} .
\]

By construction \(\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}\) is precisely a block‑diagonal matrix whose diagonal consists of orthogonal sub‑matrices  

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}=
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf Q_{1},\mathbf Q_{2},\dots ,\mathbf Q_{d/b}\bigr), \qquad 
\mathbf Q_{i}\in \mathbb R^{b\times b}, \; \mathbf Q_{i}^{\!\top}\mathbf Q_{i}= \mathbf I_{b}.
\]

Thus the trainable parameters are exactly the entries of the \(d/b\) orthogonal blocks. This is **the same object that original OFT calls “block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b\)”**: the neurons are split into groups of \(b\) dimensions and each group is rotated independently by an orthogonal matrix.

Consequently, for \(m=1\)

* **BOFT(1,\(b\)) ≡ block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b\).**  
* The only difference is not in the mathematical form but in terminology – BOFT simply views the single block‑diagonal factor as a *butterfly component* with depth \(m=1\).

---

### Why does BOFT(1,\(d\)) give the full OFT?

When the block size equals the full dimension, i.e. \(b=d\), the diagonal matrix collapses to a single block:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,d}_{(d,1)} = \mathbf Q,\qquad 
\mathbf Q^{\!\top}\mathbf Q=\mathbf I_{d},
\]

so there is only one orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\). This recovers exactly the **original OFT** formulation that learns an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix.

---

### Relation to the original OFT’s block‑diagonal structure

The original OFT introduced a *block‑diagonal* orthogonal matrix **by hand** in order to cut down the number of trainable parameters:  

\[
\mathbf R_{\text{OFT}}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots , \mathbf R_{r}), \quad
r = d/b .
\]

BOFT does not alter that pattern when \(m=1\); it merely re‑interprets the same pattern as a *single* butterfly component. The “butterfly” terminology becomes relevant only when we stack several such components (\(m>1\)). With multiple components the sparsity pattern ceases to be purely block‑diagonal and instead follows the classic Cooley–Tukey butterfly graph, which yields dense connectivity while still using only \(\mathcal O(d\,m\, (b-1)/2)\) trainable parameters.

---

### Summarising answer

*Setting \(m=1\) means we have exactly one butterfly component, which is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are orthogonal of size \(b\times b.\)*  
Therefore:

* **BOFT(1,\(b\)) = the block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b.\)**  
* **BOFT(1,\(d\)) = full (unconstrained) OFT**, because the single block now has size \(d\times d.\)

So “\(m=1\)” does not introduce any new structure beyond what OFT already used; it merely frames the same structure within the more general butterfly‑factorization view that later allows us to obtain dense orthogonal matrices by increasing \(m\).

### Can you explain Theorem 1: why multiplying several butterfly matrices can approximate any orthogonal matrix?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size and that a product of specific butterfly matrices can approximate all orthogonal matrices of size $d$. I am unsure what the notation $B_{i,j}(d)$ denotes and why alternating transposes are needed. Also, how does this construction compare to known universal factorization results for orthogonal groups (e.g., Givens rotations)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Theorem 1 the authors claim that a product of a few *butterfly* matrices can generate *any* orthogonal matrix of size $d$.  
Below I unpack the notation, explain why the transposes appear, and relate the construction to the classical Givens‑rotation factorisation of the orthogonal group.

---

### 1.  What does $B_{i,j}(d)$ mean?

A **butterfly matrix** $B(d)$ is a sparse orthogonal matrix that is built from a hierarchy of $2\times2$ “butterfly factors’’ (eq. (1) in the paper).  
The hierarchy has $\log_2 d$ levels; at level $\ell$ the matrix consists of $d/2^{\ell}$ identical blocks of size $2^{\ell}\times2^{\ell}$.  

The index pair $(i,j)$ in $B_{i,j}(d)$ simply selects a *particular* butterfly matrix from a *family* of such matrices.  
Concretely,

* for each level $\ell\in\{1,\dots,\log_2 d\}$ we can instantiate a butterfly matrix by choosing a set of parameters (the diagonal vectors $\mathbf d_1,\dots,\mathbf d_4$ that appear in each factor).  
* The superscript $(i,j)$ enumerates those independent choices.  Think of it as an “address’’ telling us which *stage* ($i$) and which *instance* within that stage ($j$) we are using.

Thus $B_{i,j}(d)\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal butterfly matrix whose non‑zero pattern is fixed (the butterfly pattern), while its actual numerical entries are free parameters.

---

### 2.  Why do we multiply alternating **transposes**?

A single butterfly matrix is orthogonal, i.e. $B^\top B = I$.  
If we take only products of the form
\[
B_{i_1, j_1}(d)\,B_{i_2, j_2}(d)\,\cdots\,B_{i_T, j_T}(d)
\]
the resulting set of reachable matrices is **not** dense in the orthogonal group. The reason is that each butterfly matrix preserves a specific block‑permutation structure; multiplying two of them without any further operation can only explore a subgroup.

A transpose flips the permutation pattern of a butterfly factor: if a butterfly factor exchanges pairs $(p,q)$, its transpose exchanges the same pairs but in the opposite direction. Consequently, by inserting transposes between factors we obtain *both* directions of every elementary exchange that appears at every level of the hierarchy.  

Mathematically, the set
\[
\bigl\{\, B_{i_1,j_1}(d)\,B_{i_1,j_2}^\top(d)\,
          B_{i_2,j_3}(d)\,B_{i_2,j_4}^\top(d)\,\dots\bigr\}
\]
contains, for each level, both a permutation and its inverse. This gives us the ability to realise **any** elementary Givens rotation (a rotation in a two‑dimensional subspace) after an appropriate re‑ordering of coordinates. Since the ordinary Givens‑rotation factorisation says that any orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of such 2‑dimensional rotations, the alternating transpose construction inherits its universality.

In short: alternating transposes supply the missing inverses so that the generated subgroup contains all elementary 2‑dimensional rotations after suitable coordinate permutations; therefore their products span the whole orthogonal group.

---

### 3.  Connection to classical universal factorizations

The classic result for $\mathrm O(d)$ is

> **Givens decomposition** – every orthogonal matrix can be written as a product of $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ planar rotations,
> i.e. matrices that act as a rotation on a chosen pair of coordinates and as identity elsewhere.

The butterfly construction mirrors this decomposition but groups many planar rotations together in each sparse factor:

* A butterfly factor with block size $k=2^r$ contains exactly $k/2$ independent $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks (each one is a Givens rotation after an appropriate basis change).  
* By recursively applying smaller‑block butterflies ($k=2,4,8,\dots,d$) we obtain all possible pairs of coordinates across all levels—in exactly the same way that the Cooley–Tukey FFT mixes low‑order bits with high‑order bits.

Thus Theorem 1 can be viewed as a **structured Givens decomposition:** instead of placing each rotation in its own elementary matrix, we pack many simultaneously into one sparse butterfly factor. The alternating transposes guarantee that every possible pair appears either in forward or backward direction, so no rotation is omitted.

Because each butterfly factor has only $\mathcal O(d)$ free parameters (the diagonal vectors inside its blocks), using $\log d$ such factors yields an overall parameter count of $\mathcal O(d\log d)$, which is far smaller than the $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ parameters required by an unrestricted orthogonal matrix yet still dense enough to approximate any element of $\mathrm O(d)$.

---

### 4.  Summary

* **$B_{i,j}(d)$** denotes one member of the family of size‑$d$ butterfly matrices; indices label different choices of parameters (different “layers’’ or “instances’’).  
* Multiplying them with alternating transposes supplies both forward and inverse permutation patterns; this gives access to all elementary planar rotations after suitable re‑ordering.  
* Consequently the product
  \[
  B_{d-1,1}(d)\,B_{d-1,2}^{\!\top}(d)\,
  \cdots\,B_{1,1}(d)\,B_{1,2}^{\!\top}(d)
  \]
  spans the whole orthogonal group, exactly like a Givens‑rotation factorisation but with far fewer trainable parameters because each factor is sparse (butterfly‑structured).

Hence Theorem 1 establishes that BOFT’s butterfly parameterisation is **universally expressive** while remaining dramatically more parameter‑efficient than plain OFT or an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix.

### What is "multiplicative Dropout" in BOFT and why doesn’t standard Dropout work?
Question:
The authors propose a multiplicative Dropout that randomly replaces selected butterfly components or their diagonal blocks by identity matrices. My question is: (a) why would zeroing out parts of an additive update (as done in LoRA) be inappropriate for a multiplicative orthogonal update; (b) how is the dropout mask applied during forward/backward passes; and (c) does this affect the orthogonality guarantee of the remaining components?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the trainable “weight” of a linear layer is not added to the pretrained matrix as in LoRA, but **multiplied** on the left by an orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
$$

Each factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a sparse, orthogonal butterfly component.  
Because the update is *multiplicative* and the whole product must stay orthogonal, the way we regularise (e.g. with dropout) has to respect this structure. The authors therefore introduce **multiplicative dropout**, which works quite differently from the ordinary additive dropout used in LoRA.

---

### (a) Why zero‑out does not make sense for a multiplicative orthogonal update  

In LoRA the finetuned weight is

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \Delta\! \mathbf{W},\qquad 
\Delta\! \mathbf{W}= \mathbf A\mathbf B,
$$

so setting some entries of $\Delta\! \mathbf{W}$ to zero simply means “do not use that low‑rank correction”.  The pretrained matrix remains untouched; the additive nature guarantees that any subset of the low‑rank factors can be dropped without breaking the definition of the model.

In BOFT the correction is *inside* a product:

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = (\underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m)}}_{\text{component }m}
        \cdots 
        \underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}}_{\text{component }1})\,\mathbf{W}^{0}.
$$

If we were to zero out an entry of a factor, the factor would no longer be orthogonal (its rows would cease to be a basis) and the whole product would cease to be a pure rotation/reflection.  Moreover, an orthogonal matrix cannot be expressed as “identity plus a sparse correction” – it must satisfy  
$\mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I$.  Consequently, dropping entries **additively** would violate this constraint and destroy the very property (preservation of angles and singular values) that OFT/BOFT is built upon.  For that reason we cannot simply apply standard dropout on the entries of $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$.

---

### (b) How multiplicative dropout is applied  

The dropout operates **at the level of whole butterfly components and at the level of their diagonal blocks**, not on individual scalar entries.

1. **Component‑wise mask** – With probability $p_{1}$ a whole factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is selected.  When it is selected, every block inside that component is replaced by an identity block, i.e. the entire factor becomes an identity matrix:
   $$
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \;\longleftarrow\; \mathbf I_{d}.
   $$

2. **Block‑wise mask** – Independently, for each factor we draw a second mask that selects a fraction $p_{2}$ of its diagonal blocks (each block has size $b\times b$).  The chosen blocks are likewise set to identity matrices while the remaining blocks keep their learned orthogonal parameterisation.

During **the forward pass** we first construct the masked product
$$
\widehat{\mathbf R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m} \widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
$$
where each $\widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is either the original learned component or an identity matrix according to the two masks.  The output of the layer is then
$$
\mathbf z = (\widehat{\mathbf R}\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x .
$$

During **back‑propagation** gradients flow only through those components and blocks that were *not* replaced by identity; components that were turned into identity are treated as constants and receive zero gradient for that step.  The masks are sampled anew for every training mini‑batch (or every iteration), exactly as in standard dropout, so over training time each component learns to be useful even though it may be temporarily omitted.

---

### (c) Does this break the orthogonality guarantee?  

No.  The product of orthogonal matrices is again orthogonal, and an identity matrix is trivially orthogonal.  Because multiplicative dropout only **replaces entire factors or blocks with identity matrices**, every factor that participates in a forward pass remains orthogonal by construction:

* The learned blocks are parameterised via Cayley transforms (or explicit $2\times2$ rotations), guaranteeing orthogonality.
* When a block is replaced by an identity, orthogonality is preserved because $\mathbf I^{\top}\mathbf I = \mathbf I$.

Therefore, after applying the masks we still have  

$$
\widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}{}^{\!\top}
    \widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} = \mathbf I_{d},
    \qquad
    i=1,\dots,m,
$$

and consequently  

$$
\widehat{\mathbf R}^{\!\top}\widehat{\mathbf R}= \mathbf I_{d}.
$$

The only effect of dropout is to **temporarily reduce** the expressive power of the current forward pass, which acts as a regulariser: different random subsets of butterfly components are forced to learn useful transformations because any one of them might be dropped later.  This mirrors how standard dropout prevents co‑adaptation of neurons, but it does so while respecting the multiplicative orthogonal structure required by BOFT.

---

#### Summary  

*Zeroing out individual entries would violate orthogonality; instead BOFT’s multiplicative dropout randomly substitutes whole butterfly components or their diagonal blocks with identities.*  
*During each forward/backward step the masked product of orthogonal factors is used, so orthogonality remains guaranteed.*  
*The procedure provides regularisation analogous to conventional dropout but compatible with BOFT’s multiplicative update.*

### How does BOFT preserve the spectral norm of pretrained weights compared to LoRA?
Question:
The paper mentions that multiplying an orthogonal matrix $R$ on the left preserves the singular values of $W^0$, whereas LoRA adds a low‑rank term that changes them. Could someone explicitly show that $\sigma_{max}(RW^0)=\sigma_{max}(W^0)$ and discuss whether any other singular values are also preserved? Additionally, does this property hold if $R$ is only approximately orthogonal due to finite precision?
Answer:
In Orthogonal‑finetuning (OFT) and its butterfly‑based variant (BOFT) the pretrained weight matrix  

$$
W^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
$$

is left‑multiplied by a trainable orthogonal matrix  

$$
R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\qquad R^{\top}R=RR^{\top}=I_{d}.
$$

The paper states that this operation “preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix”, i.e. the largest singular value does not change, whereas LoRA’s additive low‑rank update does.  
Below we spell out why *all* singular values are unchanged, not only the largest one, and we discuss what happens when $R$ is only approximately orthogonal because of finite‑precision arithmetic.

---

### Left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix

Take the singular‑value decomposition (SVD) of the frozen weight matrix  

$$
W^{0}=U\Sigma V^{\top},
$$

where $U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal and  

$$
\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r}), \qquad \sigma_{1}\ge \dots \ge\sigma_{r}>0,
$$

$r=\operatorname{rank}(W^{0})$.  
Now premultiply by $R$:

$$
RW^{0}=R\,U\,\Sigma\,V^{\top}.
$$

Since both $R$ and $U$ are orthogonal, their product is again orthogonal:

$$
(RU)^{\top}(RU)=U^{\top}R^{\top}RU=I_{d}.
$$

Thus we can write

$$
RW^{0}= \underbrace{(RU)}_{\tilde U}\;\Sigma\,V^{\top},
$$

with $\tilde U:=RU$ orthogonal. This is precisely an SVD of $RW^{0}$ because the singular values are exactly those on the diagonal of $\Sigma$; only the left singular vectors have been rotated from $U$ to $\tilde U = RU$. Consequently

* every singular value of $W^{0}$ is also a singular value of $RW^{0}$;
* the set of singular values (including multiplicities) is identical;
* in particular, the spectral norm $\sigma_{\max}(RW^{0})=\sigma_{\max}(W^{0})$.

So the statement in the paper is a direct consequence of the basic property  

> **If $R$ is orthogonal, then for any matrix $A$, $\sigma_i(RA)=\sigma_i(A)$ for all $i.$**

The same holds for right‑multiplication ($AR^\top = A(R^\top)$) because an orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean norms of vectors, and singular values are just norms of images of unit vectors.

---

### Comparison with LoRA

LoRA replaces the weight by  

$$
W_{\text{LoRA}} = W^{0}+UV^{\top},
$$

where $U,V^{T}$ have rank $r_{\text{LoRA}}$. The additive term changes the column space of $W^{0}$, and therefore generally perturbs all singular values. Only when $UV^{\top}=0$ (the trivial case) would they stay unchanged. Hence LoRA cannot guarantee preservation of any singular value, while OFT/BOFT provably keep the whole spectrum intact.

---

### Approximate orthogonality due to finite precision

In practice we store $R$ in floating‑point arithmetic, so after many updates it may deviate slightly from exact orthogonality:  

$$
R^{\top}R = I + E,\quad \|E\|_{2}= \epsilon,
$$  

with $\epsilon \ll 1.$

How does this affect the singular values?  
Let us write  

$$
RW^{0}= (I+ \Delta )\,U\,\Sigma V^{\top}, \quad \Delta = R-UU^{-1}R.
$$

Using standard perturbation results for singular values (e.g., Weyl’s inequality),

$$
|\sigma_i(RW^{0})-\sigma_i(W^{0})|
    \;\le \; \| (R-U)\,U\,\Sigma V^{\top} \|_{2}
    =  \| (R-U)\|_{2}\,\|\Sigma \|_{2}.
$$

Since $U^\top U=I,\; R=U(I+E')U^\top$, with $\|E'\|_{2}=O(\epsilon)$, we obtain  

$$
 |\sigma_i(RW^{0})-\sigma_i(W^{0})| = O(\epsilon\,\sigma_{\max}(W^{0})).
$$

In other words, a small deviation from orthogonality produces a proportionally small perturbation of each singular value. If double precision is used and training keeps the Cayley parametrisation well‑conditioned (the paper enforces exact orthogonality at every step), then $\epsilon$ is on the order of machine epsilon ($\sim10^{-16}$), far below any numerically relevant scale, and the spectral norm can be treated as perfectly preserved.

If one deliberately relaxes exact orthogonality—for example by using a soft penalty rather than Cayley parameterisation—the bound above still tells us that as long as $\|R^\top R-I_2|\|\,$ remains tiny, the singular‑value distortion will be negligible.

---

### Summary

*Multiplying a pretrained weight matrix on the left by an exact orthogonal matrix leaves **all** its singular values unchanged*. The proof follows immediately from the SVD: only the left singular vectors rotate, while the diagonal $\Sigma$ stays intact. Consequently BOFT inherits the same spectral‑norm preservation property as OFT, which LoRA lacks because it adds a low‑rank perturbation.

When using floating‑point numbers, any departure from perfect orthogonality introduces at most a proportional perturbation bounded by the norm of the deviation; with standard 32/64‑bit arithmetic and a parametrisation that enforces orthogonality (Cayley transform), this effect is negligible in practice.

### What are the practical trade‑offs between block‑diagonal OFT and butterfly BOFT in terms of runtime overhead?
Question:
The conclusion mentions that BOFT incurs slightly larger training runtime because it multiplies several sparse orthogonal matrices per forward pass. I would like quantitative insight: for a layer with dimension $d=1024$, how many FLOPs are added by using BOFT($\log d$, $2$) versus block‑diagonal OFT with block size $64$? Are there GPU‑friendly implementations or libraries that exploit the structure?
Answer:
**Answer**

Both block‑diagonal OFT and BOFT replace the ordinary linear layer  

\[
\mathbf{z}=({\mathbf W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x
\]

by  

\[
\mathbf{z}=({\mathbf R}{\mathbf W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x ,
\qquad \mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\text{ orthogonal}.
\]

The extra work comes from the multiplication of the *trainable* orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ with the frozen weight matrix ${\mathbf W}^{0}$.  
Below we count the FLOPs that have to be executed **per forward pass** for a single layer of dimension $d=1024$.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 1. Block‑diagonal OFT (single block‑diagonal matrix)

The orthogonal matrix is a block‑diagonal matrix

\[
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}), \qquad 
\mathbf R_{i}\in\mathbb R^{b\times b}, \; b=64,\; r=d/b=16 .
\]

Multiplying $\mathbf R$ by a dense input vector $\mathbf v={\mathbf W}^{0}\!\mathbf x$ therefore consists of $r$ independent dense
$b\times b$ matrix–vector products.  
Each dense product needs $2b^{2}$ FLOPs (one multiply and one add per entry). Hence

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{blk‑diag}}=
r \times 2b^{2}= \frac{d}{b}\times 2b^{2}=2db.
\]

Plugging in the numbers,

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{blk‑diag}} = 2\times1024\times64 = 131\,072 .
\]

If we count a multiply‑add as two FLOPs, this is the same as $65\,536$ *multiply‑adds*.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 2. BOFT $(m=\log_2 d,\,b=2)$

BOFT parameterises $\mathbf R$ as a product of $m=\log_2 d=10$ orthogonal butterfly components,

\[
\mathbf R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)} ,
\qquad 
|\operatorname{nnz}(\tilde{\mathbf B}_{(d,i)}) = d .
\]

Each component therefore contains exactly $d$ non‑zero entries; multiplying it by a dense vector costs $2d$ FLOPs (again a multiply‑add per non‑zero).  
The whole chain thus needs

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{BOFT}} = m \times 2d = (\log_2 d)\times 2d .
\]

For our concrete size,

\[
\text{FLOPs}_{\text{BOFT}} = 10\times 2\times1024 = 20\,480 .
\]

So the *theoretical* arithmetic cost of BOFT is **about three times smaller** than that of the block‑diagonal baseline.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 3. Why the paper observes “slightly larger training runtime”

The FLOP count tells only how many arithmetic operations are required; actual wall‑clock time on a GPU also depends on:

* **Kernel launch overhead** – BOFT performs ten separate sparse‑matrix–vector multiplications, each requiring its own CUDA kernel launch, whereas the block‑diagonal case can be fused into a single dense kernel.
* **Memory access pattern** – butterfly factors are highly irregular; even though they contain only $d=1024$ non‑zeros, they do not sit in contiguous memory like a standard dense block, so cache utilisation is lower.
* **Parallelism** – a dense $64\times64$ block fits nicely into a warp and can be executed with highly optimised GEMV kernels; the small $2\times2$ blocks used inside a butterfly component lead to under‑utilised SIMD lanes.

All these effects add a modest constant factor (often $<\,1.5\times$ on modern GPUs). In practice you typically see a **≈ 10 %–30 % increase** in training time for BOFT compared with the single block‑diagonal variant, which matches the authors’ remark that the overhead is “slightly larger”.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### 4. GPU‑friendly implementations

There are already publicly available kernels that exploit the butterfly pattern:

| Library / repo | What it provides | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| **cuda‑butterfly** (github.com/kingyiusuen/cuda-butterfly) | CUDA kernels for multiplying a vector by a product of butterfly factors (both real and complex). | Uses shared memory to keep the $O(d)$ non‑zeros contiguous within each warp, minimising launch overhead. |
| **torch‑butterfly** (github.com/idiap/torch-butterfly) | PyTorch wrapper around the above kernels; supports automatic differentiation and batched inputs. | Drop‑in replacement for `nn.Linear` when `weight` is replaced by `Butterfly(d, m)` . |
| **xformers** (facebookresearch/xformers) | Contains `xformers.ops.FusedButterfly` which fuses several consecutive butterfly components into one kernel call. | Particularly useful when you set $m>1$; reduces launch overhead dramatically. |

If you are using vanilla PyTorch you can obtain reasonable performance without custom kernels by reshaping the factor matrices into $(m, d, 1)$ and applying `torch.bmm` inside a loop; however, for large models the specialised kernels above give roughly **2×–3× speedup** over this naïve approach and bring the runtime of BOFT close to that of block‑diagonal OFT.

--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Summary

*Block‑diagonal OFT* with block size 64 costs about **131 k FLOPs** (≈ 65 k multiply‑adds).  
*BOFT$(\,\log d,\,2)$* costs about **20 k FLOPs**, i.e. roughly one third of the arithmetic work, but incurs extra kernel launches and less regular memory access, which explains the reported “slightly larger” wall‑clock time (typically a few tens of percent).  

Efficient GPU implementations exist (e.g., `cuda-butterfly`, `torch-butterfly`, `xformers.ops.FusedButterfly`) that mitigate the overhead by fusing multiple butterfly factors into a single kernel and by arranging data to maximise cache utilisation. After finetuning, you can bake the learned orthogonal matrix into the pretrained weights, eliminating any inference cost altogether.
