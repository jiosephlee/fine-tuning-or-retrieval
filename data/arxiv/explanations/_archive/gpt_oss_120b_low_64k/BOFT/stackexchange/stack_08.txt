\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is "multiplicative Dropout" in BOFT and why doesn’t standard Dropout work?
Question:
The authors propose a multiplicative Dropout that randomly replaces selected butterfly components or their diagonal blocks by identity matrices. My question is: (a) why would zeroing out parts of an additive update (as done in LoRA) be inappropriate for a multiplicative orthogonal update; (b) how is the dropout mask applied during forward/backward passes; and (c) does this affect the orthogonality guarantee of the remaining components?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the trainable “weight” of a linear layer is not added to the pretrained matrix as in LoRA, but **multiplied** on the left by an orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf{R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
$$

Each factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a sparse, orthogonal butterfly component.  
Because the update is *multiplicative* and the whole product must stay orthogonal, the way we regularise (e.g. with dropout) has to respect this structure. The authors therefore introduce **multiplicative dropout**, which works quite differently from the ordinary additive dropout used in LoRA.

---

### (a) Why zero‑out does not make sense for a multiplicative orthogonal update  

In LoRA the finetuned weight is

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \Delta\! \mathbf{W},\qquad 
\Delta\! \mathbf{W}= \mathbf A\mathbf B,
$$

so setting some entries of $\Delta\! \mathbf{W}$ to zero simply means “do not use that low‑rank correction”.  The pretrained matrix remains untouched; the additive nature guarantees that any subset of the low‑rank factors can be dropped without breaking the definition of the model.

In BOFT the correction is *inside* a product:

$$
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = (\underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m)}}_{\text{component }m}
        \cdots 
        \underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}}_{\text{component }1})\,\mathbf{W}^{0}.
$$

If we were to zero out an entry of a factor, the factor would no longer be orthogonal (its rows would cease to be a basis) and the whole product would cease to be a pure rotation/reflection.  Moreover, an orthogonal matrix cannot be expressed as “identity plus a sparse correction” – it must satisfy  
$\mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I$.  Consequently, dropping entries **additively** would violate this constraint and destroy the very property (preservation of angles and singular values) that OFT/BOFT is built upon.  For that reason we cannot simply apply standard dropout on the entries of $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$.

---

### (b) How multiplicative dropout is applied  

The dropout operates **at the level of whole butterfly components and at the level of their diagonal blocks**, not on individual scalar entries.

1. **Component‑wise mask** – With probability $p_{1}$ a whole factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is selected.  When it is selected, every block inside that component is replaced by an identity block, i.e. the entire factor becomes an identity matrix:
   $$
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \;\longleftarrow\; \mathbf I_{d}.
   $$

2. **Block‑wise mask** – Independently, for each factor we draw a second mask that selects a fraction $p_{2}$ of its diagonal blocks (each block has size $b\times b$).  The chosen blocks are likewise set to identity matrices while the remaining blocks keep their learned orthogonal parameterisation.

During **the forward pass** we first construct the masked product
$$
\widehat{\mathbf R}= \prod_{i=1}^{m} \widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
$$
where each $\widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is either the original learned component or an identity matrix according to the two masks.  The output of the layer is then
$$
\mathbf z = (\widehat{\mathbf R}\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf x .
$$

During **back‑propagation** gradients flow only through those components and blocks that were *not* replaced by identity; components that were turned into identity are treated as constants and receive zero gradient for that step.  The masks are sampled anew for every training mini‑batch (or every iteration), exactly as in standard dropout, so over training time each component learns to be useful even though it may be temporarily omitted.

---

### (c) Does this break the orthogonality guarantee?  

No.  The product of orthogonal matrices is again orthogonal, and an identity matrix is trivially orthogonal.  Because multiplicative dropout only **replaces entire factors or blocks with identity matrices**, every factor that participates in a forward pass remains orthogonal by construction:

* The learned blocks are parameterised via Cayley transforms (or explicit $2\times2$ rotations), guaranteeing orthogonality.
* When a block is replaced by an identity, orthogonality is preserved because $\mathbf I^{\top}\mathbf I = \mathbf I$.

Therefore, after applying the masks we still have  

$$
\widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}{}^{\!\top}
    \widehat{\tilde{\mathbf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} = \mathbf I_{d},
    \qquad
    i=1,\dots,m,
$$

and consequently  

$$
\widehat{\mathbf R}^{\!\top}\widehat{\mathbf R}= \mathbf I_{d}.
$$

The only effect of dropout is to **temporarily reduce** the expressive power of the current forward pass, which acts as a regulariser: different random subsets of butterfly components are forced to learn useful transformations because any one of them might be dropped later.  This mirrors how standard dropout prevents co‑adaptation of neurons, but it does so while respecting the multiplicative orthogonal structure required by BOFT.

---

#### Summary  

*Zeroing out individual entries would violate orthogonality; instead BOFT’s multiplicative dropout randomly substitutes whole butterfly components or their diagonal blocks with identities.*  
*During each forward/backward step the masked product of orthogonal factors is used, so orthogonality remains guaranteed.*  
*The procedure provides regularisation analogous to conventional dropout but compatible with BOFT’s multiplicative update.*
