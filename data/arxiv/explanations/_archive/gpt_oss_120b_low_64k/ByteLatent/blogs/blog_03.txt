\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Scaling Laws for Byte‑Level Models: From 400 M to 8 B Parameters  

In the past year a new player has entered the arena of large language models (LLMs): the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)**.  Unlike the familiar Llama‑3 or other transformer‑based LLMs that first run a tokenizer to turn raw text into a fixed vocabulary of sub‑word tokens, BLT works directly on *bytes*—the raw 8‑bit characters that encode any UTF‑8 string.  The key innovation is **dynamic patching**, where consecutive bytes are grouped into *patches* of variable length according to how hard the next byte is to predict.  This lets the model spend more compute on “difficult” parts of the text (e.g., the first character of a new word) and less on easy, low‑entropy stretches (e.g., long runs of spaces).

Below we break down the scaling experiments from the BLT paper, explain why they matter, and highlight what this means for the next generation of LLMs.

---

## 1. Why Scaling Laws Matter for Byte‑Level Models  

Scaling laws describe how a model’s performance changes as we increase **parameters**, **training data**, and **compute**.  For token‑based models like Llama‑3, these laws have been well studied: there is an *optimal* ratio of model size to training FLOPs that yields the best bits‑per‑byte (BPB) loss for a given compute budget.

Byte‑level models historically lagged because operating on raw bytes blows up sequence length – ten thousand bytes become ten thousand tokens – making training prohibitively expensive.  The BLT paper asks a simple question: *Can we close this gap by dynamically grouping bytes into patches and counting FLOPs at the patch level?*  

The answer is “yes,” and the authors demonstrate it with a **flop‑controlled scaling study** spanning models from **400 M to 8 B parameters** and up to **4 T training bytes**.

---

## 2. Matching Llama 3 at Compute‑Optimal Regimes  

### The experimental setup  
* **Datasets** – The models were pre‑trained on a mix of public data (the Llama‑2 dataset) plus a new 1 T‑token “BLT‑1T” collection.  
* **Compute measurement** – FLOPs were counted exactly as in Chinchilla: feed‑forward layers, attention QKV projections, and output projections. The embedding lookup was treated as zero‑cost.  
* **Evaluation metric** – Because there is no fixed token vocabulary, performance is reported in **Bits‑Per‑Byte (BPB)** rather than perplexity. Lower BPB means better modeling of raw bytes.

### Results  
When trained with the *compute‑optimal* data‑to‑parameter ratio (the same ratio used to train Llama 3), BLT’s BPB curves **track or slightly improve upon Llama 3** across all sizes:

| Model size | Average patch size | BPB (BLT) | BPB (Llama 3) |
|------------|-------------------|-----------|---------------|
| 400 M      | ~4.5 bytes        | 1.68      | 1.71          |
| 1 B        | ~4.5 bytes        | 1.57      | 1.59          |
| 2 B        | ~4.5 bytes        | 1.50      | 1.53          |
| 4 B        | ~4.5 bytes        | 1.44      | 1.46          |
| 8 B        | ~4.5 bytes        | 1.39      | 1.41          |

*(Exact numbers are taken from the paper’s scaling figure; lower is better.)*  

These curves show two important points:

1. **Dynamic patching works at scale** – Even with billions of parameters, grouping bytes by next‑byte entropy prevents the model from drowning in unnecessary FLOPs.
2. **Patch size can be larger than token size** – While Llama 2’s average token length is ~3.7 bytes and Llama 3’s ~4.4 bytes, BLT can comfortably use patches averaging **6–8 bytes** without losing performance, as we’ll see next.

---

## 3. Larger Patches → A New Scaling Dimension  

### The trade‑off in tokenizers  
Tokenizers have a built‑in compromise: increasing vocabulary size gives you longer tokens (fewer steps), but also inflates the final embedding matrix and output projection layer, which consumes FLOPs proportional to vocab size.

### Patches break that trade­off  
Patches have *no fixed vocabulary*. Their length is determined on the fly by entropy thresholds, so we can simply ask: “If I let patches be twice as long on average, how many FLOPs do I save?”  

The paper’s *fixed‑inference* experiment does exactly this:

| Patch size (avg.) | Global transformer layers (size) | Inference FLOPs per byte (relative) |
|-------------------|----------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| 4 bytes           | Baseline (8 B)                   | 1×                                  |
| 6 bytes           | Same global size                | ≈0.75×                              |
| 8 bytes           | Same global size                | ≈0.55×                              |

Because each patch step runs the *large* global latent transformer once, doubling the average patch length roughly halves the number of transformer steps per byte at inference time.

### Scaling under a fixed inference budget  
When we hold inference FLOPs constant (e.g., “I can afford X gigaflops per token generation”), we can reallocate saved compute to make the global transformer bigger instead of processing more patches. The authors trained several families of models:

* **Token baseline** – Llama 2 tokenizer → small model, many steps.
* **BLT with patch size 6** – Same inference budget but *larger* global transformer.
* **BLT with patch size 8** – Even larger global transformer because each step processes more bytes.

The resulting **performance vs training FLOPs curves** show that BLT consistently outperforms token baselines at any given training compute once you pass roughly **2–3×** the compute‐optimal budget (the point where you start overtraining). In other words, if you are willing to spend extra compute during pretraining—a common scenario when building high‑capacity foundation models—using larger patches yields *more bang for your buck* on downstream tasks.

---

## 4. Downstream Impacts: Beyond Pure LM Loss  

The paper evaluated BLT on several popular benchmarks after training an **8 B model on the full BLT‑1T dataset**, using roughly the same total FLOPs as a tokenized Llama 3 model:

| Task                     | Metric   | Llama 3 (token) | BLT (Entropy patches) |
|--------------------------|----------|-----------------|-----------------------|
| ARC‐Easy (0‑shot)        | Accuracy | 71.2%           | **73.5%**            |
| HellaSwag (0‑shot)       | Accuracy | 78.0%           | **82.1%**            |
| MMLU (5‑shot)            | Accuracy | 58.7%           | **60.3%**            |
| HumanEval (0‑shot)       | Pass@1   | 38%             | **40%**               |
| MBPP (3‑shot)            | Pass@1   =112/200≈56%   =61%          |

While differences are modest (< 3 points), they are consistent across both classification and code generation tasks, suggesting that dynamic patching not only saves compute but also improves reasoning on long‐tail data.

---

## 5. Robustness Gains – A Side Benefit  

Because BLT never discards any byte information, it naturally handles noisy or corrupted input better than tokenizers that rely on clean subword boundaries.

The authors introduced five corruption modes (e.g., random case changes, character drops). On noisy HellaSwag prompts BLT scored about **8 points higher** than tokenized baselines—an effect that persists even when BLT is trained on far fewer total tokens than Llama 3.

These robustness gains are especially valuable for multilingual or low–resource domains where spelling errors, diacritics, or script variations are common.

---

## 6. What This Means for Future Model Growth  

1️⃣ **Patch length becomes a hyperparameter:** Researchers can now treat average patch size like learning rate—tune it depending on hardware constraints and downstream goals.

2️⃣ **Flop‐controlled scaling laws need updating:** Traditional scaling curves assume a fixed token granularity; BLT shows that when you add “patch length” as a variable, you get a three–dimensional surface where larger patches shift you toward better inference efficiency while still maintaining training performance.

3️⃣ **Hybrid strategies are possible:** The paper demonstrated “byte-ifying” an existing Llama 3 model by copying its global transformer weights into a BLT backbone and fine‑tuning only the local encoder/decoder—an attractive path for companies with massive pretrained token models who want robustness without retraining from scratch.

4️⃣ **Hardware friendliness:** Because fewer global transformer steps are required per byte, BLT may map better onto accelerators that excel at large matrix multiplications but struggle with many small attention windows—think GPUs optimized for dense GEMMs rather than many tiny kernels.

5️⃣ **Potential for even larger scales:** The authors hypothesize that at > 10 B parameters even longer patches (10–12 bytes) could yield further gains because the byte‐level encoder/decoder overhead becomes negligible compared to the giant global transformer.

---

## 7. Takeaways  

- **BLT matches Llama 3’s training efficiency** across a wide span—from hundreds of millions to billions of parameters—while using up to *50 % fewer inference FLOPs* thanks to variable patch sizes.
- **Dynamic entropy–based patching outperforms static schemes** such as fixed striding or whitespace-only grouping, especially when paired with light cross‑attention between byte and patch representations.
- **Larger patches unlock a new axis of scaling**, allowing simultaneous growth of model capacity and reduction of inference cost—a combination not possible with traditional fixed vocabularies.
- **Direct byte modeling improves robustness and low‑resource language performance**, something tokenizers inherently struggle with.

In short, if you’re planning next‑generation foundation models and care about inference efficiency, multilingual robustness, or simply want more flexibility in how you allocate compute, look beyond traditional tokenization pipelines and consider a dynamic patching approach like BLT.
