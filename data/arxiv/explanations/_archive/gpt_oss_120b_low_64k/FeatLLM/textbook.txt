# Introduction to Few‑Shot Tabular Learning and Large Language Models  

## The few‑shot learning problem in tabular domains  

Tabular data—datasets organized into rows (samples) and columns (features)—are ubiquitous in real‑world applications such as finance, healthcare, and scientific research.  Unlike images or text, where massive labeled corpora are often available, many tabular tasks suffer from a severe scarcity of annotated examples.  In a **few‑shot** setting we are given only a small number $k$ of labeled instances (often $k \le 8$) and must learn a model that generalizes to the remaining, typically much larger, pool of unlabeled records.  

Formally, let a dataset be $\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ where each input $\mathbf{x}^i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ (or a mixture of numeric and categorical types) is described by a set of natural‑language feature names $F=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}$.  In few‑shot learning only a subset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ containing $k\ll N$ labeled points is available for training; the remaining samples constitute the test set.  The central challenge is that with such limited supervision it is easy for any learner to **overfit** spurious correlations that happen to appear in the tiny training slice.  Consequently, conventional statistical estimators (e.g., maximum‑likelihood fitting of a linear model) become highly unstable: the variance of the parameter estimates grows as $1/k$, while the bias introduced by fitting to random noise may dominate the overall error.  

Feature engineering—designing transformations of the raw columns that enhance their predictive signal—is historically one of the most effective ways to combat data scarcity.  By handcrafting domain‑specific indicators (e.g., “age > 65”, “ratio of cholesterol to HDL”), practitioners inject prior knowledge that guides the learner toward plausible patterns and away from noise.  However, manual engineering demands expertise and time, which are often unavailable for novel or rapidly changing tasks.  

Thus, few‑shot tabular learning can be viewed as a quest for **inductive bias**: we must encode useful assumptions about how features relate to the target label, using as little data as possible.  The next sections review traditional strategies for supplying such bias and argue why they fall short in modern, heterogeneous tabular settings.

## Traditional approaches and their limitations  

Early attempts to ameliorate few‑shot difficulties relied on **data‑centric** tricks.  Semi‑supervised methods augment the tiny labeled set with large collections of unlabeled rows, using self‑training or consistency regularization to extract additional signal.  While this works well when unlabeled data share the same distribution as the target task, it presumes access to massive raw tables—a condition not always satisfied in privacy‑sensitive domains.

Another line of work focuses on **pre‑training** on a broad corpus of unrelated tabular tables.  Models such as TransTab learn embeddings of column names and values by predicting masked entries across many datasets; TabPFN trains a Bayesian neural network on synthetic distributions sampled from a wide family of generative processes.  These approaches aim to acquire **task‑agnostic representations** that can be fine‑tuned on a handful of labeled points.  In practice their gains are modest: tabular data are highly heterogeneous (different column types, scales, missingness patterns), and a representation that works well for one domain often transfers poorly to another.  

A more recent family is **language‑interfaced tabular learning**, where an LLM receives a textual serialization of rows together with a natural‑language description of the prediction task.  By prompting the model with few examples (in‑context learning) or by applying parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning techniques such as LoRA or IA3, researchers have shown that LLMs can sometimes surpass classical tree ensembles in low‑shot regimes.  Nevertheless these methods share two critical drawbacks:

1. **Per‑sample inference cost** – Every test record must be sent through the LLM along with its serialized features, incurring high latency and API expense especially when using proprietary models that expose only inference endpoints.
2. **Prompt length bottleneck** – LLM context windows are limited (often < 4 k tokens).  When a table contains dozens or hundreds of columns, serializing all features plus examples easily exceeds this limit, forcing practitioners either to truncate columns (risking loss of predictive information) or to accept degraded performance.

Consequently, while LLMs possess remarkable reasoning abilities, their direct deployment as end‑to‑end predictors is impractical for many industrial tabular pipelines.

## Large Language Models: capabilities and prior‑knowledge advantage  

Large Language Models are trained on massive corpora encompassing books, scientific articles, code repositories, and web text.  During pre‑training they internalize **world knowledge** (e.g., “older patients have higher risk of heart disease”) together with an ability to **reason chain‑of‑thought**: given a prompt they can decompose a problem into logical steps before arriving at an answer.  

Two properties make LLMs attractive for tabular tasks:

* **Prior knowledge extraction** – Because LLMs have read countless domain texts, they can recall statistical regularities that would otherwise need explicit feature engineering.  For instance, when asked “What factors increase risk of diabetes?” an LLM can list age, BMI, family history—information that mirrors useful predictors in medical tables.
* **In‑context learning** – By presenting a few labeled examples within the prompt, we steer the model toward interpreting the current dataset’s idiosyncrasies without updating any weights.  The model effectively performs *meta‑learning*: it treats the examples as a miniature training set and produces predictions for new rows.

If we could harness these capabilities solely for **knowledge extraction**, rather than per‑sample prediction, we would avoid both computational overhead and prompt size limits.  This observation underlies the shift from an end‑to‑end LLM inference paradigm toward a **feature‑engineering perspective**.

## From end‑to‐end LLM inference to feature‐engineering perspective  

Consider an LLM prompted with a disease prediction task together with several patient records labeled “yes” or “no”.  An end‑to‑end approach asks: *“Given this new patient’s measurements, does she have the disease?”* The model implicitly evaluates all possible logical combinations of features each time it receives a query.

A **feature‐engineering view** asks instead: *“What logical conditions (rules) separate positive from negative cases based on what you know about medicine and these examples?”* If the model can output explicit rules such as  

\[
\text{Rule}_1:\ \text{Age} > 65 \;\land\; \text{BloodPressure} > 140,
\]  

\[
\text{Rule}_2:\ \text{Cholesterol} > 240,
\]  

then we can transform every row into binary indicators $z_1,z_2\in\{0,1\}$ describing whether each rule holds.  These engineered features are compact (few bits per rule), interpretable, and—most importantly—do not require further LLM calls at inference time; standard linear classifiers can combine them quickly.

Two practical benefits arise:

1. **Amortized cost** – The expensive LLM call occurs only once during rule extraction; downstream classification proceeds with cheap parametric models.
2. **Scalability** – Rules involve only a subset of columns identified as relevant by the LLM’s reasoning process; hence we avoid serializing all $d$ features into each prompt.

The challenge lies in designing prompts that reliably elicit high‐quality rules from an LLM given only $k$ demonstration examples.

## Overview of the FeatLLM framework  

The **FeatLLM** framework operationalizes the feature‐engineering viewpoint through three tightly coupled stages:

1. **Prompt design for rule extraction** – A structured prompt supplies (a) *basic information* (task description, feature names/types, few-shot demonstrations), (b) *reasoning instructions* directing the model first to reason about causal relationships using its world knowledge and then to refine rules using the demonstrations, and (c) *response formatting instructions* that constrain the LLM output into parseable rule syntax (e.g., ``If $f_3$ > 10 then class = 1'').  
   By fixing the number of rules per class (typically ten) and explicitly referencing feature types within each rule, FeatLLM mitigates both underfitting (too few rules) and prompt overflow.

2. **Parsing rules into binary features** – Although FeatLLM enforces a regular response format, natural language outputs still contain syntactic variability (“greater than” vs “>”).  To robustly convert rules into executable code, FeatLLM re‐invokes the LLM with a *code generation* prompt: it provides the raw rule text together with an instruction to emit Python functions that evaluate each condition on a pandas DataFrame.  The generated snippets are then executed via ``exec`` to produce binary matrices $\mathbf{Z}_k \in \{0,1\}^{N\times R}$ for each class $k$, where $R$ is the number of extracted rules.

3. **Learning rule importance & ensembling** – Not all rules are equally predictive; some may be redundant or even contradictory to observed data.  FeatLLM therefore fits a simple non‑negative linear model per class:
   \[
   \text{logit}_k = \max(\mathbf{w}_k ,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k ,\qquad
   \mathbf{p}= \operatorname{Softmax}([\text{logit}_1,\dots,\text{logit}_c]).
   \]
   The weights $\mathbf{w}_k$ are learned by minimizing cross‑entropy on the $k$-shot training set using standard stochastic optimization (e.g., Adam).  
   
   To increase robustness against noisy rule generation and to respect prompt length limits when $d$ is large, FeatLLM repeats steps 1–3 multiple times under *bagging*: each trial randomly selects a subset of columns or samples for inclusion in the prompt and raises the LLM temperature to encourage diverse rule sets.  The final prediction averages softmax probabilities across $T$ trials:
   \[
   \hat{\mathbf{p}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \operatorname{Softmax}\bigl(\max(\mathbf{w}^{[t]},0)\cdot \mathbf{z}^{[t]}\bigr).
   \]
   This ensemble leverages self-consistency—rules repeated across trials are more trustworthy—and mitigates failures arising from any single noisy query.

Collectively these components enable FeatLLM to transform an LLM’s latent world knowledge into concrete binary features that serve as high‐quality inductive biases for downstream classification—all while requiring only API access to the LLM (no fine‐tuning) and incurring minimal per-sample inference cost.

In sum, FeatLLM illustrates how large language models can move beyond being black-box predictors toward acting as *knowledge engineers*, automatically generating interpretable feature transformations that empower effective few-shot learning on tabular data.

**Problem Formulation and Notation**

In this chapter we introduce the precise mathematical language that underlies the entire framework presented in *FeatLLM*.  By recording the salient objects—datasets, feature metadata, and the few‑shot learning protocol—in a uniform notation, we make it possible to reason clearly about how a large language model (LLM) can be enlisted as a feature‑engineering engine.  The notation introduced here is reused throughout the book when we describe prompting strategies, rule extraction, binary‑feature construction, and downstream learning.  Although the ideas are illustrated primarily for classification tasks, the same formalism applies with minor modifications to regression problems.

---

### Tabular Dataset Notation

A **tabular dataset** consists of a finite collection of observations, each described by a fixed set of attributes (columns).  Formally we write

\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where

* \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) is the total number of samples.
* \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) (or \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{X}\) when categorical variables appear) denotes the *feature vector* for sample \(i\).
* \(d\) is the number of raw input features.
* \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\) is the associated target label.

#### Feature Metadata

In addition to the numerical or categorical values that appear in \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\), each column enjoys a human‑readable identifier and often an explanatory sentence.  Let

\[
F=\{f_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
\]

be the ordered list of **feature names**.  For every name \(f_{j}\) we have an optional **definition** (or description) \(\delta_{j}\), typically a short natural‑language phrase such as “*age of the patient in years*” or “*type of employment*: *private*, *government*, *self‑employed*”.  The pair \((f_{j},\delta_{j})\) constitutes **metadata** that can be supplied to an LLM; it enables the model to understand not only the raw symbol “\(f_{j}\)” but also its semantic meaning and admissible value domain (numeric interval, set of categories, etc.).

When we later serialize a table for prompting, we follow a simple template:

```
f1 is <value_1>. f2 is <value_2>. … fd is <value_d>. Answer: <label>
```

This deterministic format ensures that each demonstration example can be parsed unambiguously by the LLM and later by our rule‑parsing component.

---

### Classification versus Regression Focus

The primary focus of *FeatLLM* is **classification**, where the target space is a finite set of class identifiers,
\[
\mathcal{Y}=\{1,\dots,c\},
\]
with \(c\ge 2\).  In this setting \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{Y}\).  The downstream linear model introduced later predicts a probability distribution over these classes using a softmax transformation.

For **regression** problems, where \(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}\), an analogous formulation applies: after rule extraction each rule becomes a binary indicator feature and a linear (or other low‑complexity) regressor can be trained on those indicators.  The mathematical modifications are minimal—replace the softmax with a linear output and use squared error rather than cross‑entropy loss—but all results presented in this book assume the categorical case for simplicity.

---

### Few‑Shot (k‑Shot) Sampling and Evaluation Protocols

A central premise of *FeatLLM* is that only a **small subset** of labeled examples is available at training time.  We therefore define a *k‑shot* regime as follows:

1. Randomly sample without replacement \(k<N\) labeled pairs from \(\mathcal{D}\).
2. Denote this subsample by
   \[
   \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}^{(k)}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)}) : i \in S_{\text{train}}\},
   \]
   where \(|S_{\text{train}}| = k\).
3. The remaining examples constitute a test split,
   \[
   \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}} = \mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}^{(k)},
   \]
   on which we evaluate performance (e.g., area under ROC curve for binary classification).

Because performance may vary with different random seeds, experimental protocols typically repeat this random split multiple times (often three or five seeds) and report averages together with standard deviations.

When constructing prompts for an LLM we embed all \(k\) training examples as **few‑shot demonstrations**; these demonstrations are shown after the task description and before the request for rule generation.  The limited size of \(k\) dramatically reduces prompt length while still allowing the model to infer patterns that combine prior knowledge (encoded in its parameters) with empirical evidence from the demonstrations.

---

### Desired Properties of a Feature‑Engineering System

The ultimate goal of *FeatLLM* is to replace an end‑to‑end LLM inference pipeline with a lightweight downstream predictor that operates solely on *engineered features*.  To make this idea concrete, we enumerate several formal desiderata that any such system should satisfy; they will guide both algorithmic design and evaluation.

| Property | Formal expression | Intuition |
|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Compactness** | The transformed dataset consists of binary vectors \(\mathbf{z}_k^{(i)} \in \{0,1\}^R\) per class with modest dimension \(R<<d\). | Keeps downstream computation cheap and avoids overfitting in low‑shot regimes. |
| **Expressivity** | There exists at least one rule set such that for each class \(k\), samples belonging to class \(k\) have higher expected sum \(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{w}_k^\top \mathbf{z}_k]\). | Guarantees that useful predictive information can be captured by rules derived from LLM reasoning. |
| **Robustness to Prompt Length** | When the raw table contains many columns (\(d>100\)), rule extraction should remain feasible via *feature bagging*: randomly select a subset \(F'\subset F,\ |F'|=d'\) before prompting, with \(d'\) chosen so that serialized demonstrations fit within the model’s context window. | Mitigates context‑window limits without sacrificing performance through ensemble averaging. |
| **Statistical Consistency** | As \(k\to N\), learned weights $\mathbf w_k$ converge (up to scaling) to those obtained by training directly on raw features with an optimal classifier. | Confirms that the method does not fundamentally lose information as more data become available. |
| **Interpretability** | Each binary feature corresponds to a human‑readable logical condition (e.g., “age > 65 AND cholesterol ≤ 200”). | Enables domain experts to inspect what knowledge the LLM contributed. |

These properties are not independent; for instance, bagging serves both compactness (by limiting prompt size) and robustness (by providing diverse rule sets that can be averaged).  Throughout later chapters we will see how each component of *FeatLLM*—prompt design, two‑step reasoning, rule parsing, linear weighting, and ensembling—contributes to satisfying this checklist.

---

### Summary

We have established a rigorous notation for tabular data enriched with natural‑language metadata, clarified how classification tasks are embedded in this setting, described the few‑shot sampling protocol that fuels in‑context learning, and articulated concrete desiderata for any system that seeks to let an LLM act as an autonomous feature engineer.  With these foundations fully articulated, subsequent chapters will describe how to coax an LLM into generating logical rules from few examples, how to translate those rules into binary features automatically, and how simple linear models built on top of these features can attain state‑of‑the‑art few‑shot performance while requiring only API access to large language models.

# Designing Prompts for Rule Extraction  

In order to turn a large language model (LLM) into an automated feature‑engineer, the **FeatLLM** framework must coax the LLM to produce a set of human‑readable decision rules that can later be transformed into binary features.  This is achieved through a carefully crafted prompt that guides the LLM through a structured reasoning process.  The prompt is divided into three major blocks—**basic information**, **reasoning instruction**, and **response instruction**—each of which plays a distinct role in ensuring that the generated rules are both faithful to the underlying data and easy to parse automatically.  In what follows we describe each block in detail, explain how tabular rows are serialized, discuss how different feature types are handled, and finally address the practical issue of prompt length.

---

## Basic Information Block: Task Description, Feature Metadata, and Few‑Shot Examples  

The first component of the prompt supplies the LLM with all of the contextual knowledge it needs to understand the learning problem before it attempts any reasoning.  Three kinds of information are placed here.

1. **Task description** – The problem is framed as an explicit natural‑language question that mirrors what a human expert would ask.  For a medical prediction task the prompt might contain a sentence such as  
   > “Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no.”  
   This question conveys both the target variable (the class) and the decision‑making orientation (a binary answer).  By phrasing the objective as a question, we align with the way LLMs have been shown to attend to task goals in chain‑of‑thought prompting.

2. **Feature metadata** – Every column of the tabular dataset is introduced by its name and definition, together with its type annotation (numeric, categorical, ordinal).  For example:  
   - `age` is a numeric variable representing the patient’s age in years;  
   - `smoking_status` is a categorical variable with possible values {“never”, “former”, “current”}.  
   Supplying this metadata serves two purposes.  First, it reduces ambiguity when the LLM later refers to a column (e.g., ensuring it knows that *age* should be compared with numeric thresholds rather than string literals).  Second, it encourages the model to ignore columns whose definitions are irrelevant to the task, which mitigates spurious correlations in low‑shot regimes.

3. **Few‑shot examples** – A small set of labeled training instances (the *k* shots) is serialized into natural language and appended after the metadata.  Each example follows a fixed template:  

   \[
   \text{``}f_1\ \text{is}\ x^{(i)}_1.\ \dots\ f_d\ \text{is}\ x^{(i)}_d.\ \text{Answer:}\ y^{(i)}\text{''}
   \]

   where \(f_j\) denotes the \(j\)-th feature name and \(x^{(i)}_j\) its value for instance \(i\).  The answer token \(y^{(i)}\) is one of the class labels defined earlier.  By presenting these demonstrations directly in text, FeatLLM exploits **in‑context learning**, allowing the LLM to infer statistical regularities from only a handful of examples.

The combination of task wording, precise column definitions, and concrete demonstrations creates a self‑contained description of the learning problem that can be understood without any external code or pre‑training.

---

## Serializing Tabular Rows into Natural‑Language Statements  

Tabular data are inherently structured as rows of feature values, but an LLM operates on plain text.  The serialization step bridges this gap by converting each row into a sequence of short declarative statements.  The format adopted by FeatLLM is deliberately simple:

```
feature_name_1 is value_1.
feature_name_2 is value_2.
...
feature_name_d is value_d.
Answer: <label>
```

Key design choices in this serialization make downstream reasoning more reliable:

- **One feature per sentence** – This isolates each piece of information, preventing accidental merging of unrelated tokens that could confuse parsers or cause logical ambiguity.
- **Explicit verb “is”** – Using a consistent predicate reduces variation in phrasing across examples, enabling the LLM to more easily spot patterns.
- **Period termination** – A full stop at the end of each sentence gives clear sentence boundaries for models trained on natural language corpora.
- **Uniform ordering** – Features appear in the same order as listed in the metadata block; this regularity helps the model map each value back to its intended column when later generating rules.

Because only a few examples are needed for few‑shot learning, serializing them does not yet threaten prompt length limits; however, for datasets with many columns or when additional demonstration examples are required, strategies such as selective feature bagging (described later) become essential.

---

## Reasoning Instruction: Two‑Step Chain‑of‑Thought Prompting  

Simply providing data does not guarantee that an LLM will extract meaningful causal relationships; naive prompting often leads to superficial pattern matching.  FeatLLM therefore inserts an explicit **reasoning instruction** that directs the model through a two‑step chain‑of‑thought process.

### Step 1 – Infer Prior Knowledge About Feature–Target Relations  

The first step asks the LLM to think *only* about its background knowledge and any obvious domain facts supplied by the feature definitions.  Importantly, it does **not** consider any few‑shot examples at this stage.  A typical instruction might read:

> “First, based on your general knowledge about health records and the definitions above, list which features you expect to be most relevant for determining chronic heart failure.”

This encourages the model to surface priors such as “older patients have higher risk” or “smoking status influences cardiovascular outcomes.”  By separating prior knowledge from observed data, FeatLLM reduces reliance on spurious correlations that could arise from limited training examples.

### Step 2 – Derive Concrete Decision Rules Using Demonstrations  

Having identified candidate salient features, the second step asks the model to combine these priors with the concrete few‑shot rows and produce *exact* decision rules for each class label. An example instruction could be:

> “Next, using both your prior list and the five example patients above, write ten rules that would lead you to answer ‘Yes’ and ten rules that would lead you to answer ‘No’. Each rule should be written as a simple logical condition involving one or two features.”

The instruction explicitly fixes **the number of rules per class (10)** so that downstream parsing remains tractable while still providing enough expressive power for complex decision boundaries.  Moreover, by referencing feature types (numeric vs categorical), it nudges the model away from syntactically invalid expressions (e.g., comparing a categorical value with “>”) and toward semantically sensible conditions like “age > 65” or “smoking_status = ‘current’”.

The two-step chain‐of‐thought design serves three purposes:

1. It forces early filtration of irrelevant columns using world knowledge.
2. It guides rule generation toward concise conditions rather than verbose natural language explanations.
3. It yields a more diverse set of rules across multiple LLM queries—a property later exploited by ensemble bagging.

---

## Response Instruction: Structured Output Format for Parsable Rules  

After reasoning, the LLM must output its rules in a format amenable to automated parsing.  FeatLLM therefore adds a **response instruction** that specifies precisely how each rule should appear.

A typical response template looks like:

```
Class: Yes
Rule 1: age > 70
Rule 2: blood_pressure > 140
...
Rule 10: cholesterol >= 200

Class: No
Rule 1: age <= 45
Rule 2: smoking_status = 'never'
...
Rule 10: cholesterol < 180
```

Key aspects of this format include:

- **Explicit class heading** – Guarantees separation between rule sets for different target labels.
- **Enumerated rule identifiers (“Rule k”)** – Simplifies line‑by‑line extraction.
- **Uniform relational operators** (`>`, `<`, `=`, `>=`, `<=`) – Although LLMs sometimes spell out comparisons (“greater than”), specifying acceptable operators in the instruction steers them toward symbolic forms that parse directly into Python conditional statements.
- **One predicate per rule** – While more complex Boolean combinations are possible, limiting each rule to a single predicate avoids nested parsing complications while still allowing multiple rules per class to capture conjunctive logic collectively.

Even with these constraints some variability persists (e.g., occasional use of “>=” versus “≥”). To handle such noise without hand‑crafting fragile regular expressions, FeatLLM re‑feeds each generated rule set back into an LLM under a *parsing prompt*: it provides function signatures and asks the model to output executable Python code implementing all stated conditions. Because modern LLMs have been trained on large code corpora, they reliably translate textual comparisons into correct operators despite minor wording differences.

---

## Handling Different Feature Types  

Tabular datasets contain heterogeneous columns; treating them uniformly would cause type errors during rule evaluation. The prompt therefore distinguishes three principal types:

1. **Numeric features** – Accompanied by their units (e.g., “age is measured in years”). The reasoning instruction explicitly permits relational operators (`>`, `<`, `>=`, `<=`). During parsing these are converted into numeric comparisons.

2. **Categorical features** – Listed alongside all admissible categories in their description block (e.g., `smoking_status` ∈ {“never”, “former”, “current”}). The response instruction mandates equality checks (`=`) against one of these strings. If an LLM writes “smoking_status == current”, parsing logic strips surrounding quotes and normalizes case.

3. **Ordinal features** – Often represented numerically but possessing an inherent ordering (e.g., disease stages). In metadata we note they are ordinal; consequently both numeric comparisons and explicit category matches are accepted during parsing.

By exposing type information up front and reminding it during rule generation (“refer to each feature’s type when formulating conditions”), FeatLLM reduces mismatched expressions such as comparing a categorical string with `>` or attempting arithmetic on non‑numeric fields.

---

## Prompt Length Considerations and Mitigation Strategies  

Large language models have finite context windows; if every column name and every demonstration example were inserted naïvely, prompts could exceed this limit—especially for datasets like *Communities* or *Myocardial* that have over one hundred attributes. FeatLLM adopts two complementary tactics:

1. **Feature bagging** – For each ensemble trial only a random subset of columns is included in the basic information block (typically half of all columns). Because each trial still receives enough informative features (thanks to prior knowledge guiding selection), collective ensembles recover information lost in any single bagged view.

2. **Ensemble repetition with temperature control** – By setting a moderately high temperature (0.5) during generation, different trials produce diverse rule sets even when operating on overlapping feature subsets. Averaging predictions across many trials smooths out any errors introduced by missing columns in individual prompts.

These strategies keep every individual prompt comfortably within token limits while still exploiting all available data across ensembles. Moreover, because rule generation occurs only once per trial (rather than once per test sample), overall inference overhead remains low compared with approaches that query an LLM for every instance.

--- 

In summary, FeatLLM’s prompt design blends explicit task framing, meticulous feature metadata provision, few‑shot demonstration serialization, staged chain‑of‑thought reasoning, and rigid response formatting. This composition coaxing forces an LLM to act like an expert analyst: first recalling domain priors, then grounding them against observed examples, finally articulating concise decision criteria that can be automatically compiled into binary features for downstream linear modeling. The careful handling of feature types and prompt length ensures that this process scales across diverse real‑world tabular datasets while preserving both interpretability and computational efficiency.

**Rule Generation as Feature Engineering**

In this chapter we explain how the natural‑language rules produced by a large language model (LLM) are transformed into machine‑learnable binary features.  The discussion follows the pipeline introduced in the FeatLLM framework: from the definition of a rule, through the handling of imperfect LLM output, to the generation of executable code that creates the binary indicators \( \mathbf{z}_k \) for each class \(k\).  By the end of the chapter you will understand why rules are a convenient feature representation, how to parse noisy textual responses reliably, and how these binary features are fed to a simple downstream predictor.

---

### What Constitutes a Rule (Condition → Class)

A **rule** in the FeatLLM context is a logical condition that, when satisfied by a data record, strongly suggests that the record belongs to a particular target class.  Formally, for a classification problem with \(c\) classes, a rule for class \(k\) can be written as  

\[
\text{Rule}_{k}^{(r)}(\mathbf{x}) = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if the condition holds for } \mathbf{x},\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\]

where \(r = 1,\dots,R\) indexes the rules associated with class \(k\).  The condition itself is expressed in natural language and typically involves one or more original features \(f_j\) together with comparison operators (e.g., “\( \texttt{age} > 60\)”, “\( \texttt{cholesterol} = \texttt{high}\)”) and logical connectors such as **AND** or **OR**.  

The LLM is prompted to generate a **fixed number of rules** per class (the authors used ten).  This design choice balances two competing concerns:

1. **Expressiveness** – enough rules to capture diverse decision criteria.
2. **Compactness** – keeping the generated prompt short enough to fit within model limits.

Each rule is intended to capture a *causal tendency* inferred from the LLM’s prior knowledge combined with the few labelled examples provided during in‑context learning.  Consequently, the collection of rules represents an engineered feature space that mirrors what an expert human would write as decision criteria.

---

### Parsing Strategies for Noisy LLM Output

The raw textual output from an LLM is rarely perfectly structured.  Even with strict response instructions, variations appear such as:

- Using words instead of symbols (“greater than” vs. “>”).
- Adding extraneous punctuation or whitespace.
- Rearranging logical operators (e.g., “\(x>5\) and \(y<3\)” versus “\(y<3\) && \(x>5\)”).

A naïve string‑matching parser would fail under these variations, leading to missed or mis‑interpreted conditions.  FeatLLM therefore adopts a two‑step strategy:

1. **Normalization** – map common lexical variants to canonical symbols.  For instance, replace “greater than”, “more than”, and “above” with “>”.  Similarly, map “less than”, “below”, “under” → “<”.  This step also enforces consistent case handling and removes superfluous characters.

2. **Structure Extraction** – once symbols are normalized, a lightweight grammar (regular expressions suffice for most rules) extracts the left‑hand side feature name, the operator, and the right‑hand side value.  Logical connectors are identified and recorded so that multi‑condition rules can be reconstructed as conjunctions/disjunctions.

Because rule generation may still contain syntactic noise that evades regular expression coverage—for example, nested parentheses or ambiguous phrasing—the authors propose delegating this parsing job back to an LLM trained on code.  This approach exploits the model’s strong understanding of programming syntax while keeping implementation simple.

---

### Using the LLM Itself as a Code Generator for Robust Parsing

Instead of hand‑crafting an exhaustive parser, FeatLLM constructs a *secondary prompt* that asks the LLM to emit ready‑to‑run Python code implementing each rule.  The prompt supplies:

- The list of generated natural‑language rules.
- A brief description of each original feature’s type (numeric, categorical, boolean).  
- A template function signature, e.g.:

```python
def apply_rules(row: dict) -> dict:
    """
    row: mapping from feature name to value
    Returns: dictionary mapping class index -> binary indicator (0/1)
    """
```

The LLM then fills the body with conditional statements that evaluate each rule on `row` and set the appropriate entry in the output dictionary.  Example fragment produced by the LLM:

```python
if row["age"] > 60 and row["cholesterol"] == "high":
    out[0] = 1                     # rule for class 0
else:
    out[0] = 0
```

Because LLMs are trained on large code corpora (including Python), they routinely produce syntactically correct snippets even when faced with minor wording inconsistencies in the input rules.  Moreover, this method automatically handles type conversion (e.g., casting strings to floats where needed) and respects categorical vocabularies supplied in the prompt.

The generated code is then **validated** by executing it on a small synthetic row; any runtime errors trigger a fallback mechanism that retries generation with slightly altered prompts (e.g., adding explicit type casts).  In practice this loop converges quickly because most rule texts are simple comparisons.

---

### `.exec()` Execution of Generated Python Snippets

Once valid Python source is obtained, FeatLLM employs Python’s built‑in `exec()` function to dynamically define the rule‑application function at runtime.  The process is:

1. **Assemble Source** – concatenate all function definitions into a single string `code_str`.
2. **Create Namespace** – instantiate an empty dictionary `ns = {}` which will serve as both globals and locals for execution.
3. **Execute** – call `exec(code_str, ns)`; after execution `ns` contains references to all defined functions.
4. **Invoke** – for each dataset row `x_i`, call `ns["apply_rules"](x_i)` which returns a dictionary of binary indicators per class.

Using `exec()` has several practical advantages:

- **No external compilation** – everything happens within the interpreter.
- **Speed** – after definition, applying rules is essentially constant‑time per row because each condition reduces to elementary Python comparisons.
- **Isolation** – because `ns` is separate from the main program namespace, any accidental variable leakage does not contaminate other parts of the pipeline.

Safety precautions are taken: only limited built‑ins are exposed to `exec()`, and the generated code is never allowed to perform I/O or import modules beyond `math` if needed.

---

### Creating Binary Indicator Features \(\mathbf{z}_k\) for Each Class

The final output of rule evaluation for a sample \(\mathbf{x}^i\) consists of binary vectors

\[
\mathbf{z}_k^{\,i}= \bigl[\,\text{Rule}_{k}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^i),\,
                         \text{Rule}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{x}^i),\,\dots,\,
                         \text{Rule}_{k}^{(R)}(\mathbf{x}^i)\,\bigr]^{\!\top}
      \in \{0,1\}^{R},
\]

for every class \(k = 1,\dots,c\).  Stacking these vectors across classes yields an enlarged feature matrix  

\[
\mathbf{Z} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{z}_1^{\,1} & \dots & \mathbf{z}_c^{\,1}\\
\vdots &        & \vdots\\
\mathbf{z}_1^{\,N} & \dots & \mathbf{z}_c^{\,N}
\end{bmatrix},
\]
where each column group corresponds to one class’s rule set.

These binary indicators replace—or augment—the original raw columns when training the downstream predictor.  The downstream model used in FeatLLM is intentionally simple: a non‑negative linear layer per class

\[
\operatorname{logit}_k^{\,i}= 
   \max(\mathbf{w}_k ,0)\cdot \mathbf{z}_k^{\,i},
   \qquad
   \mathbf{p}^{\,i}= 
   \operatorname{softmax}\bigl([\operatorname{logit}_1^{\,i},\dots,\operatorname{logit}_c^{\,i}]\bigr),
\]

where \(\mathbf{w}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{R}\) are trainable weights constrained to be non‑negative (\(\max(\cdot ,0)\)) so that any rule can only increase—not decrease—the logit for its associated class.  Training proceeds by minimizing cross‑entropy on the few labelled examples; because there are only \(R\times c\) parameters (typically far fewer than original features), overfitting is mitigated even in extreme low‑shot regimes.

In practice, multiple independent ensembles are generated by varying:

- **Random seeds for LLM temperature**, producing diverse rule sets.
- **Bagged subsets of original features or training rows**, limiting prompt length when datasets have many columns.

For each ensemble member we obtain its own weight matrix \(\mathbf{w}^{[t]}\).  Final predictions average softmax probabilities across all members,

\[
\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{\,i}= 
   \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
   \operatorname{softmax}\bigl([\max(\mathbf{w}^{[t]}_{1},0)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{z}_{1}^{\,i},
                                \dots,
                                \max(\mathbf{w}^{[t]}_{c},0)\!\cdot\!\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\,i}]
                           \bigr),
\]

thereby inheriting robustness from classical ensemble theory while still enjoying negligible inference cost—only an evaluation of Boolean conditions followed by a cheap linear aggregation.

### Summary

The transformation from natural‑language rules to binary features proceeds through well‑defined stages:

1. **Rule definition**—the LLM supplies concise logical conditions linking feature values to target classes.
2. **Robust parsing**—either via hand‑crafted normalizers or more reliably by asking another LLM call to emit executable Python code.
3. **Dynamic execution**—the generated code is safely instantiated using Python’s `exec()` and applied row‑wise.
4. **Feature construction**—each satisfied rule becomes an entry in a binary vector \(\mathbf{z}_k\).
5. **Downstream modeling**—a tiny non‑negative linear model learns per‑rule importance; ensembles mitigate variability and prompt size constraints.

By treating rule generation as *feature engineering* rather than end‑to‑end prediction, FeatLLM decouples heavy LLM inference from per‑sample latency and enables interpretable binary features that directly reflect domain knowledge embedded in large language models.

Learning from Generated Features
=================================

In the **FeatLLM** framework the heavy‑weight reasoning is delegated to a large language model (LLM) that, given a few labeled examples, produces a collection of human‑readable rules.  Each rule is then turned into a binary feature indicating whether an individual record satisfies that rule.  After this transformation the original high‑dimensional tabular problem has been reduced to a much simpler classification problem: the model must infer class probabilities from a set of binary indicators.  This chapter explains in detail how the lightweight downstream model is built, how it is trained on the few‑shot data, and why its parameters can be interpreted as measures of rule importance.

### Linear Model without Bias and Non‑Negative Weight Projection

For every target class \(k\) (where \(k\in\{1,\dots,c\}\) and \(c\) is the total number of classes) FeatLLM constructs a vector of binary rule‑features
\[
\mathbf{z}^{i}_{k}= \bigl(z^{i}_{k,1},\,z^{i}_{k,2},\,\dots,\,z^{i}_{k,R}\bigr)^{\!\top}\in\{0,1\}^{R},
\]
where \(R\) denotes the fixed number of rules generated for that class (the paper adopts \(R=10\)).  Each entry \(z^{i}_{k,r}=1\) if the \(i\)-th sample satisfies rule \(r\) for class \(k\), and \(0\) otherwise.

The downstream predictor is deliberately kept *linear* and *bias‑free*:
\[
\text{logit}^{i}_{k}= \bigl[\max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\bigr]^{\!\top}\mathbf{z}^{i}_{k}
      = \sum_{r=1}^{R}\max(w_{k,r},0)\;z^{i}_{k,r}.
\tag{1}
\]
Here \(\mathbf{w}_{k}=(w_{k,1},\dots,w_{k,R})^{\!\top}\) are trainable parameters.  The projection \(\max(\cdot,0)\) enforces non‑negativity on every weight.  This design choice guarantees that a rule which fires (i.e., yields a ‘1’) can never *decrease* the logit for its associated class; at worst it leaves the logit unchanged.  Consequently the model respects the intuition that the LLM‑generated rules are intended to be *evidence* for a class rather than counter‑evidence.

Because the model contains no bias term, it cannot arbitrarily shift all logits upward or downward; all adjustments stem directly from the presence of rule activations, making the mapping from rules to predictions transparent and easy to audit.

### Logit Computation and Softmax Conversion

After computing a logit for each class with equation (1), FeatLLM converts these scores into a proper probability distribution over classes by applying the softmax function:
\[
p^{i}_{k}
   = \frac{\exp(\text{logit}^{i}_{k})}{\displaystyle \sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp(\text{logit}^{i}_{j})},
   \qquad
   \mathbf{p}^{i}= \bigl(p^{i}_{1},\,p^{i}_{2},\,\dots,\,p^{i}_{c}\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]
The softmax ensures that \(\sum_{k=1}^{c} p^{i}_{k}=1\) and that larger logits correspond to higher predicted probabilities.  Because logits are linear combinations of binary features with non‑negative coefficients, a higher count of satisfied rules for a given class tends to raise its probability, but the learned weights modulate this effect: some rules may contribute more heavily than others.

### Cross‑Entropy Loss with Few‑Shot Data

Training proceeds on the limited set of labeled examples available in a few‑shot regime (e.g., \(k=4,8,\dots\)).  Let \(\mathbf{y}^{i}\) denote the one‑hot ground‑truth label vector for sample \(i\).  The standard multiclass cross‑entropy loss is employed:
\[
\mathcal{L}
   = - \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}
      \sum_{k=1}^{c} y^{i}_{k}\,\log p^{i}_{k},
      \qquad
      K = k N_{\text{shots}}.
\tag{3}
\]
Because only a tiny number of examples are used, the loss surface can be noisy; nevertheless, equation (3) provides an unbiased estimator of the expected negative log likelihood under the model.  Minimising \(\mathcal{L}\) directly tunes the weight vectors \(\{\mathbf{w}_k\}\), thereby learning how much each rule should influence its corresponding class.

### Interpretability of Learned Weights as Rule Importance

A central advantage of this linear formulation is interpretability.  After training, each weight component \(w_{k,r}\) can be inspected:

* If \(w_{k,r}=0\), rule \(r\) contributes nothing to class \(k\)’s logit; effectively the rule has been discarded.
* Larger positive values indicate that satisfying rule \(r\) is strong evidence for class \(k\).
* Because weights are shared only within their class (there is no cross‑class coupling), one can compare importance rankings across classes directly.

When combined with the original textual description of each rule (generated by the LLM), practitioners obtain an immediately actionable explanation: “Rule *‘Age > 65 AND Cholesterol > 200’* carries weight 0.87 for predicting heart disease,” for example.  Moreover, because FeatLLM discards rules whose learned weight falls below a small threshold (e.g., 0.1), noisy or spurious rules generated from irrelevant columns are automatically filtered out during training.

### Training Hyper‑Parameters (Optimizer, Epochs, Early Stopping)

Despite its simplicity, careful configuration of optimisation hyper‑parameters helps achieve stable performance:

| Hyper‑parameter | Typical setting in the paper | Rationale |
|-----------------|------------------------------|----------|
| Optimiser       | Adam                         | Adaptive moments help when gradients are sparse due to many zero entries in binary features |
| Learning rate   | \(0.01\)                     | Large enough to make rapid progress on few data points while avoiding divergence |
| Number of epochs| 200                          | Sufficient to converge given a small dataset; early stopping based on validation loss prevents over‑fitting |
| Early stopping  | Choose epoch with best validation loss among k‑fold splits | Guarantees that we do not over‑train on noisy few‑shot signals |

Training proceeds with **\(k\)-fold cross‑validation** on the available shots: each fold leaves out a subset as a validation set while training on the remainder.  The epoch achieving the lowest validation loss across folds is selected for final inference.  This strategy mitigates variance caused by extremely small training sets.

Finally, after a single trial has produced its set of binary features and trained weights, FeatLLM repeats the entire pipeline multiple times (the paper uses \(T=20\) ensembles).  Each trial differs due to random temperature sampling in LLM generation and random shuffling of few‑shot examples; consequently multiple diverse rule sets are produced.  The final prediction for any sample is obtained by averaging the softmax probabilities across all trials:
\[
\bar{\mathbf{p}}^{i}= \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{p}^{i}[t],
\]
where \(\mathbf{p}^{i}[t]\) denotes probabilities computed with weights from trial \(t\).  Ensembling further stabilises predictions and reduces sensitivity to any single erroneous rule set.

Through this lightweight yet expressive downstream model—linear, bias‑free, non‑negative—we turn raw natural‐language rules into actionable numerical features and learn their relative relevance directly from scarce labelled data.  The resulting system retains interpretability, incurs negligible inference cost compared with end‑to‑end LLM approaches, and robustly leverages both prior knowledge encoded in LLMs and information present in few‐shot examples.
