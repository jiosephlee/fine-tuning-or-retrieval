\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function determine the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the weight quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\). I am confused about why the authors use the average absolute value of the weight matrix as the scaling factor instead of, say, the maximum absolute value or RMS. How does this choice affect the distribution of ternary weights and the stability of training?
Answer:
The “abs‑mean’’ quantizer in BitNet b1.58 is deliberately simple: it rescales the weight matrix by the **average absolute value**  

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$

and then rounds each entry to the nearest integer in ${\{-1,0,+1\}}$.  
At first sight one could also use the maximum absolute value ${\max|W|}$ or a root‑mean‑square (RMS) statistic, but the authors chose the mean‑absolute‑value for three intertwined reasons: (1) it produces a well‑balanced ternary distribution, (2) it yields a scale that is robust to outliers, and (3) it leads to stable gradients when the quantizer is used during training.

---

### 1.  Balancing the ternary histogram  

When a weight matrix is divided by $\gamma$ all entries are forced into a **common dynamic range centred around zero**. Because $\gamma$ is the *average* magnitude, roughly half of the scaled values fall in $[-1,-\tfrac12)$, half in $(\tfrac12,1]$ and the remaining part lands in $[-\,\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$. After rounding these three intervals map respectively to $-1$, $+1$ and $0$. Consequently the quantized matrix tends to contain **comparable numbers of –1, +1 and 0** (the exact proportions depend on the shape of the original distribution but are never dominated by a single symbol).  

If we used ${\max|W|}$ as the scale, almost every entry would lie well inside $[-1,1]$ because the maximum is usually much larger than a typical weight. The rounded result would be overwhelmingly *zero* and the model would lose expressive power.  
Conversely, an RMS scale is biased toward larger values (it squares before averaging); this would push more weights beyond $\pm 1$, causing many of them to be clipped at $\pm 1$ after rounding, again breaking the balance.

Thus the average absolute value gives a **self‑normalising** factor that keeps the ternary histogram roughly symmetric and avoids pathological dominance of any symbol.

---

### 2.  Robustness to outliers  

Weight matrices in deep networks are heavy‑tailed: a few parameters can be much larger than the bulk. The mean of absolute values is **linear** in each entry, so a single huge outlier changes $\gamma$ proportionally to its size but not quadratically (as with RMS). Using the maximum would make $\gamma$ equal to that outlier, discarding essentially all useful information from the rest of the weights; RMS would amplify its effect even more. The average‑abs therefore provides a middle ground: it reacts to overall magnitude changes while remaining insensitive enough that occasional spikes do not collapse the whole quantisation into zeros or saturate at ±1.

---

### 3.  Gradient stability during training  

BitNet b1.58 is trained *with* quantisation; gradients must flow through the piecewise‑constant “RoundClip’’ operation. In practice this is achieved with a straight‑through estimator: during back‑propagation we pretend the quantiser is identity and only apply its derivative $ \partial \widetilde{W}/\partial W = 1/\gamma $ (ignoring clipping). Because $\gamma$ appears in the denominator, its magnitude directly controls **the scale of surrogate gradients**.  

If we chose ${\max|W|}$, $\gamma$ could become very large when an occasional weight spikes; consequently $1/\gamma$ would shrink dramatically and gradients would vanish for most parameters. If we used RMS, $\gamma$ would also tend to be larger than necessary for typical weights, again diminishing gradient magnitudes and slowing convergence. The average absolute value stays close to the *typical* weight magnitude, ensuring that $1/\gamma$ remains on a sensible order (roughly comparable to the original unquantized weight scale). This keeps gradient updates neither exploding nor vanishing, which is crucial when thousands of layers are trained from scratch with ternary weights.

---

### 4.  Connection to prior work  

The use of an “abs‑mean’’ normaliser dates back to binary/ternary network literature (e.g., BinaryConnect, XNOR‑Net). Those works showed empirically that scaling by the mean absolute value yielded the best trade‑off between representation capacity and training stability, precisely because it preserves both **sign information** (the ±1 part) and **sparsity** (the zeros). BitNet inherits this design and demonstrates that it still works at LLM scale.

---

### Summary  

Choosing $\gamma$ as the average absolute weight:

* puts most scaled weights inside $[-1,1]$, giving a roughly balanced ternary distribution;
* limits sensitivity to rare large weights, avoiding excessive clipping or zeroing;
* yields a scale that matches typical weight magnitudes, so that surrogate gradients have appropriate size and training remains stable.

Using max‑abs or RMS would either collapse most weights to zero or saturate them at ±1, and would make gradient flow harder to control. Consequently, “abs‑mean’’ is not an arbitrary choice but a principled one that aligns quantisation with both statistical properties of neural‑network weights and practical needs of end‑to‑end training in BitNet b1.58.
