\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why does BitNet b1.58 achieve better zero‑shot accuracy than a larger FP16 LLaMA model at comparable parameter count?
Question:
The results show BitNet b1.58 3.9B outperforming FP16 3B on several benchmarks despite having lower precision weights. Is there an explanation beyond “feature filtering” from the zero weight? Could this be due to regularization effects of quantization or differences in training hyperparameters?
Answer:
**Answer**

`BitNet b1.58` 3.9 B outperforms the FP16 3 B LLaMA baseline not because it magically “adds information” with 1‑bit weights, but because the ternary weight space ($\{-1,0,+1\}$) changes the optimisation landscape in a way that is beneficial when the model is trained on the same amount of data and with (almost) the same hyper‑parameters.

---

### 1.  Sparsity‑induced feature filtering

The extra “0’’ value creates *structured sparsity*: a fraction of the parameters are forced to be exactly zero after each quantisation step. In a conventional FP16 model every weight can take a continuum of values, so even tiny connections are kept and can transmit noisy gradients. In `BitNet b1.58` many of those weak connections are pruned automatically at every training step.  

From a signal‑processing viewpoint this is a **feature filter**: only directions in weight space that have a sufficiently large average absolute magnitude survive the `absmean` scaling and rounding step. The surviving weights therefore correspond to *more salient* directions in the loss landscape, while noisy or redundant directions are discarded. The paper explicitly states that “its modeling capability is stronger due to its explicit support for feature filtering,” and this filtering is precisely what gives the 3.9 B model an edge over a denser 3 B FP16 model.

---

### 2.  Quantisation as an implicit regulariser

Quantising to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ introduces a deterministic but *non‑smooth* mapping from the underlying full‑precision parameters $W$ to the ternary parameters $\widetilde{W}$. The forward pass always sees a discretised network, while the backward pass still receives gradients w.r.t. the underlying real‑valued $W$. This discrepancy acts like adding *gradient noise* (similar to adding Gaussian noise or using dropout). Gradient noise is known to:

* push the optimiser out of sharp minima,
* encourage flatter basins that generalise better,
* reduce over‑fitting when data are limited.

Because both models are trained on exactly **100 B tokens**, the regularising effect of quantisation can compensate for the fact that `BitNet b1.58` has no extra data or larger hidden dimension.

---

### 3.  Same training recipe → fair comparison

The authors deliberately kept all other ingredients identical: same token count, same LLaMA‑style architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings), same optimiser settings and learning‑rate schedule, and no bias terms in either model. Consequently, any performance difference cannot be blamed on hidden hyper‑parameter tweaks; it must arise from the *representation* (ternary vs. FP16) and the associated optimisation dynamics described above.

---

### 4.  Why the gap disappears at larger scales

When the model size grows (e.g., 7 B or 13 B), both variants have enough capacity to fit the training distribution even without aggressive sparsity. The relative benefit of feature filtering therefore shrinks, which is why Table 2 in the paper shows that the zero‑shot gap narrows as we move from 3 B → 7 B → 13 B. At very large scales the regularisation from quantisation still helps slightly, but the dominant factor becomes raw parameter count.

---

### 5.  Intuitive picture

Imagine training a dense FP16 network as navigating a dense forest filled with many small trails (the tiny weights). It’s easy to get lost in irrelevant alleys that do not lead anywhere useful. `BitNet b1.58` repeatedly clears away most of those tiny trails (by forcing them to zero) and forces you to walk only on the main pathways that survive the “absmean” filter. The occasional “quantisation shake‑up” further prevents you from lingering too long in any one narrow valley of loss; you keep moving toward broader, flatter valleys that tend to work better on unseen data.

---

### Take‑away

The superior zero‑shot accuracy of `BitNet b1.58` 3.9 B compared with FP16 3 B is primarily **not** a mysterious effect of lower precision but a combination of:

1. **Explicit sparsity** (the zero weight) that performs automatic feature selection,
2. **Quantisation‑induced gradient noise** that acts as a strong regulariser,
3. **Identical training settings**, ruling out hidden hyper‑parameter advantages.

These mechanisms let a smaller ternary model achieve or exceed the generalisation performance of a larger dense FP16 model while enjoying huge gains in latency, memory and energy consumption—exactly what the authors set out to demonstrate.
