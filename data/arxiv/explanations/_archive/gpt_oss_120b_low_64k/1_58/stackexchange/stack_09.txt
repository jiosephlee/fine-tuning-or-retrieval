\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Is there any loss in expressivity when all linear layers use ternary weights while embeddings remain full precision?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 keeps embedding layers in full precision but quantizes all subsequent linear projections to ternary values. Could this mismatch cause representational bottlenecks, especially for tasks requiring fine‑grained token embeddings? Has any ablation been performed where embeddings are also ternarized?
Answer:
The short answer is **no significant loss of expressivity has been reported** when the embedding tables are kept in full‑precision while every other linear projection is ternarised, and the paper does not include an ablation that also ternarises the embeddings.

---

### Why the authors keep the embeddings in FP16/BF16  

In a Transformer‑style LLM the embedding matrix usually contains a large fraction of the total parameters (often ~10 %–20 % of the model size). Each token is mapped to a dense vector that is then fed into the first linear layer (the projection to the hidden dimension). If this matrix were forced to take only $ \{-1,0,1\} $ values, two things would happen:

1. **Capacity would drop dramatically.**  
   The embedding vectors are the only place where the model can store *raw* lexical information. Quantising them to three levels forces every token to share a very coarse codebook; many subtle distinctions that are useful for fine‑grained tasks (e.g., rare words, specialized terminology) would be lost.

2. **Training dynamics would become unstable.**  
   The original BitNet training pipeline relies on a *scale*‑factor (the average absolute weight $ \gamma $) that is recomputed each step for every linear weight matrix. For the embedding matrix this scaling would have to be applied *once per token* during forward‑pass, which is both expensive and noisy because the distribution of token frequencies is highly skewed.

Because of these practical considerations the authors chose to leave the embedding layer in its usual 16‑bit floating‑point format while applying their **absmean ternarisation** only to the “nn.Linear” modules (the Q/K/V projections, feed‑forward layers, and output head). This design keeps virtually all of the model’s representational power in a part of the network that is *already* dense and relatively small, while still obtaining the 1.58‑bit savings on the bulk of computation (the matrix multiplications).

---

### Does this create a “bottleneck” for fine‑grained tasks?  

The empirical results in the paper suggest it does **not**. Across a suite of zero‑shot benchmarks (ARC‑E, ARC‑C, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ) and on perplexity measured on WikiText‑2 and C4, BitNet b1.58 matches or exceeds a full‑precision LLaMA baseline once the model reaches about $3\,\text{B}$ parameters. If the embedding quantisation were a severe bottleneck we would expect to see a systematic degradation on tasks that rely heavily on lexical nuance (e.g., named‑entity recognition or rare‑word cloze), yet no such gap appears.

One can think of this outcome in terms of *capacity allocation*: most of the expressive power needed for language modelling resides in the deep transformer stack (self‑attention and feed‑forward layers). The embeddings simply provide an initial “lookup” that need not be extremely high‑resolution; after a few transformer blocks any coarse coding can be refined by learned linear combinations. Consequently, retaining full precision for embeddings already supplies more than enough resolution for downstream processing.

---

### Has an ablation where embeddings are also ternarised been performed?  

The authors do **not** report such an experiment. In Section *BitNet b1.58* they explicitly state:

> “It uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding… and removes all biases.”  
> “The architecture… replaces nn.Linear with BitLinear… The embedding remains full precision…”

Later sections discuss memory/latency trade‑offs and note that **the embedding’s contribution to memory shrinks as model size grows**, which further justifies keeping it at higher precision. No table or figure compares “full‑precision embeddings + ternary linear layers” against “ternary embeddings + ternary linear layers.” Therefore we have no direct evidence from this work that ternarising embeddings would preserve parity with FP16 models.

---

### Intuitive perspective  

Think of an LLM as a multi‑stage processor:

1. **Stage 0 – Embedding lookup:** turns discrete tokens into vectors; this stage is essentially a *dictionary*. As long as each entry can be distinguished at least roughly, later stages can recombine them arbitrarily.
2. **Stage 1…N – Transformer blocks:** perform linear mixes of those vectors using billions of ternary weights; they act like a very deep circuit that can amplify tiny differences introduced in Stage 0.

If Stage 0 were forced onto three discrete levels per component, many tokens would become indistinguishable already before they reach Stage 1, limiting what Stage 1 can ever recover. By keeping Stage 0 high‑resolution we give Stage 1 maximal wiggle room; Stage 1’s ternary weights are then sufficient because they operate on many redundant dimensions and can emulate richer functions through depth.

---

### Take‑away for practice  

- **Current best practice (according to the paper):** keep token embeddings in FP16/BF16 and quantise all other linear layers with BitLinear’s absmean ternarisation.
- **Potential future work:** an explicit ablation where embeddings are also ternarised could clarify whether further memory savings are possible without harming tasks that need fine lexical detail. Until such data appear, it is safest to assume that full‑precision embeddings are necessary for preserving expressivity across all downstream tasks.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 shows that preserving full precision for the embedding layer eliminates any noticeable representational bottleneck despite extremely low‑bit weights elsewhere, and no ablation study in the paper explores ternarising embeddings themselves.
