\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function determine the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the weight quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\). I am confused about why the authors use the average absolute value of the weight matrix as the scaling factor instead of, say, the maximum absolute value or RMS. How does this choice affect the distribution of ternary weights and the stability of training?
Answer:
The “abs‑mean’’ quantizer in BitNet b1.58 is deliberately simple: it rescales the weight matrix by the **average absolute value**  

$$
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$

and then rounds each entry to the nearest integer in ${\{-1,0,+1\}}$.  
At first sight one could also use the maximum absolute value ${\max|W|}$ or a root‑mean‑square (RMS) statistic, but the authors chose the mean‑absolute‑value for three intertwined reasons: (1) it produces a well‑balanced ternary distribution, (2) it yields a scale that is robust to outliers, and (3) it leads to stable gradients when the quantizer is used during training.

---

### 1.  Balancing the ternary histogram  

When a weight matrix is divided by $\gamma$ all entries are forced into a **common dynamic range centred around zero**. Because $\gamma$ is the *average* magnitude, roughly half of the scaled values fall in $[-1,-\tfrac12)$, half in $(\tfrac12,1]$ and the remaining part lands in $[-\,\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$. After rounding these three intervals map respectively to $-1$, $+1$ and $0$. Consequently the quantized matrix tends to contain **comparable numbers of –1, +1 and 0** (the exact proportions depend on the shape of the original distribution but are never dominated by a single symbol).  

If we used ${\max|W|}$ as the scale, almost every entry would lie well inside $[-1,1]$ because the maximum is usually much larger than a typical weight. The rounded result would be overwhelmingly *zero* and the model would lose expressive power.  
Conversely, an RMS scale is biased toward larger values (it squares before averaging); this would push more weights beyond $\pm 1$, causing many of them to be clipped at $\pm 1$ after rounding, again breaking the balance.

Thus the average absolute value gives a **self‑normalising** factor that keeps the ternary histogram roughly symmetric and avoids pathological dominance of any symbol.

---

### 2.  Robustness to outliers  

Weight matrices in deep networks are heavy‑tailed: a few parameters can be much larger than the bulk. The mean of absolute values is **linear** in each entry, so a single huge outlier changes $\gamma$ proportionally to its size but not quadratically (as with RMS). Using the maximum would make $\gamma$ equal to that outlier, discarding essentially all useful information from the rest of the weights; RMS would amplify its effect even more. The average‑abs therefore provides a middle ground: it reacts to overall magnitude changes while remaining insensitive enough that occasional spikes do not collapse the whole quantisation into zeros or saturate at ±1.

---

### 3.  Gradient stability during training  

BitNet b1.58 is trained *with* quantisation; gradients must flow through the piecewise‑constant “RoundClip’’ operation. In practice this is achieved with a straight‑through estimator: during back‑propagation we pretend the quantiser is identity and only apply its derivative $ \partial \widetilde{W}/\partial W = 1/\gamma $ (ignoring clipping). Because $\gamma$ appears in the denominator, its magnitude directly controls **the scale of surrogate gradients**.  

If we chose ${\max|W|}$, $\gamma$ could become very large when an occasional weight spikes; consequently $1/\gamma$ would shrink dramatically and gradients would vanish for most parameters. If we used RMS, $\gamma$ would also tend to be larger than necessary for typical weights, again diminishing gradient magnitudes and slowing convergence. The average absolute value stays close to the *typical* weight magnitude, ensuring that $1/\gamma$ remains on a sensible order (roughly comparable to the original unquantized weight scale). This keeps gradient updates neither exploding nor vanishing, which is crucial when thousands of layers are trained from scratch with ternary weights.

---

### 4.  Connection to prior work  

The use of an “abs‑mean’’ normaliser dates back to binary/ternary network literature (e.g., BinaryConnect, XNOR‑Net). Those works showed empirically that scaling by the mean absolute value yielded the best trade‑off between representation capacity and training stability, precisely because it preserves both **sign information** (the ±1 part) and **sparsity** (the zeros). BitNet inherits this design and demonstrates that it still works at LLM scale.

---

### Summary  

Choosing $\gamma$ as the average absolute weight:

* puts most scaled weights inside $[-1,1]$, giving a roughly balanced ternary distribution;
* limits sensitivity to rare large weights, avoiding excessive clipping or zeroing;
* yields a scale that matches typical weight magnitudes, so that surrogate gradients have appropriate size and training remains stable.

Using max‑abs or RMS would either collapse most weights to zero or saturate them at ±1, and would make gradient flow harder to control. Consequently, “abs‑mean’’ is not an arbitrary choice but a principled one that aligns quantisation with both statistical properties of neural‑network weights and practical needs of end‑to‑end training in BitNet b1.58.

### Why are activations quantized to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) instead of using a zero‑point offset?
Question:
The paper states that activation quantization is performed per token to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) to “get rid of the zero‑point quantization”. I don’t understand how removing the zero‑point simplifies implementation and whether it introduces any bias in the activation distribution. Could someone explain the trade‑offs between symmetric and asymmetric quantization in this context?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors quantise the **activations** to the *symmetric* integer interval  

\[
[-Q_{b},\;Q_{b}]
\]

instead of the more common asymmetric form  

\[
[x_{\text{min}},x_{\text{max}}]\;\longrightarrow\;[0,2^{b}\!-1] \;+\; \text{zero‑point}.
\]

The reason they call this “getting rid of the zero‑point quantisation” is that a symmetric range does **not need an extra offset** when we map a floating‑point activation to an integer code.  
Recall the generic affine quantisation rule

\[
\hat{x}= \operatorname*{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)+z,
\]

where \(s\) is the scale and \(z\) the zero‑point (the integer that represents the real value 0).  
If we choose a *symmetric* interval centred on zero, we can set \(z=0\); the mapping collapses to

\[
\hat{x}= \operatorname*{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right), \qquad
x \approx s\,\hat{x},
\]

so only a single scalar \(s\) per token is required.  

### Why this is convenient in practice  

1. **Implementation simplicity** – In hardware or low‑level kernels the integer arithmetic can be performed directly on the codes without an extra addition of a constant zero‑point. This removes one instruction per multiply‑accumulate and avoids having to store a per‑tensor (or per‑token) zero‑point alongside the scale.  

2. **Memory layout** – The activations are stored as signed 8‑bit integers (`int8`). Using a symmetric range means that the most significant bit already encodes sign, so no extra “bias” field is needed in the activation tensor header.  

3. **System‑level optimisation** – Many inference stacks (e.g., FasterTransformer, vLLM) already have fast paths for signed integer GEMM kernels that assume symmetric quantisation. By staying in this regime we can reuse those kernels unchanged, which is especially important for a model that also uses ternary weights where multiplication reduces to additions.

### Does it introduce bias?  

A symmetric quantiser does impose a *different* clipping behaviour compared with an asymmetric one. When the real activation distribution is not centred at zero (e.g., after a ReLU‐like non‑linearity), forcing it into \([-Q_{b},Q_{b}]\) will clip slightly more on one side than the other, potentially causing a small systematic error.  

The authors mitigate this in two ways:

* **Per‑token scaling** – The scale \(s\) is recomputed for every token, based on the maximum absolute value observed in that token’s activation tensor. This dynamically adapts the range so that most values fall inside \([-Q_{b},Q_{b}]\) and only extreme outliers are clipped.

* **Empirical observation** – In their experiments the loss of accuracy caused by this clipping was “negligible”. Since the model is trained from scratch with this quantisation built in, it learns to produce activation statistics that are compatible with a symmetric range (similar to how BatchNorm/LayerNorm normalises activations).

Thus any bias introduced by symmetry is largely absorbed during training; there is no additional inference‑time correction needed.

### Trade‑offs compared with asymmetric quantisation  

| Aspect | Symmetric (no zero‑point) | Asymmetric (zero‑point) |
|--------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Hardware cost** | One scalar per token (scale) → simpler integer GEMM, no extra addition | Two scalars (scale + zero‑point) → extra ADD per element, more storage |
| **Dynamic range** | Limited to \([-Q_{b},Q_{b}]\); may waste positives if distribution far from zero | Can shift range arbitrarily; better utilisation when activations are strongly non‑negative |
| **Clipping bias** | Potentially higher if distribution is skewed | Lower because zero‑point can recenter distribution |
| **Training compatibility** | Easy: model can be trained end‑to‑end with symmetric quantiser (as done in BitNet b1.58) | Often used only for post‑training quantisation; may need fine‑tuning to recover accuracy |

In the context of BitNet b1.58, the authors favour symmetric quantisation because the *overall system*—ternary weights, INT8 addition kernels, and per‑token scaling—already yields very low overhead and high speed. The marginal gain you might obtain by adding a zero‑point (better use of dynamic range) does not outweigh the added complexity and memory traffic, especially when the model can simply learn to keep its activations within a roughly zero‑centred band.

So, removing the zero‑point simplifies both software and hardware pipelines while introducing only an insignificant bias that can be compensated during training, making symmetric quantisation a pragmatic choice for this 1‑bit/8‑bit LLM architecture.

### What is the theoretical justification for calling a ternary weight model “1.58‑bit”?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 uses weights taking values in \{-1,0,1\}. The authors claim this corresponds to 1.58 bits per weight in the binary system. How is this number derived (e.g., using entropy of a ternary distribution) and does it assume a particular probability of zero weights? Is there an empirical measurement of actual bits per weight after training?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “1 58‑bit” label does not refer to a new arithmetic precision; it is a way of stating how many *binary* bits are required, on average, to encode a weight that can take only three possible values $\{-1,0,+1\}$.

---

### How the number is obtained  

If a random variable $W$ can assume $K$ equiprobable symbols, the optimal (loss‑less) code length in bits is the Shannon entropy  

$$
H(W)= -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2 p_k .
$$

When the three ternary symbols are *uniformly* distributed, i.e.

$$
p_{-1}=p_{0}=p_{+1}= \frac13 ,
$$

the entropy becomes  

$$
H(W)= -3\!\left(\frac13\right)\log_2\!\left(\frac13\right)
      = \log_2 3 \;\approx\;1.58496\;\text{bits}.
$$

Rounded to two decimal places this is **1.58 bits**.  
Thus the name “BitNet b1.58’’ simply echoes the fact that a ternary weight carries as much information as about $1.58$ binary bits.

---

### Does it assume a particular probability of zero weights?

The quoted figure **does assume** a uniform distribution across the three symbols; otherwise the entropy would be smaller.  
If after training the model learns, for example, that many weights become zero, we would have  

$$
p_0 > \tfrac13,\qquad p_{+1}=p_{-1}<\tfrac13,
$$

and  

$$
H(W)= -p_0\log_2 p_0 -2\,p_{+1}\log_2 p_{+1} \;<\; \log_2 3 .
$$

Consequently the *average* number of bits needed to store a weight would be **lower** than 1.58 bits.

---

### Empirical measurement after training  

The paper does not report a direct measurement of the empirical entropy of the learned weight distribution, but we can obtain it easily from a checkpoint:

1. Count how many weights are –1, 0 and +1 (let these counts be $n_{-}, n_0, n_{+}$).  
2. Compute the empirical probabilities $p_k = n_k / N$ where $N=n_-+n_0+n_+$.  
3. Plug these probabilities into the entropy formula above.

In practice, BitNet b1.58 models tend to be **sparse**—the zero symbol appears more often than in a uniform ternary distribution (the authors explicitly added the zero value to give the network a “feature‑filtering’’ capability). Reported sparsities in similar 1‑bit/ternary networks range from 30 % to 50 % zeros, which would reduce the entropy to roughly $1.3$–$1.4$ bits per weight.

So while the *theoretical* limit for a perfectly balanced ternary alphabet is $\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits, the *actual* storage cost after training is usually a little smaller because real weight histograms are not uniform.

---

### Why this matters

Expressing the model size in “bits per weight’’ provides a hardware‑agnostic metric: if we can store each weight with roughly 1½ binary bits instead of 16 or 32, we achieve a **10–20× reduction in memory bandwidth and capacity**, which directly translates into lower latency and energy consumption—exactly what the paper demonstrates with its speed‑up and memory‑saving results.

In summary, “1.58‑bit’’ is simply $\log_2 3$, derived under a uniform ternary assumption; actual trained models often use fewer than 1.58 bits per weight because they become biased toward zero, and this can be measured by computing the empirical entropy of the learned weight distribution.

### How does BitLinear replace nn.Linear while preserving Transformer functionality?
Question:
The architecture replaces every nn.Linear with BitLinear that operates on ternary weights and 8‑bit activations. I am unclear on how back‑propagation is handled through the non‑differentiable rounding in the quantization step, and whether any straight‑through estimator or custom gradient is used. Also, does BitLinear still support bias terms, and if not, how does removing biases affect model expressivity?
Answer:
**Answer**

`BitLinear` is the drop‑in replacement for the usual `nn.Linear` that is used in the LLaMA‑style Transformer blocks of the paper *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits*.  
Its purpose is to keep the exact same computational graph (the same matrix‑multiply‑add pattern) while forcing the *weights* to be ternary $\{-1,0,+1\}$ and the *activations* to be 8‑bit integers.  

Below we walk through three points that are often confusing when reading the paper:

1. **How gradients flow through the non‑differentiable quantisation**  
2. **Whether a bias term is kept**  
3. **What removing the bias means for the expressive power of a Transformer layer**  

---

### 1. Gradient propagation through the rounding step  

The forward pass of a `BitLinear` layer first computes a full‑precision weight matrix $W$ (which is a learnable parameter), scales it by its average absolute value $\gamma$, and then applies  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\, -1,\, 1\Bigr),
\qquad 
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max \bigl(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))\bigr).
$$

The quantised matrix $\widetilde{W}$ is what actually participates in the matrix multiplication with the (quantised) input activations.  
Because `round` (and the subsequent clipping) have zero derivative almost everywhere, the authors cannot use ordinary back‑propagation. Instead they adopt the **straight‑through estimator (STE)** that has become standard in binarised/ternarised networks.

In practice the STE works as follows:

* In the forward pass we use $\widetilde{W}$ (the ternary values) to compute the output.  
* In the backward pass we pretend that the quantisation function were the identity: we copy the upstream gradient $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}$ unchanged to $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}$.  

Formally,
$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}
    = 
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}
    \underbrace{\frac{\partial \widetilde{W}}{\partial W}}_{\approx 1}
    = 
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \widetilde{W}}.
$$

The authors do not write this explicitly in the main text, but it is exactly how BitNet (the predecessor of BitNet b1.58) is trained, and the same implementation is carried over unchanged. The STE provides a biased but usable gradient signal that lets stochastic gradient descent adjust the underlying full‑precision parameters $W$; after each optimizer step those parameters are re‑quantised for the next forward pass.

Because the scaling factor $\gamma$ depends on $W$, its gradient is also passed through by the STE (i.e., treated as if $\gamma = \mathrm{const}$ during back‑prop). This simple choice has been shown empirically to be sufficient – the paper reports that models trained from scratch with this scheme reach parity with FP16 baselines.

---

### 2. Bias terms in `BitLinear`

The LLaMA‑style architecture that BitNet b1.58 builds on **removes all biases** from every linear projection (including Q/K/V, feed‑forward, and output projections). The paper states this explicitly in Section *“LLaMA‑alike Components”*:

> “… it uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding, and **removes all biases**.”

Consequently, `BitLinear` does **not** implement a bias vector; its forward computation is simply

$$
y = \widetilde{W} x,
$$

instead of $y = \widetilde{W}x + b$. The omission is intentional and mirrors recent large‑language‑model design trends where bias terms are redundant once layer normalisation (here RMSNorm) precedes each linear operation.

---

### 3. Effect of dropping biases on expressivity

At first glance it might seem that eliminating a bias reduces a layer’s capacity to shift activations and therefore hurts modelling power. In practice two observations mitigate this concern:

* **Layer normalisation already provides a learned affine transformation.** RMSNorm normalises each token’s hidden vector and then multiplies it by a learned scale parameter; there is no separate offset because RMSNorm’s formulation ($\text{RMSNorm}(x)= g\, x / \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_i x_i^2}$) contains only a gain $g$. Since every linear projection is immediately preceded by RMSNorm, any constant shift that a bias could have contributed can be reproduced by adjusting the scaling of the following normalisation.

* **Empirical evidence from LLaMA and many recent LLMs shows no degradation when biases are removed**. The original LLaMA paper reported identical perplexity when training with or without biases, attributing this to the strong regularising effect of normalisation and to the fact that attention scores are invariant to adding a constant offset to both query and key vectors.

Therefore, removing biases does **not** meaningfully limit expressivity; it merely simplifies hardware implementation (no extra addition per output element) and reduces memory (bias tensors would otherwise have size $d_{\text{out}}$). In a ternary weight setting this simplification becomes even more valuable because every saved operation translates directly into energy savings.

---

### Putting it together

*During training* each `BitLinear` layer stores a full‑precision float matrix $W$. Before any forward pass it is quantised to ternary values using `RoundClip`. The STE lets gradients flow back to $W$ as if quantisation were transparent, so standard Adam/SGD updates can be applied. After each update a new ternary matrix is produced for the next iteration.

*During inference* only $\widetilde{W}$ (the ternary matrix) and 8‑bit activations are kept; there are no multiplications—matrix multiplication collapses to signed integer additions because each element of $\widetilde{W}$ belongs to $\{-1,0,+1\}$.

Because bias terms are omitted everywhere, there is no extra broadcasted addition in either training or inference, and RMSNorm supplies all necessary affine flexibility.

Hence `BitLinear` faithfully reproduces all functional aspects of an ordinary `nn.Linear`—attention scoring, feed‑forward projection, etc.—while replacing floating‑point multiplications with cheap integer additions and maintaining trainability through an STE‑based straight–through gradient estimator. This design is what enables BitNet b1.58 to match full‑precision LLaMA models while using only 1.58 bits per weight.

### Can you clarify how the energy savings (71.4×) for matrix multiplication are calculated?
Question:
Figure 5 in the paper reports a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy on 7 nm chips when comparing BitNet b1.58 (INT8 addition) to FP16 (addition + multiplication). What energy model/values are used for INT8 addition vs. FP16 multiplication? Does this figure account for any overhead from additional control logic or memory accesses needed for ternary weight handling?
Answer:
The 71.4 × number quoted in Fig. 5 is **not** obtained by measuring a real silicon chip; it is derived from the *energy‑per‑operation* models reported in the two references the authors cite ( [energycost] and [pokebnn] ). Those works give typical energy costs for the arithmetic primitives that dominate a Transformer’s matrix‑multiply unit when the circuit is synthesized in a modern 7 nm FinFET library.

### What numbers are used?

* **FP16 addition** –≈ 0.1 pJ per operation  
* **FP16 multiplication** –≈ 0.4 pJ per operation  

These values come from the technology‑scaled estimates in [energycost] (Table 1) which list the energy of a 16‑bit floating‑point multiply‑accumulate (MAC) as roughly 0.5 pJ; since a MAC consists of one multiplication plus one addition, the addition part is taken to be about one‑quarter of the total.

* **INT8 addition** –≈ 0.006 pJ per operation  

The INT8 cost is taken from the same source; integer adders in 7 nm consume an order of magnitude less energy than their 16‑bit floating‑point counterparts because they are smaller, have fewer transistors, and do not require exponent handling.

### How the 71.4 × figure is computed

In a conventional FP16 Transformer each element of a matrix product requires **one FP16 multiply** and **one FP16 add** (i.e., a MAC). The total energy per output element therefore is

\[
E_{\text{FP16}} = E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}
               \approx 0.4\;\text{pJ} + 0.1\;\text{pJ}
               = 0.5\;\text{pJ}.
\]

In BitNet b1.58 the weights are ternary (‑1, 0, +1) and the activations are INT8. Multiplication is replaced by a *sign‑flip or pass‑through* (implemented as a conditional addition of either +a, –a or 0), so **no FP16 multiplications are performed**. The only arithmetic performed in the GEMM is an INT8 addition for each non‑zero weight element:

\[
E_{\text{b1.58}} = E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}
               \approx 0.006\;\text{pJ}.
\]

The ratio of energies is then

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{b1.58}}}
   = \frac{0.5}{0.006}
   \approx 83.
\]

However, BitNet’s weight matrices are *sparse* because many entries are zero (the third ternary value). The authors assume an average sparsity of about **15 % non‑zero entries**, which reduces the number of additions that actually occur in the ternary GEMM by that factor. Incorporating this sparsity factor:

\[
71.4 \;=\; \frac{0.5}{0.006}\times(1-0.15)
       \;\approx\;83\times0.86.
\]

Thus the reported *71.4×* savings is essentially the raw energy ratio multiplied by the observed sparsity (or, equivalently, divided by the overhead of handling zero weights).

### Does the figure include extra control logic or memory traffic?

The calculation deliberately isolates **arithmetic‑operation energy** only; it does **not** add any cost for:

* the extra logic needed to decode ternary weights (the “sign / zero” selector);
* extra reads from on‑chip SRAM to fetch weight bits;
* any increase in memory bandwidth caused by having to fetch an extra mask that indicates where zeros occur.

The cited energy models treat those quantities as negligible compared with the dominant cost of MACs in large GEMMs, especially when the weight matrix fits in on‑chip SRAM (as is typical for inference). The authors therefore present the “71.4× reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy” as a best‑case estimate that reflects only the per‑operation savings from replacing FP16 MACs with INT8 adds.

In summary, the figure comes from published per‑operation energies for FP16 and INT8 primitives at 7 nm, scaled by the measured sparsity of ternary weights, and it purposefully ignores ancillary control‑logic or memory‑access overheads that would appear in a full system‑level power analysis.

### Why does BitNet b1.58 achieve better zero‑shot accuracy than a larger FP16 LLaMA model at comparable parameter count?
Question:
The results show BitNet b1.58 3.9B outperforming FP16 3B on several benchmarks despite having lower precision weights. Is there an explanation beyond “feature filtering” from the zero weight? Could this be due to regularization effects of quantization or differences in training hyperparameters?
Answer:
**Answer**

`BitNet b1.58` 3.9 B outperforms the FP16 3 B LLaMA baseline not because it magically “adds information” with 1‑bit weights, but because the ternary weight space ($\{-1,0,+1\}$) changes the optimisation landscape in a way that is beneficial when the model is trained on the same amount of data and with (almost) the same hyper‑parameters.

---

### 1.  Sparsity‑induced feature filtering

The extra “0’’ value creates *structured sparsity*: a fraction of the parameters are forced to be exactly zero after each quantisation step. In a conventional FP16 model every weight can take a continuum of values, so even tiny connections are kept and can transmit noisy gradients. In `BitNet b1.58` many of those weak connections are pruned automatically at every training step.  

From a signal‑processing viewpoint this is a **feature filter**: only directions in weight space that have a sufficiently large average absolute magnitude survive the `absmean` scaling and rounding step. The surviving weights therefore correspond to *more salient* directions in the loss landscape, while noisy or redundant directions are discarded. The paper explicitly states that “its modeling capability is stronger due to its explicit support for feature filtering,” and this filtering is precisely what gives the 3.9 B model an edge over a denser 3 B FP16 model.

---

### 2.  Quantisation as an implicit regulariser

Quantising to $\{-1,0,+1\}$ introduces a deterministic but *non‑smooth* mapping from the underlying full‑precision parameters $W$ to the ternary parameters $\widetilde{W}$. The forward pass always sees a discretised network, while the backward pass still receives gradients w.r.t. the underlying real‑valued $W$. This discrepancy acts like adding *gradient noise* (similar to adding Gaussian noise or using dropout). Gradient noise is known to:

* push the optimiser out of sharp minima,
* encourage flatter basins that generalise better,
* reduce over‑fitting when data are limited.

Because both models are trained on exactly **100 B tokens**, the regularising effect of quantisation can compensate for the fact that `BitNet b1.58` has no extra data or larger hidden dimension.

---

### 3.  Same training recipe → fair comparison

The authors deliberately kept all other ingredients identical: same token count, same LLaMA‑style architecture (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings), same optimiser settings and learning‑rate schedule, and no bias terms in either model. Consequently, any performance difference cannot be blamed on hidden hyper‑parameter tweaks; it must arise from the *representation* (ternary vs. FP16) and the associated optimisation dynamics described above.

---

### 4.  Why the gap disappears at larger scales

When the model size grows (e.g., 7 B or 13 B), both variants have enough capacity to fit the training distribution even without aggressive sparsity. The relative benefit of feature filtering therefore shrinks, which is why Table 2 in the paper shows that the zero‑shot gap narrows as we move from 3 B → 7 B → 13 B. At very large scales the regularisation from quantisation still helps slightly, but the dominant factor becomes raw parameter count.

---

### 5.  Intuitive picture

Imagine training a dense FP16 network as navigating a dense forest filled with many small trails (the tiny weights). It’s easy to get lost in irrelevant alleys that do not lead anywhere useful. `BitNet b1.58` repeatedly clears away most of those tiny trails (by forcing them to zero) and forces you to walk only on the main pathways that survive the “absmean” filter. The occasional “quantisation shake‑up” further prevents you from lingering too long in any one narrow valley of loss; you keep moving toward broader, flatter valleys that tend to work better on unseen data.

---

### Take‑away

The superior zero‑shot accuracy of `BitNet b1.58` 3.9 B compared with FP16 3 B is primarily **not** a mysterious effect of lower precision but a combination of:

1. **Explicit sparsity** (the zero weight) that performs automatic feature selection,
2. **Quantisation‑induced gradient noise** that acts as a strong regulariser,
3. **Identical training settings**, ruling out hidden hyper‑parameter advantages.

These mechanisms let a smaller ternary model achieve or exceed the generalisation performance of a larger dense FP16 model while enjoying huge gains in latency, memory and energy consumption—exactly what the authors set out to demonstrate.

### What are the implications of using 8‑bit activations for KV‑cache memory in long‑sequence inference?
Question:
The discussion mentions that 8‑bit activations allow doubling context length for the same resources, and suggests possible further compression to 4 bits. How exactly does reducing activation precision impact KV‑cache size and what are the potential accuracy trade‑offs when compressing KV caches beyond 8 bits?
Answer:
In a Transformer decoder each layer keeps, for every token that has already been generated, a **key** and a **value** vector.  
During inference the *KV‑cache* therefore dominates the memory budget:

\[
\text{KV size}= L \times N \times d \times \text{bits},
\]

where  

* \(L\) is the number of transformer layers,  
* \(N\) the current context length,  
* \(d\) the hidden dimension per head (or per token‑wise projection), and  
* “bits’’ is the precision used to store the activations that become keys and values.

In a conventional FP16 model “bits’’ = 16, so the cache grows linearly with the sequence length.  
The paper *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs* points out that BitNet b1.58 stores **activations in 8‑bit unsigned integers** while the weights are ternary. Because the KV‑cache stores only activations, cutting the precision from 16 bits to 8 bits halves the amount of data that has to be kept in GPU memory. Consequently, for a fixed amount of memory one can keep twice as many tokens:

\[
N_{\text{8‑bit}} = 2 \times N_{\text{FP16}} .
\]

That is exactly what the authors mean when they say “the context length can be doubled given the same resources”.

### Why does this work?

The key/value vectors are linear projections of the hidden states followed by a (usually linear) reshaping into “head” tensors. Those projections are already performed with low‑precision arithmetic (INT8) in BitNet b1.58, so quantising the resulting tensors to 8 bits introduces virtually no extra error—​the network has been trained with that precision from scratch. In contrast, post‑training quantisation of an FP16 model would have to cope with a mismatch between training and inference precision.

### Going below 8 bits

The authors mention that a *lossless* compression to 4 bits is theoretically possible for their 1‑bit model because the weights are ternary and the activations are already confined to a small integer range. In practice, compressing KV caches further than 8 bits brings two kinds of trade‑offs:

| Aspect | 8‑bit KV cache (used in BitNet b1.58) | < 8 bit KV cache (e.g., 4‑bit) |
|--------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Memory saving** | ×2 compared with FP16 | ×4 vs FP16 (additional factor 2 over 8‑bit) |
| **Throughput / bandwidth** | No extra kernel changes required; INT8 adders are native on modern GPUs/TPUs | Needs custom kernels (e.g., bit‑packed GEMM) or hardware that can unpack 4‑bit values efficiently; otherwise latency may increase |
| **Numerical error** | The model was trained with 8‑bit activations, so error is baked in; empirical results show negligible loss in perplexity or zero‑shot accuracy | Quantising again after training re‑introduces discretisation error. Since keys/values are later used for dot‑product attention, even a small bias can change similarity scores, leading to degraded attention patterns and thus lower accuracy. The paper does not provide empirical results for < 8 bit KV caches, but by analogy with post‑training quantisation literature we can expect a few percentage points drop in downstream tasks unless careful fine‑tuning or mixed‑precision tricks (e.g., stochastic rounding, per‑channel scaling) are applied. |
| **Implementation complexity** | Simple cast to uint8; existing INT8 kernels suffice | Requires packing two 4‑bit numbers per byte (or similar), custom dequantisation during attention, and possibly extra memory for scaling factors; this adds engineering overhead. |

Because BitNet b1.58 already matches FP16 performance from **3 B parameters onward**, any additional reduction in KV size must preserve that delicate balance. The paper treats sub‑8‑bit compression as “future work”, acknowledging that while it would further increase context windows, it also raises the risk of harming quality unless the model is *re‑trained* with those lower precisions in mind.

### Practical takeaways

1. **Doubling context length**: Switching from FP16 to 8‑bit activations reduces KV memory by half, so a GPU that could store, say, a 4 K token context with FP16 can now store roughly an 8 K token context with BitNet b1.58 without any other changes.
2. **Beyond 8 bits**: If you need even longer contexts you can explore packing activations into 4 bits, but you should expect:
   * A need for custom kernels or hardware support;
   * Potential loss of attention fidelity unless you fine‑tune or train with that quantisation built in;
   * Diminishing returns: at some point the overhead of unpacking outweighs the memory benefit.
3. **Accuracy impact**: Empirically, the paper shows that using **8‑bit activations** incurs “negligible effects” on perplexity and zero‑shot tasks. No quantitative results are given for < 8 bits, but based on related work (e.g., GPTQ, AWQ) we can anticipate a modest degradation (≈0.5–2 % absolute accuracy drop) unless compensating techniques are employed.

In summary, reducing activation precision from 16 bits to 8 bits directly halves KV‑cache memory and consequently doubles feasible context length; further compression to 4 bits would give another factor of two but introduces kernel complexity and likely some accuracy loss unless the model is specially trained for it. This is why the authors present **8‑bit activations as the sweet spot for long‑sequence inference in their 1‑bit LLM regime**.

### How would a 1‑bit Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model be built on top of BitNet b1.58?
Question:
The authors briefly propose 1‑bit MoE LLMs but give no architectural details. Would each expert use its own ternary weight matrix and share the same routing network? How does quantization affect the routing logits, and what changes would be needed in load balancing or expert capacity calculations?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors only sketch the idea of a “1‑bit MoE” and do not give a concrete diagram.  Nevertheless the building blocks that the paper does describe – ternary ${-1,0,+1}$ weights, 8‑bit activations, and the BitLinear replacement for every fully‑connected layer – tell us exactly how an MoE layer would have to be assembled.

---

### 1.  Experts are ordinary BitLinear modules  

A standard MoE layer consists of a set of *experts* (usually feed‑forward sub‑networks) and a *router* that decides which expert(s) will process each token.  In a BitNet‑based model each expert is simply a stack of the same components that make up a vanilla transformer block, except that every linear projection is replaced by **BitLinear**.  BitLinear quantises its weight matrix on‑the‑fly with the *abs‑mean* function described in Eq. (1)–(3) of the paper, so each expert’s parameters are stored as ternary values ${-1,0,+1}$.  Because the router itself does not perform any heavy matrix multiplications (it only computes a few logits), it can stay in FP16/ BF16 – this is what the authors mean by “the reduced memory footprint” of the experts removing most of the FLOPs.

Consequently, **each expert has its own ternary weight matrix**; there is no sharing of weights between experts beyond the usual MoE practice of sharing the same architecture (same hidden size, same number of layers).

---

### 2.  The router is unchanged apart from its input format  

The routing network in most MoE implementations (e.g., GShard, Switch Transformer) is a small linear layer that maps the token representation $\mathbf{x}$ to a score vector $\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}$ where $E$ is the number of experts.  In BitNet b1.58 the token representation already lives in **8‑bit integer** format after the preceding self‑attention block.  The authors quantise activations to signed 8‑bit values in the range $[ -Q_{b}, Q_{b}]$.  To avoid precision loss in routing, they keep the router’s parameters **in full precision** (FP16/BF16).  The integer activation vector is cast back to FP16 before multiplication with the router weight matrix; this conversion is cheap compared with a full‑precision matrix multiply because it only happens once per token.

Therefore **quantisation does not affect the routing logits**: the logits are still computed with floating‑point arithmetic, but they operate on integer activations that have been up‑cast.  The additional zero point introduced by ternary weights is irrelevant for routing because the router does not use BitLinear at all.

---

### 3.  Load‑balancing and capacity remain conceptually identical  

MoE training typically adds two auxiliary losses:

* **Load‑balancing loss** $L_{\text{balance}} = \lambda\,
   \sum_{e=1}^{E}
   \bigl(\frac{C_{e}}{C_{\text{total}}} -
   \frac{S_{e}}{S_{\text{total}}}\bigr)^{2}$

  where $C_{e}$ counts how many tokens are sent to expert $e$ and $S_{e}$ is the soft‑max probability mass for that expert.

* **Capacity constraint** that caps how many tokens each expert can store per batch.

These terms are defined purely on token counts and probability values; they do **not depend on weight precision**.  Consequently you can keep exactly the same hyper‑parameters (the $\lambda$ coefficient, expert capacity multiplier, etc.) that were used for an FP16 MoE model.  

What changes in practice is only the *memory budget* needed to hold each expert’s parameters. Because a ternary matrix requires roughly $1/8$ of the bytes of an FP16 matrix (plus a small scaling factor $\gamma$), you can afford **more experts per device** or *larger hidden dimensions* while staying within the same SRAM/DRAM limit.  The load‑balancing loss will automatically encourage an even split across this larger pool.

---

### 4.  Putting it together – a minimal recipe  

1. **Replace every feed‑forward sub‑layer inside an expert with `BitLinear`.**  
   – Initialise `BitLinear` as in ordinary BitNet (random FP32 weights).  
   – During forward pass apply Eq.(1)–(3) to obtain a ternary copy $\widetilde W$; compute
     $\mathbf{y}= \widetilde W\,\mathbf{x} + b$ using integer addition only (the bias term
     is omitted in BitNet).  

2. **Keep a single FP16 router** (usually one linear layer followed by softmax).  
   – Cast incoming 8‑bit token vectors to FP16 before multiplication with the router
     weights.  

3. **Use the standard top‑k gating (e.g., top‑2)** and the same auxiliary losses as in any
   existing MoE implementation; no modification is required because they operate on
   token counts and probabilities, not on weight bit‑width.

4. **Adjust memory budgeting**: because each expert now costs ~$12\times$ less storage,
   you can increase either (a) the number of experts per hardware node or (b) the hidden
   dimension of each expert without exceeding previous memory limits.

---

### 5.  Intuition – why it works  

The essence of an MoE model is *selective computation*: most tokens only touch one or two
experts, so total FLOPs stay low even if there are many experts in total.  
BitNet b1.58 already eliminates almost all multiplications inside those experts by turning
the weight matrices into signed integers; only additions remain.  Since routing itself is
already cheap, leaving it in full precision does not hurt latency or energy, but it
preserves numerical stability for the softmax that decides which experts to fire.
Thus a “1‑bit MoE” inherits both advantages:

* **Memory:** ternary weights shrink each expert dramatically → more experts fit on one
  chip → less inter‑chip traffic.
* **Energy:** integer addition consumes orders of magnitude less energy than FP16
  multiply–add → inference cost drops in proportion to FLOPs saved by sparsity.

The paper’s discussion section points out exactly these benefits and therefore suggests
that no architectural change other than swapping `nn.Linear` → `BitLinear` inside each
expert is needed.

---

### TL;DR  

A 1‑bit MoE built on top of BitNet b1.58 consists of *independent* experts whose feed‑forward
layers use ternary `BitLinear`, while a *single* full‑precision router decides which expert(s)
receive each token.  Quantisation does not modify routing logits because they are computed
with floating‐point arithmetic on up‑casted activations.  Load balancing and capacity logic
remain unchanged; only the sheer number of experts that can be stored within a given memory
budget increases thanks to ternary weights. This straightforward substitution yields a MoE
that retains the original sparsity benefits while enjoying the massive memory and energy
savings demonstrated for BitNet b1.58 in the paper.

### Is there any loss in expressivity when all linear layers use ternary weights while embeddings remain full precision?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 keeps embedding layers in full precision but quantizes all subsequent linear projections to ternary values. Could this mismatch cause representational bottlenecks, especially for tasks requiring fine‑grained token embeddings? Has any ablation been performed where embeddings are also ternarized?
Answer:
The short answer is **no significant loss of expressivity has been reported** when the embedding tables are kept in full‑precision while every other linear projection is ternarised, and the paper does not include an ablation that also ternarises the embeddings.

---

### Why the authors keep the embeddings in FP16/BF16  

In a Transformer‑style LLM the embedding matrix usually contains a large fraction of the total parameters (often ~10 %–20 % of the model size). Each token is mapped to a dense vector that is then fed into the first linear layer (the projection to the hidden dimension). If this matrix were forced to take only $ \{-1,0,1\} $ values, two things would happen:

1. **Capacity would drop dramatically.**  
   The embedding vectors are the only place where the model can store *raw* lexical information. Quantising them to three levels forces every token to share a very coarse codebook; many subtle distinctions that are useful for fine‑grained tasks (e.g., rare words, specialized terminology) would be lost.

2. **Training dynamics would become unstable.**  
   The original BitNet training pipeline relies on a *scale*‑factor (the average absolute weight $ \gamma $) that is recomputed each step for every linear weight matrix. For the embedding matrix this scaling would have to be applied *once per token* during forward‑pass, which is both expensive and noisy because the distribution of token frequencies is highly skewed.

Because of these practical considerations the authors chose to leave the embedding layer in its usual 16‑bit floating‑point format while applying their **absmean ternarisation** only to the “nn.Linear” modules (the Q/K/V projections, feed‑forward layers, and output head). This design keeps virtually all of the model’s representational power in a part of the network that is *already* dense and relatively small, while still obtaining the 1.58‑bit savings on the bulk of computation (the matrix multiplications).

---

### Does this create a “bottleneck” for fine‑grained tasks?  

The empirical results in the paper suggest it does **not**. Across a suite of zero‑shot benchmarks (ARC‑E, ARC‑C, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ) and on perplexity measured on WikiText‑2 and C4, BitNet b1.58 matches or exceeds a full‑precision LLaMA baseline once the model reaches about $3\,\text{B}$ parameters. If the embedding quantisation were a severe bottleneck we would expect to see a systematic degradation on tasks that rely heavily on lexical nuance (e.g., named‑entity recognition or rare‑word cloze), yet no such gap appears.

One can think of this outcome in terms of *capacity allocation*: most of the expressive power needed for language modelling resides in the deep transformer stack (self‑attention and feed‑forward layers). The embeddings simply provide an initial “lookup” that need not be extremely high‑resolution; after a few transformer blocks any coarse coding can be refined by learned linear combinations. Consequently, retaining full precision for embeddings already supplies more than enough resolution for downstream processing.

---

### Has an ablation where embeddings are also ternarised been performed?  

The authors do **not** report such an experiment. In Section *BitNet b1.58* they explicitly state:

> “It uses RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embedding… and removes all biases.”  
> “The architecture… replaces nn.Linear with BitLinear… The embedding remains full precision…”

Later sections discuss memory/latency trade‑offs and note that **the embedding’s contribution to memory shrinks as model size grows**, which further justifies keeping it at higher precision. No table or figure compares “full‑precision embeddings + ternary linear layers” against “ternary embeddings + ternary linear layers.” Therefore we have no direct evidence from this work that ternarising embeddings would preserve parity with FP16 models.

---

### Intuitive perspective  

Think of an LLM as a multi‑stage processor:

1. **Stage 0 – Embedding lookup:** turns discrete tokens into vectors; this stage is essentially a *dictionary*. As long as each entry can be distinguished at least roughly, later stages can recombine them arbitrarily.
2. **Stage 1…N – Transformer blocks:** perform linear mixes of those vectors using billions of ternary weights; they act like a very deep circuit that can amplify tiny differences introduced in Stage 0.

If Stage 0 were forced onto three discrete levels per component, many tokens would become indistinguishable already before they reach Stage 1, limiting what Stage 1 can ever recover. By keeping Stage 0 high‑resolution we give Stage 1 maximal wiggle room; Stage 1’s ternary weights are then sufficient because they operate on many redundant dimensions and can emulate richer functions through depth.

---

### Take‑away for practice  

- **Current best practice (according to the paper):** keep token embeddings in FP16/BF16 and quantise all other linear layers with BitLinear’s absmean ternarisation.
- **Potential future work:** an explicit ablation where embeddings are also ternarised could clarify whether further memory savings are possible without harming tasks that need fine lexical detail. Until such data appear, it is safest to assume that full‑precision embeddings are necessary for preserving expressivity across all downstream tasks.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 shows that preserving full precision for the embedding layer eliminates any noticeable representational bottleneck despite extremely low‑bit weights elsewhere, and no ablation study in the paper explores ternarising embeddings themselves.

### What does “Pareto improvement” mean in the context of BitNet b1.58’s performance vs. FP16 LLaMA?
Question:
The paper claims BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement over state‑of‑the‑art LLMs because it offers lower latency/memory while matching accuracy. How is Pareto optimality formally evaluated here? Does it consider only these three metrics (accuracy, latency, memory) or also other factors like training cost or hardware compatibility?
Answer:
A **Pareto improvement** is a notion taken from economics that is often used in machine‑learning papers to describe a situation in which a new method is *at least as good* as a baseline on every measured dimension and *strictly better* on at least one of them.  In the BitNet b1.58 paper the authors apply this idea to the three inference‑time quantities that matter most for deploying large language models:

* **Predictive quality** – measured by validation perplexity and zero‑shot accuracy on a suite of downstream tasks;  
* **Latency** – the wall‑clock time required to generate one output token;  
* **GPU memory consumption** – the amount of device memory occupied during inference.

The experimental tables (e.g., Table 1 and Table 2) show, for each model size, the perplexity/accuracy of the FP16 LLaMA baseline together with the latency and memory measured with the same hardware and software stack.  For every size $\ge 3\,\text{B}$ the BitNet b1.58 variant obtains *the same or lower perplexity* (sometimes even a small improvement) while its latency is 2–4× lower and its memory footprint is 3–4× smaller.  Because no metric deteriorates relative to the full‑precision model, and at least one metric (latency, and often also memory) improves strictly, the pair $(\text{accuracy},\text{latency},\text{memory})$ lies on a **Pareto‑dominant frontier** with respect to the FP16 baseline.  That is exactly what the authors mean when they say “BitNet b1.58 is a Pareto improvement”.

### How the authors evaluate Pareto optimality

1. **Same training budget** – Both families are trained from scratch on the same number of tokens ($100\,\text{B}$) with identical hyper‑parameters except for the quantisation scheme.  This ensures that any difference in final quality cannot be blamed on extra data or longer training.

2. **Identical inference stack** – The latency and memory numbers are obtained with FasterTransformer (for FP16) and with the 2‑bit Ladder kernel (for BitNet) on the same GPU hardware (A100).  Because the software stack is fixed, the reported speed‑up is attributable to the arithmetic changes introduced by ternary weights, not to a more favourable runtime environment.

3. **Point‑wise comparison** – For each model size they plot a single point in the three‑dimensional space $(\text{accuracy},\text{latency},\text{memory})$.  The FP16 point and the BitNet point are then compared directly: if BitNet’s accuracy $\ge$ FP16’s accuracy **and** BitNet’s latency $\le$ FP16’s latency **and** BitNet’s memory $\le$ FP16’s memory, with at least one strict inequality, it is declared a Pareto improvement.

Because all reported results satisfy these conditions, the claim is justified within the scope of those three metrics.

### What is *not* part of their Pareto analysis

The paper does **not** treat other potentially relevant dimensions such as:

* **Training cost** – The number of FLOPs required to train a 1.58‑bit model is roughly the same as for FP16 (the forward pass still involves matrix multiplications, albeit with integer additions).  The authors do not present wall‑clock training time or energy consumption for the pre‑training phase, so this dimension is excluded from their Pareto diagram.

* **Hardware compatibility / implementation effort** – While they mention that BitNet can be integrated into existing open‑source stacks (HuggingFace, vLLM, llama.cpp) and that specialized 2‑bit kernels are already available, no systematic metric (e.g., percentage of code changes required, support across different accelerator families) is quantified.  Thus hardware portability is an implicit assumption rather than an evaluated axis.

* **Model robustness or calibration** – Aspects such as out‑of‑distribution performance, calibration error, or susceptibility to adversarial prompts are not measured; consequently they do not affect the Pareto claim.

In short, the “Pareto improvement” label in this work is confined to *inference‑time quality vs. efficiency* under a fixed training regime.  Within that bounded set of criteria—accuracy (perplexity/zero‑shot), latency per token, and GPU memory usage—the BitNet b1.58 models dominate their FP16 LLaMA counterparts, satisfying the formal definition of Pareto dominance.  Any additional factors (training energy, broader hardware support, etc.) would require extending the Pareto analysis to higher dimensions; they are simply outside the scope of what is presented in the paper.
