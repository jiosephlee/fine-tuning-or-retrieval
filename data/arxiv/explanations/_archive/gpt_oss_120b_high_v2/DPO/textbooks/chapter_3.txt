\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: 3. Human Preferences as Training Signals  

Large language models acquire broad world knowledge through unsupervised pre‑training, yet their raw generations reflect the uncontrolled mixture of goals present in the training corpus.  To endow such models with safe and helpful behavior practitioners attach **human preference signals** after the initial pre‑training phase.  In contemporary reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipelines this signal takes the form of *pairwise* judgments rather than absolute quality scores.  The following sections explain why comparative feedback is favored, how it is gathered at scale, what synthetic alternatives exist, how raw prompts are turned into labeled comparison triples, and which datasets currently serve as benchmarks for research on learning from preferences.

# Types of Feedback: Scalar Ratings vs Pairwise Comparisons  

Human evaluators can be asked either to assign a numeric **rating** \(s(x,y)\in \mathbb R\) to a single completion \(y\) given a prompt \(x\), or to indicate which of two presented completions is better according to some criterion (helpfulness, safety, relevance, …).  Scalar ratings appear attractive because they provide an immediate notion of “how good” an answer is, yet practical experience documented throughout the RLHF literature shows several obstacles.  First, judges must internally calibrate their rating scales across examples and across themselves—a process prone to drift and personal bias, especially when evaluations span multiple sessions or diverse annotators.  Second, numeric scores tend to have high variance; small differences in rating rarely convey reliable ordering information when many examples are considered jointly.

By contrast, **pairwise comparisons** ask respondents only a binary question—“which answer do you prefer?”—which drastically reduces cognitive load and eliminates the need for an external anchor point such as “zero represents neutral”.  Humans are reliably able to judge relative quality even when both answers are mediocre or both are excellent; this consistency is reflected mathematically by the Bradley–Terry (BT) formulation employed throughout modern preference‑learning work~\citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}.  Under BT the probability that a judge prefers answer \(y_{w}\) over \(y_{l}\) given context \(x\) follows  

\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)=
    \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
         {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+
          \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)} ,
    \tag{\ref{eq:bradley-terry}}
\]  

where \(r^{*}\) denotes an unknown latent reward function mapping prompt–completion pairs onto real numbers.  
Because only *differences* \(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\) matter for predicting choices,
the absolute scale of \(r^{*}\) is irrelevant—a fact formalised later via Lemma 1 showing that any two rewards differing only by a prompt‑dependent constant induce identical preference distributions.
Consequently learning algorithms can treat each comparison as a binary classification problem with logistic link~$\sigma$, leading directly to the maximum‑likelihood loss  

\[
\mathcal L_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal D)=
   -\, \mathbb E_{(x,y_w ,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
      \Bigl[\log\sigma
      \bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
    \tag{\ref{eq:reward_model}}
\]  

where $r_{\phi}$ denotes a parametric reward model fitted on a static set $\mathcal D$ of annotated triples.
The binary nature of this objective makes it straightforwardly optimizable with stochastic gradient descent and robust against calibration errors that would cripple scalar regression losses.



# Crowdsourcing Workflows for Preference Collection  

In practice the construction of $\mathcal D$ proceeds through an *offline crowdsourcing loop*.  
The starting point consists of a collection of textual prompts $x$, often drawn from domain‑specific corpora such as movie‑review prefixes~(\citep{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}), Reddit forum posts~(\citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}), or open‑ended user queries appearing in dialogue corpora (\citep{bai2022training}).  

A **reference language model**, typically denoted $\pi_{\text {ref}}$, supplies candidate completions for each prompt.  In most RLHF systems $\pi_{\text {ref}}$ is simply the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) version $\pi^{\text {SFT}}$ obtained after initial instruction tuning~(\citep{ouyang2022training}).  For every chosen prompt $x$, two independent samples $(y_1 , y_2)$ are drawn independently from $\pi_{\text {ref}}(\cdot\,|\,x)$,
producing two plausible answers drawn from roughly the same distribution as any downstream generation task would encounter.

These candidate answers are presented side‑by‑side through a web interface designed for crowd workers
(e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk or bespoke annotation platforms).  
Annotators receive concise instructions stating which attribute(s) should guide their judgment—for example “Select the answer that most accurately addresses the user request while remaining polite”.  
Workers indicate whether $y_1$ or $y_2$ is preferred;
the system records both choices together with their originating prompt,
forming an ordered tuple $(x , y_{w} , y_{l})$
with $y_{w}$ denoting the chosen completion
and $y_{l}$ its counterpart.
To improve reliability Schmidt et al.\ have sometimes collected multiple independent judgments per tuple,
allowing noisy responses either to be resolved by majority vote or retained explicitly within probabilistic models;
however all published RLHF pipelines adopt at least one comparison per example because even single judgements capture strong ordinal information under Eq.~(\ref{eq:bradley-terry}).  

Once collected,
the raw annotations constitute precisely the data structure needed both for conventional reward modelling
(Eq.~(\ref{eq:reward_model})) 
and—via Direct Preference Optimization—
for direct policy inference without fitting an intermediate trainer branch.



# Synthetic Preference Generation Using Classifiers  

Human annotation remains costly when very large comparison corpora are required,
especially when fine‑tuning models spanning billions of parameters.
A pragmatic compromise consists in *synthetically* generating preferences through automatic classifiers whose predictions serve as proxy judges.
This approach appears explicitly in our controlled sentiment‐generation experiments,
where instead of asking humans whether response $y$ conveys positive affect,
we employ an off‑the‑shelf sentiment classifier trained on standard polarity datasets.
For every sampled prefix $x$, two continuations $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$ are obtained from $\pi_{\text {ref}}$;
the classifier computes probabilities $p(\text {positive}\mid x , y_i)$,
and whichever continuation receives higher positivity probability becomes $y_{w}$ while its counterpart becomes $y_{l}$.
Formally

\[
p(\text {positive}\mid x , y_w ) > p(\text {positive}\mid x , y_l )
    \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
    y_w \succ y_l .
\]

Because modern classifiers attain high accuracy on domain‐relevant sentiment detection,
the resulting synthetic labels closely approximate true human rankings while being infinitely scalable—the supervision budget reduces essentially to compute time rather than paid labor.
Nevertheless researchers keep an eye on potential bias introduced by imperfect classifiers,
and frequently cross‑validate synthetic indices against small sets of genuine human judgments before deploying them at full scale.



# Prompt → Multiple Completions → Labeling Pipeline  

The full end‑to‑end recipe traditionally employed across RLHF studies can be described step by step:

1️⃣ **Prompt acquisition** – A repository $\mathcal P$ containing diverse input texts $x$ is assembled;
   sources range from benchmark corpora such as IMDB reviews,
   Reddit TL ;DR subreddit posts,
   up until richly annotated dialogue turns used in helpfulness studies (\citep{bai2022training}).  

2️⃣ **Reference generation** – For each prompt $x$, invoke the reference policy $\pi_{\text {ref}}$;
   draw at least two independent generations
   $y_{1},\,y_{2}\sim \pi_{\text {ref}}(\cdot|\,x)$.
   When computational budgets permit,
   more than two candidates may be sampled subsequently filtered down through random pairing,
   although most published pipelines retain exactly one pair per prompt because Eq.~(\ref{eq:DPO}) already leverages every observed ordering efficiently.  

3️⃣ **Preference elicitation** – As described above,
   either crowd workers observe $(y_{1},\,y_{2})$ together with their prompting context
   and indicate their preferred completion,
   or an automatic metric supplies a deterministic ranking.
   The outcome manifests formally as $(\, x,\; y_w,\; y_l\,)$ satisfying 
   $y_w \succ y_l|\, x$.  

4️⃣ **Dataset assembly** – All observed triples aggregate into 
   
   \[
   \mathcal D = 
     \bigl\{
        ( x^{(i)},\, y^{(i)}_w,\,
                 y^{(i)}_l ) 
     : i=1,\dots,N
     \bigr\},
   \tag{\hspace{-0pt}}
   \]
   
   wherein $N$ denotes total number of annotated comparisons.
   This static set serves dual purposes:
   *As input‐side information*, it defines an empirical estimation problem for any parametric model predicting preferences;
   *As downstream supervision*, it fuels either traditional reward modelling approaches—
     fitting $r_\phi$ through minimizing $\mathcal L_R$—
     or Direct Preference Optimization where one directly minimizes
  
   \[
    \mathcal L_{\mathrm DPO} (\pi_\theta ;\, 
        \pi_{\text {ref}})
        = -\, {\mathbb E}_{(x , y_w , y_l)\sim 
        {\mathcal D}}
        [\, 
        \log\sigma 
        (\beta 
               {\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|\, x)}
                            {\pi_{\mathrm ref}(y_w|\, x)}}
               -
               {\beta}
               {\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|\, x)}
                            {\pi_{\mathrm ref}(y_l|\, x)}})
        ] .
      \tag{\ref{eq:DPO}}
    ]
   ] 

   where $\beta>0$ controls how aggressively new policies may deviate from $\pi_{\text {ref}}$
and where implicit rewards arise naturally via 
$\hat r_\theta(x , y)=
      β · {\log[\pi_\theta(y| {x}) / π_{\mathrm ref}(y| {x})]}.$  
   The loss therefore encourages higher log‑probability mass under $\pi_\theta$ 
   for preferably ranked completions while attenuating mass assigned their dispreferred counterparts—precisely what traditional RL objectives aim at—but without any inner reinforcement loop nor separate reward fitting stage.



# Dataset Statistics Commonly Used in RLHF Research  

Empirical studies have converged around three canonical collections whose statistics illuminate prevailing scales:

* **Anthropic Helpful–Harmless Dialogue Corpus** – As introduced by \citet{bai2022training},
  this resource comprises roughly $170\,000$ distinct dialogue turn triples—
  each consisting of a user query together with two assistant responses generated by a large language model,
  followed by a binary label indicating which response users deemed more helpful while remaining harmless.
  Because every entry already encodes $(x,\!y's…)$ format required by Equation (\ref{eq:DPO}),
  researchers may reuse it verbatim without performing additional sampling steps—providing immediately comparable benchmarks across papers employing Direct Preference Optimization or PPO‑based RLHF.\footnote{
    All methods discussed herein treat these entries uniformly as elements belonging	to	$\mathcal D$.}

* **Reddit TL ;DR Summarization Set** –
  The task dataset initially curated by Stiennon et al.~(\citeyear{stiennon2022learning}) couples forum posts ($x$) drawn from Reddit’s TL ;DR subreddit
  with crowdsourced rankings between competing abstractive summaries ($y_w$, $y_l$).
  While exact counts differ between publicly released splits,
  prior works repeatedly quote “tens of thousands” of labeled summary pairs—a magnitude sufficient for fitting high‑capacity transformer‐based reward models ($r_\phi$), yet modest enough that Direct Preference Optimization converges rapidly using standard minibatch stochastic optimization.\footnote{
    In Figure 4 our experiments employ this public set unchanged after tokenisation;} 

* **IMDb Sentiment Prefixes** –
  To evaluate fine tuning under controlled conditions researchers extract review beginnings from IMDB’s public movie‐review archive~(\citep{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}),
  couple these prefixes ($x$) with synthetic pairs generated via best‑of‐two sampling from an SFT decoder $\pi_{\mathrm ref}$,
  then let an off‑the‑shelf sentiment detector assign preferential labels rooted purely in positivity scores.
  Although this construction yields virtually limitless ($>10^5$ possible) comparison tuples,
  experimental protocols typically sample several thousand pairs so that statistical estimates reliably reflect underlying truth‐valued sentiment measure employed during evaluation.\footnote{
    Our implementations follow Appendix Section A regarding batch configuration;}

Collectively these three sources exemplify standard practice:
datasets usually contain anywhere between ten thousand up through several hundred thousand distinct triples;
coverage spans instructional prompting (\emph{summaries}), conversational assistance (\emph{dialogue}), and affective control (\emph{sentiment}).
Such scales suffice both for traditional REWARD learning---in which deep networks must infer latent score functions---and for Direct Preference Optimization---which learns policies directly whilst preserving identical statistical footing.



Together these components constitute the backbone upon which modern alignment methodologies rest.
By gathering *relative* judgements instead of absolute grades,
harnessing scalable crowdsourced pipelines or reliable automatic proxies,
and unifying those observations into neatly packaged $(prompt,\!preferred,\!dispreferred)$ triples,
researchers enjoy clear probabilistic semantics embodied by Bradley–Terry theory,
while simultaneously enabling efficient algorithms—including Direct Preference Optimization—that transform raw feedback directly into improved language‐model behaviour without costly reinforcement‐learning loops.
