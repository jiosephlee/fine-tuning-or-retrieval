\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does ignoring the partition function $Z(x)$ introduce any bias or approximation error in practice?
Question:
The derivation shows that $Z(x)$ cancels when forming preferences between two candidates, but does omitting $Z(x)$ from any other part of training (e.g., during gradient computation) implicitly assume something about its value? Could there be pathological cases where different prompts have very different normalizing constants that affect learning stability?
Answer:
The short answer is **no** – dropping the partition function \(Z(x)\) does not introduce a systematic bias in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  
The reason is that under the Bradley‑Terry (or more generally Plackett‑Luce) model all that matters are *differences* of rewards for a fixed prompt \(x\). Since \(Z(x)\) is a function only of \(x\), it appears as an additive term in every reward for that prompt and therefore cancels whenever a preference probability or a gradient with respect to \(\theta\) is formed.

Below I unpack why this cancellation is exact, what it implies for learning stability, and when one might see pathological behaviour.

---

### 1.  Where \(Z(x)\) comes from

The KL‑constrained RL objective (Eq.\,(2) in the paper)

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\E_{\,x\sim \mathcal D,\; y\ssim \pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|\,x)}[\,r_\phi(x,y)\,]
-\beta\,{\rm KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|\,x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\rm ref}(\,\cdot\,|\,x)\bigr]
}
\]

has an analytic optimum (Eq.\,(3))

\[
\pi_r(y|x)=
      \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,%
      \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
      \exp!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
      \qquad
      Z(x)=
      \sum_{y}\,
      \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
      e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}.
}
\]

Taking logs gives Eq.\,(4)

\[
r(x,y)=%
      \beta~\log%
      {\frac{\displaystyle \pi_r(y| x)}
                 {\displaystyle \pi_{\rm ref}(y|\!
                 x)}}%
      +%
      \beta~\log Z(x).
      \tag{4}
}
Notice that \\(\log Z{x}\\) does **not** involve the policy parameters \\(\theta\\); it depends only on the prompt \\(x\\).

### 3\. No bias in gradients

The DPO loss (Eq.\,(7))

```tex
L_DPO(θ)=−E_{( $x$, *y*w,* *y*l*)}
          [
           log σ(
               β·underbrace{
                  log ⎛πθ(w│✸)/πref(w│✸⎞−⎛πθ(l│✸)/πref(l│✸⎞}_{ĥ𝚛θ}
               )
          ]
```


contains only log‑ratios of probabilities. When we differentiate w.r.t.\ \(θ:\)

*the derivative of any constant term such as \(\\beta~\\log Z{x}\\)* vanishes,
because \\(Z{x}\\) has no dependence on \\(θ\\).

Therefore the stochastic gradient computed from (7) is exactly  
the maximum‑likelihood gradient for the original Bradley–Terry model—
no approximation or hidden assumption about magnitude \(Z{x}\\).

### 4\. When could “different \(Z{x}\\)” matter?

In practice there are two situations where one might wonder whether varying  
\(Z{x}\\) could cause trouble:

1. **Non‑Bradley‑Terry preference data** –
