\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### What is incoherent processing with an orthogonal matrix M and why does it preserve attention results?
Question:
The paper multiplies both Q and K by a random orthogonal matrix M prior to FP8 quantization (“incoherent processing”). I understand M · Mᵀ = I guarantees exact preservation of Q·Kᵀ mathematically, but I am confused whether applying M changes distribution of individual elements enough to improve quantization error without affecting downstream computations such as rotary embeddings.
Answer:
**Incoherent processing** in *FlashAttention‑3* is simply a random orthogonal mixing that is performed **just before** the FP8 quantisation of the query and key matrices.  
Concretely the authors draw a matrix  

$$
\vect M=\vect D_{1}\,\vect H\,\vect D_{2},
$$

where $\vect D_{1},\,\vect D_{2}$ are diagonal matrices whose entries are independent signs ($\pm 1$) and $\vect H$ is a Hadamard matrix. Because $\vect H$ is orthogonal ($\vect H\vect H^{!\top}=I$) and $\vect D_{i}\vect D_{i}^{!\top}=I$, their product $\vect M$ satisfies  

$$
\vect M\,\vect M^{!\top}=I .
$$

The algorithm therefore replaces  

$$
\vect Q \leftarrow \vect Q\vect M , \qquad 
\vect K \leftarrow \vect K\vect M
$$

before the values are scaled, rounded‑to‑nearest‑even and stored in FP8 format.



### Why does this leave the attention result unchanged?

The self‑attention scores are obtained from the inner–product matrix  

$$
\vect S = \alpha\, \vect Q\,\vect K^{!\top},
  
\a lpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}},
$$

and all subsequent operations (row‑wise softmax, multiplication by $\vect V$) use only $\vect S$. If we insert the same orthogonal matrix on both sides we obtain  

$$
(\vect Q\vect M)\bigl(\vect K\vect M\bigr)^{!\top}
   = \vect Q\,(\underbrace{\vect M\,\vect M^{!\top}}_{=I})\,\vect K^{!\top}
   = \vect Q\,\vect K^{!\top}.
$$

Hence every entry of $\vect S$ – and therefore every entry of the final attention output  

$$
\vect O=
      \operatorname{softmax}\bigl(\alpha\,\vect Q\,\vect K^{!\top}\bigr)\,\vect V
$$  

– is exactly the same as if we had omitted $\vect M$.  
The proof works irrespective of finite‑precision arithmetic because the mixing happens **before** any rounding takes place; after mixing we still compute the exact dot products (in FP32 accumulator) before casting back to FP8.



### What does multiplying by $\boldsymbol{\!M}$ achieve?

Although $\vect S=\alpha\,QV^{T}$ does not change, the **distribution of the individual entries** in $Q'M=QM$ and $K'M=KM$ does change dramatically:

* Each component of $Q'M_{i,j}$ becomes a sum of roughly $d/2^{k}$ randomly signed entries from row $i$ of $Q$. Large “outlier’’ values that appear only in a few coordinates of $Q$ are therefore spread over many positions with alternating sign.
* The resulting vectors have almost identical second‑order statistics – their variances become nearly uniform across rows and columns – which means that within any block used for **block‑wise quantisation** the dynamic range shrinks considerably.
* When we later scale each block to fit into FP8’s limited exponent–mantissa range (3 mantissa bits, 4 exponent bits), fewer entries hit the clipping threshold and the rounding noise becomes roughly isotropic rather than being dominated by a few huge spikes.
* Empirically this “random rotation’’ reduces the root‑mean‑square error of FP8 attention by a factor of about $2.6\times$ compared with naïve per‑tensor scaling (see Table 3 in §5.3).

In other words, incoherent processing does not alter the mathematically relevant inner products but *decorrelates* the data so that low‑precision representation harms them less.



### Does it interfere with downstream operations such as rotary embeddings?

Rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) are also linear maps acting on the head dimension:

```latex
$\tilde Q_{i}=R(\theta_{i})\,({Q}_{i}),   
~~
$\tilde K_{i}=R(\theta_{i})\,({K}_{i})$
```

where each $R(\theta_{i})$ is an orthogonal block‑diagonal matrix consisting of $2\times 2$ rotations. Because both RoPE and the mixing matrix $\vect M$ are linear, they can be **composed** without changing semantics:

```latex
$R(\theta_{i})(Q_{i}\,M)=(R(\theta_{i})\,M)\,Q_{i}.$
```

The composition $R'(\theta_{i})=R(\theta_{i})\,M$ is itself an orthogonal transform, so the inner products after RoPE remain invariant:

```latex
$(R'(\theta_{i})\,Q_{i})(R'(\theta_{i})\,K_{j})^{T}
   = Q_{i}\,M\,M^{T}\,K_{j}^{T}
   = Q_{i}\,K_{j}^{T}. $
```

The authors exploit precisely this property: they fuse the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform implementing $\vect H$ with the memory‑bound RoPE kernel so that no extra arithmetic or latency is incurred. Consequently incoherent processing propagates transparently through any subsequent linear operation that treats all heads symmetrically.



### Practical cost

Applying a dense orthogonal matrix would be $O(d^{2})$, which would nullify any speedup from using Tensor Cores. The chosen construction (${\pm 1}\,\mathrm{diag}\times H\times {\pm 1}\,\mathrm{diag}$) admits an $O(d\,log d)$ implementation via an *in‑kernel fast Hadamard transform* (often called “butterfly’’ or “FWHT’’). This extra work occupies only a few warp cycles – negligible compared with the GEMMs whose throughput dominates overall runtime.



### Summary

Incoherent processing consists of premultiplying queries and keys by a random orthogonal matrix prior to FP8 quantisation. Because an orthogonal transform preserves inner products ($(QM)(KM)^{T}=QK^{T}$), it leaves every downstream computation—including softmax and value weighting—exactly unchanged. At the same time it spreads rare large activations across many positions, making each block’s value distribution tighter and enabling much more faithful FP8 representation when combined with block‑wise scaling. The technique commutes with other linear head‑dimensional operations such as rotary positional embeddings, can be realised cheaply with a fast Hadamard transform, and therefore delivers substantial numerical accuracy improvements without any loss of model behaviour.
