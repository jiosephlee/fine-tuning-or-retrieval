\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why is softmax a performance bottleneck on H100 despite its low FLOP count?
Question:
The authors state that exponential units deliver only ~3.9 TFLOP/s while Tensor Cores deliver ~989 TFLOP/s for FP16 matmul on H100. Could someone explain why softmax can occupy up to half of execution cycles even though it performs far fewer floating point operations than GEMM, and how overlapping it with async WGMMA resolves this imbalance?
Answer:
**Answer**

The reason why the soft‑max step can dominate the runtime of attention on an H100 even though it contains far fewer arithmetic operations than the two matrix‑multiplications is that the two parts of the algorithm run on completely different hardware pipelines whose peak throughputs differ by two orders of magnitude.

On Hopper every FP16 *GEMM* is carried out by the Tensor‑Core array. A single Tensor‑Core can issue eight fused‑multiply‑add (FMAD) operations every clock cycle, and because an H100 contains 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs) running at 1.83 GHz this yields a theoretical peak of roughly  

$$
989\,;\;\text{TFLOP/s}
$$

for FP16 matrix multiplication. By contrast, the exponential required for soft‑max is not executed by a Tensor‑Core but by the **special‑function unit (SFU)** inside each SM. The SFU can perform at most sixteen independent transcendental operations per SM per clock tick, which translates into an effective ceiling of  

$$
3.9\,;\;\text{TFLOP/s}
$$

for the whole device when counting an exponential as one floating‑point operation. In other words, although computing an exponential costs only a single “flop” in an abstract operation count, the *hardware* that implements it can sustain only about $4/989\,≈\,0.4\,\%$ of the rate of a Tensor‑Core FMAD.

In the attention forward pass each block $B_r\times B_c$ of scores $\mathbf S=\mathbf Q\,\mathbf K^{!\top}$ produces exactly $B_rB_c$ exponentials (one per element). For a typical head dimension $d=128$ we have roughly $512\times$ more FMADs than exponentials in a single block, but because FMADs run at ≈ 256× higher throughput than exponentials their *wall‑clock* contribution is comparable: roughly half of the cycles are spent executing $\exp(\cdot)$ while the other half wait for or perform tensor‑core GEMMs. This explains why FlashAttention‑2 attains only ~35 % utilization on an H100—most of the compute resources sit idle while the SFU works through its exponential queue.

The paper solves this imbalance by **hiding** the low‑throughput soft‑max work behind the high‑throughput matrix multiplies using **asynchronous WGMMA** (warpgroup‑wide matrix‑multiply‑accumulate) together with *warp‑specialization*. A WGMMA instruction launches a tensor‑core GEMM but does **not** stall the issuing warp; execution proceeds until an explicit wait is inserted or until a dependent value is read back from registers. The authors therefore restructure the inner loop so that each iteration performs three steps:

1. Issue $\mathbf S_{\text{next}}=\mathbf Q_i\,\mathbf K_j^{!\top}$ with a WGMMA and **do not wait** for its result.
2. While that GEMM is still running, load $\mathbf V_{j-1}$, compute $\tilde{\mathbf P}_{\text{cur}}=\exp(\mathbf S_{\text{cur}}-m_i)$ and accumulate $\mathbf O_i←\mathbf O_i+\tilde{\mathbf P}_{\text{cur}}\,\mathbf V_{j-1}$.
3\. Only after both asynchronous GEMMs have completed does the code read back $\mathbf S_{\text{next}}$ and launch the exponential for the next block.

Because step 1 belongs to *producer* warps (which issue only tensor‑core work or TMA loads) and step 2 belongs to *consumer* warps (which execute floating‑point logic such as max reduction, subtraction, exponentiation and division), both units are kept busy at all times—a classic **producer–consumer pipeline** sometimes called “ping‑pong scheduling”. The explicit `bar.sync` barriers enforce just enough ordering so that data dependencies are respected while still allowing overlapped execution across iterations.

With this two‑stage pipeline each block’s soft‑max cost is amortized over two successive GEMMs: while one pair of tensor cores computes $\mathbf Q_i\,\mathbf K_{j+1}^{!\top}$, another pair already finishes $\tilde{\mathbf P}_j\,\mathbf V_j$ and feeds its exponentials into registers. Consequently the fraction of cycles spent inside the SFU drops dramatically—from roughly 50 % down to under 15 % in practice—allowing overall attention throughput to climb from ≈ 570 TFLOP/s (the level reported for FlashAttention‑2) up to **620–640 TFLOP/s** for FP16 forward passes and even higher when FP8 tensor cores are used.

In summary:

*The apparent paradox stems from measuring performance in FLOP/s while ignoring that different operations run on hardware pipelines with wildly different peak rates.*  
*Soft‑max contains few FLOPs but those FLOPs must be serviced by an SFU whose maximum rate is ≈ 250× lower than that of Tensor Cores.*  
*By restructuring attention into a producer–consumer pipeline that runs GEMMs asynchronously via WGMMA while simultaneously executing exponentials on another warp group, FlashAttention‑3 hides almost all of this low‑throughput work under high‑throughput tensor‑core work.*  
*The result is a much higher utilization of the H100’s compute fabric—up to 75 % of its theoretical FP16 peak—and consequently a dramatic reduction in overall latency.*
