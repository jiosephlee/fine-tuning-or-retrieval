### Why ordinary Dropout fails

Standard Dropout works by element‑wise masking  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf R}= \mathbf M\odot \mathbf R,
$$

where $\displaystyle\M$ contains independent Bernoulli variables and “$\odot$” denotes element‑wise multiplication.  
If we replace the true update by $\displaystyle~\tilde {\bf R}$ in a forward pass, the resulting matrix is almost never orthogonal:

* **Loss of unit columns:** Orthogonality requires every column vector of $\displaystyle~ {\bf R}$ to have unit norm and to be mutually perpendicular. Zeroing arbitrary entries changes column norms and introduces correlations, so generally  
  $$%
  \tilde {\bf R}^{\,!\top}\,\tilde {\bf R}\neq {\bf I}_ { d}.%
  $$

* **Rank deficiency:** Even a single zero in a row/column can make the factor singular. A singular factor destroys invertibility, which means that subsequent back‑propagation may encounter exploding or vanishing gradients because the product no longer has all singular values equal to one.

* **Breaking the parameterisation:** In BOFT each factor $\displaystyle~ {\bf \tilde B}{^{\, b}} _ {( d , i )}$ is obtained from a *skew–symmetric* generator ${\bf Q}_i$ via Cayley transform,  

$$
\tilde {\bf B}^{\, b } _ {( d , i )}=({\bf I}+ {\bf Q}_i)\bigl({\bf I}- {\bf Q}_ i\bigr)^{-1}.
$$  

Masking individual entries does not correspond to any choice of ${\hat {\bf Q}}_i$, i.e., there exists no skew–symmetric ${\hat {\bf Q}}_i$ such that ${(\hat {\bf Q}_i)}$ produces exactly ${\tilde {\bf B}} ^ { b } _ {( d , i )}$ with some entries forced to zero. Hence the optimisation would have to continually project a non‑orthogonal masked matrix back onto the manifold – an operation far more expensive than simply applying standard Dropout in additive settings like LoRA.

Because BOFT’s regulariser must stay *on* the Stiefel manifold ${\cal O}( d )$, naïve element‑wise Dropout violates this manifold constraint and therefore cannot be used directly.
