\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley transform guarantee orthogonality during training?
Question:
The paper mentions using Cayley parameterisation \(\mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\) where \(\mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^\top\). I am unsure how gradient updates on \(\mathbf Q\) keep \(\mathbf R\) exactly orthogonal at every step. Could someone walk through why \((\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\) always yields an orthogonal matrix when \(\mathbf Q\) is skew‑symmetric, and what happens if \(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q\) becomes singular?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Cayley transform is used in OFT/BOFT as a *retraction* from the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(d)$ onto the Stiefel manifold $\mathrm O(d)$.  
In plain terms it says “let us move freely in the space of skew‑symmetric matrices,
and whenever we need an actual orthogonal matrix we map the current point back
with a closed‑form formula”.  
Because this mapping is applied **after every gradient step**, the weight
matrix that actually multiplies a pretrained layer is guaranteed to stay exactly
orthogonal – provided two conditions hold:

* the parameter matrix $\mathbf Q$ remains perfectly skew‑symmetric;
* the matrix $\mathbf I-\mathbf Q$ stays invertible (i.e. it does not become singular).

Below we spell out why these conditions are satisfied in practice and why they
imply exact orthogonality.

### Why gradients on $\boldsymbol{Q}$ preserve this property

During training one does **not** update $\boldsymbol R$
directly.
Instead a set of independent scalar variables parametrises only the upper
triangular part of $\boldsymbol Q$.  
At every forward pass the full skew‑symmetric matrix is reconstructed,
the Cayley map is applied and the resulting $\boldsymbol R$
multiplies the frozen pretrained weight matrix.
In back‑propagation autograd first computes derivatives w.r.t.
the entries of $\boldsymbol R$, then propagates them through the closed‑form map;
the update therefore modifies only those independent scalars that appear
in $\boldsymbol Q$.  

Because these scalars are added/subtracted symmetrically,
the reconstructed $\boldsymbol Q$ stays exactly skew‑symmetric up to floating‑point
round‑off error – an error that never destroys sign symmetry again before the next
reconstruction.
Consequently **after each optimizer step** we obtain a fresh pair  
$(\,\boldsymbol,Q ,\,F )$, where $F=( {E})(… )$
and hence remains perfectly orthogonal **by construction**.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### What would happen if $\boldsymbol {·⋯}$ becomes singular

The denominator ``$(\,E−F)=({F−G})'' could become singular only if  
$\boldsymbol F$ had an eigenvalue equal to $+\,1$.  
For a *real* skew‑symmetric matrix all non‑zero eigenvalues lie on the pure–
imaginary axis ($±iλ$, $λ∈ℝ$); therefore none satisfies $λ=+ 1$.  
Consequently **in exact arithmetic** $(E−F)$ is never singular – its spectrum lies entirely off $+ 1$, whose modulus is at least $| E |\geqslant 0.$  

In finite precision arithmetic two practical issues may arise:

* **Ill conditioning** – when entries of $\boldsymbol F$ grow very large,
the factors $(E±F)$ become poorly conditioned; solving the linear system  
instead of forming an explicit inverse mitigates overflow/NaNs.
Implementations therefore evaluate  

```python
   R = torch.linalg.solve(I - F.T , I + F)
```  

or add a tiny jitter e.g. ``$(E-F)+ϵE’’’’'' before inversion.
* **Large learning rates** – an excessively aggressive update can push some
entries of $\boldsymbol F$ far enough that round‑off makes ``det(E-F)’’
effectively zero, causing gradients explode.
The cure employed in OFT/BOFT is simply a modest learning rate together with optional norm–clipping on $\boldsymbol F$ (or penalising its Frobenius norm), exactly as one would do when optimising on any manifold.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Connection with the butterfly factorisation used in BOFT

In BOFT each sparse block–matrix factor is itself parametrised by a tiny    
skew‑symmetric sub–matrix and transformed through its own Cayley map.
Because every factor belongs to ${O(d)}$, their product also belongs to ${O(d)}$
(the set of orthogonal matrices forms a group under multiplication).
Hence even after many factors – e.g.  

$$   
   {\cal R}=∏_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\cal B}_{(d,i)}
$$   

—the overall matrix stays exactly orthogonal throughout training,
and all arguments above apply unchanged at block level.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take‑away

* The Cayley transform maps any **skew‑symmetric** matrix continuously onto an
orthogonal matrix; the proof rests only on the simple observation that 
$(I±Q)$ are transposes of one another.
* Gradient descent works on the unconstrained *tangent space*
$\mathfrak {so}(d)$; after each step we recompute $\boldsymbol R$ via the closed form,
so exact orthogonality holds automatically.
* The denominator becomes singular only for impossible eigenvalues;
numerical pathologies can be avoided by stable solves or tiny regularisers,
and practical implementations adopt modest learning rates or norm clipping.



Thus the Cayley parameterisation employed in OFT/BOFT guarantees that **the model’s learned transformation remains perfectly orthogonal at every training iteration**, while keeping updates simple and inexpensive.
