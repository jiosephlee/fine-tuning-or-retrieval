# Orthogonal Finetuning Demystified: Preserving Neural Geometry During Model Adaptation  

*An introductory guide to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), why keeping pair‑wise neuron angles intact matters, how a learnable orthogonal matrix reshapes pretrained weights, and what you gain compared to standard finetuning.*

---

## 1. Why “neural geometry” matters  

When a transformer or diffusion model is pretrained on billions of tokens or images, its weight matrices encode a delicate geometry: each column of a linear layer can be thought of as a **neuron vector** living in a high‑dimensional space. Two neurons are related not only by their magnitudes but also by the **angle** between them.  

If during downstream finetuning we perturb those angles arbitrarily, we effectively scramble the relational structure that the model learned during pretraining. This can lead to:

* **Catastrophic forgetting** – the pretrained knowledge collapses when the model adapts too aggressively.  
* **Spectral instability** – large changes in singular values can make training noisy or diverge.  
* **Poor generalisation** – downstream tasks may over‑fit because the model loses the regularising effect of its original geometry.

Preserving **pair‑wise angles** therefore acts like a geometric anchor: it keeps the relative orientation of neurons untouched while still allowing the model to specialise on new data.

---

## 2. The core idea of Orthogonal Finetuning  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) tackles the geometry problem head‑on by *multiplying* each pretrained weight matrix with a **learnable orthogonal matrix**.  

Formally, let  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
\]

be the frozen weight matrix of a linear layer (e.g. a query projection in a transformer). OFT introduces an orthogonal matrix  

\[
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}, \qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_d,
\]

and rewrites the forward pass as  

\[
\mathbf{z}= \big(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0}\big)^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is the input activation and \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) is the output. Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, each column of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) is simply rotated (or reflected). Rotations preserve **inner products** and therefore preserve all pair‑wise angles among neurons:

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle
= \langle \mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{w}_j\rangle,
\quad
\forall i,j.
\]

Consequently, the relational structure encoded during pretraining stays intact while still giving us a trainable degree of freedom to adapt to downstream data.

### 2.1 Keeping \(\mathbf R\) orthogonal throughout training  

A naïve parametrisation (e.g., an unconstrained dense matrix) would quickly drift away from orthogonality under gradient descent. OFT sidesteps this by employing a **Cayley transform**, which maps any skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf Q = -\mathbf Q^{\top}\) to an orthogonal one:

```math
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)\bigl(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q\bigr)^{-1}.
```

During training we optimise \(\mathbf Q\); by construction \(\mathbf R\) remains perfectly orthogonal at every step. Initialising \(\mathbf Q=\mathbf 0\) yields \(\mathbf R = \mathbf I_d\), i.e., *zero‑initialisation*: at the start of finetuning nothing changes in the pretrained model—a safe starting point that mirrors conventional small‑learning‑rate finetuning.

---

## 3. What makes OFT different from ordinary finetuning?  

| Aspect | Standard (additive) finetuning | Orthogonal Finetuning |
|--------|--------------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Update rule** | Additive change: `\(\tilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf W^{0} + \Delta\)` | Multiplicative rotation: `\(\tilde{\mathbf W}= (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\)` |
| **Geometric preservation** | No explicit guarantee; angles may drift | Pair‑wise angles are *exactly* preserved |
| **Spectral norm** | Can increase arbitrarily → unstable training | Spectral norm of `\(\tilde{\mathbf W}\)` = spectral norm of `\(\mathbf W^{0}\)` |
| **Parameter budget** | Full fine‑tune → \(d\times n\) trainable params | Only `\(d(d-1)/2\)` free parameters for a dense `R`; often reduced via structure |
| **Inference cost** | Same as base model (if only low LR used) | Same as base model after merging `R` into `W^0` |

In practice these differences translate into **more stable optimisation**, especially for large foundation models where preserving pretraining knowledge is crucial.

---

## 4. Making OFT practical: parameter efficiency tricks  

A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix needs \(O(d^2)\) parameters – prohibitive for layers with thousands of dimensions. The original OFT paper therefore introduced a **block‑diagonal structure**:

```
R = diag(R1, R2, … , Rr),   each Ri ∈ ℝ^{b×b},   br = d.
```

With block size \(b << d\), trainable parameters drop to \(O(bd)\). While this works well, it forces each block to act independently on disjoint subsets of dimensions, which can limit expressivity (the overall \(R\) can’t emulate classic transforms like FFT).

The recent follow‑up work *Orthogonal Butterfly* (BOFT) solves this limitation by factorising \(R\) into a product of **sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly pattern**, inspired by the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. The key points are:

* A dense orthogonal \(d\times d\) matrix can be written as  
  \[
  \mathbf R = \underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf B}_1\, \tilde{\mathbf B}_2\, …\, \tilde{\mathbf B}_m}_{m = O(\log d)},
  \]
  where each factor $\tilde{\bf B}_i$ has only $O(d)$ non‑zero entries.
* This yields only $O(d\,\log d)$ trainable parameters—far fewer than $O(d^2)$ yet capable of expressing many classic linear transforms.
* After training we can simply multiply all factors together and merge them into the original weight matrix; inference incurs **zero latency overhead**.

Even if you aren’t ready to adopt BOFT directly, knowing that block‑diagonal OFT is just one point on a spectrum helps you reason about trade‑offs between parameter budget and expressivity.

---

## 5. Why preserving geometry pays off – deeper benefits  

### 5.1 Spectral stability  
Because an orthogonal left‑multiplication does not change singular values,
```math
\sigma_{\max}( \tilde{\mathbf W} ) = 
\sigma_{\max}( \mathbf W^{0} ),
```
the dominant eigenvalue remains fixed throughout training. Recent work links such spectral preservation to smoother loss landscapes and better generalisation.

### 5.2 Implicit regularisation  
Rotations are *norm‑preserving* linear maps; they cannot arbitrarily amplify activations. This built‑in constraint acts as an implicit regulariser, reducing overfitting especially when downstream data are scarce.

### 5.3 Bilinear similarity learning  
The transformed forward pass looks like  
```math
z_i = (\mathbf w_i^{0})^{\top}   \underbrace{\! \big( \! \mathbf R^{\top}   \! x   \big)}_{\text{rotated input}},
```
or equivalently  
```math
z_i = (\underbrace{\! (\!\!\;x^\top   \!\!)}_{\text{query}}\, 
      \underbrace{\!   (\!\!\;\! {\bf R})}_{\text{learned bilinear metric}}\,
      {\bf w}_i^{0} ).
```
Thus OFT can be interpreted as learning a *bilinear similarity* metric under an orthogonality prior—a perspective shared with metric learning literature.

---

## 6. When should you use Orthogonal Finetuning?  

* **Large language or vision models** where full weight updates are too expensive or risky.
* **Low‑resource downstream tasks**, e.g., few‑shot classification or domain adaptation with limited data—preserving geometry avoids overfitting.
* **Safety‑critical settings** where you need guarantees that fine‑tuned behaviour stays close to a vetted pretrained baseline.
* **Scenarios demanding stable training**, such as diffusion model conditioning or reinforcement learning from foundation models.

If compute budget allows slight extra overhead during *training*, but you want zero extra cost at inference time, OFT (or its butterfly variant) is an attractive default choice.

---

## 7. A quick checklist for applying OFT  

1️⃣ Identify the layers you wish to adapt (commonly query/key/value projections or MLP layers).  
2️⃣ Replace each target weight matrix `W⁰` with `R·W⁰` where `R` starts as identity via Cayley’s `Q=0`.  
3️⃣ Optimise only the skew–symmetric parameters (`Q`) while keeping `W⁰` frozen; pick a modest learning rate (same magnitude used in ordinary fine‐tuning).  
4️⃣ Optionally add *multiplicative dropout*: randomly replace some butterfly components with identity during training to improve robustness (especially for BOFT).  
5️⃣ After convergence, multiply all learned `R`s into their respective `W⁰`s—no extra parameters remain at inference time.

---

## 8. Looking ahead  

The story doesn’t end with the block‑diagonal version described above. By viewing the construction of an orthogonal matrix as an *information transmission problem* on a graph, researchers have uncovered richer factorizations—most notably **butterfly factorisations** that achieve near‐optimal parameter scaling (`O(d log d)` vs `O(d²)`).

Future directions include:

* Exploring alternative network topologies from computer networking theory that may further reduce parameter count without sacrificing expressivity.
* Combining OFT with other PEFT methods such as LoRA or adapters for even finer control over memory/compute budgets.
* Extending OFT’s geometric intuition to non‑linear modules (e.g., normalising flows).

---

## 9. Takeaway  

Orthogonal Finetuning provides a principled way to *tweak* massive foundation models while keeping their learned neural geometry intact. By rotating pretrained weight matrices rather than adding arbitrary perturbations, we preserve pairwise neuron angles, maintain spectral norms, and enjoy smoother training dynamics—all without incurring any inference penalty once finetuning completes.

Whether you adopt plain block‑diagonal OFT for simplicity or dive into butterfly‐based BOFT for maximal parameter efficiency, this geometric lens offers a fresh perspective on responsible and effective model adaptation.

# The Parameter Bottleneck in Orthogonal Finetuning and How Block‑Diagonal Schemes Try to Solve It  

*Why a full orthogonal matrix costs $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ parameters, how the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) paper chops it down with a block‑diagonal trick, and what we lose when we lock the sparsity pattern.*

---

## 1. Why “Orthogonal” Finetuning Matters  

Large foundation models—think GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion—are pre‑trained on massive corpora. When we adapt them to a downstream task we usually want **two things**:

1. **Preserve what the model already knows** (the rich semantic patterns learned during pretraining).  
2. **Introduce only a small amount of new knowledge**, so training stays stable and data‑efficient.

A neat way to achieve (1) is to keep the *pairwise angles* between neurons unchanged. If all neurons in a layer are rotated by an **orthogonal matrix** $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ (i.e. $\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$), then every inner product $\langle \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle$ stays exactly the same after finetuning. This property underlies **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** \cite{qiu2023controlling}:  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ is the frozen pretrained weight matrix and $\mathbf{x}$ is the input activation. Because $\mathbf{R}$ is initialized as identity (via a Cayley transform), training starts from “no change at all” and then learns a subtle rotation that adapts the model without destroying its original geometry.

In theory this looks perfect—any orthogonal transformation can be expressed by some $\mathbf{R}$. In practice it becomes **parameter‑hungry**.

---

## 2. The Parameter Bottleneck: Full Orthogonal Matrices Are Expensive  

An orthogonal matrix of size $d\times d$ has $d^{2}$ entries. Even though orthogonality imposes constraints (the true degrees of freedom are $d(d-1)/2$), learning $\mathbf{R}$ still requires on the order of $d^{2}$ trainable numbers:

\[
\text{#parameters for }\mathbf{R}= \mathcal{O}(d^{2}).
\]

When $d$ runs into thousands—as it does in modern vision transformers or language models—the cost quickly dwarfs any realistic finetuning budget. For example, a $4096\times4096$ orthogonal layer would need roughly $16$ million parameters just for its rotation matrix alone, far exceeding what most PEFT (parameter‑efficient finetuning) methods allow.

---

## 3. The Block‑Diagonal Fix in Original OFT  

The first remedy proposed by OFT is **block‑diagonal factorisation**:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}\bigr), \qquad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, \;
br = d.
\]

Each block $\mathbf{R}_{i}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix (often parametrised by a small skew‑symmetric matrix via Cayley transform). Because each block lives in a sub‑space of dimension $b$, the number of free parameters drops dramatically:

\[
\text{#parameters for block‑diag }\approx r \cdot \frac{b(b-1)}{2}
    = \mathcal{O}(bd).
\]

If we pick a modest block size—say $b=64$ for $d=4096$—the total becomes roughly $250\,000$ parameters instead of millions. This simple structural constraint lets us stay within typical PEFT budgets while still preserving orthogonality **within each block**.

### How It Works in Practice  

* **Zero initialization:** All blocks start as identity matrices, so training begins from the exact pretrained model.
* **Cayley transform:** Guarantees each $\mathbf{R}_{i}$ stays orthogonal throughout optimisation.
* **Layer‑wide sharing:** The same block‑diagonal $\mathbf{R}$ multiplies every row of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$, preserving intra‑block geometry while allowing inter‑block adaptation through other network components (e.g., layer norms).

---

## 4. What We Lose When We Freeze the Sparsity Pattern  

The price for saving parameters is **expressiveness**. A block‑diagonal $\mathbf{R}$ imposes two hard constraints:

1. **No cross‑block mixing:** Information from dimensions belonging to one block never reaches another block during the rotation.
2. **Fixed sparsity pattern:** The nonzero entries are locked into $b\times b$ diagonal blocks irrespective of data or task.

### 4.1 Limited Ability to Approximate Classic Transforms  

Classic linear transforms such as the **Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)** or **Hadamard transform** are dense—they couple every input dimension to every output dimension. A pure block‑diagonal matrix cannot emulate these operations because each column lives entirely inside its own subspace. The OFT paper notes that “the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix reduces expressiveness and cannot approximate classic linear transforms.” Consequently, tasks that benefit from global mixing of features may suffer under this restrictive structure.

### 4.2 Trade‑off Between Block Size and Performance  

| Block size $b$ | Parameters ($\approx bd$) | Expressiveness | Typical behaviour |
|----------------|---------------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| Small ($b=8$)  | Very few                  | Low – each subspace is tiny | Fast convergence but sometimes underfits |
| Medium ($b=64$) | Moderate                 | Moderate – more intra‑block interactions | Good compromise for many vision tasks |
| Large ($b=d$)   | Full $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ | Max – essentially unrestricted | Same expressive power as full OFT but loses parameter savings |

In short, *the smaller the blocks*, the cheaper the method—but also the more likely that important cross‑dimensional relationships are ignored. Conversely, *larger blocks* regain expressivity at the cost of more parameters, eventually converging back to the original full‐matrix bottleneck we tried to avoid.

### 4.3 Inductive Bias from Fixed Sparsity  

From a learning theory perspective, imposing a fixed sparsity pattern injects an **inductive bias**: we favor solutions where each group of features evolves independently. This bias can be beneficial if tasks truly decompose into nearly independent subspaces (e.g., certain modular vision problems). However, when downstream tasks require holistic feature recombination—common in language modelling or image generation—the bias becomes a liability and can lead to slower convergence or lower final accuracy.

---

## 5. An Intuitive Lens: Information Transmission on a Grid  

The OFT authors propose an *information transmission* viewpoint: think of building a dense $d\times d$ connectivity map by chaining together sparse matrices layer by layer on a $d\times(m+1)$ grid of nodes. A block‑diagonal factorisation corresponds to connecting each node only within its designated group at every step—a very restrictive routing scheme that never lets signals hop across groups.

By contrast, more sophisticated factorizations (e.g., butterfly structures introduced later in BOFT) create richer routing patterns while still using only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ trainable numbers. Though outside our present focus, this analogy helps illustrate why *fixed* sparsity hurts expressiveness: you are essentially wiring a network with dead ends between groups.

---

## 6. Bottom Line: When Block‑Diagonal OFT Is Worth It  

*Block‑diagonal OFT* provides a clean answer to the quadratic parameter problem:

- ✅ **Parameter reduction** from $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ down to $\mathcal O(bd)$ with minimal engineering effort.
- ✅ **Preserves intra‑block geometry**, keeping many benefits of pure orthogonal updates (stable gradients, preserved spectral norm).
- ❌ **Loses cross‑block interaction**, limiting ability to capture global patterns or emulate classic dense linear transforms.
- ❌ **Fixed sparsity induces an inductive bias** that may be misaligned with certain downstream tasks.

If you are finetuning a vision transformer where most useful information lives locally within channel groups—or you simply need an ultra‑lightweight adaptation head—block‑diagonal OFT can be an excellent baseline. For tasks demanding richer feature mixing (large language models, text‐to‐image diffusion control), you may soon hit its expressivity ceiling and will have to look at alternatives such as *Orthogonal Butterfly* finetuning (BOFT), which replaces static blocks with more flexible sparse factorizations while retaining an $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ parameter budget.

In short, the block‑diagonal trick is an elegant first step toward making orthogonal finetuning practical—but it comes with a clear tradeoff between *parameter efficiency* and *expressive power*. Understanding where your downstream problem sits on this spectrum will tell you whether this compromise is acceptable or whether you should explore richer factorizations that retain efficiency without freezing all cross­dimensional paths.



# Seeing Orthogonal Matrices as Information Networks: The Transmission Perspective  

*In recent work on adapting huge foundation models, a fresh way of looking at orthogonal finetuning has emerged. By treating the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information‑flow problem on a simple grid graph, researchers have uncovered why certain sparse factorizations—most notably the butterfly structure—are so effective. In this post we unpack that “information transmission” view, illustrate how sparsity patterns translate into connectivity constraints, and show how it guides us to a highly parameter‑efficient finetuning scheme.*

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Finetuning?  

When we adapt a pretrained model (a language model, a vision transformer, or a diffusion generator) we usually **freeze** most of its weights and only adjust a small set of parameters. A well‑known family of methods—LoRA, adapters, prompt tuning—adds low‑rank or auxiliary modules to the network. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a different angle: instead of adding something new it **multiplies** every weight matrix by a single layer‑wise orthogonal matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,
\qquad \mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I},
\]

so that pairwise angles between neurons are preserved. This guarantees that the geometric relationships learned during pretraining survive the adaptation step.

The catch? A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix contains $\Theta(d^{2})$ free entries—far too many to tune when $d$ runs into thousands. The original OFT therefore imposes a **block‑diagonal** pattern on $\mathbf{R}$, chopping the $d$ dimensions into $r$ independent blocks of size $b$ ($br=d$). While this slashes the trainable count to $\Theta(bd)$, it also forces each block to act in isolation, limiting the expressive power of the transformation.

Is there a way to retain *dense* interactions among all dimensions while still using only *few* parameters? The answer lies in re‑thinking what an orthogonal matrix really does—*it routes information* between input and output dimensions.

---

## 2. From Matrices to Grids: The Information Transmission View  

Imagine we want to build $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as a product of $m$ square factors  

\[
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{m}\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\dots \mathbf{B}_{1}.
\]

Each factor $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ can be interpreted as a **layer** of a directed graph whose nodes are indexed by the row/column positions. Concretely:

- The graph has $m+1$ *levels* (or columns), labelled $0,\dots,m$.  
- Level $0$ contains $d$ *source* nodes (the input dimensions); level $m$ holds $d$ *sink* nodes (the output dimensions).  
- Within level $i$, there are also $d$ *intermediate* nodes representing the state after applying $\mathbf{B}_{i}$.

An entry $[\mathbf{B}_{i}]_{jk}\neq0$ corresponds to an edge from node $k$ at level $i-1$ to node $j$ at level $i$. Multiplying by $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ is exactly propagating information along those edges. After all $m$ stages have been applied, the overall connectivity from source to sink is described by the product $\mathbf{R}$.

**Dense connectivity requirement** – For $\mathbf{R}$ to be a full dense matrix every source node must be able to reach *every* sink node through some directed path across the $m$ intermediate levels. In graph terms we need **full reachability** from level 0 to level $m$.

**Parameter budget** – Each non‑zero entry in any $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ corresponds to a trainable scalar (once we enforce orthogonality). If we can achieve full reachability with far fewer than $d^{2}$ edges then we have exactly what we need: a dense orthogonal transformation realized with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ free parameters.

The block‑diagonal OFT corresponds to a very sparse graph where edges never cross block boundaries; consequently many source–sink pairs are disconnected → an *incomplete* reachability graph and limited expressivity.

---

## 3. What Does a Good Graph Look Like?  

The problem now becomes combinatorial: **design an edge set across $m+1$ levels such that**

1. Every source–sink pair is connected (full reachability).  
2. The total number of edges is minimized *subject to each $\mathbf{B}_{i}$ being orthogonal* (which forces each level-to-level map to be a permutation plus possible rotations within small blocks).

A naïve solution would be to place **all possible edges** between consecutive levels—a dense bipartite graph with $d^{2}$ edges at each stage—yielding $\Theta(m d^{2})$ parameters and defeating our purpose.

Enter *butterfly graphs*. Originating from the Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm, they provide precisely the type of sparse but highly mixing connectivity we need:

- With $\log_{2} d$ stages ($m=\log_{2} d$) each stage contains only $d$ edges (each node connects to exactly two nodes in the next level).  
- After $\log_{2} d$ layers every source can reach any sink; indeed this is how FFT spreads frequency information across time samples.
- Total edge count scales as $d\log d$, far below quadratic while guaranteeing full reachability.

Visually one can picture an eight‑dimensional example ($d=8$, $\log_{2}8=3$):  

- Stage 1 pairs neighboring indices (0↔1, 2↔3,…).  
- Stage 2 pairs indices offset by two (0↔2, 1↔3,…).  
- Stage 3 pairs indices offset by four (0↔4,…).  

These “cross‑links” form the classic butterfly shape when drawn as connections between adjacent columns.

---

## 4. From Butterflies to Orthogonal Matrices  

A butterfly **factor** at scale $k=2^{\ell}$ can be written

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb R^{k/2},
\tag{1}
\]

where each diagonal block holds independent scalar parameters that later become rotation angles after applying an orthogonality constraint (e.g., via Cayley’s transform or directly as 2‑by‑2 rotation matrices).

A full **butterfly matrix** $\mathbf B(d)$ for dimension $d=2^{N}$ is built recursively:

\[
\mathbf B(d)=
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
\begin{bmatrix}
 \mathbf B(d/2) & \\
 & \mathbf B(d/2)
\end{bmatrix},
\tag{2}
\]

with each multiplier

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(
   \underbrace{\mathbf B^{F}(k),\, \dotsc ,\, \mathbf B^{F}(k)}_{d/k~\text{times}}
\bigr)
\tag{3}
\]

being block‑diagonal but with varying permutations of rows / columns across stages. Crucially:

- Every $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ can be made orthogonal by enforcing each tiny block in Eq.(1) to be an orthogonal transformation on its own 2‑dimensional subspace.
- Multiplying all such components across decreasing values of $k$ yields exactly the sparsity pattern of a butterfly graph.
- The total number of free scalars equals $(k/2-1)\times d$ per stage; summing over $\log_{2} d$ stages gives roughly $d\,(\log_{2} d - 1)$ trainable parameters – i.e., **$\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$** instead of $\mathcal O(d^{2})$.

In practice one may also enlarge each elementary block from size 2 to size $b$, forming **block butterflies** that trade off depth versus width while preserving orthogonality via larger rotation matrices.

---

## 5. Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT): Putting It All Together  

Armed with this factorization, BOFT replaces the single orthogonal matrix in OFT by a product of butterfly components:

\[
\boxed{
   \mathbf R(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
   \qquad 
   (\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^\top 
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} = \mathbf I_d,
}
\tag{4}
\]

where:

- $m=\log_{2}(d/b)+1$ determines how many stages are used,
- $b$ is the block size (with $b=2$ giving the classic butterfly),
- each $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ consists of many independent $b\times b$ or $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks,
- initialization sets every block to identity so that initially $\mathbf R=\mathbf I_d$, guaranteeing zero deviation from the pretrained model.

The forward pass through any linear layer thus becomes

```text
z = (R(m,b)·W⁰)^⊤ x ,
```

exactly mirroring OFT but with dramatically fewer parameters. Special cases recover known methods:

| $(m,b)$ | What it becomes |
|--------|-------------------|
| $(1,d)$ | Full‐matrix OFT (no sparsity) |
| $(1,b)$ | Block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$ |
| $(\log_2 d , 2)$ | Pure butterfly BOFT – dense yet only $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ parameters |

### Parameter Savings

| Model size ($d\times n$, e.g., transformer FFN) | Full OFT | Block‐diagonal OFT | BOFT ($m=\log_2 d$, $b=2)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trainable params per layer | $\mathcal O(d^{\,2})$ | $\mathcal O(b\,d)$ | $\approx 0.5\, d\,(\log_2 d -1)$ |

Thus on a hidden size of 4096 BOFT needs roughly **50 K** extra scalars versus **16 M** for full OFT—over two orders of magnitude reduction.

### Expressivity Gains

Even though BOFT uses fewer free numbers than block‐diagonal OFT with sizable blocks,
its structured composition can **reconstruct classic fast linear transforms** (DFT, Hadamard, cosine transforms) which are impossible under strict block‐diagonsl constraints.
A simple simulation showed that matching the approximation error of an OFT block size 16 requires BOFT with just nine stages and tiny blocks—a clear win in parameter efficiency.
Formally one can prove (see Theorem 1 in the paper) that multiplying appropriate alternating butterfly matrices can generate any element of the orthogonal group, confirming that BOFT’s hypothesis class strictly contains that of block‑diagonal OFT with equal block size.

### Training Tricks

- **Identity init:** All blocks start as identity so that early training behaves like exact zero‑update.
- **Multiplicative dropout:** Instead of zero‑ing activations like classic dropout (which would break orthogonality), BOFT randomly replaces selected butterfly components—or inside them selected diagonal blocks—with identities during training. This regularises without violating constraints.
- **Spectral preservation:** Because an orthogonal left‐multiplication does not change singular values of $\mathbf W^0$, BOFT inherits OFT’s guarantee that spectral norms stay unchanged → improved stability.

---

## 6. Why Does This Graph Perspective Matter?  

Thinking about matrix factorization as routing information through a grid does three useful things:

1. **Makes sparsity decisions transparent:** We can directly read off whether two dimensions ever interact by checking if there exists any path in the induced graph.
2. **Guides design beyond butterflies:** Any network topology known for efficient broadcasting—e.g., hypercubes or de Bruijn graphs—could be repurposed into an orthogonal factorization if its adjacency respects bijective mappings required by orthogonality.
3. **Bridges disciplines:** By importing decades‑old results from parallel computing and communication theory into deep learning fine‑tuning we gain mature tools for reasoning about tradeoffs between depth ($m$), bandwidth (edges per stage), and latency (number of multiplications).

In practice butterflies hit a sweet spot: they achieve full reachability with just enough edges ($d\,\log_2 d$) while keeping each stage trivially parallelisable—a win both mathematically and implementation‑wise.

---

## 7. Takeaways  

- Orthogonal finetuning preserves angular geometry but suffers from quadratic parameter growth when using dense matrices.
- Recasting matrix construction as an information transmission problem reveals that dense connectivity does *not* require dense factors; sparse factors arranged cleverly suffice.
- Butterfly graphs provide exactly such an arrangement: after $\log_2 d$ stages every input dimension influences every output dimension while only $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ trainable scalars are needed.
- The resulting method—Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning—subsumes both original OFT and its block‑diagonal variant and empirically outperforms state‑of‑the‑art PEFT methods across vision transformers, large language models and diffusion generators.
- Looking at linear algebra through a network lens opens avenues for future designs—perhaps even more compact topologies than butterflies could deliver similar expressivity.

If you’re intrigued by turning linear algebraic objects into tiny communication networks, give BOFT a try in your next adaptation experiment—you’ll likely see sharper performance without paying any extra inference cost!



# Butterfly Factorization 101: Turning Fast Fourier Tricks into Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning  

**What you’ll learn** – How the butterfly pattern behind the classic Cooley–Tukey FFT gives us a way to assemble *dense* orthogonal matrices using only **\( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \)** trainable parameters, and why this matters for adapting gigantic foundation models without blowing up the memory budget.

---

## 1. Why do we need “orthogonal” finetuning at all?

Large foundation models (GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, CLIP‑VIT, …) are pre‑trained on massive corpora and then re‑used on dozens of downstream tasks.  
Re‑training them from scratch is out of reach for most labs, so researchers have focused on **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** – methods that change only a small fraction of the weights while keeping inference speed unchanged.

Three popular PEFT families are:

| Method | How it works | Typical trade‑off |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| **Adapter tuning** | Insert small trainable modules between layers | Adds extra latency |
| **Prompt tuning** | Feed extra learnable tokens | Limited expressivity |
| **Model finetuning** | Update the *original* weights directly | No extra latency but risk of destroying pretrained knowledge |

Within model finetuning, **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** was proposed as a principled alternative to naïve gradient updates. Instead of adding an arbitrary perturbation, OFT learns an **orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} \)** per layer and multiplies it on the left:

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\qquad \mathbf{W}^{0}\;\text{: frozen pretrained weight}.
\]

Because an orthogonal transformation preserves all pairwise angles between neurons, it tends to keep the semantic structure learned during pretraining intact. The original OFT paper showed superior stability compared with low‑rank PEFT methods such as LoRA.

### The bottleneck  
A full orthogonal matrix needs **\( d^{2} \)** entries – far too many for typical layers where \( d \) can be several thousands. The authors therefore introduced a **block‑diagonal** restriction:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1}, \dots, \mathbf{R}_{r}), 
\qquad \mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, \; br = d,
\]

which reduces parameters roughly linearly in \( b \).  
However, this sparsity pattern forces independent transformations within each block and cannot express many useful linear maps (e.g. Fourier or Hadamard transforms). The question becomes:

> **Can we build a *dense* orthogonal matrix without paying \( \mathcal{O}(d^{2}) \) parameters?**

---

## 2. An information‑transmission lens

Imagine each column of \( \mathbf{R} \) as a “source node” that needs to talk to every “receiver node” (row). If we factor \( \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{B}_{m}\dots\mathbf{B}_{1} \), each sparse factor \( \mathbf{B}_{i} \) defines a set of directed edges between two neighboring “levels” in an unrolled graph:

```
Level 0   →   Level 1   →   …   →   Level m
   (source nodes)                 (receivers)
```

*Dense connectivity* means every source has at least one path to every receiver after passing through all \( m \) levels.  
The goal is therefore:
1. **Dense connectivity** – full information flow across the graph.
2. **Minimum free edges** – use as few trainable non‑zero entries as possible while still respecting orthogonality (each \( \mathbf{B}_{i} \) must be full rank).

A naïve design would put a dense matrix at one level ⇒ \( d^{2} \) edges. The clever trick is to arrange *logarithmic* stages where each stage mixes only tiny subsets of dimensions, yet after enough stages every dimension has interacted with every other one.

---

## 3. From FFT to butterflies

The **Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform** repeatedly splits an \( N = 2^{L} \) signal into halves, mixes them pairwise with tiny \( 2\times 2 \) rotation matrices, and recombines them. If you draw the data flow you obtain the familiar *butterfly diagram*: each stage couples two “wings” at increasing distances.

### The butterfly factor

For an even integer \( k = 2^{s} \), define a *butterfly factor*:

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\qquad 
\mathbf{d}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}.
\]

Each diagonal block holds independent scalar parameters—essentially many parallel \( 2\times 2 \) transforms placed side by side.

### Recursive construction

Let \( d = 2^{N} \). A *butterfly matrix* \( \mathbf{B}(d) \) is built recursively:

\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf B_{1}\!\left(\tfrac d2\right)&0\\[3pt]
0&\mathbf B_{2}\!\left(\tfrac d2\right)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(
     \mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\, 
     \dots,\,
     \mathbf B^{F}_{d/k}(k)
\bigr).
\]

Repeating this decomposition yields

```
        B̃(d,d) → B̃(d,d/2) → … → B̃(d,2)
```

— exactly the cascade seen in an FFT’s butterfly diagram with **\( L = \log_2 d \)** stages.

### Edge count

Each stage \( \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k) \) contains exactly \( d \) non‑zero entries (each column contributes one edge). With \( L=\log_2 d \) stages the total number of trainable edges is

\[
E = d\,L = d\,\log_2 d,
\]

i.e., **\( \mathcal O(d\,\log d) \)** parameters—orders of magnitude smaller than the naive quadratic count while still achieving dense overall connectivity thanks to the cascading mixings.

---

## 4. Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)

The authors call their method **BOFT** because they treat every butterfly component as an *orthogonal* building block:

1. **Make each component orthogonal** – Use a Cayley transform or directly parametrize each embedded \( 2\times 2\bigr( or \( 2b\times 2b \bigr)\) block as a rotation matrix:
   - For a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^{\top} \),
     \( 
     \tilde{\mathbf B}= (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\,\mathbf I-\mathbf Q\,)^{-1}
     .
     $
   - This guarantees \( \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf B}=I_{d}.\)

2. **Compose multiple components** – Let  

   \[
   \boxed{\;
      \mathbf R(m,b)=
      \prod_{i=1}^{m}
      \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} , 
      ~~ 
      (\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} )^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=I_{d}
      }
   .
   }
   }

   Here:
   - \( m=\log_2(d/b)+1` is the number of stages,
   - \( b≥2` controls the size of each block (the “width” of a butterfly factor).
   
3. **Finetune using**  

   ```
   z = ( R(m,b)*W⁰ )ᵀ x .
   ```

### Parameter budget

If we use blocks of size \( b=2 (\text{i.e., pure } 2×2 \\text{'butterflies'}) and \(m=\log_2 d )\),

- Trainable scalars per layer ≈ \(½(b-1)d m = d\,\log_2 d.\)

Special cases:
- **BOFT(1,b)** → exactly the original block‑diagonal OFT with block size \(b.\)
- **BOFT(1,d)** → full unrestricted OFT.
- **BOFT(\(\log_{\,}{(​ ​?)​​}, b)\)** → fully dense orthogonal matrix with only \( ℴ(d log d)\  isn’t crazy!

Thus BOFT gracefully interpolates between sparsity for parameter saving and density for expressivity—all under a single unified framework.

---

## 5. Expressivity vs Regularity

### More power than block‑diagonal OFT  

The paper proves:

> **Theorem:** For any dimension \(d,\) stacking a sequence of butterfly matrices and their transposes can approximate any orthogonal matrix exactly.

Intuitively, by flipping the order of mixing stages you can emulate any permutation followed by arbitrary rotations—exactly what traditional linear algebra tells us about decomposing an orthogonal group into Householder reflections or Givens rotations. The key takeaway:

- With the same *block size* \(b,\) BOFT’s feasible set strictly contains that of block‑diagonal OFT ⇒ more expressive under identical parameter budgets.
- Yet BOFT does *not* revert to a completely free orthogonal matrix—its structured sparsity still enforces an inductive bias reminiscent of classic fast transforms (Fourier, Hadamard). This bias helps prevent overfitting while preserving enough flexibility for downstream tasks.

### Spectral sanity check  

Because we multiply only by an orthogonal matrix on the left,

```
W_finetuned = R * W⁰ = R U Σ Vᵀ .
```

Singular values (\(Σ​)\ are untouched ⇒ spectral norm stays identical to that of the pretrained weight matrix. Preserving the spectral norm has been linked repeatedly to training stability and better generalisation for deep networks.

---

## 6. Practical tips from BOOT

| Aspect | How BOFT handles it |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Initialization** | All skew‑symmetric generators start at zero ⇒ each component equals identity ⇒ zero init mirrors typical PEFT practice (“stay close to pretrained”). |
| **Regularisation** | Standard Dropout cannot be applied directly because updates are multiplicative (`R·W`). The authors propose *Multiplicative Dropout*: randomly replace whole butterfly components or random diagonal blocks inside them with identity matrices during training. |
| **Runtime** | Multiplying several sparse factors adds modest overhead compared with one dense edit in plain OFT; however after training you can collapse all factors into a single dense orthogonal matrix and fuse it into `W⁰`, incurring no inference penalty.|

---

## 7. Do experiments back up all this theory?

The authors performed an extensive empirical sweep:

- **Vision Transformers** – fine‑tuned ImageNet‑scale ViTs on downstream classification tasks.
- **Large Language Models** – adapted LLMs up to billions of parameters on instruction following and benchmark datasets.
- **Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models** – controlled Stable Diffusion generation using BOFT rather than additive prompt tuning.

Across these domains BOFT consistently **outperformed LoRA** (the current low‑rank gold standard) and also beat the original block‑diagonal OFT when matched for parameter count. In particular:

- For a layer size \(d=2048,\) BOFT reduced trainable parameters from ≈ \(4 M​ (\text {full })​\\text {or } 0.5 M​(\text {block diag })​\\) down to ≈ \(24 K​ (\text {butterfly})​​.\)
- Despite this compression it retained or improved task accuracy by several percentage points—demonstrating that dense connectivity plus structured inductive bias outweighs pure sparsity alone.

The empirical section also included a synthetic study approximating random dense orthogonal matrices: BOFT attained lower approximation error than its block-diagonal counterpart for comparable numbers of trainable values.

---

## 8. Takeaways & Where we go next

1️⃣ **Butterfly graphs give us cheap dense mixing** – just like FFT spreads frequencies across time steps using only log₂ N stages; here they spread “information” across neuron dimensions using only O(d log d) trainable entries.

2️⃣ **Orthogonality preserves geometry** – pairwise angles and singular values stay unchanged, which empirically translates into smoother optimization and better transferability than additive low‑rank updates.

3️⃣ **Structured sparsity ≠ poor capacity** – despite using far fewer parameters than a full rotation matrix, butterflies can reconstruct classic linear transforms and even approximate any rotation when stacked appropriately.

4️⃣ **BOFT unifies prior tricks** – it collapses back onto block‐diagonal OFT when you set `m=1`, so existing pipelines can adopt it incrementally without redesigning architectures.

5️⃣ **Open doors:**  
   - Faster training kernels for chained sparse multiplications could close the slight runtime gap versus plain OFT.  
   - The information transmission viewpoint suggests exploring alternative network topologies from computer networking literature—maybe there exists an even tighter graph than butterflies for our purpose!

In short, by borrowing centuries‑old signal processing wisdom we now have a practical toolkit for turning massive foundation models into lean, well‑behaved adapters—without sacrificing expressivity or inducing heavy inference overheads.

--- 

*Ready to try BOFT on your own model? The key steps are:*  

1️⃣ Build `m` sparse `tilde_B` blocks according to Eq.(5);  
2️⃣ Parametrize each via Cayley transforms so they stay orthogonal throughout training;  
3️⃣ Multiply them together on-the-fly during forward passes;  
4️⃣ After finetuning collapse everything into one dense rotation and flush it into your backbone model.* 

Happy fiddling with butterflies!



# Introducing Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning (BOFT): Combining Sparsity and Orthogonality  

*In this post we unpack the new **Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning (BOFT)** method, explain how it builds a dense orthogonal transformation from a handful of sparse pieces, why it only needs $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable parameters, and how a custom “multiplicative dropout” keeps the model from over‑fitting.*

---

## 1. Why Orthogonal Finetuning?  

When we adapt a gigantic foundation model (GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, a Vision Transformer…) we want to keep the knowledge learned during pretraining while still allowing task‑specific adjustments. A principled way to do this is **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**: instead of adding arbitrary changes to a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ we learn an *orthogonal* matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and replace  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x}.
\]

Because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal ($\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$), all pairwise angles between the columns of $\mathbf{W}^0$ are preserved. This preservation translates into two practical benefits:

* **Spectral stability** – the singular values of $\mathbf{W}^0$ stay unchanged, which improves training dynamics.
* **Regularization** – the model cannot drift arbitrarily far from its pretrained weights.

The Achilles’ heel of vanilla OFT is that a full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix carries $\Theta(d^2)$ free parameters—completely impractical for $d$ in the thousands or millions.

The original OFT paper tackled this with a **block‑diagonal** constraint: split $\mathbf{R}$ into $r$ independent $b\times b$ blocks ($br=d$). This slashes the parameter count to $\Theta(bd)$ but at the cost of expressivity—different groups of neuron dimensions are forced to transform *independently*, an artificial bias that can hurt performance.

BOFT asks a sharper question:

> *Can we build a truly dense orthogonal matrix while still keeping the number of learnable parameters sub‑quadratic?*

The answer lies in **factorizing** the orthogonal transform into a product of sparse orthogonal matrices inspired by the Fast Fourier Transform.

---

## 2. From Information Transmission to Butterfly Factorization  

Imagine each column of $\mathbf{W}^0$ as a node that needs to send information to every output node after finetuning. A dense orthogonal matrix provides exactly those $d^2$ connections. If we decompose $\mathbf{R}$ as  

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_m}_{\text{sparse}}\,
          \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m-1}}_{\text{sparse}}\cdots
          \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_1}_{\text{sparse}},
\]

then each factor $\mathbf{B}_i$ can be interpreted as a **layer** of a tiny directed graph; multiplying them sequentially “passes” information from the source nodes (first layer) to the destinations (last layer). The goal becomes:

1. **Dense connectivity** – every source must eventually reach every destination.
2. **Few free edges** – we want as few trainable non‑zero entries as possible while still satisfying orthogonality.

A classic solution from signal processing is the **butterfly graph** used in the Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm. In a radix‑2 butterfly network each node only connects to two partners at each stage, yet after $\log_2 d$ stages every input has communicated with every output. The total number of edges scales as $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$—exactly what we need.

### 2.1 The Butterfly Factor  

For an even integer $k$, define a 2×2 block‑structured *butterfly factor*  

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 
\mathbf{d}_i\in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}.
\]

If every $2\times 2$ sub‑block inside $\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)$ is an orthogonal rotation (or more generally any $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix), then the whole factor is orthogonal.

### 2.2 Building a Full Butterfly Matrix  

Let $d=2^{N}$ for simplicity. We recursively stack butterfly factors:

1. Split the $d$ dimensions into two halves.
2. Apply an independent butterfly factor on each half.
3. Interconnect the halves through another butterfly factor with block size $k=d$, then repeat downwards.

Formally,
\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\,\mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\,\dots,\,
                           \mathbf B^{F}_{d/k}(k)\bigr),
\qquad k=2,4,\dots,d,
\]
and the full **butterfly matrix** is the product
\[
\mathbf B(d) = \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
               \tilde{\mathbf B}\bigl(d,\tfrac d2\bigr)\,
               \cdots\, 
               \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2).
\]

Each component $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ contains exactly $d$ non‑zero entries—one per row and column—so they are trivially bijections once made orthogonal.

---

## 3. Orthogonal Butterfly Fine‑tuning (BOFT)  

### 3.1 The Parameterization  

In BOFT we replace the OFT orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ with a product of **orthogonal butterfly components**:

\[
\boxed{
   \mathbf R(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}},
   \qquad
   (\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{\!\top}
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf I_d .
}
\]

* $m$: number of butterfly stages (typically $m=\log_2(d/b)$).
* $b$: block size inside each factor (e.g., $b=2$ yields classic radix‑2 butterflies; larger $b$ groups more dimensions together).
* When $m=1$, BOFT collapses to the block‑diagonal OFT with block size $b$.  
  When $m=1$ and $b=d$, we recover vanilla OFT with an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix.

Each block‐wise factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is built from many $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal sub‑blocks that are either rotations or Cayley‐parameterized matrices. Because only the off‑diagonal entries inside these small blocks are learnable, an **effective** trainable parameter count per component equals
\[
\frac12(b-1)d .
\]
Hence overall BOFT parameters are

\[
N_{\text{params}} = 
   \frac12(b-1)d\,m 
   = \mathcal O(d\,m) .
\]

Choosing the classic choice $b=2$, $m=\log_2 d$, we obtain

\[
N_{\text{params}} = \mathcal O(d\,\log d),
\]

a dramatic reduction from OFT’s $\Theta(d^2)$ while still producing *dense* $\mathbf R(m,b)$.

### 3.2 Identity Initialization  

Finetuning methods usually start from exactly the pretrained model so training begins “close” to what we already know. In BOFT we simply set every sub‑block’s skew‑symmetric generator to zero in its Cayley transform:

```
Q ← 0   →   R = (I + Q)(I – Q)^{-1} = I .
```

Thus every butterfly component starts as identity; their product is also identity and our initial forward pass exactly reproduces $(\mathbf W^0)^{\!\top}\!\!x$. As training proceeds each component deviates smoothly from identity—no sudden jumps are introduced.

### 3.3 Multiplicative Dropout  

Standard Dropout works on additive updates (e.g., LoRA’s low‑rank addition). BOFT’s update is *multiplicative*: we apply an orthogonal matrix on the left side of $\mathbf W^0$. Dropping individual entries would break orthogonality, so we propose **multiplicative dropout** at two hierarchical levels:

| Level | What gets dropped? | Effect |
|------|-------------------|--------|
| Component‐wise | Randomly deactivate up to $p_1$\% of entire butterfly components | The corresponding factor becomes identity for that training step |
| Block‐wise inside component | Within each active component randomly zero out up to $p_2$\% of its $2b\times 2b$ blocks | Those local rotations stay as identity |

Because any dropped component or block is simply replaced by an identity matrix, the overall product stays orthogonal automatically. This stochastic “pruning” forces each active part to carry more meaningful information and reduces overfitting—especially useful when fine‐tuning on small downstream datasets.

---

## 4. Intuition Behind BOFT’s Expressivity  

Why does mixing many sparse transformations give us “the best of both worlds”?

* **Dense connectivity via few edges** – Like an FFT network where each stage mixes pairs of signals, after $\log_2 d$ stages any input can affect any output.
* **Structured inductive bias** – The pattern matches classic fast linear transforms (Fourier, Hadamard). Empirically those transforms capture global interactions efficiently; imposing this pattern nudges finetuning toward solutions that generalize well.
* **Controlled regularity** – While any full orthogonal matrix could overfit by freely rotating all directions, BOFT’s limited degrees of freedom keep it nearest to identity unless data demands otherwise.
* **Spectral preservation** – Because $\|\!\;\cdot ;\!\|_2$ is unchanged by left‑multiplying with an orthogonal matrix, gradients remain well‑scaled throughout training—a known reason why OFT already beats low‑rank methods like LoRA in stability.

The authors also proved formally that BOFT strictly subsumes block‑diagonal OFT for identical block size and can approximate any orthogonal matrix if enough alternating products are used (Theorem 1 in the paper). In practice, however, maximal expressivity isn’t required; moderate depth ($m≈6–9$) already yields excellent trade‑offs between performance and parameter budget.

---

## 5. Empirical Highlights (Brief)

The paper evaluates BOFT across three families of models:

| Model family | Downstream tasks | Comparison |
|--------------|------------------|------------|
| Vision Transformers (ViT‑B/16) | Image classification and detection | BOFT outperforms LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT with ~10× fewer parameters |
| Large Language Models (LLaMA 7B) | Question answering & summarization | Similar gains; stability observed even with larger learning rates |
| Text–to–Image Diffusion (Stable Diffusion) | Style control & prompt editing | BOFT matches OFT’s strong controllability while slashing trainable parameters |

Across all settings BOFT retains zero inference overhead because after finetuning its learned orthogonal matrices are multiplied into the frozen backbone once—exactly like standard full fine‑tuning but far cheaper to store and transmit.

---

## 6. Putting It All Together – A Quick Checklist for Using BOFT

1️⃣ **Pick your dimension**  
   Let $d$ be the hidden dimension of the layer you wish to adapt (e.g., hidden size 1024).

2️⃣ **Choose $(m,b)$**  
   *If you need maximal compression*: set $b=2$, compute $m=\lceil \log_2(d/ b)\rceil$.  
   *If you prefer slightly larger blocks*: pick e.g., $b=4$ or $8$, then reduce $m$ accordingly.

3️⃣ **Initialize**  
   Use Cayley parametric form with all skew matrices zero → identity initialization.

4️⃣ **Configure multiplicative dropout**  
   Reasonable defaults: drop 10 % of components ($p_1=0.10$) and 5 % of internal blocks ($p_2=0.05$). Tune if you see overfitting/underfitting.

5️⃣ **Training loop**  
   Multiply current $\bm R(m,b)$ onto frozen weight matrices before forward pass; backpropagate only through learnable entries inside each small block.

6️⃣ **Post‑finetuning deployment**  
   Collapse all factors into one dense orthogonal matrix once training ends and bake it into your model weights—for zero latency at inference time.

---

## 7. Closing Thoughts  

BOFT demonstrates that *sparsity need not sacrifice density*. By borrowing an elegant communication pattern from FFT theory—the butterfly network—we can assemble an expressive dense rotation using only $\mathcal O(d\,log d)$ tunable numbers. The method respects critical inductive principles:

* **Angle preservation**, guaranteeing semantic knowledge stays intact.
* **Spectral norm invariance**, stabilizing optimization.
* **Implicit regularization**, thanks to structured sparsity.
* **Flexibility**, because OFT shows up as a special case when you set $(m,b)=(1,d)$ or $(1,b)$.

If you’ve been using LoRA or plain block diagonal OFT for parameter-efficient adaptation and feel boxed in by either under‑expressivity or heavy storage costs, give BOFT a try—especially when you’re working with very large layers where even modest reductions translate into gigabytes saved across all fine–tuned heads.

Happy fine‑tuning! 🚀



# BOFT vs LoRA & Adapters: Which Parameter‑Efficient Strategy Wins?

> *A comparative look at the newest orthogonal‑butterfly finetuning method (BOFT) against the classic PEFT workhorses—LoRA, adapters and prompt tuning—across vision transformers, large language models and diffusion models.*  

---

## 1. Why Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning Matters

Foundation models such as GPT‑4 or Stable Diffusion have billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is no longer feasible for most research labs, so **adapting** a pretrained checkpoint to a downstream task has become the de‑facto workflow.  

Three families dominate the PEFT landscape today:

| Method | How it works | Typical parameter budget | Inference impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LoRA** | Adds a rank‑$r$ low‑rank update $\Delta\mathbf W = \mathbf A\mathbf B^\top$ (with $r\ll d$). | $2dr$ trainable params per weight matrix ($\approx$ 0.1–1 % of full model). | No extra layers → zero inference overhead after merging the update. |
| **Adapters** | Inserts small bottleneck MLP blocks inside each layer (e.g., down‑project → nonlinearity → up‑project). | $2dh$ params per adapter where $h \ll d$. | Slight increase in FLOPs because adapters are executed at inference time. |
| **Prompt Tuning** | Prepends learnable token embeddings (or virtual tokens) to the input sequence. | Usually a few thousand token vectors regardless of model size. | No change to weights → essentially free at inference; limited to language‑type inputs. |

All three approaches trade **expressivity** for **parameter thriftiness**—they either constrain how weights can change or add lightweight modules that steer hidden activations.

---

## 2. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): A Different Angle

Instead of adding parameters or new modules, **Orthogonal Finetuning** changes *how* each hidden neuron sees its incoming weights:

\[
\mathbf z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^\top \mathbf x,\qquad
\mathbf R^\top\!\mathbf R = \mathbf I .
\]

Here $\mathbf W^{0}$ is frozen pretrained weight matrix and $\mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ is an **orthogonal matrix** learned during adaptation.  

Why orthogonal?  
* Preserves pairwise angles between neurons → the relational geometry learned during pretraining stays intact.  
* Keeps the singular values of $\mathbf W^{0}$ unchanged → spectral norm is preserved → more stable training and better generalisation.

The original OFT tackled efficiency by imposing a **block‑diagonal** sparsity pattern on $\mathbf R$. While this cuts down parameters dramatically ($\mathcal O(bd)$ for block size $b$), it also fragments the transformation—different blocks act on disjoint groups of dimensions—reducing expressivity and preventing $\mathbf R$ from representing many classic linear transforms such as FFT or Hadamard matrices.

---

## 3. Enter BOFT: Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

### 3.1 From Dense Orthogonals to Sparse Products

A full dense orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ entries—clearly impractical for a 12 B‐parameter model where $d$ can be tens of thousands per layer. The key insight behind **BOFT** is:

> **Represent a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of several very sparse orthogonal factors**  

Each factor contains only $\mathcal O(d)$ non‑zero entries, yet their composition yields a dense transformation thanks to clever connectivity patterns inspired by fast Fourier transform (FFT) butterflies.

### 3.2 Information Transmission View

Think of each factor as a step in an information‑routing graph:

* Nodes = dimensions at a specific “layer” in the factorization chain.
* Edges = non‑zero entries linking nodes between consecutive steps.

To end up with a dense overall matrix every source node must reach every sink node via some path—exactly what FFT’s butterfly network guarantees with only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ edges total. In other words:

* **Dense connectivity** ✔︎  
* **Minimum free edges under orthogonality** ✔︎

### 3.3 The Butterfly Factor

Formally, a butterfly factor $\mathbf B^F(k)$ consists of four diagonal blocks packed into a $k\times k$ matrix:

\[
\mathbf B^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\quad \mathbf d_{i}\in\mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

By recursively stacking such factors with block sizes halving at each stage we obtain the **butterfly matrix** $\mathbf B(d)$ for any power‑of‑two dimension $d$. Making every factor orthogonal is straightforward—each $2\times2$ diagonal subblock can be written as a rotation via Cayley transform or direct parametrisation by an angle.

### 3.4 Parameter Count

For a linear layer of shape $d\times n$, BOFT with $m$ butterfly stages and block size $b$ needs only

\[
\frac{(b-1)}{2}\,d\,m = \mathcal O(d\log d)
\]

trainable scalars when we set $m=\log_2(d/b)$ and choose $b=2$. Compare this to:

* Full OFT        → $\mathcal O(d^{2})$
* Block‑diagonal OFT → $\mathcal O(bd)$
* LoRA ($r$ fixed)  → $\mathcal O(dr)$  

Thus **BOFT stitches together dense orthogonality and strong parameter thriftiness** without resorting to ad hoc sparsity patterns.

### 3️⃣ Additional Tricks

* **Identity initialization:** All butterfly factors start as identity matrices (Cayley’s skew‐symmetric part zeroed), guaranteeing zero deviation from the pretrained model at step 0.
* **Multiplicative dropout:** Randomly replace whole butterfly factors—or blocks inside them—with identities during training, analogous to standard dropout but respecting the multiplicative nature of OFT updates.

The authors evaluated BOOT (**B**utterfly **O**rthogonal **F**inetuning) on three families of foundation models:

> **Key observations**

These findings confirm that **the structured inductive bias introduced by butterfly factorisation is not merely theoretical—it translates into tangible gains on real-world benchmarks**.

# Why BOFT Generalizes Better: Theory of Expressivity, Spectral Norm Preservation & Inductive Bias

*In recent months there has been a surge of “parameter‑efficient finetuning” (PEFT) techniques that let us adapt gigantic foundation models without the cost of full retraining. Among them, **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** stood out because multiplying a pretrained weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserves the geometry of the learned features. Yet OFT still needs a lot of trainable weights when the orthogonal matrix is dense.*

*Enter **BOFT** – Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning – which replaces the blunt dense orthogonal map with a clever product of sparse *butterfly* matrices. The result is a finetuning method that keeps the nice geometric properties of OFT while dramatically cutting down on parameters and adding a useful structural bias.*  

Below we unpack three theoretical pillars that explain why BOFT tends to **generalize** better than its predecessors:

1. **Higher expressivity than block‑diagonal OFT** (and a formal theorem).  
2. **Exact preservation of spectral norms**, which stabilises training.  
3. **An inductive bias baked into the butterfly factorisation** that aligns the model with classic linear transforms.

---

## 1 From Block‑Diagonal Orthogonality to Butterfly Expressivity

### 1.1 What OFT does – and why it can be limiting  

In classic OFT a learnable orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) multiplies a frozen layer \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\):
\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]
If we naïvely treat \(\mathbf{R}\) as an unconstrained dense matrix we would need \(\Theta(d^{2})\) trainable entries – untenable for modern models where \(d\) often runs into thousands.

The original solution was **block‑diagonalisation**:
\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}),
\quad 
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\, br=d,
\]
which chops \(\mathbf{R}\) into \(r\) independent blocks each requiring only \(\Theta(b^{2})\) parameters. This reduces storage dramatically (\(\Theta(bd)\)), but at a price:

* The transformation only mixes dimensions inside each block – dimensions belonging to different blocks never communicate during finetuning.
* Consequently the reachable set of orthogonal matrices is severely restricted and cannot represent many useful linear maps (e.g., Fourier or Hadamard transforms).

### 1.2 Butterfly factorisation – sparse matrices that talk to everyone  

BOFT reframes the problem as an **information‑transmission** task on a grid of nodes (the paper’s Figure 2). Imagine we have \(d\) source nodes (input dimensions) that must reach all \(d\) destination nodes after passing through several “layers” of sparse connections \(\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{m}\):
\[
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{m}\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_{1}.
\]
If each \(\mathbf{B}_{i}\) is chosen cleverly, the product becomes a *dense* orthogonal matrix while each factor contains only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries — far fewer than \(d^{2}\).

The insight comes from the **Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform**. Its “butterfly graph” connects every source to every sink using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges across \(\log d\) stages. Translating this pattern into linear algebra gives us *butterfly factors*:
\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\dots,\mathbf B^{F}_{d/k}(k)),
\qquad
k=2^{t},
\]
where each small factor \(\mathbf B^{F}(k)\) has a very sparse layout (two diagonal blocks). By chaining together factors for decreasing powers of two,
\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\]
we obtain an orthogonal matrix built from *\(m=\log_{2}(d/b)\)* components each of block‑size \(b\).

Even when we pick the extreme case \(b=2,\ m=\log_{2} d\), BOFT needs only
\[
\tfrac12(b-1)d\,m = \mathcal O(d\,\log d)
\]
trainable scalars – orders of magnitude fewer than OFT’s \(\Theta(d^{2})\). Yet because information propagates through many stages, **every input dimension can affect every output dimension**, guaranteeing dense connectivity.

### 1.3 Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT)

> **Theorem 1 (from the paper).** *BOFT is strictly more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT with the same block size.* Moreover, any orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) can be approximated arbitrarily well by a product
> \[
>   \mathbf B_{d-1,1}(d)\,\mathbf B_{d-1,2}^{\!\top}(d)
>   \cdots
>   \mathbf B_{1,1}(d)\,\mathbf B_{1,2}^{\!\top}(d),
> \]
> where each \(\mathbf B_{i,j}(d)\) is a butterfly matrix.

In plain language: if you give both methods the same “budget per block”, BOFT’s composition of multiple butterfly components can span a strictly larger part of the orthogonal group than a single block‑diagonal matrix can. Empirically (Figure 3 in the paper), increasing depth \(m\) while shrinking block‑size \(b\) yields lower approximation error for the same number of parameters.

---

## 2 Spectral Norm Preservation – A Built‑In Training Stabiliser

### 2.1 Why spectral norms matter  

Training deep networks often runs into exploding or vanishing gradients if layer weights dramatically change their singular values during optimisation. A large spectral norm amplifies gradients; too small one shrinks them away—both hurt learning and generalisation.

### 2.2 Orthogonal updates keep singular values intact  

Consider an SVD of a frozen linear layer:
\[
\mathbf W^{0}= \mathbf U\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{\!\top},
   \quad
   \boldsymbol{\Sigma}= \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},…,\sigma_{n}).
\]
Multiplying on the left by any orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R^{\,}\)(i.e., satisfying
\( {\bf R}^{\!\top}{\bf R}= {\bf I}_d)\)
yields
\[
{\bf R}{\bf W}^{0}=({\bf R}{\bf U})\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\mathbf V^{!\top}.
\]
Since both ${\\bf R}{\\bf U}$ and $\bf V$ stay orthogonal, **the singular values $\sigma_i$ are unchanged**:
the spectral norm $\|\mathbf W^{0}\|_{2} = \sigma_{\max}$ remains exactly what it was before finetuning.

Both OFT and BOFT inherit this property because their learned $\mathbf R$ is always orthogonal (enforced via Cayley parametrisation). In contrast, additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA change $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ and thus alter spectral norms—often requiring extra regularisation tricks to remain stable.

### 2.3 Consequences for optimisation  

* **Stable gradient flow:** Because forward activations preserve energy (\(‖{\bf Rx}‖ = ‖x‖$), back‑propagated gradients also retain their magnitude across layers.
* **Consistent Lipschitz constant:** An unchanged spectral norm implies that each layer’s Lipschitz constant stays identical before and after finetuning—a fact linked theoretically to better generalisation [Miyato 2018].
* **No extra inference cost:** After training, butterfly components can be multiplied into ${\\bf W}^{0}$ once, yielding a single dense weight matrix ready for fast inference without runtime overhead.

Thus BOFT inherits all stability benefits of OFT while being far more parameter‑efficient.

---

## 3 Inductive Bias from Butterfly Structure

### 3.1 What “inductive bias” really means here  

An inductive bias is any design choice that restricts the hypothesis space in a way that matches expected structure in data or tasks. By constraining $\mathbf R$ to be a product of butterfly factors we:

* impose sparsity patterns known to efficiently transmit information,
* force each factor to be orthogonal,
* limit how dimensions can interact at each stage (pairwise mixing then permutation).

These constraints shape learning toward transformations that look like known fast linear maps—Fourier, cosine/sine, Hadamard—without explicitly encoding any particular task data.

### 3.2 Classic transforms emerge automatically  

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm shows that *any* discrete Fourier transform (DFT) can be expressed as a cascade of $2\times2$ rotation blocks arranged in exactly the butterfly pattern used by BOFT. By allowing each $2\times2$ block to learn its own rotation angle (via Cayley parametrisation), BOFT can reconstruct:

* The DFT,
* The discrete cosine transform,
* The Walsh–Hadamard transform,
* And many other “fast” linear operators used broadly in signal processing and computer vision.

Because these transforms are known to capture **global frequency relationships**, they serve as useful priors when adapting vision transformers or language models whose hidden representations encode such patterns implicitly.

### 3.3 Why this bias helps generalisation  

Empirically—see Table 4 in the paper—BOFT outperforms state-of-the-art PEFT methods on tasks ranging from image classification with Vision Transformers to instruction tuning large language models. The authors argue:

* The butterfly subspace is *larger* than block‑diagonal space but still *smaller* than unrestricted $\mathrm O(d)$; this middle ground yields enough capacity while preventing overfitting.
* Structured sparsity introduces an implicit regulariser akin to what happens in dropout or weight decay—but without sacrificing expressiveness.
* Since classic transforms have excellent numerical properties (e.g., unitary energy preservation), they naturally enforce well‑behaved parameter updates across layers.

All these points make BOFT’s hypothesis class well‑suited for downstream tasks that require subtle adjustments rather than wholesale rewiring—a sweet spot for high‑capacity foundation models where preserving core knowledge matters most.

---

## 4 Putting It All Together – Why Does BOFT Generalise?

| Aspect | What BOFT Does | Why It Helps Generalisation |
|--------|----------------|-----------------------------|
| **Expressivity** | Uses products of sparse butterfly matrices → dense orthogonal transformation with $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ trainable params | Can approximate any orthogonal map while still being far smaller than full dense parametrisation → richer adaptation space than block‑diagonal OFT |
| **Spectral Norm Preservation** | Orthogonal left-multiplication leaves singular values untouched | Guarantees stable gradient magnitudes and consistent Lipschitz constants → smoother optimisation and reduced risk of overfitting |
| **Inductive Bias** | Butterfly pattern mirrors fast linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard…) | Implicitly steers learning toward globally coherent operations that align with natural data structures → better out‑of‑distribution performance |

Because these three effects reinforce one another—more expressive yet structured transformations that stay numerically well‑behaved—BOFA successfully bridges the gap between *full finetuning*, which often overfits despite maximal capacity, and *tiny low‑rank adapters*, which sometimes lack sufficient flexibility for challenging domains.

---

## 5 Final Thoughts & Outlook

BOFT demonstrates how classic algorithmic ideas—here, Cooley–Tukey’s butterfly network—can enrich modern deep learning pipelines far beyond their original domain. By interpreting anorthogonal finetuning operation as an information‑transmission problem on a grid graph, we discover an elegant parameterisation that simultaneously reduces storage costs (\(\mathcal O(d\,log d)\)), respects geometric invariants (spectral norms), and injects useful inductive biases derived from decades‑old signal processing theory.

Open questions remain:

* Can other network topologies from computer networking improve upon butterflies?
* How does training runtime scale when many butterfly components are chained?
* Might adaptive depth or variable block sizes further sharpen the tradeoff between expressivity and regularity?

Nevertheless, current experiments across vision transformers, large language models, and text‐to‐image diffusion systems already showcase BOFT’s practical impact: ***better performance under tighter parameter budgets***—a compelling recipe for anyone looking to adapt today’s gigantic foundation models responsibly and effectively.

# The Road Ahead for Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning: Limitations of BOFT & Emerging Network Topologies

## Introduction – Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters  

Large foundation models have reshaped what is possible in vision, language, and generative AI, but training them from scratch is still out of reach for most researchers.  The practical solution is **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** – adapting a frozen pretrained model by updating only a small set of weights.  

A recent line of work proposes **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**, where each linear layer is multiplied on the left by a learnable orthogonal matrix $ \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} $.  Because an orthogonal transform preserves all pairwise angles between neurons, OFT retains much of the pretrained knowledge while still allowing task‑specific adaptation.  The main drawback is that a full orthogonal matrix carries $ \mathcal{O}(d^{2}) $ trainable parameters, far more than low‑rank methods such as LoRA.

The paper *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** – a clever factorization that shrinks the parameter budget from $ \mathcal{O}(d^{2}) $ to $ \mathcal{O}(d\log d) $ while still generating a dense orthogonal matrix.  BOFT does this by expressing $ \mathbf{R} $ as a product of **butterfly components**, each being a sparse orthogonal matrix inspired by the Cooley‑Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT).  This idea is framed as an **information transmission problem** on a grid‑shaped graph: every source node must be able to reach every sink node through a few sparse layers of edges.

BOFT has already shown strong performance across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models.  Yet every new method comes with trade‑offs.  Below we examine BOFT’s current limitations and sketch how ideas from computer networking could inspire even more efficient constructions.

---

## BOFT in a Nutshell  

1. **Orthogonal factorization** – Instead of learning one dense $ \mathbf{R} $, BOFT learns $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices  
   $$\mathbf{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$$  
   where each factor $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ follows a butterfly sparsity pattern with block size $b$.  

2. **Parameter count** – Each $2\times2$ block contributes $(b/2)$ trainable parameters (via Cayley or rotation parametrization).  Overall BOFT needs  
   $$\frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m \;\;\text{parameters}$$  
   With the classic butterfly choice $b=2$ and $m=\log_2 d$, this becomes $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ — orders of magnitude smaller than $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$.

3. **Zero‑initialization** – All factors start as identity matrices so that finetuning begins at the pretrained checkpoint itself.

4. **Multiplicative dropout** – To curb overfitting, random subsets of factors or sub‑blocks are forced to stay identity during training.

These ingredients give BOFT its appealing balance between **expressivity** (dense connectivity) and **regularity** (structured sparsity).  Nevertheless, several practical drawbacks remain.

---

## Current Limitations of BOFT  

### 1. Extra Training Runtime Overhead  

While parameter count drops dramatically, each forward/backward pass now has to multiply **$m$ sparse matrices** before reaching the frozen weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{0}$.  Even though each factor is highly sparse ($\mathcal{O}(d)$ non‑zeros), chaining them adds:

* **Additional kernel launches** on GPUs/TPUs,
* **Memory traffic** for intermediate activations,
* Slightly larger **compute graphs**, which can outweigh the savings in storage especially on small hardware budgets.

In contrast, block‑diagonal OFT or LoRA involve only one simple additive or multiplicative term per layer and therefore incur almost no extra compute at training time.

### 2. Expressivity vs Regularity Trade‑off Still Open  

BOFT improves over block‑diagonal OFT because butterfly graphs densely connect source and sink nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges.  Yet it does **not** span the full orthogonal group unless we increase $m$ dramatically or stack many butterfly blocks as suggested by Theorem 1 in the paper.  Excessive depth may reintroduce training overhead and can make optimization harder due to repeated inverses in Cayley parametrizations.

### 3. Sensitivity to Block Size ($b$) and Depth ($m$)  

Choosing $(m,b)$ determines both parameter budget and expressivity:

| Setting | Approximation error (vs random orthogonal) | Trainable params |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|------------------|
| $(1,b)$ = block‑diagonal OFT | High | $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ |
| $(\log d , 2)$ = classic butterfly | Low | $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ |
| Intermediate $(m,b)$ | Moderate | $\mathcal{O}((b-1)dm/2)$ |

Empirically, smaller blocks ($b=2$) with more layers tend to be more parameter‑efficient than larger blocks with fewer layers, but the optimal point depends on model size and task complexity — an extra hyperparameter tuning burden for practitioners.

### 4. Compatibility With Existing PEFT Toolchains  

Most open‑source PEFT libraries are built around additive updates (LoRA) or simple scaling/tuning heads.  Incorporating BOFT requires custom implementations of Cayley transforms on sparse blocks and specialized dropout logic, which may limit immediate adoption in production pipelines.

---

## Beyond Butterflies – Could Other Network Topologies Do Better?  

The core insight behind BOFT is that **dense information flow can be achieved with few edges** if those edges are arranged cleverly.  This is precisely a problem studied in computer networking: designing topologies that move data between many sources and many destinations using minimal wiring while keeping latency low.  Borrowing from that literature opens several promising alternatives:

| Candidate Topology | Typical Edge Complexity | Intuition for Orthogonal Factorization |
|--------------------|------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Hypercube** (dimension $\log_2 d$) | $d \log_2 d$ edges | Each node connects to logs’ worth of neighbours; repeated “shuffle” layers could mimic butterfly mixing while offering more flexible routing patterns |
| **De Bruijn / Kautz Graphs** | $d \cdot k$ edges for degree $k$ | Small diameter ($\log_k d$ hops) makes it possible to achieve dense connectivity with constant-degree sparsity; could lead to shallower factor chains |
| **Clos / Benes Networks** | $2d\log_2 d - d$ edges | Known to be rearrangeably non‑blocking; guarantees any permutation can be realized—potentially allowing exact representation of any orthogonal matrix with fewer layers |
| **Fat‑Tree** | $\approx d \log_2 d$ edges | Hierarchical aggregation mirrors multiscale FFT stages but allows uneven branching factors, possibly adapting better to non‑power‑of‑two dimensions |
| **Expander Graphs** | Linear in $d$ (constant degree) yet high spectral gap | Strong connectivity guarantees may lead to robust information spreading even with very few layers |

All these structures satisfy the two desiderata introduced in the paper’s information transmission view:

1️⃣ *Dense connectivity*: Every source node has at least one path to every sink node after stacking enough layers.  
2️⃣ *Minimum free edges*: Each layer contributes only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ trainable entries because each incoming edge corresponds to a free parameter under an orthogonality constraint.

A concrete research direction would be **formalizing “orthogonal factor” constraints on each topology**—e.g., enforcing that each adjacency matrix corresponds to an orthogonal permutation mixed with learned rotations—then measuring how closely the product approximates a random dense orthogonal matrix.

### Potential Advantages Over Butterflies  

* **Reduced depth** – Some topologies (e.g., De Bruijn) achieve logarithmic diameter with smaller base degrees than binary butterflies, possibly lowering $m$.  
* **Better parallelism** – Many topologies have regular patterns amenable to SIMD execution without needing index permutations unique to butterflies.  
* **Flexibility for non‑power‑of‑two dimensions** – While butterflies require $d=2^N$, hypercubes or expander constructions work for any $d$, removing padding overheads seen in practice.  

---

## Promising Research Directions  

Below we outline concrete avenues that could turn these ideas into practical improvements over BOFT:

1. **Graph‑Driven Design Space Exploration**  
   * Define a library of candidate sparse graphs (butterfly variants, hypercubes, etc.).  
   * For each graph compute theoretical bounds on expressivity (e.g., ability to approximate any element of $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ within tolerance).  
   * Use reinforcement learning or evolutionary search to pick both topology and per‑node parametrizations (Cayley rotations vs learned scalars).

2. **Learnable Topology + Parameters**  
   * Allow binary mask variables on potential edges that are regularized toward sparsity (similar to sparsity‐inducing penalties).  
   * Jointly train masks and rotation angles so that the network discovers its own efficient routing pattern rather than committing upfront to butterflies.

3. **Hybrid Factorizations**  
   * Combine a low‑rank additive component à la LoRA with an orthogonal butterfly component ‑ this could capture both coarse directional shifts and fine angular adjustments while keeping total parameters low.
   * Alternatively embed small dense blocks inside an otherwise sparse mega‑matrix—akin to “block butterfly” but where block size varies adaptively per layer.

4. **Optimized Kernels & Auto‑Fusion**  
   * Implement fused GPU kernels that multiply several consecutive butterfly / hypercube factors in one pass—reducing launch overhead dramatically.
   * Explore integer‐only representations for Cayley transforms so that multiplication reduces to sign flips plus few additions/subtractions—a potential speed boost on edge devices.

5. **Theoretical Guarantees Based on Graph Spectra**  
   * Leverage known expansion properties of expander graphs or non‑blocking guarantees of Benes networks to bound approximation error versus number of layers.
   * Derive conditions under which a given topology can represent any orthogonal matrix exactly when paired with enough layers—a counterpart to Theorem 1 for butterflies.

6. **Task‑Specific Inductive Bias Analysis**  
   * Empirically probe whether certain topologies favor specific downstream domains—e.g., FFT‐like butterflies may excel on frequency‐rich vision tasks while hypercubes could better suit sequential language data where permutation symmetry matters.
   * Tie observed performance differences back to spectral characteristics preserved by each topology’s adjacency matrix.

7. **Unified PEPE Framework (Parameterized Efficient Parameterized Embedding)**  
   * Build an abstraction layer where any sparse orthogonal factor can be plugged into existing PEFT toolkits alongside LoRA/PAdapter/Prefix tuning.
   * Provide automatic conversion scripts such that after finetuning the learned product collapses into a single dense matrix—preserving inference latency exactly like vanilla OFT/BOFT.

---

## Concluding Thoughts  

BOFT marks an elegant step forward: it replaces costly dense orthogonal updates with structured butterfly factorizations that keep both memory footprints and parameter counts modest while preserving angle information crucial for stable finetuning.  However:

* Its multi‑stage multiplication adds nontrivial training overhead,
* Its expressive power depends delicately on chosen block size and depth,
* And it inherits design choices rooted specifically in FFT butterflies—an arguably narrow slice of possible sparse connectivity patterns.

By reframing orthogonal finetuning as an information transmission problem on a graph, we open up a whole toolbox from computer networking—hypercubes, De Bruijn graphs, Clos networks—each promising denser data exchange with comparable or even fewer trainable edges than butterflies.  Systematically exploring these alternatives could yield:
* Faster training pipelines,
* Improved approximation guarantees,
* Greater flexibility across model dimensions,
* And perhaps new inductive biases aligned with particular downstream modalities.

The road ahead thus invites interdisciplinary collaboration: marrying graph theory and network topology insights with deep learning’s need for parameter efficiency and stability.  As researchers begin probing these richer families of sparse orthogonal factorizations, we anticipate not only lighter finetuning methods but also deeper understanding of why certain structural priors help large models adapt gracefully—and ultimately bring powerful foundation models into even more hands worldwide.
