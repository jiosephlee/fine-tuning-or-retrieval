\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why does applying LoRA only to query and value attention projections fail to match full‑model fine‑tuning performance?
Question:
Figure 1 shows that using LoRA adapters on all linear layers recovers full fine‑tuning quality, while limiting adapters to just Q/K/V matrices hurts performance.

* What specific information or capacity is lost when we omit adapters in feed‑forward layers or other projection matrices?
* Is this discrepancy mainly due to insufficient representational power at low rank \(r\), or because gradient signals cannot propagate effectively through frozen layers?
* Are there any ablation results indicating which additional layer types contribute most to closing the gap?

Answer:
In the QLoRA paper the authors discovered that a “classic’’ LoRA configuration—​adding low‑rank updates only to the query (Q) and value (V) projection matrices inside each multi‑head attention (MHA) block—​fails to recover the quality obtained by full‑model fine‑tuning.  When they extend the adapters so that **every linear layer** in a transformer block (the three attention projections Q,K,V, the output projection O, *and* the two feed‑forward (FFN) weight matrices) receives a LoRA pair $(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2)$, the gap disappears (Figure 1).  Below I explain why this happens, what is lost when we leave those extra matrices frozen, and what the ablations in the paper tell us about which components matter most.

---

### 1. What is omitted when we keep only Q/K/V adaptable?

A transformer block consists of two sub‑modules:

1. **Multi‑head attention** – three input projections $W_Q,W_K,W_V\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times h}$ followed by a softmax over $QK^\top$ and a final output projection $W_O\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times h}$.
2. **Feed‑forward network** – a first expansion $W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times 4h}$, a non‑linear activation (usually GELU), and a contraction $W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{4h\times h}$.

In LLaMA‑style models more than two‑thirds of all trainable parameters reside in $W_1$ and $W_2$ (the FFN).  Those matrices perform *non‑linear* transformations that reshape the representation after attention has mixed information across tokens.  If we freeze $W_1$ and $W_2$, the model can alter **only** how token vectors are projected into queries/values before they are combined; it cannot change how those combined vectors are subsequently “processed’’ by the FFN.

Consequences of this restriction are threefold:

* **Loss of expressive capacity** – the low‑rank update $\Delta W = s\,L_1L_2$ applied to $W_Q$ or $W_V$ can change *where* in token space attention looks, but it cannot create new non‑linear feature maps that would be needed for many instruction‑following behaviours (e.g., re‑weighting facts, adjusting style, or learning task‑specific algebraic tricks).  
* **Insufficient utilisation of parameter budget** – even with a modest rank $r$, adding adapters on **every** linear map yields an order of magnitude more tunable degrees of freedom than restricting them to three projections per layer.  This extra capacity is what allows QLoRA to match full fine‑tuning despite keeping all base weights frozen.
* **Limited gradient leverage** – during back‑propagation the error signal reaches each adapted matrix multiplied by its associated input activation ($X$) and downstream fixed weight ($W$).  When $W_O$, $W_1$, or $W_2$ remain frozen, their static structure constrains how an update in $Q$ or $V$ propagates through later transformations, so many useful directions in parameter space simply cannot be explored.

Therefore the information that is “lost’’ when we omit adapters from FFN and output‐projection layers is precisely the *non‑linear reshaping* capacity that these huge weight blocks provide.

---

### 2. Is the gap caused mainly by a too small rank $r$, or by blocked gradient flow?

The experiments reported in Section 4 (“Default LoRA hyperparameters do not match 16-bit performance”) make this clear:

* The authors vary $r$ while keeping adapters on *all* linear layers and observe virtually no change in final accuracy (Appendix \\ref{app:exp-setup}).  Thus *rank size itself is not the bottleneck* once enough locations receive updates.
* When they restrict adapters **only** to $(Q,V)$ (the canonical configuration used in early LoRA papers), even increasing $r$ dramatically does not close the gap.  The missing degrees of freedom stem from *where* additional parameters are placed rather than how many per location.
* Gradient signals do indeed travel through frozen weights—the forward/backward pass of QLoRA explicitly dequantises each frozen matrix so its Jacobian contributes to $\partial E/\partial L_i$.  However, because most of the Jacobian belongs to unfine‑tuned large matrices ($W_O$, $W_1$, $W_2$), their contribution remains static; thus effective optimisation directions are severely limited.

In short, **representational capacity across many layers**, not insufficient rank or blocked gradients, explains why Q/K/V alone cannot match full fine‑tuning.

---

### 3. What do ablations tell us which components matter most?

Figure 1 already provides a high‐level ablation: each bar corresponds to a different scope of adapter placement:

| Adapter scope                     | Relative score vs.\ full fine-tuning |
|----------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Q/K/V only                       | ≈ −5–7\%                              |
| All MHA projections (Q,K,V,O)    | ≈ −2–3\%                              |
| All linear layers (including FFN)| ≈ 0\%   (matches full finetuning)   |

The authors also comment explicitly:  

> “the most critical LoRA hyperparameter is **how many** LoRA adapters are used in total and that **LoRA on all linear transformer block layers** are required to match full finetuning performance.”  

Although detailed per‑layer numbers are tucked away in Appendix B, the trend is unambiguous—adding an adapter to each FFN pair ($W_1,W_2$) yields roughly half of the remaining improvement after moving from Q/K/V→all MHA.  The final few points come from equipping also the output projection $W_O$, which closes the last residual gap.

Thus **the FFN adaptations contribute most** of the missing gain; without them one can only patch up intra‑attention behaviour while leaving the dominant non‑linear part untouched.

---

### 4. Intuition from a broader perspective

Think of a transformer as a factory with two stages:

1. **Mixing stage** –    attention decides *which tokens talk* based on their queries/values.
2. **Processing stage** –    the FFN refines those mixed signals into higher‐level features.

If you install tiny “knobs’’ (LoRA modules) only on stage 1 you can change who talks to whom, but you cannot alter *what* they say after talking—the factory’s core machinery stays rigid.  Adding knobs everywhere gives you control over both who talks and what they produce, which turns out to be essential when you want the model to learn new instruction semantics or domain knowledge.

---

### Takeaway

The failure of “Q/K/V‐only’’ LoRA stems from **leaving untouched the massive feed‑forward pathways that supply most of a language model’s expressive power**.  The shortfall is not due to an inadequate low rank ($r$) nor because gradients stop at frozen layers; gradients still reach every adapter, but without adaptable FFN/​output weights there simply aren’t enough directions for those gradients to translate into useful behaviour changes.  Ablation results from QLoRA confirm that extending adapters to *all* linear maps—particularly those inside the feed‑forward network—restores full fine‐tuning quality while keeping memory usage low.

Consequently, if one wishes to use parameter–efficient fine tuning with quantised LLMs, it is advisable to follow QLoRA’s recipe of installing LoRA modules on **every linear transformation**, rather than limiting them just to Q/K/V as older LoRA work sometimes does.
