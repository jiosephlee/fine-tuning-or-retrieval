\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

**Introduction and Motivation**

# The success of pretrained LLMs and instruction following  

The past few years have witnessed an unprecedented surge in the capabilities of large language models (LLMs). When trained on massive corpora of text, these models acquire a broad repertoire of linguistic knowledge that enables them to generate coherent prose, solve reasoning tasks, and even exhibit emergent behaviours such as code generation or creative writing. A key factor behind this progress is the ability of LLMs to follow natural‑language instructions: by simply describing a desired task in a prompt, one can coax a pretrained model into performing classification, summarisation, translation, or dialogue generation without any further parameter updates. Empirical work has repeatedly shown that instruction‑following dramatically amplifies downstream performance across diverse benchmarks \citep{min2021metaicl, wei2021finetuned, ouyang2022training, wang2022super, wang2022self, liu2022few}. Moreover, instruction tuning serves as a principled pathway to alter model behaviour—adding new abilities or suppressing unwanted behaviours—by exposing the model to high‑quality human‑written input–output pairs \citep{ouyang2022training, askell2021general, bai2022training}. In short, pretrained LLMs provide a powerful foundation, but unlocking their full potential for specific applications typically requires additional finetuning on task‑oriented data.

# Finetuning as a tool for improving capabilities and safety  

Finetuning reshapes the parameters of an LLM so that its internal representations become specialised for a target distribution. This process can improve raw predictive accuracy on tasks such as reading comprehension or factual recall, but it also offers a practical mechanism for aligning models with human values. By selecting high‑quality instruction datasets—often curated through crowd‑sourcing or derived from expert annotations—researchers can steer models toward harmless or helpful behaviours while mitigating toxic or biased outputs \citep{ouyang2022training, askell2021general, bai2022training}. Consequently, finetuning has become an essential tool not only for performance engineering but also for responsible AI development. However, the most straightforward way to perform finetuning—updating all model weights using 16‑bit floating point arithmetic—demands an amount of GPU memory that quickly eclipses what is available on typical research hardware.

# Memory bottlenecks in conventional 16‑bit finetuning  

In conventional full‑model finetuning each weight tensor is kept in 16‑bit precision (often BFloat16) throughout training. For a modern decoder‑only model such as LLaMA 65 B parameters \citep{touvron2023llama}, this design translates into more than $7\!80$ GB of GPU memory consumption when employing naive gradient checkpointing strategies. The majority of this footprint arises from storing activations needed for backpropagation rather than from the trainable parameters themselves. Even when parameter‑efficient methods such as Low‑rank Adapter (LoRA) are applied—thereby reducing trainable parameters to a few hundred megabytes—the activation memory still dominates. As detailed in the background discussion of LoRA’s memory profile (e.g., $567$ MB for input gradients versus only $26$ MB for adapter weights on a 7 B model), scaling these approaches to hundreds of billions of parameters remains infeasible without drastic reductions in memory usage. The net effect is that only organisations equipped with multi‑GPU clusters can afford to finetune state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.

# Limitations of inference‑only quantization techniques  

Quantisation was originally introduced as an inference optimisation: by representing weights with fewer bits (e.g., 8‑bit integers or even 4‑bit formats) one can shrink model size and accelerate matrix multiplications while preserving most of the original accuracy \citep{dettmers2022llm, dettmers2022case, frantar2022gptq, xiao2022smoothquant}. These methods typically rely on per‑block scaling factors that map a high‑dynamic‑range tensor into a narrow integer range via equation
\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{Int8}} = \text{round}\!\bigl(c \cdot \mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}}\bigr),\qquad 
c = \frac{127}{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}})}.
\]
While highly effective for forward inference passes, such schemes break down during training because gradients must flow through the discretised representation. Existing work has shown that backpropagation through low‑bit weight tensors leads to unstable optimisation and severe degradation of downstream performance \citep{wortsman2023stable}. Consequently, prior quantisation approaches have been limited to “inference‑only” scenarios; they cannot be directly reused for finetuning large models at scale.

# Goals of an efficient finetuning method  

The central ambition behind QLoRA is to close precisely this gap: enable full **finetuning** of extremely large models while retaining **the memory efficiency** traditionally reserved for inference only. Concretely, QLoRA seeks to (i) compress a frozen pretrained model into 4 bits per weight using a representation that respects the statistical properties of neural network parameters; (ii) attach lightweight low‑rank adapters (LoRA) that remain trainable in higher precision; (iii) backpropagate gradients through the quantised base model without incurring any loss in task performance; and (iv) keep the overall GPU memory demand under a modest budget (e.g., <$48$ GB) so that even a 65 B parameter model can be tuned on a single professional GPU. Achieving these targets would dramatically lower the barrier to entry for researchers wishing to adapt state‑of‑the‑art LLMs to novel domains or safety constraints. It would also lay groundwork for future extensions such as personalising models on edge devices or mobile phones—an ambition hinted at by preliminary runtime measurements indicating that 7 B models can be trained within gigabytes of RAM after quantisation. In summary, an efficient finetuning pipeline promises both scientific accessibility and broad societal impact by democratising high‑performance language model adaptation without compromising quality.

2. Foundations: LLM Architectures, Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning, and Quantization  

# Decoder‑only, Encoder‑only, and Encoder‑Decoder Transformer Families  

Large language models (LLMs) are built from the transformer architecture introduced by \citet{vaswani2017attention}.  Three broad families of transformers appear repeatedly in the QLoRA study because they serve distinct training objectives and downstream tasks.  

Decoder‑only transformers predict the next token in a sequence given all preceding tokens; this formulation is called causal language modeling.  Models such as LLaMA \citep{touvron2023llama}, which are central to QLoRA’s experiments, fall into this family.  The decoder stack consists solely of self‑attention layers whose attention mask blocks information flow from future positions, ensuring that at inference time tokens are generated one after another in left‑to‑right order.  

Encoder‑only transformers ingest an entire input sequence at once and produce contextualized representations without any autoregressive constraint.  RoBERTa‑large \citep{liu2019roberta}, used in the comparison between QLoRA and full‑model finetuning on GLUE \citep{wang2018glue}, exemplifies this family.  Because every token can attend to every other token, encoder‑only models excel at discriminative tasks such as classification or token‐level tagging where a single hidden vector per token is sufficient for downstream heads.  

Encoder‑decoder transformers combine both components: an encoder converts an input sequence into a set of latent vectors, and a decoder autoregressively generates an output sequence conditioned on those latent vectors via cross‑attention mechanisms.  The T5 family \citep{t5}—employed by QLoRA when evaluating Super‑NaturalInstructions—belongs here.  This design supports text‐to‐text problems ranging from translation to instruction following because it can both encode instructions and generate responses while preserving causality only where needed (in the decoder).  

Understanding these families clarifies why QLoRA needs a unified approach that works across all three architectures: the low‑rank adapters are inserted into every linear transformation—whether it appears inside purely self‑attention layers (decoder), purely feed‑forward layers (encoder), or cross‑attention projections (encoder‑decoder).  

# Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning Overview  

Finetuning a gigantic pretrained model modifies all its millions or billions of parameters using backpropagation on task‐specific data.  For a LLaMA 65B model this naïve approach demands more than $780\,\text{GiB}$ of GPU memory when performed in half precision (bfloat16), a requirement far beyond the capacity of a single consumer GPU.\footnote{The memory figure follows directly from the values reported in Section 1 of the QLoRA paper}  

Parameter‐efficient finetuning (PEFT) mitigates this cost by freezing the vast majority of pretrained weights and introducing only a modest collection of new trainable tensors—often called adapters—that capture task‐specific knowledge.\footnote{In QLoRA Section 2 we treat these adapters as Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA) \citep{hu2021lora}} Because the frozen backbone stays unchanged during gradient descent, its storage cost does not increase when training; only the additional adapter parameters consume extra memory and are themselves small enough that their gradients fit comfortably within typical GPU budgets.\footnote{In Section 2 it is emphasized that “the memory footprint of LoRA is so minimal” that many adapters can be placed throughout a model without harming overall consumption}  

The QLoRA work demonstrates that an appropriately sized set of LoRA modules—when combined with a quantized base model—can replicate full‐model performance while keeping peak memory below $48\,\text{GiB}$ on a single GPU.\footnote{The $48\,\text{GiB}$ figure appears repeatedly as “the average memory requirements… reduced … <48 GB”.} This shift makes instruction tuning of very large models accessible outside industrial compute clusters.\footnote{Section 1 claims this “marks a significant shift in accessibility”.}  

# Mathematical Formulation of LoRA Adapters  

Consider one linear transformation inside a transformer layer,
\[
\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W},
\]
where $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ denotes the input activation matrix, $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$ represents the pretrained weight matrix, $b$ is the batch size, $h$ is the hidden dimension entering the projection, and $o$ is its output dimension.\footnote{This notation matches Equation (3) in Section 2 (“Low-rank Adapters”).}  

LoRA augments this projection by adding a low‑rank update,
\[
\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}\;+\; s\,\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2.
\]
Here $\mathbf{L}_1\in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ and $\mathbf{L}_2\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$ are freshly initialized trainable matrices whose rank $r\ll \min(h,o)$,\footnote{Section 2 defines $r$ as “the projection dimension”.} while $s$ is a scalar multiplier controlling the overall magnitude of the adaptation.\footnote{Equation (3) uses exactly this formulation}. The product $\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ has rank at most $r$, so it can represent only a thin subspace relative to $\mathbf{W}$. Nevertheless, by injecting this term into every linear layer—and optionally repeating it across many layers—the model can learn task‐specific directions without ever touching $\mathbf{W}$.  

The number of trainable elements contributed by one LoRA module equals $(h+r)+(r+o)=r(h+o)$ after accounting for both matrices jointly—a dramatic reduction compared with $h\times o$ elements originally present in $\mathbf W$. For instance, if $h=o=4096$ and $r=64$, one adapter introduces only about $0.06\,\%$ of the weight count yet can convey sufficient expressive power when deployed across all transformer blocks.\footnote{The exact percentages vary across model scales but match discussion in Section 2 regarding “0.2 %”.}  

# Memory Consumption Analysis for PEFT Versus Full‑Model Updates  

PEFT reshapes where memory is spent during training.  The frozen backbone still occupies GPU RAM to hold its quantized weights—for QLoRA using NF4 \citep[see Section 3]{...} this amounts to roughly $5\,\text{GiB}$ for a $7\,\text{B}$ parameter model.\footnote{Section 2 reports “the 4‑bit base model consumes 5,\!048 MB”.}  

Activations generated during forward passes dominate peak usage because their gradients must be retained until backpropagation can occur (unless checkpointing removes them). In raw terms – before any optimization – storing activation gradients for every linear layer costs on the order of hundreds of megabytes even for modest batch sizes.\footnote{
Section 2 provides concrete numbers: “the LoRA input gradients have a memory footprint
of 567 MB while … gradient checkpointing reduces … to an average
of 18 MB per sequence.”
}
Gradient checkpointing however trades compute for space by recomputing portions of activations during backward passes; after applying it QLoRA’s non‑parameter overhead collapses from several hundred megabytes down to roughly tens of megabytes per sequence length.\footnote{
The same paragraph notes that after checkpointing “the … average [is] 18 MB per sequence”.
}

The adapters themselves are tiny compared with either activations or frozen weights:\ footnote{
Section 2 states “the LoRA parameters take up only 26 MB”.
}
a $7\,\text B$ model using adapters amounting to $0.2\,\%$ of its parameters stores just tens-of-megabytes worth at training time.\footnote{
“0\.2 %” appears several times regarding common adapter budgets.
}

Summarizing these components yields total GPU demand well below half fifty gigabytes:
\[
\underbrace{\textstyle5~\text GiB}_{\substack{\text{{Base model}\\(NF4)}}}
+
\underbrace{\textstyle0.{06}\,\text GiB}_{\substack{\text{{Adapter}\\weights}}}
+
\underbrace{\textstyle0.{02}\,\text GiB}_{\substack{\text{{Activation\\gradients}\\(\!$\!$with\\checkpoint)}}
}
<48~\text GiB .
\]
By contrast full fine‑tuning would retain all full‐precision weight gradients ($>$500 GiB) plus activations (\>100 GiB), resulting in more than $780\,GiB$ – precisely what QLoRA avoids.\footnote{
Numbers follow directly from Section 1 (“regular 16­-bit finetuning … requires >780 GB”).}
Thus PEFT eliminates almost all extra memory beyond storing frozen parameters plus modest auxiliary tensors.

# Principles of Block‑Wise Scalar Quantization  

Quantization reduces numerical precision by mapping continuous values onto a discrete set defined by fewer bits per entry—a process essential for fitting very large tensors onto limited GPU memory.\footnote{
Section “Block-wise k-bit Quantization” frames this formally.
}  
A naïve global scaling that divides every tensor element by its absolute maximum would allocate all available code points across an entire layer’s distribution:
\[
 \mathbf X^{\, \text{k-bit}}=
   \operatorname*{\mathrm {round}}\!\bigl(c^{\, \text {FP32}}\cdot \mathbf X^{\, \text {FP32}}\bigr).
 \tag{$1$}
\]
Here \(c^{\, \text {FP32}}=\dfrac{\displaystyle 2^{k-1}\!-\!1}{|\!\operatorname {absmax}(\mathbf X^{\, \text {FP32}})\!|}\) rescales values so that after rounding they lie within $\bigl[-(2^{k-1}\!-\!1),\, +(2^{k-1}\!-\!1)\bigr]$, which matches an unsigned symmetric integer range such as $\bigl[-127,\dots ,127\bigr]$ when $k=8$.  
Dequantization simply reverses this operation,
\[
   \operatorname {dequant}(c^{AF}, \,\mathbf X^{k}) 
    = \frac{\mathbf X^{k}} {c^{AF}}
    = \mathbf X^{\, \text {FP32}},
    \tag{$2$}
\]
as guaranteed by Equation (2) from Section Background.\footnote{
I.e., Eq.~(2) holds verbatim.
}

If any single outlier carries extreme magnitude relative to most entries (“the outlier issue”), applying Equation (1)’s universal scale squashes almost all other numbers into just a few central bins—information loss becomes severe.
Blockwise quantization resolves this by first partitioning each weight tensor into contiguous sub–tensors (“blocks”) each containing \(B\) elements,
which are then independently rescaled using their own constant \(c_i=\dfrac {2^{k-1}-1}{|\!\operatorname {absmax}(X_i)\!|}\).
Formally,
let 
\(n=\frac {b\times h}{B}\)
be the number
of blocks resulting from flattening $\mathbf X$, then
each block \(i=1,\dots,n\) yields its own pair 
$(c_i,\;\mathbf X_i^{k})$, producing
an overall quantized tensor comprising $(c_1,\dots,c_n)$ plus  
the stacked low–bit representation.
Due to independent scalings,
every bin receives roughly equal usage within each block,
so even heavy tails do not dominate any single scale.
This principle underlies both integer encodings such as Int8/(Int4)\
and floating point encodings such as FP8/(FP4); each merely differs
in how bit patterns map onto numeric values once scaled via its own
\(c_i$\,. 

# Quantize–Dequantize Pipeline for Integer and Floating‑Point Formats  

At runtime QLoRA stores **only** two pieces per block:
(a) The low‑bit payload ($k=4$, e.g., NF4 or FP4),
and (b) its accompanying scale factor(s) ($c_i$, possibly further quantized).
When an operation needs that weight—for instance multiplying an activation
matrix $\mathbf X^\mathrm {BF16}$ against a frozen linear layer—the system
first reconstructs full precision values:
```math
   \tilde {\!\mathbf W}= 
   {\displaystyle 
   {\operatorname {dequant}}\bigl(c_{i}^{(1)}, 
      {\operatorname {dequant}}\bigl(c_{i}^{(2)}, 
         \mathbf W^\mathrm {k{-bit}}\bigr)\bigr)},
```
where \(c_{i}^{(2)}\) may itself be stored in compressed form (Double Quantization – Section 3).  
For simple integer schemes like Int8,
\(c_{i}^{(2)}=c_{i}^{(1)}=c_i^\mathrm {FP32}\); dequantizing proceeds directly via Equation (2).
For floating point schemes such as FP4,
the payload already encodes mantissa/exponent bits;
the dequant step interprets these bits according to IEEE-like rules,
then multiplies by its blockwise scale \(c_i^\mathrm {FP32}\).

After dequantisation both operands live in bfloat16 (“Brain Float”) –
the computation data type prescribed throughout QLoRA.
Matrix multiplication therefore enjoys full half‐precision accuracy even though weights occupy merely four bits on storage.
Crucially,
backpropagation never needs gradients w.r.t.~these frozen weights;
the chain rule evaluates
$\partial E/\partial (\tilde {\!\mathbf W})$
through dequantisation steps,
but since $\tilde {\!\mathbf W}$ remains fixed,
only gradients flowing into adapter matrices $\mathbf L_1,\;\mathbf L_2$ persist.
Consequently,
memory allocated for optimizer state concerns merely BF16 adapter variables,
while storage savings stem entirely from keeping $\tilde {\!\mathbf W}$ compressed.
This separation between **storage datatype** ($k$–bit NF4 or FP4)
and **computation datatype** (BF16)
is what enables QLoRA’s dramatic reduction
from hundreds of gigabytes down to tens—without sacrificing predictive quality.
The remaining sections built upon these foundations explore how NF4’s optimality
for normally distributed weights further improves empirical fidelity,
how Double Quantization trims even the scale constants themselves,
and how Paged Optimizers keep training stable when occasional spikes arise during gradient checkpointing—all rooted firmly in these basic mechanisms introduced above.

3. Memory Challenges in Large‑Scale Finetuning  

Large language models (LLMs) contain billions of trainable parameters and thus require correspondingly large GPU memories when they are finetuned end‑to‑end. Even when parameter‑efficient finetuning techniques such as Low‑rank Adapter (LoRA) are employed, the dominant portion of GPU memory consumption is not the weight tensors themselves but the intermediate data that must be retained for back‑propagation. This chapter dissects where the memory budget is spent during finetuning, explains how gradient checkpointing reshapes the allocation pattern, contrasts concrete footprints for mid‑size (≈ 7 B) versus very large (≈ 65 B) models, and finally clarifies why reducing the precision of stored weights alone does not eliminate the critical “spikes” in memory usage that make large‑scale finetuning difficult.



# Breakdown of Memory Usage  

When training a transformer‑based LLM the GPU must hold three broad categories of data:

* **Model weights** – the immutable pretrained parameters $\mathbf{W}$ together with any newly introduced trainable adapters $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$. In standard mixed‑precision finetuning each weight occupies sixteen bits (bfloat16). In QLoRA the pretrained weights are quantized offline to four bits using the NormalFloat data type (NF4). The quantized storage therefore costs $4$ bits per entry plus a small set of per‑block scaling constants $c$.  

* **Optimizer states** – modern adaptive optimizers such as Adam maintain two auxiliary tensors (first‑order moment $\mathbf{m}$ and second‑order moment $\mathbf{v}$) whose shapes match $\mathbf{W}$. Consequently every parameter incurs roughly another two full‑precision tensors regardless of how compactly $\mathbf{W}$ itself is stored. In QLoRA these states remain in bfloat16 because they must be updated with high precision during training.  

* **Activations and their gradients** – during the forward pass each transformer layer produces an activation tensor $\mathbf{X}$ which is subsequently needed by back‑propagation to compute gradients w.r.t.\ $\mathbf{W}$. The activation footprint grows linearly with batch size, sequence length, and hidden dimension. When LoRA adapters are used only a tiny fraction ($\approx0.2\%$) of parameters receive gradient updates; however all intermediate activations must still be cached or recomputed because they act as inputs to both the frozen base model and the adapter paths.

A convenient way to express the total GPU consumption $M_{\text{total}}$ is

\[
M_{\text{total}} \;=\; M_{\text{weights}} \;+\; M_{\text{optim}} \;+\; M_{\text{act}} .
\]

Empirically, for a $7\,\text{B}$ LLaMA model trained on FLAN v2 with batch size 1, LoRA adds only $26\,$MiB ($M_{\text{weights}}^{\text{LoRA}}$) while storing input‐gradient tensors (“activations”) consumes $567\,$MiB when no checkpointing is applied (\(M_{\text{act}}\)). Thus even after discarding most trainable parameters most of the GPU budget resides in $\allowbreak M_{\text{act}}$, not in $M_{\text{weights}}^{\text{LoRA}}$. When gradient checkpointing is enabled those input gradients shrink dramatically—down to an average of $18\,$MiB per sequence—yet they still surpass by an order of magnitude all LoRA parameters combined.



# Effect of Gradient Checkpointing on Activation Memory  

Gradient checkpointing trades compute for memory by discarding selected intermediate activations during the forward pass and recomputing them on demand during back‑propagation. Conceptually one selects a subset $\mathcal{C}$ of layers whose outputs are cached; all other layers are “checkpointed”. During backward traversal every unfixed layer first re-executes its forward computation before emitting its local gradients.

In practice this technique cuts down $M_{\text{act}}$ roughly proportional to how many checkpoints are kept. The QLoRA experiments highlighted this effect: without checkpointing a $7\,\text{B}$ model required $567\,$MiB just to hold LoRA input gradients; after inserting checkpoints this number fell to roughly $18\,$MiB per training example. Nonetheless note that even at this reduced level any increase in batch size or sequence length directly inflates $M_{\text{act}}$, because every example contributes its own set of cached or recomputed activations.

It follows that checkpointing alone cannot guarantee safe operation at very large scales: as soon as one processes longer sequences than those seen during hyperparameter search—or expands batch size beyond what fits comfortably into device RAM—the temporary recomputation buffers may temporarily exceed available GPU capacity. This phenomenon motivates additional engineering such as “paged optimizers”, discussed below.



# Illustrative Memory Footprints for 7 B versus 65 B Models  

The raw numbers reported in the QLoRA study make concrete how dramatically memory requirements grow with scale:

| Model size | Quantized weight footprint | Optimizer state footprint | Typical activation footprint (batch = 1) |
|------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| $7\,\text{B}$ | ≈ 5 GiB (NF4 with blocksize 64) | ≈ 10 GiB (two bfloat16 copies) | ≈ 0.6 GiB without checkpointing; ≈ 0.02 GiB with checkpointing |
| $65\,\text{B}$ | ≈ 45 GiB (NF4 + double quantization) | ≈ 130 GiB (two bfloat16 copies) | > 600 GiB if full precision activations are kept |

The table above reflects several observations made in the paper:

* A naïve 16‑bit finetuning run for LLaMA $65\,\text{B}$ needs more than **780 GiB** GPU RAM—the dominant term being full‑precision activations plus optimizer states.
* By compressing $\mathbf W$ from sixteen bits down to four bits using NF4 (and further applying *double quantization* to shrink scaling constants), QLoRA reduces **only** the weight storage component from an estimated ≈ 260 GiB down to ≈ 45 GiB—roughly a factor five improvement.
* The savings from *double quantization* amount to about \(0.373\) extra bits per parameter:
  
  \[
    \underbrace{\frac{32}{64}}_{0.5\,\text{bits/param}}
    \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{DQ}\;}
    \underbrace{\frac{8}{64} \,+\, \frac{32}{64\times256}}_{0.127\,\text{bits/param}}
    =0.127 .
  \]
  
  For a $65\,\text{B}$ matrix this translates into roughly \(0.373\times65\times10^{9}\approx3\times10^{9}\) bits saved, i.e., about **3 GiB**.
* Even after shrinking weight storage dramatically, **activations dominate**: once sequence length reaches typical instruction tuning windows ($>1024$ tokens), temporary buffers needed for back‑propagation swell far beyond what fits into a single consumer GPU unless special care is taken.
* The final QLoRA configuration—four‑bit NF4 weights plus bfloat16 computation—fits inside **48 GiB** on a professional V100‐class GPU while preserving full precision performance on benchmarks such as Vicuna and MMLU.



# Why Naïve Low‑Bit Storage Does Not Solve Training Spikes  

One might expect that converting every tensor—including intermediate results—to four bits would automatically eliminate out‑of‑memory crashes during finetuning. The QLoRA analysis shows this intuition is incomplete for three reasons:

1. **Activations Are Not Quantized During Backward Pass**  
   In QLoRA only *model weights* remain stored as four‐bit NF4 values on disk or device memory; whenever a weight tensor participates in computation it is first dequantized into bfloat16 (“dequant → BF16”) before being multiplied with an activation matrix (\(\mathbf X^{\!\mathrm{BF16}}\)). This dequantization step occurs lazily at every layer call and does not affect how much space must be reserved for \(\mathbf X^{\!\mathrm{BF16}}\) itself – which stays at full sixteen‐bit precision throughout training because its values enter subsequent layers directly.
   
2. **Optimizer States Must Stay Full Precision**  
   Adaptive optimizers maintain per‑parameter momentum statistics (\(\mathbf m,\mathbf v\)) that influence future updates through division operations sensitive to roundoff error. Quantizing these auxiliary tensors would jeopardize convergence stability—a tradeoff avoided by keeping them at bfloat16 even when \(\mathbf W\) lives in NF4.
   
3. **Transient Spikes Arise From Gradient Checkpoint Recomputations**  
   When gradient checkpointing forces intermediate layers to be recomputed on demand, short-lived “burst” allocations appear at each recomputation step: temporary buffers holding regenerated activations temporarily coexist with those already cached for earlier layers before being released again after their backward contribution finishes computing. If many such bursts overlap—say when processing very long conversations—the peak resident set exceeds what would be predicted by static accounting based solely on permanent tensors.
   
   The QLoRA authors address this issue by employing **paged optimizers**, which rely on NVIDIA’s unified‐memory mechanism to page optimizer state tensors between GPU RAM and host RAM automatically whenever a potential spike threatens an out‑of‑memory condition. Crucially this technique does **not** rely on lowering data precision—it simply ensures that transient enlargements can spill over safely without halting training.


In summary, compressing pretrained weights to four bits cuts down one component (\(M_{\text{weights}}\)) dramatically but leaves \(M_{\text{act}}\) largely untouched because both forward activations and backward gradients remain high–precision by necessity. Gradient checkpointing mitigates—but does not eliminate—their contribution; additional system‐level solutions such as paged optimizers become essential once sequence lengths grow or batch sizes increase.



---  

Through careful decomposition of where GPU resources go during large‐scale finetuning, we see why naïve low‐bit storage alone cannot tame out‐of‐memory failures and why techniques such as NF4 quantization, double quantization, gradient checkpointing, and paged optimizers cooperate synergistically within QLoRA to make finetuning even massive ($65\,\text B$) language models feasible on a single modern GPU.
