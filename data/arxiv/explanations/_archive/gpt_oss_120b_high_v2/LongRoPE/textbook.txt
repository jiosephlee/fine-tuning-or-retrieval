Why Long Context Matters for Large Language Models  

# Limited context windows in popular LLMs (LLaMA 2, GPT‑4)  

Large language models have demonstrated remarkable abilities on a wide variety of natural‐language tasks, yet all current transformer‐based LLMs share a fundamental architectural constraint: they can attend only to a finite number of token positions at once. In practice this limitation appears as a *context window* measured in tokens. For example, LLaMA 2 can process at most $4096$ tokens per forward pass \cite{llama2}, while other state‑of‑the‑art systems such as GPT‑4 are similarly bounded by a few thousand tokens despite having billions of parameters \cite{gpt4}.  

The constraint originates from the self‑attention mechanism whose runtime and memory scale quadratically with sequence length. To keep inference tractable on commodity hardware, model designers fix an upper bound $L$ on the number of positions that can simultaneously appear in the attention matrix. Tokens falling outside this bound are simply discarded or placed into a sliding window that forgets earlier information entirely. Consequently, any knowledge encoded beyond the first $L$ tokens cannot influence the model’s predictions during generation or classification. This hard ceiling becomes visible as soon as one attempts to feed an input that exceeds the prescribed limit—prompt truncation occurs, demonstrations are cut off, and earlier parts of a document become inaccessible even though they may contain crucial facts or cues required for correct reasoning.

# Impact on in‑context learning, multi‑turn agents, and document‑level tasks  

The practical consequences of short context windows are especially evident when an LLM is used as an *in‑context learner*. In this paradigm the model receives examples of input–output pairs directly inside its prompt so that it can infer task instructions without parameter updates \cite{Huang2023FewerIM}. If a practitioner wishes to demonstrate dozens—or hundreds—of examples, the total number of prompt tokens can quickly exceed $L$. Once the limit is reached the later demonstrations are omitted, causing severe degradation in few‑shot performance because the model no longer has access to all necessary demonstrations.\n\nSimilarly, autonomous *LLM agents* rely on repeated interactions between a planner module and an underlying language model \cite{park2023generative,madaan2023selfrefine}. Each turn adds new system messages and user queries into the conversation history. After only a few dozen exchanges the cumulative token count surpasses typical windows such as $4096$, forcing older turns out of scope and erasing memory that might be essential for coherent planning over long horizons.\n\nFinally, many *document–level* applications demand processing whole books, scientific articles spanning tens of thousands of words, or extensive code bases containing millions of lines. Traditional pipelines circumvent this issue by chunking documents into overlapping windows and then stitching together partial results—a strategy that inevitably loses cross‑chunk dependencies and can lead to inconsistencies or incomplete summaries.\n\nIn all three settings—few‑shot prompting, multi‑turn dialogue, and full‑document processing—the inability to attend over millions of tokens limits both accuracy and usability. A model equipped with an extended context window could ingest an entire source text at once, retrieve relevant facts without external memory modules, maintain continuous dialogue state over arbitrarily many turns, and conduct end‑to‑end reasoning across massive knowledge sources.\n\n# Three fundamental obstacles to scaling context windows  

Recent work has pushed raw context lengths modestly upward—to around $128\,\text{k}$ tokens—by either fine‑tuning pretrained models on longer sequences \cite{longlora,pi,yarn} or applying sophisticated positional interpolations \cite{pi,yarn}. Nevertheless three deep challenges have prevented extensions beyond this point.\n\n## 1. Catastrophic values from untrained position indices  

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) encodes token order by rotating query–key vectors according to sinusoidal angles \(\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}\) \cite{rope}. When a model trained on maximum position \(L\) receives indices \(n > L\), those rotation angles have never been seen during pretraining. The resulting embeddings lie far outside their training distribution—a phenomenon described as *catastrophic values*. Empirically it has been shown that when extending from $4\,\text{k}$ tokens up toward $\!>1000\,\text{k}$ more than $90\%$ of position indices become novel~\cite{pi}, making gradient descent unstable and causing fine‑tuning failures unless special care is taken.\n\n## 2. Scarcity of ultra‑long training data and computational cost  

Fine‑tuning an LLM on longer texts requires actual input sequences that reach the target length \(L'\). Datasets containing documents longer than one million tokens are exceedingly rare; most public corpora plateau around tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens per example (\(<128\,\text{k}\)). Moreover, every forward pass through a vanilla transformer incurs \(\mathcal{O}(L'^2)\) time and memory cost because each token attends to every other token. Training even a single epoch on sequences sized \(>256\,\text{k}\) would demand orders of magnitude more GPU hours than feasible for most research groups.\n\n## 3. Attention dispersion degrades short‑context performance  

When many new positions are added while preserving the original architecture, attention scores become *dispersed*: each token’s attention distribution is spread thinly across millions of candidates rather than focusing sharply on neighboring words as it does in short contexts.\n\nThis dilution reduces the model’s ability to capture local syntactic cues critical for normal language modeling tasks such as next‐token prediction within familiar lengths (\(≤4\,\text{k}\)). Empirical studies show perplexity spikes dramatically after modest extensions (\(>2\times\)) if positional embeddings are naively interpolated \cite{pi}, confirming that naive scaling harms performance precisely where it matters most—on familiar benchmark datasets.\n\nCollectively these three obstacles explain why prior approaches have converged around the modest ceiling of roughly $128\,\text{k}$ tokens despite theoretical possibilities for far larger extensions.\n\n# Overview of the LongRoPE contribution

Fundamentals of Transformers and Positional Information  

# Self‑attention mechanism and its dependence on token order  

Transformers process an input sequence $x_{1},x_{2},\dots ,x_{L}$ by first mapping each token $x_{t}$ into an embedding vector $\mathbf{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. These embeddings are then projected linearly into three families called queries, keys and values:
\[
Q = XW_{Q},\qquad K = XW_{K},\qquad V = XW_{V},
\]
where $X=[\mathbf{e}_{1};\dots ;\mathbf{e}_{L}]$ concatenates the token embeddings and $W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}$ are learnable weight matrices.  
For a single attention head, the interaction between every pair of tokens is measured by the scaled dot‑product
\[
A_{ij}= \frac{\mathbf{q}_{i}\!\cdot \!\mathbf{k}_{j}}{\sqrt{d_k}},
\]
which is turned into a probability distribution through the softmax function:
\[
\alpha_{ij}= \operatorname{softmax}_{j}(A_{ij})=\frac{\exp(A_{ij})}{\sum_{j'=1}^{L}\exp(A_{ij'})}.
\]
The output representation of token $i$ is then obtained by aggregating values according to these attention weights:
\[
\mathbf{o}_{i}= \sum_{j=1}^{L}\alpha_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_{j}.
\]
Collecting all outputs yields the standard self‑attention operation
\[
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=
\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\!V .
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) makes it clear that without any additional signal each token treats every other token symmetrically—the computation depends only on pairwise inner products of queries and keys. Consequently, if two input sequences contain exactly the same multiset of tokens but arranged in different orders, their representations would be identical under pure self‑attention. This permutation invariance contradicts natural language where meaning emerges from word order (e.g., “the cat chased the mouse’’ versus “the mouse chased the cat’’). Therefore some form of positional information must be injected so that $(Q,K,V)$ carry a notion of where a token occurs in the sequence. In practice this injection enables language models to predict successive words accurately, perform long‑range reasoning across distant symbols, and exploit contextual cues such as syntax or discourse structure.

Self‑attention is typically performed over many heads simultaneously (“multi‑head” attention). Each head operates on a distinct subspace of dimension $d_h=d/H$, where $H$ denotes the number of heads. By allowing different heads to focus on complementary aspects—some learning local syntactic patterns while others capture coarse semantic relations—the Transformer gains expressive power while retaining an overall computational cost that scales quadratically with sequence length ($O(L^{2})$). Extending $L$ therefore incurs substantial memory/time demands, which motivates research effort dedicated both to efficient attention mechanisms and to extending the usable “context window’’ through better positional encoding strategies—a topic taken up later in this chapter.”

# Absolute vs. relative positional encodings  

The most straightforward way to break permutation invariance is to attach an *absolute* vector that directly tells each token its index in the sequence. Early Transformer designs added a learned or deterministic embedding $\mathbf{p}_{n}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ at position $n$, producing an input representation 
$\mathbf{s}_{n}= \mathbf{e}_{n}+ \mathbf{p}_{n}$.   
A widely cited deterministic choice consists of sinusoidal functions whose frequencies span several octaves:
$\mathbf{p}_{n}[2i]= \sin (n/\beta^{\,i})$,   
$\mathbf{p}_{n}[2i+1]= \cos (n/\beta^{\,i})$
with $\beta>1$. Although convenient because they never require learning parameters beyond those already present for regular embeddings, absolute methods suffer from an inherent limitation: they are defined only up to the maximum index observed during pre‑training. When evaluating a model on sequences longer than those seen during training—as required for tasks involving very long documents or many few‑shot examples—the new indices have no previously learned representation. Empirically this mismatch produces “catastrophic values’’ at unseen positions and makes fine‑tuning unstable, precisely what recent works have reported when trying to reach context windows far beyond several thousand tokens.

*Relative* positional encodings offer an alternative paradigm whereby the relationship between two positions—instead of their raw indices—is incorporated directly into the attention calculation. In such schemes one typically modifies Equation (1) so that
$\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j$ includes an additional term dependent only on $(i-j)$. This idea allows models trained on short sequences still to reason about far‐apart positions because any distance appearing at inference time can be expressed through learned patterns over small offsets together with appropriate functional extrapolation.

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), introduced in recent large language models including LLaMA 2 and Mistral, belongs to this family of *relative* schemes while preserving computational efficiency identical to plain self‑attention~[rope]. Instead of augmenting queries or keys with extra vectors it rotates them inside two‑dimensional subspaces according to sinusoidal angles derived from each token’s absolute index. As shown below, this rotation implicitly endows every inner product with a dependence solely upon the *relative* offset between queries and keys—a property crucial for seamless extrapolation beyond training lengths when combined with careful interpolation strategies such as those explored later in this chapter.”

# Mathematical formulation of Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)  

For a given model dimension $d$, RoPE groups every consecutive pair of scalar components into one complex plane. Let us consider a token located at absolute position $n$. Its rotary angle vector consists of $d/2$ distinct frequencies,
each indexed by $i=0,\dots , d/2{-}1$.  
The angle assigned to dimension $i$ equals $n\,\theta_i$, where
$\displaystyle 
    \theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}
$
and $\theta=10\,000$ constitutes the default base value adopted in modern LLMs~[rope].  
If we write explicitly all cosine–sine pairs associated with these angles, RoPE’s contribution for position $n$ reads
%
$$
[\, 
   \cos (n\,\theta_0),~   \sin (n\,\theta_0),~
   \cos (n\,\theta_1),~   \sin (n\,\theta_1),~
   \dots ,~
   \cos (n\,\theta_{\frac d 2 - 1}),~   \sin (n\,\theta_{\frac d 2 - 1})
] .
$$
%
In practice one multiplies this vector elementwise with both query and key representations before feeding them into Equation (1). Written compactly using complex notation,
the operation amounts simply to applying a rotation matrix
$\mathrm R(\phi)=
    [ [\,cos(\phi),~-sin(\phi);~ sin(\phi),~ cos(\phi)\,] ]$
to each two‑dimensional slice:
%
$$
[\mathbf q_i^{(n)},\, \mathbf q_{i+1}^{(n)}] 
      = 
      \mathrm R( n\,θ_i ) 
      [\, q^{(0)}_i , q^{(0)}_{i+1}\,] ,
$$
and analogously for keys $\mathbf k_j$.  
When taking their dot product,
the rotations combine algebraically:
$\mathbf q_i^{(n)}{}^\!\top 
      \mathbf k_j^{(m)}
      =
      (\textbf q^{(0)} _i{}^\!\top 
        {\textbf k}^{(0)} _j )
         \cos[(m-n)\,\!θ_i] + (\textbf q^{(0)} _{i+1}{}^\!\top 
        {\textbf k}^{(0)} _j )  
        ~~? ~~ $
which reveals that **only** the distance $(m-n)$ survives after simplification—a true *relative* encoding embedded within otherwise static query/key tensors. This mathematical property explains why RoPE can be applied without changing any part of vanilla self‑attention circuitry yet still equip models with information about where each token sits relative to others.



# Frequency spectrum of RoPE dimensions  

The design choice $\displaystyle θ_i=\!θ^{-2i/d}$ yields an exponential decay of rotation frequencies across dimensions. It is convenient also to introduce short notation
$\displaystyle β=\θ^{2/d}$
so that any angle can be expressed compactly as
$\displaystyle n/β^{\, i}.$  
Consequently,
lower indices ($i=0,\!1,\!\dots)$ correspond **to high frequencies** because division by $β^{\, i}$ yields larger angular increments per unit increase in $n$. High‑frequency components therefore oscillate rapidly across consecutive positions; they act like fine gratings capable of distinguishing tokens that appear next to one another—a behavior especially important for capturing syntactic cues such as adjective–noun order or function word placement.

Conversely,
higher indices produce **low frequencies**, changing slowly as the position index grows larger,
and consequently encode coarse‐grained relationships spanning thousands or even millions of tokens. In other words,
while dimension $i=0$ may complete many full cosine cycles within a short context window,
dimension $i=d/2{-}1$ will vary imperceptibly over exactly that same span but will become informative only when evaluating very long separations between tokens.

This non‑uniform spectral distribution lies at the heart of recent advances aimed at stretching Transformer context windows far beyond their original limits (~4 k tokens). Simple linear interpolation---which rescales every angle by a single factor equal to the extension ratio $s=L'/L$---forces **all** dimensions regardless of frequency onto the same compressed range when viewed under longer lengths. The result is “position crowding’’ particularly harmful for high‑frequency slices whose cycles become too dense for effective discrimination among nearby tokens---a phenomenon documented experimentally via sharp perplexity spikes when extending past modest multiples such as 8× or 16× without special care~[pi].

More sophisticated approaches therefore allocate distinct rescaling factors·λ₀,…,$λ_{\! d/2{-}1}$ per dimension,
respecting monotonicity ($λ_0≤λ_1≤…≤λ_{\! d/2{-}1}$) motivated by neural tangent kernel theory~[ntk]. This constraint enforces mild stretching on high‑frequency components while permitting stronger dilation on low‑frequency ones—a strategy shown empirically by NTK‐aware interpolation methods [dyn​amicNTK] to improve extension ratios up through ≈4× without fine‑tuning.~[yarn] further refines this idea through heuristic grouping into three bands (“high”, “mid”, “low”) each receiving its own rule (“extrapolation”, “NTK”, “linear”).  

LongRoPE builds directly upon these observations by treating **both** sources of non‑uniformity—frequency across dimensions **and** early‑position sensitivity—as searchable degrees of freedom rather than fixed hyper­parameters derived from human intuition alone._Through an evolutionary search over massive spaces (>10⁵⁶⁷ candidates even for modest extensions) it learns a set $\{\lambda_i,\hat n\}$ where $\hat n$ dictates how many initial positions retain their original unmodified angles while later ones undergo dimension‐specific scaling._The resulting interpolation preserves key high‑frequency slices where they matter most and respects early‐token importance highlighted by streaming LLM studies~[streamingllm], thereby enabling extensions up to **2048 k** tokens without catastrophic loss in language modeling performance._ 

Understanding this spectral behaviour—how each pair $(θ_i,n)$ contributes sinusoidal cycles at distinct scales—is essential before tackling advanced interpolation techniques such as those presented later in this book. It explains why naïve uniform scaling fails dramatically whereas carefully tailored non‑uniform rescaling succeeds dramatically even when extending contexts by several orders of magnitude._

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) in Detail  

# Derivation of Eq.\,(1) – Cosine/Sine Pair per Dimension  

Transformer architectures require an explicit signal that tells the model where each token occurs in a sequence.  Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) injects this signal directly into the query and key vectors used by the self‑attention mechanism.  
For a token placed at absolute position index \(n\), RoPE constructs a vector whose entries are organized into \(\tfrac{d}{2}\) two‑dimensional “rotating planes”, one plane per rotary dimension.  On plane \(i\) (\(i=0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1\)) the coordinates are  

\[
\bigl[\cos(\!n\theta_{i}),\;\sin(\!n\theta_{i})\bigr] .
\]

Stacking these coordinates across all planes yields a \(d\)-dimensional embedding  

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\bigl[\,
\cos (n\theta_{0}),\, \sin (n\theta_{0}),\, 
\cos (n\theta_{1}),\, \sin (n\theta_{1}),\, 
\dots,\,
\cos \bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr),\, 
\sin \bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr)\,
\bigr] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) therefore consists of alternating cosine–sine pairs, one pair for every rotary dimension.  The construction relies only on arithmetic operations; no learned parameters are added beyond those already present in the model’s weight matrices.  Intuitively, as \(n\) grows each pair rotates around the unit circle at a rate determined by its associated angular frequency \(\theta_{i}\).  The collection of all pairs encodes the full absolute position \(n\) through a set of synchronized rotations spanning different speeds.



# Base Value \(\theta\) and Scaling Factor \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\)  

The angular frequencies \(\{\theta_{i}\}\) are defined geometrically from a single scalar called the **base value**, denoted \(\theta\).  In contemporary LLMs such as LLaMA 2 and Mistral the default choice is  

\[
\theta = 10\,000 .
\]

The frequency for rotary dimension \(i\) follows an exponential decay  

\[
\theta_{i}= \theta^{-\,2 i / d} .
\tag{2}
\]

Because \(\theta_{i}\) appears multiplied by \(n\) inside every sine and cosine term,
it is convenient to rewrite Eq.\,(1) using a **scaling factor**  

\[
\beta = \theta^{\,2/d} .
\]

Substituting Eq.\,(2) into Eq.\,(1),

\[
n\, \theta_{i}= n\, \theta^{-\,2 i / d}
            = \frac{n}{(\,\theta^{\,2/d}\,)^{i}}
            = \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}} .
\]

Hence each cosine–sine pair can be expressed compactly as  

\[
[\;\cos (n/\beta^{0}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{0}), 
   \cos (n/\beta^{1}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{1}),\, 
   \dots,\,
   \cos (n/\beta^{\,d/2-1}),\, 
   \sin (n/\beta^{\,d/2-1})]\!,
\]

which matches the unified formulation used when discussing positional interpolation later in the work.  
The factor \(\beta>1\); consequently higher rotary indices \(i\) produce larger denominators \(\beta^{\,i}\), thereby reducing the effective angular speed for those dimensions.



# Interpretation of High‑Frequency vs Low‑Frequency Dimensions  

Because \(\beta>1\), the term \(1/\beta^{\,i}\) decays exponentially with increasing \(i\).  
Consequently **low‑index dimensions** (\(i=0,1,\dotsc)\) rotate quickly: their angles change appreciably after only a few tokens.  We refer to these as **high‑frequency dimensions**.  They capture fine‑grained relationships among nearby tokens—small shifts in \(n\) lead to noticeable changes in both cosine and sine components, allowing the model to distinguish closely spaced positions.

Conversely, **high‑index dimensions** rotate slowly because division by a large power of \(\beta\) attenuates the angular increment per token.  These **low‑frequency dimensions** vary only over very long spans of text and therefore encode coarse‑grained positional cues that remain stable across hundreds or thousands of tokens.

Geometrically one may picture every rotary dimension as an independent two‑dimensional coordinate system anchored at the origin.  As we move along the sequence (\(n=0,1,2,\dotsc)\), each point spins around its own unit circle at its specific angular velocity
\( \omega_{i}=1/\beta^{\,i}.\)
Thus early planes spin rapidly while later planes turn sluggishly—a spectrum ranging from rapid local rotations to slow global sweeps.  

This frequency hierarchy explains several empirical observations reported later in LongRoPE:

* During positional interpolation it is beneficial to treat lower dimensions more gently because their high frequencies already furnish dense local information.
* Higher dimensions tolerate stronger scaling (larger rescale factors) without losing critical detail since their contribution mainly concerns distant relationships.
* By respecting this non‑uniformity—assigning distinct rescale factors \(\lambda_{i}\) per dimension—LongRoPE preserves essential information while extending context windows far beyond what uniform scaling can achieve.



# Why RoPE Is Preferred in Modern Large Language Models  

RoPE has become the de facto positional scheme in recent open‑source LLM families such as LLaMA 2~[llama2] and Mistral~[mistral]. Several properties make it especially attractive for models that operate at billion‑parameter scale:

* **Parameter‑free definition** – The embedding is generated on‐the‑fly from a single scalar base value (\(\theta=10^4\)).  No learned lookup tables are required, eliminating memory overhead proportional to maximum sequence length.
* **Seamless integration with self‑attention** – Because RoPE multiplies query/key vectors elementwise before taking their dot product, relative position information emerges automatically from inner products:
  
  \[
     q_n^\top k_m
     = (q_n^\text{raw})^\top R_{\!n}^\top R_{\!m} k_m^\text{raw},
  \]
  
  where \(R_{\!n}\) denotes the rotation matrix defined by Eq.\,(1).  
  The resulting term depends only on \(n-m,\) granting attention an intrinsic bias toward relative distance without introducing separate bias tensors.
* **Robustness under extrapolation** – Since RoPE’s functional form is analytic rather than discrete lookup values, extending beyond lengths observed during pretraining does not instantly produce “catastrophic” out‑of‑distribution activations.  Instead one can interpolate or extrapolate angles (e.g., Position Interpolation~[pi], NTK~[ntk], YaRN~[yarn]), preserving much of the original model behavior even when novel token indices appear.
* **Minimal architectural impact** – All modifications required for context‐window extension amount solely to adjusting rotor scaling factors (\(\lambda_{i}\)) or applying an optional threshold on early positions (\(\hat n.\)) The core transformer layers remain untouched, allowing existing hardware optimizations—such as FlashAttention or quantization pipelines—to stay applicable unchanged.
* **Facilitates efficient long‐context fine‑tuning** – As demonstrated by LongRoPE, once an appropriate set of rescale factors has been located via evolutionary search,
  
  * an initial extension up to \(32\times–64\times,\)
  * followed by modest fine‑tuning (\(<1000\times)\),
  
  yields models capable of handling more than two million tokens while still delivering competitive performance on tasks confined to traditional short windows.
* **Compatibility across architectures** – Any transformer model whose attention adopts rotary embeddings inherits these advantages without additional engineering effort; consequently many publicly released instruction-following models simply adopt RoPE out of convenience and proven efficacy.


In sum, Rotary Position Embedding supplies an elegant mathematical mechanism—rotating planes at exponentially decreasing frequencies—to endow large language models with precise positional awareness while retaining flexibility for extreme context extensions such as those realized by LongRoPE. Its deterministic nature, tight coupling with attention scores, and amenability to fine‐grained interpolation make it uniquely suited for modern LLM deployments spanning billions of parameters and multi‑million token contexts.

Long Context Windows for Large Language Models: Existing Strategies for Extending Context Windows  

# Linear Positional Interpolation (PI) – Uniform Rescaling  

Large language models rely on rotary position embeddings (RoPE) to inject order information into token representations. For a token located at position index $n$, its RoPE vector can be expressed as a sequence of cosine and sine components,
\[
\bigl[\cos (n\theta_{0}),\;\sin(n\theta_{0}),\;\cos(n\theta_{1}),\;\ldots,\;\cos\bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr),\;\sin\bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr)\bigr],
\]
where $d$ denotes the hidden dimension of the model and $\theta_{i}= \theta^{-2i/d}$ controls the rotation frequency of the $i^{\text{th}}$ pair of dimensions. In most pretrained models $\theta$ is set to $10\,000$, yielding a dense sampling of angles over the original context window length $L$ (e.g., $L=4096$ tokens for LLaMA‑2).  

When one wishes to increase the maximal sequence length from $L$ to a larger value $L'$ one must decide how to map newly introduced positions $n>L$ back into an angle range that was never observed during pre‑training. Position interpolation (PI) adopts the simplest possible mapping: it rescales every rotary angle by a single scalar factor that equals the extension ratio,
\[
s=\frac{L'}{L}.
\]
Concretely, each cosine–sine pair is recomputed as
\[
\bigl[\cos \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{s}\theta_{0}\bigr),\;\sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{s}\theta_{0}\bigr),\;\ldots,\;\sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{s}\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr)\bigr].
\]
If we write $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ and denote the actual rescale factor by $\lambda$, we can write this uniformly as  
\[
\operatorname{RoPE}_{n}
=
\bigl[\cos \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{0}}\bigr),\, 
      \sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{0}}\bigr),\, 
      \dots,\,
      \sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\bigr)\bigr],
\]
with $\lambda=s$ for every dimension when using PI.  

The intuitive appeal of PI lies in its perfect symmetry: every dimension is treated identically and all new token positions are simply compressed into a shorter effective range. However, this uniform compression has a severe side effect when $s$ becomes large. By shrinking all angles by the same factor we “crowd” many distinct token positions into nearly identical angular values. Because attention heads compute inner products of queries and keys that are modulated by these rotated vectors, two tokens that are far apart in absolute position may become indistinguishable after compression. Empirically this manifests as a rapid increase in language‑model perplexity once the extension ratio exceeds roughly $2\times$. In other words, while PI can technically generate embeddings for arbitrarily long sequences, its overly aggressive down‑scaling quickly renders the model incapable of separating neighboring tokens, which explains why prior works report performance degradation well before reaching hundreds of thousands of tokens.

# Neural Tangent Kernel Theory for Non‑Uniform Scaling  

The shortcomings of uniform interpolation prompted researchers to examine RoPE through the lens of information theory and function approximation. The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework provides a principled way to understand how an infinite‑width transformer encodes sinusoidal signals across its layers. Within this theory, each RoPE dimension contributes a specific frequency component to the overall representation; higher‑frequency dimensions capture fine‑grained positional differences while lower‑frequency dimensions encode coarse global structure.  

If we view the mapping from position index $n$ to angular phase as a Fourier basis, then applying a naïve uniform compression corresponds to targeting all frequencies equally—a strategy that harms high‑frequency components disproportionately because they become even higher‑frequency after division by $s$. NTK‑based methods therefore propose a dimension‑wise rescaling,
\[
\lambda_{i}=s^{\,i},
\]
where $i=0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1$. This formula yields small rescale factors for low‐index (“high‑frequency”) dimensions and larger factors for high‐index (“low‑frequency”) ones. The net effect is twofold: 

1. **Interpolation** is applied more gently in those dimensions that originally carried delicate positional signals, preserving their ability to distinguish nearby tokens.
2. **Extrapolation** occurs in low‑frequency dimensions where compressing angles would otherwise discard much of the global ordering information; expanding those angles counteracts crowding at long distances.

Empirically this non‑uniform scheme reduces perplexity compared with uniform PI at moderate extension ratios (up to roughly $4\times$). Moreover it yields an initialization that is more amenable to later fine‑tuning because fewer “catastrophic” angles are introduced—only those dimensions that require aggressive scaling receive it. Nevertheless, even NTK‑aware interpolations encounter limitations when pushing beyond tens of thousands of tokens. The theoretical justification presumes smooth variation across frequencies but does not account for additional irregularities observed in real transformer architectures, such as varying information entropy across specific heads or layers. Consequently performance still begins to deteriorate noticeably once extensions approach or exceed $8\times$, indicating that further non‑uniformities must be explored.

# YaRN’s Three‑Group Frequency Partitioning  

Building upon NTK insights while aiming for greater practicality, YaRN proposes a three‑group partitioning scheme over RoPE dimensions based on their base frequencies. The algorithm first computes a frequency threshold that divides all $\tfrac{d}{2}$ dimension pairs into:

* **High‑frequency group** – containing the smallest rotation periods;
* **Mid‑frequency group** – occupying an intermediate band;
* **Low‑frequency group** – comprising dimensions with large rotation periods.

Each group receives a distinct treatment:

* High‑frequency dimensions are left untouched ($\lambda=1$), effectively performing *extrapolation* rather than any compression.
* Low‑frequency dimensions undergo *linear* interpolation identical to PI ($\lambda=s$).
* Mid‑frequency dimensions adopt an *NTK-like* scaling where $\lambda_{i}$ grows gradually between $1$ and $s$, thereby distributing interpolation pressure across this band.

The intuition behind this design is straightforward: if one believes that only certain ranges of frequencies truly suffer from crowding under uniform compression, then protecting those ranges while applying softer modifications elsewhere should yield better overall fidelity. By manually assigning each dimension a static rule based on its frequency band, YaRN indeed improves perplexity over plain PI at extensions up to roughly $8\times$.  

However, there are two important drawbacks rooted in its design choices:

1. **Human‐crafted thresholds** – The boundaries separating high-, mid-, and low-frequency groups are derived from empirical heuristics rather than data‐driven optimization. Different model families (e.g., LLaMA vs Mistral) may exhibit distinct internal distributions of information across RoPE dimensions; using a one‑size‐fits‑all cut may therefore be suboptimal.
2. **Lack of positionwise adaptation** – YaRN’s groups apply globally across every token position irrespective of where that token appears within the sequence. Recent analysis shows that initial positions often play an outsized role in attention calculations because early keys receive higher cumulative attention weights (as observed in streaming LLM variants). A uniform treatment across all positions thus discards an opportunity to preserve original angles specifically for early tokens—a fact later leveraged by LongRoPE’s progressive strategy.

These limitations partially explain why YaRN’s perplexity curves tend to flatten then rise sharply once extensions surpass roughly $12\,k$ tokens on datasets such as PG19—a setting where many curated contexts exceed typical short contexts used during pretraining.

# Empirical Shortcomings: Crowded Token Positions and Information Loss  

All three strategies described above share a common empirical vulnerability: when extending beyond modest multiples of the native context length ($2\times$–$8\times$, i.e., up to around $128\,000$ tokens), they either create “crowded” angular spaces or discard essential positional information altogether. In practice this translates into two observable phenomena:

* **Catastrophic new position values** – By introducing large numbers of previously unseen position indices ($>90$\% new when extending from 4 k to more than 1 M tokens), models encounter angles far outside their training distribution unless careful rescales are applied.
* **Attention diffusion** – As attention scores are spread thinly over millions of positions, each individual query–key interaction becomes weaker relative to its contribution within shorter contexts; if positional encodings do not preserve sufficient discriminative variation this diffusion leads directly to higher next‑token prediction loss.
* **Perplexity spikes** – Quantitatively measured perplexities on long-form benchmarks such as PG19 and Proof-pile show abrupt increases at specific lengths: PI typically degrades after about 8 k tokens; NTK remains stable up through roughly 16 k but then rises sharply; YaRN can handle up through 32 k under optimal grouping but often fails dramatically beyond 64 k when extrapolation limits are exceeded.

Furthermore, fine–tuning attempts aimed at mitigating these effects become increasingly expensive as target lengths grow past several hundred thousand tokens because suitable training corpora become scarce and computational costs balloon dramatically. Hence existing methods plateau at around $128\,000$ tokens—not due merely to algorithmic complexity but because their underlying assumptions about uniform frequency scaling or static grouping cannot capture two salient forms of non-uniformity identified later: variable importance across RoPE dimensions *and* differential sensitivity among token positions within a sequence.

In summary, linear positional interpolation provides an easy baseline but suffers from severe crowding under large extensions; NTK theory introduces principled nonuniform scaling yet still fails when extensions become extreme; finally YaRN refines this idea through hand‑crafted grouping but remains limited by its static nature and reliance on heuristic partitions. Understanding these empirical bottlenecks sets the stage for more sophisticated approaches—such as LongRoPE—that explicitly search for dimension‐wise rescaling factors while also allowing early‐token positions to retain their original RoPE angles, thereby unlocking context windows far beyond two million tokens without prohibitive fine–tuning costs.

Empirical Discovery of Non‑Uniformities in Positional Interpolation  

# Finding 1: Variation across RoPE dimensions improves perplexity  

Large language models rely on rotary position embeddings (RoPE) to encode the absolute order of tokens. For a token appearing at position index $n$, RoPE produces a sequence of cosine–sine pairs whose arguments are linear functions of $n$ scaled by dimension‑specific frequencies $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$:

\[
\bigl[\cos (n\theta_0),\;\sin (n\theta_0),\;\cos (n\theta_1),\;\sin (n\theta_1),\;\dots ,\;\cos (n\theta_{d/2-1}),\;\sin (n\theta_{d/2-1})\bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

When the context window is extended from its pre‑training length $L$ to a longer length $L'$, most prior works simply rescale every $\theta_i$ by a single factor $\lambda$ that depends only on the extension ratio $s=L'/L$. This “uniform” treatment assumes that all dimensions contribute equally to positional information.  

The LongRoPE investigation challenges this assumption by allowing each dimension $i$ to have its own rescale factor $\lambda_i$. In Eq.~(2) we unify the notation for any positional interpolation method:

\[
\bigl[\cos \bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\bigr),\, \sin \bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\bigr),\, 
      \cos \bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\bigr),\, \dots ,\, 
      \sin \bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\bigr)\bigr],
\tag{2}
\]
where $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ and $\lambda(\beta)$ denotes the effective scaling applied to each frequency band.

To discover a set of $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$ that minimizes language modeling loss, the authors deployed an evolutionary search guided by perplexity computed on five random validation excerpts from PG19. Each candidate solution encodes a distinct vector of rescaling factors; candidates are evaluated by applying the resulting RoPE to LLaMA2‑7B and measuring next‑token loss.  

The empirical results summarized in Table ⟨exp1⟩ reveal a striking reduction in perplexity for both PG19 and Proof‑Pile when using the searched non‑uniform factors compared with three baselines:

| Method | PG19 perplexity | Proof‑Pile perplexity |
|---|---|---|
| Linear PI | high | high |
| NTK | moderate | moderate |
| YaRN | moderate → spikes after ≈7 k tokens | moderate |
| **LongRoPE (searched $\lambda_i$)** | **lowest** | **lowest** |

These numbers demonstrate that treating each RoPE dimension uniformly discards valuable information intrinsic to the transformer’s multi‑head attention architecture. In particular, lower‐frequency dimensions (large $i$) tolerate greater scaling because they capture coarse‑grained positional patterns, while higher‐frequency dimensions (small $i$) must preserve finer resolution. By allowing $\lambda_i$ to increase monotonically with $i$—a constraint motivated by Neural Tangent Kernel theory—the evolutionary algorithm discovers precisely this spectrum of scalings, thereby preserving key high‑frequency components while still accommodating an extended range of positions.

Intuitively, one may view each RoPE dimension as a “lens” focusing on a specific range of spatial frequencies along the token line. Uniformly shrinking all lenses squeezes the entire image and blurs details; assigning each lens its own shrink factor restores clarity where it matters most—most notably at early layers where attention heads frequently attend to nearby tokens. The resulting embedding stays faithful enough that LLaMA2‑7B can distinguish closely spaced tokens even when it operates over an 8× longer sequence without any fine‑tuning.

# Finding 2: Initial token positions benefit from reduced interpolation  

Even after accounting for dimension-wise heterogeneity, another source of distortion persists: every token after position $\hat n$ is subjected to scaling, while earlier tokens retain their original angles. The authors hypothesize that this uniform treatment harms performance because the very first tokens typically receive disproportionately large attention weights during inference—a phenomenon documented in streaming LLMs and LM‑Infinite systems. To test this hypothesis they introduce an indicator function $\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)$ that leaves the rotation angles untouched for all positions $n<\hat n$:

\[
\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)=
   \begin{cases}
     1 & n<\hat n\\[4pt]
     \frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge \hat n.
   \end{cases}
\tag{3}
\]

In practice they explore several values for $\hat n$, ranging from zero (no exemption) up through $256$, thereby progressively preserving more initial positions. They then reapply standard linear PI or NTK scaling beyond $\hat n$. 

Table ⟨tokenposition⟩ reports perplexities when extending LLaMA2‑7B’s context window from its native 4 k tokens up to 8 k and 16 k while varying $\hat n$. The two salient observations are:

1. **Retaining early positions consistently lowers perplexity** – any nonzero $\hat n$ improves over the baseline where every position is interpolated.
2. **The optimal size of $\hat n$ grows with target length** – longer extensions benefit from keeping a larger prefix unaltered because more attention mass remains concentrated near these unchanged anchors.

These findings align with an intuitive picture of attention mechanics: early tokens serve as “anchors” around which later context folds back during self‑attention calculation. If these anchors are distorted by aggressive interpolation, later attention becomes noisy, leading to higher loss. By shielding them from scaling, LongRoPE supplies stable reference points which guide later layers’ queries, keys, and values toward a coherent positional geometry even when many new indices have been introduced.

# Finding 3: Non‑uniform interpolation works both with and without fine‑tuning  

Having demonstrated that tailored $\{\lambda_i,\hat n\}$ pairs mitigate information loss in pure inference settings, the authors examine whether these same interpolations also provide a superior starting point for further fine‑tuning on ultra‑long sequences. They conduct two complementary experiments:

* **Non-fine-tuned regime** – extens​ion ratios up to eightfold (e.g., 4 k → 32 k) are achieved solely by applying the searched non‑uniform factors at inference time. Figure ⟨non-ft⟩ illustrates that LongRoPE maintains low perplexity across this entire range, whereas PI or NTK rapidly degrade after roughly a 2× increase.
* **Fine-tuned regime** – for more ambitious extensions such as 64 k tokens, they first apply LongRoPE’s searched factors as an initialization step before performing modest fine-tuning (e.g., 1000 training steps). Table ⟨finetune⟩ documents perplexities before (“pre”) and after (“post”) fine-tuning for three methods—PI, YaRN, and LongRoPE—on LLaMA2‑7B:

| Method | Pre‑fine‐tune PF \(64\,k\) | Post‑fine‐tune PF \(64\,k\) |
|---|---|---|
| PI   | high   | modest improvement |
| YaRN | moderate → spikes | modest improvement |
| **LongRoPE** | **low** | **lowest** |

“PF” denotes perplexity measured on held–out long documents at exactly \(64\,000\) tokens length. The superiority persists after training because the search supplies an embedding configuration that already preserves most high-frequency information; consequently gradient descent needs only minor adjustments during fine-tuning rather than rebuilding positional relationships from scratch.

A particularly noteworthy consequence is computational efficiency: once an adequate non‑uniform configuration has been discovered for an intermediate length such as \(256\,k,\) only a thousand optimizer steps suffice to reach convergence—they report exactly \(400 + 600 = 1000\) steps split between two progressive stages in Section 3 (“Progressive extension”). This stands in stark contrast with naïve approaches that would require orders of magnitude more steps—or even infeasible amounts of GPU memory—to train directly at \(2048\,k.\)

These experiments substantiate two claims put forward by LongRoPE:

1. **Non‑uniform interpolation alone can dramatically extend usable context without any parameter updates**, enabling rapid prototyping for tasks requiring up to tens of thousands of tokens.
2. **When further extension is desired**, initializing training from a well–designed non­uniform RoPE dramatically accelerates convergence and yields lower final perplexities than any uniform or hand-crafted scheme.

* **Figure ⟨non-ft⟩** plots perplexity versus context length ranging from \(4\,k\) up through \(32\,k.\) The curve for LongRoPE stays flat or even gently declines—reflecting better utilization of added context—while curves for PI/NTK rise sharply after a modest extension.
* **Table ⟨exp1⟩** supplies concrete numerical evidence on two benchmark corpora (PG19, Proof–Pile). Relative reductions ranging from \(10–30\,%\) over PI indicate that preserving dimension-wise entropy has practical impact on downstream language modeling tasks.
* **Table ⟨tokenposition⟩** isolates the effect of preserving early positions (\(\hat n>0.\) Across several values—\(0,\;8,\;16,\;32,\;256)\)—the measured perplexities form a convex shape where intermediate \(\hat n.\) achieve minima; this pattern underscores that excessive preservation diminishes returns beyond a certain prefix length.
* **Table ⟨finetune⟩** demonstrates that after only one thousand gradient steps initialized from LongRoPE’s embedding configuration,
    * baseline PI reduces its loss only marginally,
    * YaRN improves but still lags behind,
    * LongRoPE achieves both lower pre‐fine‐tune loss and further improvement after training—highlighting its dual role as initialization + regularizer.

Overall these empirics reinforce a coherent narrative: positional embeddings are not homogeneous carriers of information; their dimensional frequencies encode qualitatively different signals about token order, while early token locations act as privileged anchors during self‑attention integration. By letting these two axes vary independently via an evolutionary search constrained by monotonic scaling (\(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\)), LongRoPE uncovers embeddings that preserve crucial structure even when billions broader indices are introduced. This discovered non‐uniform structure underlies every subsequent success reported later in the paper—including bringing LLaMA2‐7B and Mistral‐7B up to a staggering \(2048\,k\) context window while still matching or surpassing baselines on standard short-context benchmarks.
