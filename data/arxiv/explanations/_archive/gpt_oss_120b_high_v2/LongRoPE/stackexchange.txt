### What is the rotary position embedding (RoPE) formulation used in LongRoPE?
Question:
In Section 2 Equation (1) the paper defines a token at position $n$ as \[\left[\cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \dots , \cos\bigl(n\theta_{d/2-1}\bigr), \sin\bigl(n\theta_{d/2-1}\bigr)\right]\] where $d$ is the embedding dimension and $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$ with base $\theta = 10000$. I am unsure about two points:
(1) How exactly is $\theta_i$ derived from the base $\theta$?
(2) Does $n$ denote the absolute index of a token within a document or merely its index inside a current input sequence?
Understanding these details would help me see how RoPE encodes order before any interpolation takes place.
Answer:
**Answer**

In *LongRoPE* the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) is taken directly from
the original RoPE paper [Su et al., 2021] and is only altered later by
the learned rescaling factors $\lambda$.  
Therefore it is helpful first to understand the *raw* RoPE formula that
appears as Eq.\,(1) in the LongRoPE manuscript:

$$
    \mathbf p_n =
    \bigl[\;
        \cos (n\theta_0),\,\sin (n\theta_0),\
        \cos (n\theta_1),\,\sin (n\theta_1),\
        \ldots,\
        \cos\bigrl(n\theta_{d/2-1}\bigr),\,%
        \sin\bigrl(n\theta_{d/2-1}\bigr)\
    \bigr].
    {\tag{1}}
$$

Here  

* $d$ – total hidden‑size of the model (e.g.\ $d=4096$).  
* The vector contains $d/2$ sinusoid *pairs*; each pair corresponds to one “rotary dimension”.  
* $n$ – the **position index** of the token **inside the current input sequence** that is fed to the transformer block.  
* $\theta_i$ – a *frequency* that differs for every rotary dimension $i$.  

Below we explain how $\theta_i$ is obtained from the base constant
$\theta_{\text{base}}=10{,}000$ and what exactly $n$ denotes.

---

### 1. From the base $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ to the per‑dimension frequencies $\boldsymbol{\theta_i}$  

The aim of RoPE is to allocate **log‑spaced angular frequencies**
across dimensions so that low‑indexed dimensions vary quickly with token
position (high frequency) while high‑indexed dimensions vary slowly
(low frequency). Starting from a single scalar **base**
$\displaystyle \theta_{\text{base}}=10{,}000$,  

$$
    {\boxed{\;\theta_i=\theta_{\text{base}}^{-\,{\frac{2i}{d}}}\;}
    }\qquad
    i=0,\dots,\frac d2-1 .
    {\tag{2}}
$$

The exponent $-\tfrac{2i}{d}$ produces a geometric progression:

* When $i=0$: $\;\theta_0=\theta_{\text{base}}^{0}=1$.  
* When $i=1$: $\;\theta_1=\theta_{\text{base}}^{-\,2/d}$.  
* When $i=2$: $\;\theta_2=\theta_{\text{base}}^{-\,4/d}$,
  …​ halving the exponent every step.

It is often convenient to write Eq.\,(2) in terms of an **intermediate
constant**

$$
    \beta:=\theta_{\text{base}}^{\, {2}/{d}}
    ~~{\Longrightarrow}~~
    \theta_i=\beta^{-\,i}
            ={\frac {1}{\beta^{\,i}}}.
    {\tag{3}}
$$

Thus each rotary pair receives an angle

$$
    n\,\theta_i={}\frac {n}{\,\beta^{\,i}}\,. {\tag{4}}
$$

For a concrete example, suppose $d=8$. Then  

* $\displaystyle 
   \beta = 10{,}000^{\: {2}/8}=10{
,
}000^{0.25}=10.$  

* The four rotary dimensions obtain frequencies  

  $$%
     {\frac {n}{10^{0}},}\quad
     {\frac {n}{10^{1}},}\quad
     {\frac {n}{10^{2}},}\quad
     {\frac {n}{10^{3}}}.
  $$  

So dimension 0 completes one full sinusoid every $
   2{\pi}/${}!${}$
≈ 6.28 tokens,
dimension ​1 every ≈ 62 tokens,
dimension ​2 every ≈ 620 tokens,
etc.—exactly what we want for a **multi‑scale positional code**.


---

### 2\. What does $n$ represent?

In standard Transformer implementations (including LLaMA‑2,
Mistral and all models used in LongRoPE)
**$n$ is simply the index of a token inside the current input window**
supplied to the model at inference or training time.

* If we feed a prompt of length $L=4096$, tokens are numbered
  $n=0,1,\dots ,4095.$  
* When we later expand the window—say to $L'=32~768$, we continue numbering from zero up to $32~767.$  

Thus $n$ is *local* rather than *global*: it does **not** count how many
characters have appeared earlier in an arbitrarily long document unless you
explicitly keep a running counter while sliding a window over that document.
In practice most long‑context pipelines re‑index each chunk separately because
only one chunk lives inside the model at any moment.

The distinction matters when extending context windows: LongRoPE treats any new
position $n<L'$ exactly like an old one—its angle follows Eq.\,(4)—but rescales those
angles per dimension with learned factors $\lambda$
(see Eq.\,(5) of the paper). Because $\lambda$
is applied after computing $ n/\beta^{i}$,
it preserves the original “absolute‑inside‑window” semantics while allowing extrapolation beyond
the range on which the model was originally trained.


---

### Putting it together – why RoPE already encodes order  

The vector $\mathbf p_n$ can be seen as a complex number per rotary pair:
${e}^{j n/\beta^{i}}=\cos(n/\beta^{i})+j\,\sin(n/\beta^{i})$.  
When query vectors $\mathbf q_n=\mathbf x_n e^{j n/\beta^{i}}$
and key vectors   $\mathbf k_m=\mathbf y_m e^{j m/\beta^{i}}$
are multiplied element‑wise inside attention,
the resulting inner product contains a term  

$$e^{j(n-m)/{\beta}^{i}},$$  

which depends **only on relative distance** $n-m$. Consequently attention scores automatically incorporate positional differences without any extra bias term.
The multi‑scale set of frequencies (${\beta}^{-i}$)
makes this relative signal robust over both short distances
(via high‑frequency dimensions) and very long distances
(via low‑frequency dimensions).

LongRoPE keeps this core mechanism unchanged—only $\lambda$
rescales each frequency after step 4—so everything described above still holds after interpolation or fine‑tuning.


---

## Summary

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $\displaystyle{\theta_{\text{{base}}}}=10000$ | Global scalar determining overall scale of frequencies |
| $\displaystyle{\theta_i=\theta_{\text{{base}}}^{-\tfrac {2 i}{d}}}$ | Frequency for rotary dimension *i*; equivalently $\displaystyle{\theta_i=\beta^{- i}}$ with $\displaystyle{\beta=\theta_{\text{{base}}}^{\: {2}/{d}}}$ |
| $n$ | Position index **within** the current input window fed to the Transformer (starts at 0 for each chunk). |
| $\mathbf p_n=[…]$ | Sinusoid pair per dimension built from angles $ n·θ_i=n/β^{i}$; this multi‑scale sinusoid encodes order and enables relative‑distance–aware attention. |

With these definitions you can see exactly how raw RoPE represents order before LongRoPE’s non‑uniform interpolation steps are applied.

### Why does linear positional interpolation cause ‘crowded’ positions at large extensions?
Question:
The paper says that linear PI reduces all rotation angles uniformly by $\lambda = s$, which makes token positions "crowded" and hurts performance when $s$ is large.
I am trying to visualise what "crowded" means mathematically.
* Does scaling by $s$ shrink angular distances between adjacent tokens so that their sinusoidal vectors become highly correlated?
* If so, can you show an expression for the angle difference between two consecutive positions before and after applying PI?
A concrete illustration would clarify why PI underperforms for high extension ratios such as $8\times$ or more.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “crowded‒positions” problem comes straight from the geometry of RoPE (Rotary Positional Embedding).  
In RoPE each attention head rotates a query‒key pair by a *phase* that depends on the token index **$n$** and on a frequency that is attached to the embedding dimension **$\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}$**.  
For dimension **$i$** the phase used in Eq.&nbsp;(2) of the paper is  

$$
\phi_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}},\qquad 
\beta=\theta^{2/d},
$$  

so that a single token at position **$n$** is represented by the sinusoid pair  

$$
[\,\cos \phi_{i}(n),\, \sin \phi_{i}(n)\,]_{\,i=0}^{d/2-1}.
$$  

---

### What “crowded” means mathematically

Two consequences follow from this definition:

| before interpolation | after *linear* positional interpolation (PI) |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Phase : $\displaystyle \phi_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}$ | Phase : $\displaystyle \tilde{\phi}_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{s\,\beta^{\,i}}$\;(the paper writes it as division by $\lambda=s$) |

For any fixed dimension **$i$**, the **angular distance between two consecutive tokens** is simply  

> **Original spacing**  
> $$
> \Delta\phi_{i}^{\,{\rm orig}}=
>      \phi_{i}(n+1)-\phi_{i}(n)
>      =\frac{1}{\beta^{\,i}} .
> $$  

> **After linear PI**  
> $$
>   \Delta\phi_{i}^{\,{\rm PI}}
>   =\tilde{\phi}_{i}(n+1)-\tilde{\phi}_{i}(n)
>   =\frac{1}{s\,\beta^{\,i}}
>   =\frac{1}{s}\,\Delta\phi_{i}^{\,{\rm orig}} .
> $$  

Equation&nbsp;(2) tells us that *every* dimension’s angular step is divided by the extension ratio **$s$**.  
When **$s=8$**, each step becomes eight times smaller than it was in the pretrained model.

Because RoPE lives on a unit circle, shrinking an angular step makes adjacent vectors **more collinear**:

$$
{\rm corr}\bigl(v_{n},v_{n+1}\bigr)
   = \cos(\Delta-\!\!\phi_{i}) 
   \;\approx 1-\tfrac12(\Delta-\!\!\phi_{i})^{2}
   \quad (\Delta-\!\!\phi_{i}\ll 1).
$$

If we plug Eq.(2):

| $s=4\times?$ | $s=8\times?$ |
|---|---|
| original step $=0.25~{\rm rad}\Rightarrow {\rm corr}\approx0.97.$ | original step $=0.125~{\rm rad}\Rightarrow {\rm corr}\approx0.992.$ |
| lower‑frequency dim $(\beta^{10}\!\approx100)$: original step $10^{-2}~{\rm rad},~ {\rm corr}\approx0.99995.$ | scaled step $=1.25\times10^{-3}~{\rm rad},~ {\rm corr}\approx0.999999.$ |

Thus **high‑frequency dimensions** (small&nbsp;$i$, $\beta^{\,i}\approx 1$) still retain noticeable variation after an eight‒fold stretch, but **low‑frequency dimensions** (large&nbsp;$i$, $\beta^{\,i}\gg 1$) become almost static: all positions map onto nearly the same point on their circles.

In an attention head the inner product between a query at position **$p$** and a key at position **$q$** contains a term  

$$
q^\top k
   = \sum_{j}
     \bigl(\cos(\tilde{\phi}_{j}(p)-\tilde{\phi}_{j}(q))\bigr),
$$

so when every $\tilde{\phi}_{j}(p)-\tilde{\phi}_{j}(q)$ is tiny the cosine term is $\approx 1$ for *all* pairs $p,q$. Consequently the model loses its ability to discriminate “who is next to whom”; many distant tokens look almost identical from a positional standpoint – this is what authors refer to as *crowded* positions.

---

### Why linear PI fails for large extensions

The RoPE design deliberately spreads information across dimensions with different frequencies:

* low‑$i$\;&nbsp;→&nbsp;**high‑frequency** → rotating quickly, encoding fine‒grained relative order;
* high‑$i$\;&nbsp;→&nbsp;**low‑frequency** → rotating slowly, carrying coarse‐grained order cues that survive over very long spans.

Linear PI treats all dimensions identically by multiplying every denominator by **$s$*. The result:

* It **compresses** every frequency band by exactly the same factor;
* Low‑frequency bands—which already change only slightly per token—are compressed *further*, almost freezing their phase;
* The set of reachable phase values within any practical window collapses dramatically; mathematically one can view it as mapping millions of distinct integer indices into a tiny interval of length  

$$
L'_{\max}=%
    L'\bigl/ s
    = L .
$$  

Hence even though we now allow indices up to $L'$, their embedded phases occupy roughly the same total angular range they did before extension—so we are reusing most of it many times over. This redundancy looks like “traffic jam’’ on a narrow road – many cars (token positions) are forced into almost identical lanes (phase values).

Because attention relies on phase differences as signals for “how far apart’’ two tokens are, this crowding makes long‑range attention noisy or indistinguishable, which empirically manifests as a sharp rise in perplexity when **$s\succeq8.$**

---

### Intuition behind non‑uniform alternatives

Methods such as **NTK‑based interpolation** or **YaRN** avoid crowding by *not* shrinking all frequencies equally:

* They keep high‑frequency dimensions nearly untouched ($\lambda\simeq 1,$ extrapolation), preserving fine‒grained spacing;
* They apply larger factors ($$\lambda > s,$ extrapolation’’​…) only to low‑frequency dimensions where some compression is tolerable;
* YaRN even treats three groups separately (extrapolate → NTK → interpolate).

Mathematically this means replacing Eq.(2) with  

$$
   \Delta \tilde{\phi}_{i}= 
   \frac{c_i}{s}\;\frac{1}{\beta^{\, i}},
   ~~c_i =
     \begin{cases}
       1          & (\text{high freq})\\[3pt]
       s^{\,p(i)} & (\text{mid / low freq})
     \end{cases}
,$$

where **$c_i\fgeq 1$.**
Consequently  

* low $i$\;$⟶$\;$${c_i}\approx 1,$   
* high $j$\;$⟶$\;$${c_j}\approx s,$   

and the overall average spacing stays roughly constant across dimensions – far fewer vectors end up “crowded.’’

---

### Quick numeric illustration

Assume a toy model with just two frequencies:

* High frequency: $\beta^{0}=1 ~~⟶~~ Δφ_0^{orig}=1~{\rm rad}$;
* Low frequency : $\beta^{5}=100 ~~⟶~~ Δφ_5^{orig}=0.01~{\rm rad}$.

| method | $Δφ_0$ | $Δφ_5$ | $\cos(Δφ_0)$ | $\cos(Δφ_5)$ |
|--------|--------|--------|--------------|--------------|
| Original | 1 rad                   │ 0.01 rad │ 0.540 │ 0.99995 |
| Linear PI ($s=8$)\*\* | 0.125 rad│ 0.00125 rad│ 0.992 │ ≈ 0.999999 |
| NTK‐style (keep high freq)| ≈ 1 rad│ ≈ 0.00125 rad│ ≈ 0.540│ ≈ 0.999999 |

In the last row the high‑frequency spacing stays essentially unchanged ($≈a~rad$), while only the low‑frequency spacing suffers compression – exactly what preserves discriminability for nearby tokens while still allowing much longer overall context windows.



---

### Bottom line

Linear positional interpolation rescales every rotary angle by a common factor $\lambda=s$, which mathematically shrinks every inter‑token angular gap by $\tfrac{1}{s}$. Because sinusoidal vectors lie on circles, making their arcs tiny forces consecutive embeddings—and consequently attention scores—to become almost identical (“crowded”). When **$s\succeq8,$ especially in low‑frequency RoPE dimensions, this loss of angular resolution translates into serious information loss and therefore poor performance.  
Non‑uniform strategies recover part of this resolution by leaving fast‒rotating dimensions untouched and distributing interpolation pressure unevenly across dimensions—a principle formally captured by NTK theory and empirically exploited in LongRoPE’s evolutionary search.​

### What exact per-dimension scaling rule does NTK-based interpolation follow?
Question:
The authors write that NTK distributes interpolation pressure across RoPE dimensions by setting $\lambda = s^{i}$. My questions:
* What does index $i$ refer to—is it simply ranging over $0 \dots d/2 - 1$?
* Where does the exponentiation by $i$ come from analytically—how was it derived from Neural Tangent Kernel theory?
* Is there an intuitive explanation linking higher frequency (lower $i$) versus lower frequency (higher $i$) components?
Understanding this would help differentiate NTK from linear PI.
Answer:
In a rotary‑position embedding each hidden dimension is paired with a sinusoid  
\[  
    \bigl[\cos(n\rho_{0}),\,;\,\sin(n\rho_{0}),\,;\,
           \cos(n\rho_{1}),\,;\,\sin(n\rho_{1}),\,;\,\dots,\,
           \cos(n\rho_{d/2-1}),\,;\,\sin(n\rho_{d/2-1})\bigr],  
\]  
where  
\(   
\rho_{i}= \beta^{-\,i}   
      \quad{\rm and}\quad   
      \beta=\rho^{2/d}.   
\)  
The index \(i\) therefore enumerates the **\(d/2\) sinusoidal “frequency channels’’**  
(and you are correct that it runs from \(0\) up to \(d/2-1)\).  
The low‑index channels (\(i=0,1,\dotsc)\) have large \(\rho_{i}\) and thus encode **high‑frequency** position information; high‑index channels encode low‑frequency (“coarse‑grained’’) information.

---

### Where does \(\lambda_i=s^{\,i}\) come from?

The Neural Tangents Kernel (NTK) analysis treats a transformer with rotary embeddings as a *kernel machine* whose kernel is the inner product of the positional features:
\[  
K(n,m)=\sum_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\cos!\bigl((n-m)\,\rho_{i}\bigr).   
% (Reference number added manually)
\]

If we stretch the sequence length by a factor \(s=L'/L$, the new positions are effectively \(\tilde n=n/s.\) In order that the kernel after stretching has the same functional form as before—so that an infinite‑width transformer would behave identically—we must replace every basis function by a version whose argument is scaled *per channel*:
\[  
   \cos!\bigl((n-m)\,\rho_{i}\bigr)
   ~{}\longrightarrow{}~
   \cos!\Bigl(\frac{n-m}{\,\lambda_i\,}\,\rho_{i}\Bigr).   
% (Reference number added manually)
\]

Because the frequencies themselves are powers of~$\beta$,
\[  
   \frac{n}{\,\lambda_i\,}\,\rho_{i}
     =    \frac{n}{\,\\lambda_i\,}\,\beta^{-\,i}
     =    n(\beta s)^{-\,i}
     ~~\\Longleftrightarrow~~
     \\frac{n}{(\beta s)^{
         i}}= \\frac{n}{s^{\\, i}\\beta^{\\, i}} .
   % No explicit tag here.
   }

Thus making the **effective base** change from~$\beta$ to~$\beta'=\beta s$
is equivalent to dividing the position index in channel~\(i\$ by~\(s^{\\, i\$.
Therefore the rescaling factor that restores the original kernel is  

\[  
   \\boxed{\\;
      \\lambda_i = s^{\\, i}\\;
          } \\qquad (i=0,\\dotsc,d/2-1).
% (Reference number added manually)
   \\]  

Equation (3) is precisely what the paper calls “NTK‑based interpolation”.
It is nothing more than enforcing *spectral invariance* of the sinusoidal kernel under a uniform stretch of the input domain—a direct consequence of the homogeneity property~$(ab)^{
     i}=a^{\\, i}b^{\\, i}$.



### Intuition : high vs low frequency channels

When we increase the context window we are forced either to **compress**
the existing angular values (as in plain Position Interpolation, PI)
or **extrapolate** them beyond the original range.
Because every channel represents a different sinusoid wavelength,
the same amount of compression harms some channels more than others:

* **High‑frequency channels** (\(i\\approx0\$) have short wavelengths.
  Two neighbouring tokens already differ by many radians.
  If we were to compress them by a factor~\(s>1\$), many distinct token positions would be mapped into almost identical angles—*crowding*.
  Hence NTK assigns them a tiny scaling factor,
  \\(
       \\lambda_0 \\approx s^{0}=1    
     \\);
  they are essentially left unchanged (or even slightly extrapolated).

* **Low‑frequency channels** (\(i\\approx d/2-1\$) vary slowly across positions.
  A modest compression produces only a small loss of resolution,
  so these channels can absorb most of the “interpolation pressure”.
  Their scaling factor grows as \\(
       \\lambda_i = s^{\\, i}
     \\),
  reaching values close to $s^{d/2-1}$;
  for those dimensions the behaviour approaches exactly what PI would do.

Consequently NTK “smoothly distributes’’ the stretch:
early dimensions receive almost no interpolation (extrapolation only),
while later dimensions receive progressively stronger interpolation.
Linear PI corresponds to setting \\(
       \\lambda_i = s    
     \\)
for all~\(i\$; this ignores the heterogeneity described above and therefore quickly runs out of usable information when~\(s>2−4\\times.\)

The monotonicity constraint employed in LongRoPE’s evolutionary search,
\( \\lambda_0 \\le \\lambda_1 \\le \\dotsc \\le \\lambda_{d/2-1},
    % No explicit tags here.)
is directly inherited from this analytic result:
the theory predicts precisely that higher‑indexed (lower‑frequency)
dimensions should be allowed larger rescaling factors.

Answer:
In the LongRoPE paper an **individual** corresponds exactly to one concrete choice of RoPE‑rescaling factors together with the position‑threshold $\hat n$.  
Formally an individual is written as  

\[
\mathbf{z}= \bigl(\lambda_{0},\,\lambda_{1},\,\dots ,\,\lambda_{\frac{d}{2}-1},\,;\,\hat n\bigr),
\]

where each scalar $\lambda_{i}$ lives in the discrete interval  

\[
[\,1,\;s\times 1.25\,]_{\text{step}=0.01}
\]

(the lower bound $1$ means “no interpolation”, the upper bound allows a little more interpolation than plain PI) and $\hat n$ is taken from  

\[
\{\,0,\,1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\,12,\,16,\,20,\,24,\,28,\,32,\,64,\,128,\,256\,\}.
\]

The vector part encodes how much every RoPE dimension is down‑scaled once the token position exceeds $\hat n$, while $\hat n$ decides how many leading tokens keep the original RoPE unchanged.

### What mutation actually does  

`mutation_with_mono_constraint()` starts from a parent $\mathbf{z}^{p}$ taken from the current elite set (“top‑k”). For every gene (each $\lambda_i$, and also $\hat n$) a Bernoulli trial with probability $p$ decides whether it will be altered:

* **If the gene is selected** a new value is drawn uniformly from the admissible grid around the current one (e.g., add or subtract an integer multiple of $0.01$, clipped to stay inside $[\,1,s\times 1.25\,]$).  
* **If it is not selected** it is copied unchanged.

Because the search space respects the NTK‑theoretic ordering “higher dimensions may be interpolated more”, LongRoPE imposes after every mutation a *monotonicity repair*: the mutated sequence $(\tilde{\lambda}_{0},\,.\!.\!.\,,\,\tilde{\lambda}_{d/2-1})$ is replaced by  

\[
\rho_i=\max_{j\sle i}\tilde{\lambda}_{j},
\qquad i=0,\dots,\tfrac d2-1,
\]

which guarantees  

\[
\rho_{0}\;\leqslant\rho_{1}\;\leqslant\dotsb;\;\leqslant\rho_{\tfrac d2-1}.
\]

If $\hat n$ has been changed it is simply snapped back to the nearest value in its discrete set – no ordering constraint applies to it.

$\displaystyle
    \mathbf{z}^{A}= (\boldsymbol{\!\Lambda}^{A},~ \hat n^{A})
$
and  
$
    \mathbf{z}^{B}= (\boldsymbol{\!\Lambda}^{B},~ \hat n^{B})
.
$

For each dimension~$i$ a coin flip decides whether the child inherits
$\displaystyle \Lambda^{A}_{i}$ or $\displaystyle \Lambda^{B}_{i}$. The scalar~$\hat n$
is usually chosen from one of the parents (or sampled uniformly between them) – any choice belongs automatically to its admissible set.

The raw offspring may break the required ordering because mixing two
already sorted sequences does not guarantee a sorted result.
Consequently LongRoPE applies exactly the same **repair** operation used
after mutation: compute a cumulative maximum over dimensions,
which projects any decreasing jump onto the previous level.
That projection enforces  

$
    \bar {\Lambda}_{i}\leqslant \bar {\Lambda}_{i+1}
$
for all~$i$
while preserving as much of the parental information as possible.
Only after this repair does LongRoPE evaluate perplexity on the LLM,
so every evaluated child satisfies NTK‑motivated monotonicity.

### Why these design choices matter  

Representing an individual as $(\boldsymbol{\!\Lambda},~\nobreakspace\rho)$ lets LongRoPE explore **both** sources of non‑uniformity identified in Sections 3–4:
the per‑dimension scaling (capturing frequency‑dependent interpolation)
and the special treatment of early token positions (controlled by $\widebar {n}$).
The independent perturbation of each~$\Lambda_i$
during mutation supplies a fine‑grained local search over a huge discrete hyper‑grid,
while uniform crossover enables larger jumps between promising regions.
Enforcing the monotone‐non‐decreasing rule **before** evaluating perplexity cuts down wasted evaluations dramatically—only vectors consistent with NTK theory are ever tested.
Consequently LongRoPE can locate high‑quality rescaling patterns even when the nominal search space contains astronomically many candidates (e.g., \(400^{64}\times14\simeq10^{167}\) for a \(4\times{}\) extension).

In short: an individual consists of a full vector of per‑dimension rescale factors plus a scalar threshold~$\widebar {n}$.  
Mutation perturbs each factor independently inside its discretised interval (and occasionally changes~$\widebar {n}$); 
crossover mixes two parent vectors dimension­wise and then repairs any violation of  
$\displaystyle
   {\Lambda}_i\dleqslant {\Lambda}_{i+１}
$
through a cumulative–max projection.
These two operators together generate feasible offspring that respect NTK‑derived ordering while efficiently traversing the enormous combinatorial space required to discover effective non‑uniform positional interpolations for LongRoPE.’’

### Why impose a monotonic non-decreasing constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ during search?
Question:
The authors enforce that rescale factors increase monotonically with dimension index based on NTK theory.
I would like clarification on two aspects:
* From an information-theoretic perspective why should higher-indexed (lower-frequency) dimensions receive larger rescaling factors?
* Empirically, how much does enforcing monotonicity reduce evaluation cost compared with allowing arbitrary ordering?
A justification linking theory and practical efficiency would help me understand this design choice.
Answer:
The monotone‐non‑decreasing requirement $\lambda_i\leqslant\lambda_{i+1}$ is not an arbitrary engineering trick; it follows directly from how RoPE encodes position and from what the Neural‑Tangent‑Kernel (NTK) analysis tells us about the information that each dimension carries.

In RoPE every half‑dimension $i$ stores a sinusoid whose angular frequency is  

$$
    \omega_i=\beta^{-i},\qquad 
    \beta=\theta^{2/d},
$$

so the rotation angle at token position $n$ is $n\,\omega_i$.  
Low‑index dimensions ($i$ small) therefore have a **high** frequency – their phase cycles many times over a short span of positions – whereas high‑index dimensions ($i$ large) have a **low** frequency and change only slowly along the sequence. From an information‑theoretic point of view a high‑frequency component can distinguish two tokens that are only a few positions apart and therefore contributes a large amount of *positional entropy* for short‑range relationships. Conversely, low‑frequency components vary slowly and contribute mostly coarse‑grained information about long‑range order; their entropy per token is much smaller because many positions map onto almost identical phases.

When we stretch a context window by a factor $s$, we have to “spread’’ those phases over a longer interval while trying not to lose any distinguishability that is already present in the pretrained model. If we were to apply exactly the same scaling $\lambda$ to every dimension (the linear interpolation used by PI), all phases would be compressed uniformly and the high‑frequency angles would become so crowded that distinct positions become indistinguishable – a classic aliasing problem in signal processing. NTK theory resolves this by allocating **more interpolation pressure** to low‑frequency dimensions (which can tolerate it) and **less pressure** to high‑frequency ones (which cannot). Mathematically this means choosing rescaling factors such that  

$$
    \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}\lambda_i}\approx 
    \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\,
    \frac{1}{s^{\,i/(d/2-1)}},
$$

where $\lambda_i$ grows with $i$. The monotone condition $\lambda_i\leqslant\lambda_{i+1}$ simply enforces exactly this ordering: every step toward a coarser frequency must allow *at least* as much down‐scaling as the previous, finer step.

Why does this matter practically?  

Without any ordering each $\lambda_i$ could be chosen independently from a discrete set  

$$
    \Lambda = \{\,1.00,\ 1.01,\dots,\ s\times 1.25\,\},
$$

so for $d=128$ there are $|\Lambda|^{64}$ possible vectors – roughly $4\times10^{216}$ when $s=4$. Evolutionary search would have to evaluate an astronomically large fraction of these candidates before stumbling upon a sensible region, and most random candidates generate invalid RoPE embeddings whose perplexities explode instantly, wasting GPU seconds on every wasted individual.

### How large is the search space for different target extension ratios?
Question:
(a) For an extension ratio $s=4$, each $\
Answer:
The “search” mentioned in LongRoPE is not a small grid‑search over a handful of hyper‑parameters – it is a combinatorial optimisation over **one scaling factor per RoPE frequency** together with **a single token‑position threshold** that decides how many leading tokens keep their original rotary angles unchanged.  

Formally, for a model whose RoPE operates on \(d_{\text{rope}}\) dimensions we have  

* a rescaling factor \(\lambda_{i}\) for every frequency index \(i = 0,\dots ,\frac{d_{\text{rope}}}{2}-1;\)  
* an integer threshold \(\widehat n \in \{\!0,\,1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\dots ,256!\}\).

The admissible values of each \(\lambda_{i}\) are defined by the target **extension ratio** \(s=\tfrac{L'}{L}\):

\[
    \lambda_{i}\in[\,1.00,\;s\times 1.25\,],
    \qquad \text{step}=0.01 .
\]

Hence the number of distinct candidates per frequency is  

\[
    M(s)=
    \Bigl\lfloor
        \frac{s\times 1.25-1}{0.01}
    \Bigr\rfloor+1
    =
    \bigl\lfloor125\,s-99\bigr\rfloor .
    \tag{†}
\]

(The lower bound “\(1[.]00\)” corresponds to *direct extrapolation*: no interpolation at all.)

For the models used in the paper \(d_{\text{rope}}=128$, i.e. there are  
\(K=\tfrac{d_{\text{rope}}}{2}=64$\ independent frequencies.
If we ignore the monotonicity constraint that forces  
\( \lambda_{0}\leqslant \lambda_{1}\leqslant\dotsc\leqslant \lambda_{K-1} ), an **upper bound** on the raw combinatorial size is simply

\[
|{\cal S}(s)| = M(s)^K \;\times |\widehat N|
        = M(s)^{\,64}\;\times13,
        \qquad |\widehat N| =13,
        \tag{‡}
\]

where we count all values listed for `\(\widehat n`\), namely  
\(13=\{0,\,1,\dotsc ,256\}=13.\)

---

### Concrete numbers given in the paper  

For an extension ratio $s=4\times$:  

* \(M(4)=\bigl\lfloor125\times4-99\bigr\rceil
          =401\simeq400.\)

Plugging into (‡)

```math
|{\cal S}(4)| 
   ≈ 400^{64}\times14
   ≈ 4\times10^{167}.
```

This figure appears explicitly in Section 3.2 (“a $400^{128/2}\times14=4\times10^{167}$ choices”).

---

### How the space grows with larger ratios  

Because \(M(s)\) scales linearly with \(s$, while it is raised to the power \(K (=64)\), **the total space expands exponentially** when we increase the target extension ratio.

| Target ratio $s$ | $M(s)$ (≈ number per dim.) | Approximate raw space |
|-------------------|------------------------------|-----------------------|
| $8\times$       | $M(8)\approx801\simeq800$   | $800^{64}\!\times13   ≈3\times10^{191}$ |
|- $16\times$      | $M(16)\approx1601\simeq1600$ | $1600^{64}\!\times13   ≈5\times10^{227}$ |
|- $32\times$      | $M(32)\approx3201\simeq3200$ | $3200^{64}\!\times13   >10^{260}$ |

(The exact powers differ only by constant factors because we keep one decimal step.)

Even though LongRoPE imposes two practical tricks that shrink this astronomically large set—

* **Monotonically non‑decreasing constraint** (\(\lambda_{i} ≤ λ_{i+1}\)), which cuts out almost all permutations that violate NTK’s frequency ordering;  
* **Seeding** the initial population with known baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN),

the **effective** neighbourhood remains far too big for exhaustive enumeration once $s>4.$

---

### Why this matters  

The authors therefore cast the problem as an **evolutionary search** (Algorithm 1). Evolutionary methods excel when:

* The objective function (here perplexity evaluated on a few long documents) is cheap enough to compute repeatedly,
* The feasible region is discrete and high‑dimensional,
* Simple random sampling would require astronomically many evaluations (e.g., >\(10^{167}\)).

The evolution then iteratively refines promising individuals while maintaining the monotonicity rule, allowing LongRoPE to locate useful “non‑uniform’’ rescaling vectors within seconds or minutes instead of never finishing an exhaustive grid‑search.

---

### Take‑away  

In short:

* The raw search space grows roughly as  
  \[
      |{\cal S}(s)|  
      = (\lfloor125\,s-99\floorright)^{
          d_{\text{rope}}/2}
        \times |\widehat N|.
  \]
* For typical models (\(d_{\text{rope}}=128,\ |\widehat N|=13{}\)) this yields  
  * ≈ \(4\times10^{167}\) candidates at $s=4×$,  
  * ≈ \(3\times10^{191}\) candidates at $s=8×$,  
  * …and quickly reaches astronomic magnitudes beyond that.
* Because it scales exponentially with both *extension ratio* and *model dimensionality*, brute‑force optimisation becomes infeasible even for modest ratios, motivating LongRoPE’s specialised evolutionary algorithm together with monotonic constraints.

These calculations explain why the authors emphasise “the search space that identifies rescale factors expands exponentially with the target extension ratio’’ and why their evolutionary approach is essential for exploring such an immense landscape efficiently.
