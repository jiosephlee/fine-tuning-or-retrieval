\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: Why Control Text‑to‑Image Generation?
====================================

Large diffusion models that generate images conditioned on natural‑language prompts have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize photorealistic pictures.  State‑of‑the‑art systems such as Imagen \cite{saharia2022photorealistic}, DALL·E 2 \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} and Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} are trained on billions of image–text pairs and can produce high‑quality results across a broad range of concepts.  Nevertheless, when a user supplies only a textual description the model often produces an output that only loosely matches the intended visual composition.  This section clarifies why pure text guidance is insufficient for many practical applications and introduces two concrete downstream tasks—subject‑driven generation and controllable generation—that explicitly require tighter control over the diffusion process.

## Limitations of Pure Text Guidance

Text prompts convey semantic information at the level of objects, attributes and coarse relationships (“a red cat sitting on a wooden chair”), but they generally lack detail about *how* these concepts should be arranged spatially, what exact pose an object should take, or what fine‑grained visual identity a particular instance should exhibit.  In practice this ambiguity manifests in three ways:

1. **Underspecified spatial layout** – A prompt rarely specifies exact edge contours or region boundaries.  The diffusion sampler therefore fills the scene according to its learned priors, which may deviate from the user’s intended composition.
2. **Ambiguous instance identity** – When a user wishes to generate images of a particular person or object that they have previously seen (e.g., “my pet rabbit in a park”), the generic textual description does not contain enough information to recover the unique visual appearance of that instance.
3. **Fine‑grained style or pose constraints** – Artists often need precise control over pose, lighting direction or background geometry that cannot be described faithfully with natural language alone.

Because diffusion models learn a stochastic mapping from noise to image conditioned on text embeddings, any missing cue is inferred from the model’s internal distribution learned during pretraining.  This inference can be reasonable on average but may yield results that are semantically plausible yet visually mismatched with user expectations.  Consequently, research has turned toward augmenting the conditional signal with additional modalities that directly encode the missing information.

## Definition of Subject‑Driven Generation (Personalized Generation)

Subject‑driven generation—the focus of works such as DreamBooth \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}—addresses the second limitation above.  Formally, let $\{I_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ denote a small set (often $K\leq5$) of reference images depicting a single visual entity (a person’s face, a pet’s fur texture, an object’s design).  The goal is to adapt a pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model $M^{0}$ so that given any textual prompt $p$ describing a *new* context (“$<$subject$>$ playing guitar on stage”), the finetuned model $M^{\star}$ generates images that retain the distinctive appearance of the subject while honoring the new semantic description.

Two challenges are intrinsic to this task:

* **Identity preservation** – The adapted model must retain enough of the original latent representation that encodes the subject’s unique visual features (e.g., facial geometry or fur pattern).  
* **Contextual flexibility** – At the same time it must remain capable of synthesizing arbitrary backgrounds and actions required by $p$, which demands that finetuning does not collapse the rich diversity learned during large‑scale pretraining.

In practice these demands translate into an optimization problem where only a handful of new parameters are introduced (to avoid catastrophic forgetting) while the bulk of $M^{0}$ remains unchanged.  Successful methods achieve this balance by carefully regularizing how far $M^{\star}$ can diverge from $M^{0}$, thereby preserving both semantic fidelity and generative diversity.

## Definition of Controllable Generation (Edge Maps, Segmentation, Landmarks)

Controllable generation addresses the first limitation listed above by providing auxiliary spatial cues alongside text.  In this setting one supplies an additional *control signal* $c$—for instance a Canny edge map $c_{\text{edge}}$, a semantic segmentation mask $c_{\text{seg}}$, or facial landmark coordinates $c_{\text{land}}$—and asks the diffusion model to produce an image $\hat I$ whose structure respects $c$ while still being guided by text prompt $p$.

Mathematically we extend the conditional distribution from $p(\mathbf{x}\mid p)$ to $p(\mathbf{x}\mid p,c)$ where $\mathbf{x}$ denotes an image sample.  The control signal supplies explicit geometric information:

* **Edge maps** encode contour locations without specifying color or texture.
* **Segmentation maps** assign each pixel to a class label (e.g., “sky”, “tree”, “person”) thus shaping high‑level layout.
* **Landmarks** give sparse keypoint locations on objects such as faces or bodies, enforcing accurate pose geometry.

The central difficulty is learning how to fuse these disparate modalities without sacrificing any component’s influence.  If we simply concatenate embeddings from $c$, we risk overwhelming previously learned knowledge and destabilizing training; conversely if we emphasize $c$ too weakly we lose alignment with user intent.  Therefore controllable generation calls for finetuning strategies that simultaneously respect existing pretrained representations and flexibly incorporate new control pathways.

## Application Scenarios and User Expectations

Both subject‑driven and controllable generation target real production pipelines where users demand precise visual outcomes:

* **Creative storytelling** – Scriptwriters may want their protagonist (a specific actor) placed in varied scenes described by script lines; subject ­driven finetuning ensures that each generated frame recognizably portrays that actor.
* **Product design** – Designers frequently sketch rough outlines or supply CAD rendering masks; controllable generation lets them transform those sketches into high‑resolution photorealistic renders while maintaining brand identity specified by short textual tags.
* **Virtual avatars** – In games or virtual meetings users often supply an exemplar portrait together with pose landmarks so that generated avatars can mimic real facial expressions accurately.
* **Rapid prototyping** – Engineers may wish to iterate over concept images quickly by adjusting only edge maps or segmentation masks rather than rewriting extensive textual instructions every time.
* **Personalized art** – Hobbyists want their pets or personal memorabilia rendered in imaginative settings (“my dog as an astronaut”). A handful of reference photos suffices when subject‑driven finetuning is available.

Across these scenarios users share three core expectations:

1. **High fidelity** – The output must appear photorealistic and free from artifacts introduced by finetuning.
2. **Semantic consistency** – The generated image must honor both textual description *and* auxiliary control signals without contradictions.
3. **Efficiency** – Because large diffusion models are computationally expensive, practitioners expect finetuning to require only modest GPU resources (few epochs, limited trainable weights).

When any expectation is violated—for example if finetuning collapses into memorization of training examples or fails to respect edge contours—the resulting workflow becomes impractical despite impressive underlying model capacity.

## Desiderata for Fine‑Tuning: Efficiency and Preservation of Generative Ability

Designing a fine‑tuning method for large text‑to‑image diffusion models therefore revolves around two intertwined desiderata:

### Training Efficiency

Diffusion backbones typically contain hundreds of millions of parameters; updating all weights would demand prohibitive memory consumption and training time.  Efficient approaches restrict updates either by limiting *where* updates occur (e.g., only attention layers) or by constraining *what* form those updates take (e.g., low‑rank matrices as in LoRA \cite{hulora2022}).  The goal is to introduce just enough expressive capacity to capture task‑specific knowledge while keeping computational overhead low enough for everyday users equipped with consumer‑grade GPUs.

### Generalizability Preservation

A subtler requirement concerns *how* much the finetuned model deviates from its pretrained counterpart $M^{0}$.  Early works relied on Euclidean distance $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^{0} \|$ between weight tensors as an implicit proxy for preserving generative quality—a smaller distance was assumed to indicate less disruption of learned semantics.  However, this scalar measure ignores relational structure among neurons within each layer.  

Recent insights reveal that *pairwise angular relationships* between weight vectors encode crucial semantic information \cite{liu2017deep,liu2018decoupled}.  These relationships are formalized by hyperspherical energy:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad
\hat{\bm w}_i = \frac{\bm w_i}{\|\bm w_i\|},
\]
which measures how uniformly neurons lie on the unit hypersphere in their embedding space.  Empirically preserving $\mathrm{HE}$ across fine‐tuning correlates with retaining high‐fidelity diversity after adaptation—a hypothesis central to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).  

Consequently an ideal fine‐tuning scheme should satisfy both practical constraints:
* **Parameter sparsity**—few trainable parameters per layer,
* **Training speed**—rapid convergence within few hundred iterations,
* **Structural preservation**—maintain hyperspherical energy so that angles among neurons—and thus their semantic organization—remain stable,
* **Explicit deviation control**—optionally bound how far orthogonal transformations drift away from identity so that the updated model stays within a calibrated neighborhood of its pretrained initialization (as implemented in Constrained OFT).

Meeting these conditions provides engineers with a reliable toolkit for customizing powerful diffusion generators without sacrificing their original creative potency—a prerequisite for deploying such models in real-world pipelines where both artistic nuance and computational pragmatism matter deeply.
