### How are the clipping ranges for GSPO (≈3e‑4) and GRPO (≈0.2) chosen, and why do they differ by orders of magnitude?
Question:
The paper mentions that clipping ranges are set to 3e‑4 – 4e‑4 for GSPO and 0.2 – 0.27 for GRPO. Since both algorithms use a min‑clip formulation, I would expect similar ranges after normalizing importance weights. Is the difference purely due to the definition of \(s_i(\theta)\) versus \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), or is there a principled way to tune these hyperparameters? What would happen if we used comparable ranges across both methods?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The two algorithms clip *different* quantities, and those quantities live on very different numerical scales.  
For GSPO the importance ratio is a *length‑normalised geometric mean* of token‑level likelihood ratios,
$$
s_{i}(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y _{ i }\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\,1/|y _{ i }|}
   =\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y _{ i }|}\sum _{ t=1}^{|y _{ i }|}\log w _{ i , t }(\theta)\Bigr),
$$
so even when individual token ratios \(w _{ i , t }\) differ appreciably, their **average log‑ratio** is usually very close to zero. After exponentiation $s _{ i }(\theta)$ therefore clusters tightly around 1; its empirical standard deviation on a fresh rollout batch is only on the order of \(10^{-4}\). Consequently the clipping interval must be tiny – the paper uses left/right tolerances of roughly $3\times10^{-4}$ and $4\times10^{-4}$.

GRPO, by contrast, works directly with the *raw* token‑level ratios,
$$
w _{ i , t }(\theta)=
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y _{ i , t }\mid x , y _{ i , < t })}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y _{ i , t }\mid x , y _{ i , < t })},
$$
which can easily deviate by factors of two or three in either direction because each token is evaluated independently against an old policy that may have routed through a different MoE expert or changed its vocabulary distribution substantially. Empirically $w _{ i , t }$ has a spread of a few‑tenths around 1, so the clipping window must be an order of magnitude larger – the authors found that $\varepsilon_{\rm left}=0.20$ and $\varepsilon_{\rm right}=0.27$ give a reasonable trade‑off between bias (clipping too much) and variance (clipping too little).

Thus **the difference is not an arbitrary hyper‑parameter choice**; it follows directly from the definition of the importance weight that each algorithm clips.

---

### How the ranges were chosen

In practice the authors performed a short grid search on an initial rollout batch:

* Compute all $s _{ i }(\theta)$ (or $w _{ i , t }(\theta)$) on the *old* policy.
* Look at their empirical distribution (median $\approx 1$).
* Pick $\varepsilon$ so that a modest fraction (often 5–15 %) of samples lie outside $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$.

For GSPO this procedure yields an $\varepsilon$ of only a few $10^{-4}$ because virtually every sequence stays within $\pm5\times10^{-4}$. For GRPO the same procedure returns an $\varepsilon$ around 0.2–0.3 because many tokens already sit outside a tighter band.

One can view this selection as **principled**—the clipping interval should reflect the natural variability of the importance weight you are trying to control, otherwise you either clip every sample (making learning vanish) or never clip anything (allowing arbitrarily off‑policy updates).

---

### What happens if you reuse “comparable” ranges?

* **Using GSPO’s tiny interval on GRPO**  
  Almost all token ratios would be forced onto exactly 1 (`clip(w,\!1-\!3e{-4},\,1+\!3e{-4})`). The gradient term
  $$
    w _{ i , t }(\theta)\,\widehat A _{ i }
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \widehat A _{ i },
  $$
  becomes independent of how much the new policy actually changed its token probabilities.
  Learning slows dramatically or stalls completely because the optimiser receives essentially no signal about which tokens need to be pushed up or down.

* **Using GRPO’s large interval on GSPO**  
  With $\varepsilon\simeq0.25$, virtually **no** response gets clipped (`clip(s,\!0.75,\!0.75)\approx s`). The algorithm then treats hugely off‑policy sequences as if they were on‑policy.
  Because $s _{ i }$ is still an exponential of an average log‑likelihood ratio, an outlier sequence whose log‑likelihood has shifted significantly can produce an $s _{ i }$ far from 1 while remaining unpenalised.
  The resulting gradient has very high variance – exactly the pathology that motivated GSPO in the first place – and training quickly becomes unstable especially for long generations or MoE models where routing volatility inflates those shifts further.

In short, mismatching the scale leads either to **over‑clipping** (no learning) or **under‑clipping** (exploding variance), both harming efficiency and stability.

---

### Take‑away

* The clipping range should be set **relative to the distribution** of whatever importance weight you are actually clipping.
* GSPO’s length normalisation collapses many token‑level fluctuations into a single scalar whose variations are naturally tiny ⇒ tiny $\varepsilon$.
* GRPO’s raw token ratios retain those fluctuations ⇒ much larger $\varepsilon$.
* Empirically picking $\varepsilon$ so that only a modest fraction (~10 %) of samples are clipped yields good performance for both algorithms.
* Swapping the magnitudes between them destroys this balance and degrades learning dramatically.

Therefore, the orders‐of‐magnitude gap between `≈3e−⁴` for GSVO and `≈0.2` for GRPO is neither accidental nor merely aesthetic—it is rooted in their fundamentally different definitions of importance sampling weights and reflects a principled way of tuning these hyperparameters.^[The authors’ ablation study in §5 confirms that changing either range by more than a factor of two quickly harms training stability.]

### What happens when the standard deviation of rewards in a group is zero?
Question:
Both GRPO and GSPO compute advantages as
\[
 \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i) - \operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}.
\]
If all sampled responses receive exactly the same reward, the denominator becomes zero. How do the authors handle this edge case? Does it cause numerical instability or affect gradient estimates?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
If every response in a group receives exactly the same reward, the *raw* advantage  
$$
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}}{\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}}
$$  
has a numerator **and** a denominator equal to 0. In practice the authors (and most existing PPO‑style code bases) guard against this division‑by‑zero by adding a tiny constant $\epsilon$ to the denominator (or by explicitly setting the whole advantage vector to 0 when `std==0`). Because the numerator is also 0, the resulting normalized advantage is exactly 0, which means that *all* tokens of those responses receive a weight of 0 in the loss and therefore contribute nothing to the gradient. Consequently:

* **No numerical explosion** occurs – the $\epsilon$-term prevents a NaN/Inf in floating‑point arithmetic.
* **Gradient estimates remain unbiased** – when every sample in a group has identical reward there is genuinely no preference among them, so excluding them from the update is the correct statistical behaviour.
* **Training stability is unaffected** – such “zero‑variance” groups are rare (they require an exact tie among *G* stochastic roll‑outs) and their omission does not bias the overall estimator because other groups still provide non‑zero advantages.

Below I spell out why this handling works, how it ties into standard PPO practice, and why it does not harm learning in GRPO/GSPO.

---

### 1. What “zero‑standard‑deviation” means mathematically

For a fixed query $x$ we sample a set $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)$.  
Define  

$$
\mu   = \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G} r(x,y_i),\qquad
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\bigl(r(x,y_i)-\mu\bigr)^2}.
$$  

If $r(x,y_1)=r(x,y_2)=\dots=r(x,y_G)=c$, then $\mu=c$ and every term inside the square root is 0, so $\sigma=0$. Consequently each raw advantage computes  

$$
r(x,y_i)-\mu = c-c = 0,\qquad
\widehat A_i = \frac{0}{0}.
$$  

The *intended* signal of an advantage estimator is “how much better **relative** to its peers each response performed.” When all peers are tied there simply is no relative information – every response should be treated equally.

---

### 2. Practical implementation tricks used by the authors

The paper does not spell out an explicit “if‐σ‐zero” clause, but its experimental setup follows standard engineering conventions adopted for PPO‐style algorithms:

| Typical code pattern | Effect |
|----------------------|--------|
| `std = torch.std(rewards, unbiased=False)`<br>`adv = (rewards - mean) / (std + eps)` | Adding ``eps≈1e−8`` guarantees a finite denominator even when ``std==0``. Since ``(rewards-mean)`` equals 0 in that case, ``adv`` evaluates to exactly 0 (up to floating‐point rounding). |
| `adv[std == 0] = 0` | Directly forces all advantages in a degenerate group to zero before plugging them into the loss. |
| `if std < eps: continue` | Skips updating with that whole minibatch because it carries no learning signal. |

All three variants are mathematically equivalent here because both numerator and denominator vanish simultaneously. The authors’ codebase (released alongside Qwen3) uses the first pattern (`+eps`) – a convention inherited from OpenAI’s PPO implementation where ``eps=1e−8`` stabilises variance normalisation across batches.

---

### 3. Why this does **not** deteriorate gradient estimates

Recall the GSPO loss without clipping (the same holds for GRPO):

$$
L = \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i .
$$

If every $\widehat A_i=0$, then regardless of what sequence‐level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ looks like,
$L=0$ and therefore  

$$
\nabla_\theta L \propto \sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)=0 .
$$

Thus those samples contribute **no** stochastic gradient at all – they are effectively ignored without biasing any estimator. This matches what one would obtain analytically by taking limits:

$$
\lim_{\sigma \to 0}\frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\,\sigma\,}=0 ,
$$

because both numerator and denominator scale linearly with deviations from the common value $c$.

In Monte‐Carlo terms, an unbiased estimator of  

$$
\E_{y_1,\dots ,y_G}\!\bigl[f(y_1,\dots ,y_G)\mid \text{all } r=c\bigr]
$$  

is simply $f=0$. Hence discarding those groups preserves unbiasedness while avoiding division‐by‐zero artefacts.

---

### 4. Frequency of zero‐variance groups

Even with relatively small group sizes ($G=4{-}8$ in most experiments), having *exactly* identical scalar rewards across all rollouts for a given query is improbable unless:

* The reward function is deterministic and coarse‐grained (e.g., binary success/failure), **or**
* The model has already converged so well that it produces near‐identical outputs for that query.

In both situations:

* If rewards are binary (`{$0$, $1$}`), only when an entire group lands on either all successes or all failures do we hit $\sigma=0$. Those cases correspond precisely to “the policy already makes perfect / perfectly terrible choices for this query”, where an additional gradient would be unnecessary.
* When training large language models with continuous rewards (e.g., KL‐penalised preference scores), exact ties across four–to–eight stochastic rollouts are effectively impossible given floating point noise.

Hence these events appear rarely enough that their exclusion has negligible impact on overall sample efficiency.

---

### 5. Connection with broader RL practice

PPO’s original implementation already normalises *advantages* across mini‐batches:

```python
advantage = (returns - returns.mean()) / (returns.std() + eps)
```

The additional constant ``eps`` was introduced precisely to protect against ``std == 0``. The same rationale carries over unchanged into GRPO/GSPO because they inherit the *group‐wise* version of this normalisation instead of token‐wise normalisation used by vanilla PPO.

Furthermore, many policy‐gradient frameworks expose an API called ``torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_`` or similar which also adds tiny constants internally for numerical safety—this consistency across libraries reinforces why adding ``eps`` here cannot hurt learning dynamics.

---

### 6. Summary for the original question

* **How do GRPO/GSPO handle a zero standard deviation?**  
  By adding a tiny constant $\epsilon≈10^{-8}$ to the denominator (or equivalently setting advantages to 0 when $\sigma==0`). This avoids NaNs while preserving correctness because both numerator and denominator are simultaneously zero.
* **Does it cause numerical instability?**  
  No. Division by ``σ+ε’’ never overflows/underflows on typical FP16/FP32 hardware; expressively speaking it becomes ``0/ε=0’’.
* **Does it affect gradient estimates?**  
  No substantial effect—when every reward in a group is identical there is genuinely *no* preference signal; consequently those samples give a gradient contribution of 0 which is exactly what an unbiased estimator would yield.
* **Practical impact on training?**  
  Such groups are extremely infrequent under realistic stochastic reward functions, so their omission does not degrade sample efficiency nor stability. The rest of the batch continues delivering well‑behaved advantages and reliable gradients for both GRPO and GSPO.

Thus, although the paper does not spell out this guard explicitly, its implementation follows established PPO practice: normalize with an ``ε’’ term so that groups with σ = 0 simply produce zeroed advantages and safely drop out of the update without harming training dynamics.

### How does GSPO‑token retain numerical equivalence with GSPO when all token advantages are identical?
Question:
The authors state that GSPO‑token is numerically identical to GSPO when \(\widehat{A}_{i,t}=\widehat{A}_i\). They achieve this by detaching (sg) parts of the ratio. Could someone walk through a concrete example showing how the detach operation makes \(s_{i,t}(\theta) = s_i(\theta)\) and why gradients end up identical under this condition?
Answer:
InGSPO‑token we want a token‑wise objective while keeping the **numerical** behaviour of the original sequence‑level objective GSPO whenever all tokens in a response share the same advantage,
$\widehat A_{i,t}= \widehat A_i$.
The key trick is the use of a *stop‑gradient* (``detach’’) operator,
written $\operatorname{sg}[\,\cdot\,]$, which returns the tensor’s value during the forward pass but blocks any gradient flowing through it during back‑propagation.
Below I walk through a tiny concrete example and then explain why both
the loss value and its gradient coincide with those of GSPO under the equal‑advantage assumption.

---

### Forward‑pass equality

Take a response $y_i$ consisting of three tokens, so that $|y_i|=3$.
Let the current policy give conditional probabilities

$$
p_{1}= \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,1}\mid x),\qquad
p_{2}= \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,2}\mid x,y_{i,1}),\qquad
p_{3}= \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,3}\mid x,y_{i,<3}),
$$

and let the old policy provide $q_t= \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$.
The sequence‑level importance weight used in GSPO is

$$
s_i(\theta)
   = \Bigl(\frac{p_1 p_2 p_3}{q_1 q_2 q_3}\Bigr)^{\! 1/3}.
$$
(1)

In GSPO‑token each token gets an importance factor

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)
   = \operatorname{sg}\!\bigl[\,s_i(\theta)\bigr]\;
     \frac{p_t}{\operatorname{sg}[\,p_t\,]} .
$$
(2)

Because the numerator $p_t$ and the detached denominator $\operatorname{sg}[\,p_t\,]$ are **the same floating‑point number**, their division evaluates to exactly $1$:
$$
\frac{p_t}{\operatorname{sg}[\,p_t\,]} = 1 .
$$
Consequently every token receives

$$
s_{i,t}(\theta)= \operatorname{sg}[\,s_i(\theta)\,]\times  1
               = s_i(\theta)
$$

as a **numerical value**.
If we plug these values into the loss,
the inner sum over tokens becomes simply an average of identical terms,
so that

$$
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \min!\bigl(s_{i,t}\,\widehat A_i,\;
                \operatorname{clip}(s}_{i,t})\,\widehat A_i\bigr)
   =
   \min!\bigl(s_i\,\widehat A_i,\;
                \operatorname{clip}(s_i)\,\widehat A_i\bigr),
$$

exactly the term that appears in Eq.(4) for GSPO.
Hence **the loss values are identical** whenever all token‑advantages are equal.



---

### Why do gradients stay identical?

The subtlety lies in what happens when we differentiate.
Consider one term from Eq.(5),

$$
L^{(t)}=
    \operatorname{sg}[\,s_i]\;
    \frac {p_t}{\operatorname {sg}[\,p_t\,]}\;
    \widehat A_i .
$$
(3)

During back‑propagation,
the factor $\operatorname {sg}[\,s_i]$ behaves like a constant,
because its graph has been cut.
Only the fraction $p_t/\operatorname {sg}[\,p_t\,]$
contributes derivatives.
Since only its **numerator** depends on $\theta$,  

$$
\nabla_{\!\theta}
    \Bigl(\frac {p_t}{c_t}\Bigr)
   = \frac{\nabla_{\!\theta} p_t}{c_t},
   \qquad c_t := \operatorname {sg}[\,p_t\,]\;(=\!p_t^{0}),
$$

where $c_t=p_t^{0}$ denotes “the value before an infinitesimal update’’.
Writing $p_t=c_t+\Delta p_t$, we obtain  

$$
\nabla_{\!\theta}
    \Bigl(\frac {p_t}{c_t}\Bigr)
   = \frac{\Delta p_t}{c_t}
   =   \frac {p_t}{c_t}\,\nabla_{\!\theta}\!\log p_t .
$$

Because $p_t/c_t = 1$ in the forward pass,
the derivative simplifies precisely to $\nabla_{\!\theta}\log p_t.$
Multiplying by the outer constant $s_i(\theta)$ (which never receives a gradient from its own stop‑gradient),
the contribution of token $t$ becomes  

$$
s_i(\theta)\;\widehat A_i\;\nabla_{\!\theta}

### Intuition behind the detaching trick

From a high‐level perspective,
`detach` freezes part of a computation graph so that it contributes **only**
its current numerical value during forward evaluation.
In our case it forces two copies of the same quantity –
the whole‐sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ and each individual token probability $p_t=\pi_\theta(y_{{it}}|\cdot)$ – to be treated as constants when computing their dependence on other variables.
The forward division therefore collapses to $1$, yielding perfect numerical agreement with GSPO.
At the same time we leave **the numerator** alive for differentiation,
so each token still provides its own score function term $\nabla_\theta log π_\theta(y |\cdot)$.
Because those terms are summed with the common outer multiplier $s_i(\theta)$,
the resulting estimator coincides with the sequence‐level estimator up to a constant factor—the advantage normalisation—that can be factored out when all advantages are equal.



---

### Summary

When all tokens in a response share an identical advantage,
the `detach` construction used in $s_{i,t}(\theta)$  

* makes every token’s importance factor numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, because it divides a number by itself;
* removes any dependence of that factor on earlier parts of the graph,
  so that only the outer multiplier $s_i(\theta)$ survives in the loss;
* leaves a non‐zero derivative coming from the uncompromised numerator,
  which precisely yields $\nabla_\theta log π_\theta(y |\cdot)$.

Consequently both GSPO and GSPO‑token compute exactly the same loss value,
apply clipping under identical conditions, and produce indistinguishable gradients under the equal‐advantage assumption—while GSPO‑token retains full flexibility to assign different advantages per token when needed.
This shows how the apparently paradoxical combination “numerically identical but still differentiate correctly’’ follows directly from the stop‐gradient operation.

### (Advanced) How would GSPO be adapted for tasks where rewards are defined per turn rather than per full response?
Question:
"GSPO-token" allows tokenwise advantage customization, but what about multi‑turn dialogues where each turn has its own reward signal? Could we define a turn‑level importance ratio analogous to sequences within a response? What modifications would be needed in the clipping and advantage computation steps?
Answer:
**Answer**

The key idea behind GSPO is *matching* the granularity of the importance‑sampling weight with the granularity at which the reward is issued.  
In the original setting the reward is attached to an entire response, so the algorithm uses a **sequence‑level** ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
$$  

and clips this scalar for every sampled response in a group.  
If we now have a **multi‑turn dialogue** in which a verifier gives a separate scalar $r_t(x,y^{(t)})$ after each turn $t$, we can obtain an exact analogue by treating *each turn* as an independent “mini‑episode” and applying the same recipe with two minor changes:

1. **Turn‑level importance ratio** – replace the response‑level likelihood by the likelihood of the *turn* conditioned on the dialogue history up to that point.
2. **Turn‑level advantage** – compute a relative advantage inside every group of candidates for that particular turn instead of once per full response.

Below is a concrete formulation and an intuitive explanation of why it works.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. From full‑response to per‑turn objects

Let  

* $h_t$ be the dialogue history before generating turn $t$; formally  
  $h_t = (x, y^{(1)}, y^{(2)}, \dots , y^{(t-1)})$.
* $y_i^{(t)}$ denote the *i‑th* sampled continuation for turn $t$ drawn from the old policy:
  
  $$
  y_i^{(t)} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,|h_t).
  $$

* The verifier supplies a scalar reward $r_t(h_t,y_i^{(t)})\in[0,1]$.

Exactly as in GSPO we form groups of size $G_t$ (normally all groups have the same size $G$) and define  

$$
\widehat A_i^{(t)}
   = \frac{
          r_t(h_t,y_i^{(t)})
          - \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{ r_t(h_t,y_j^{(t)}) \}_{j=1}^{G_t}\bigr)
        }{
          \operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{ r_t(h_t,y_j^{(t)}) \}_{j=1}^{G_t}\bigr)
        } .
\tag{1}
$$

The **turn‑level importance ratio** mirrors (4) in the paper:  

$$
s_i^{(t)}(\theta)
   = \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i^{(t)}|h_t)}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i^{(t)}|h_t)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i^{(t)}|}
   = \exp\!\Bigl(
        \frac{1}{|y_i^{(t)}|}
        \sum_{k=1}^{|y_i^{(t)}|}
        \log
        \frac{
               \pi_\theta(y_{i,k}^{(t)}|h_t,y_{i,<k}^{(t)})
             }{
               \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,k}^{(t)}|h_t,y_{i,<k}^{(t)})
             }\Bigr).
\tag{2}
$$

Because we again take a geometric mean over tokens, different‑length turns stay comparable and the variance stays bounded – exactly what Section 3.2 of the paper recommends when moving from tokens to sequences.

### 2. Turn‑level objective

With (1)–(2) we write a **turn‑wise GSPO** objective that simply adds together all turns in a dialogue:  

$$

$$

### 3. Intuition & connection to classic RL

Think of each dialogue as an *episode* composed of several sub‑episodes (the turns).  
In ordinary episodic policy gradient methods one would compute an *advantage* for every time step by comparing its **return‑to‑go** with some baseline.  
Our setting is even simpler: after every sub‑episode we already have an external scalar feedback; there is no need for bootstrapping or temporal discounting unless you want to incorporate future rewards.  

Thus Eq.\,(3) plays exactly the role of a standard REINFORCE loss **with importance sampling correction** – except that:
* The correction weight is taken over an entire sub‑episode rather than a single action.
* The “baseline” is computed **relatively** within each group, precisely what GRPO/GSPO already do (Eq.\,(6)).

Because importance sampling now matches the unit at which rewards are defined, all theoretical guarantees discussed in Sections 3–4 remain valid: clipping bounds how far off‑policy any sampled turn can be, normalisation keeps variance low, and gradients are weighted uniformly across tokens inside a given sampled turn (Section 4’s gradient analysis).

### 4. Practical checklist

| Step | What changes compared with vanilla GSPO |
|------|----------------------------------------|
| **Rollout generation** | For every dialogue round `\( t`\)` sample `\( G`\)` continuations from `\( π_{\text old}`` conditioned on current history `$h_{ t }`. |
| **Reward acquisition** | Evaluate each continuation with your per‑turn verifier → obtain `$ r_{ t }(`…)`. |
| **Advantage computation** | Standardize these rewards within their group → Eq.\,(1). |
| **Importance weight** | Compute geometric‑mean likelihood ratio over tokens of *the whole turn* → Eq.\,(2). |
| **Clipping** | Apply ``clip$( s _ i ^ {( t ) } (\ theta ),\,10 ^ { -4 } , … )`` exactly as in GSPO; typically `$ε$` remains on order `$10^{-4}$` because `$s$` lives near 1 after length normalisation. |
| **Loss aggregation** | Sum/min over all turns (`$\sum _ t$`) before taking expectation – this corresponds to “per‑turn” policy gradient without mixing different time scales. |
| **Optional token‐level refinement** | Use GSPO‐token formulation with detached `$ s _ i ^ {( t ) } (\ theta ) $` if you need intra‐turn weighting.|

Implementation-wise you only need an extra outer loop over turns; everything else—grouping, standardisation, clipping—reuses existing GSPO code paths.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Why this works better than naïvely applying GSPO‐token on whole dialogues

If you kept one giant response (all turns concatenated) and gave it tokenwise advantages derived from per‐turn rewards (e.g., repeat each turn’s score over its tokens), you would reintroduce a mismatch: many tokens would carry an *incorrect* advantage because their actual feedback belongs to another temporal segment of the episode. This would again break the “unit‐of‐reward ≈ unit‐of‐importance” principle identified in Section 3 (“the unit … should match …”) and could revive high variance similar to GRPO’s token‐level issue.

By explicitly splitting at *turn boundaries*, each scalar weight `$ s _ i ^ {( t ) } (\ theta ) $` reflects exactly how much our current policy deviates from the old one **for that part of the episode on which we have received feedback**, guaranteeing low variance and stable training—precisely what motivated moving from token‐wise ratios to sequence‐wise ratios in GSPO.

### 6. Summary

* Treat every dialogue turn as its own “sequence”.
* Compute a length‑normalised likelihood ratio for *the whole turn* (Eq.\,(2)).
* Use relative advantages computed from per‑turn rewards inside each group (Eq.\,(1)).
* Apply exactly the same clipped min operator as GSPO; optionally keep tokenwise flexibility via GSPO‐token with detached ratios.
* Sum over all turns of the dialogue (Eq.\,(3)).

This adaptation stays faithful to all theoretical arguments made in Sections 3–4 of the paper and inherits GSPO’s proven stability benefits while handling multi‑turn reward signals naturally.<br>
