# Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models  

Reinforcement learning has become an indispensable component when seeking to push the boundaries of what large language models (LLMs) can achieve.  Whereas supervised fine‑tuning trains a model merely to reproduce human demonstrations, reinforcement learning equips a model with a mechanism for improving its own behaviour through interaction with an explicit signal that rewards desired outcomes and penalizes undesired ones.  In modern practice this signal often comes from a learned verifier or “reward model” that evaluates completed generations on criteria such as factual accuracy, usefulness or adherence to user intent.  By repeatedly generating responses under its current parameters, receiving rewards, and updating its parameters to increase expected reward, an LLM can acquire capabilities far beyond those obtainable from static supervision alone—including competition‑level mathematics solving and sophisticated program synthesis—as reported throughout recent work on scaling language models through reinforcement learning [[o1];[dpsk-r1];[qwq32b];[qwen3]].

In what follows we introduce the theoretical underpinnings of this paradigm, explain how autoregressive language models naturally give rise to stochastic policies suitable for reinforcement‑learning optimization, describe typical forms taken by reward functions over textual outputs, outline the overall feedback loop commonly referred to as “RL with Human Feedback” (RLHF), and finally collect the principal symbols and terms that recur throughout this textbook.



## Why reinforcement learning is essential for scaling LLM capabilities  

Supervised pretraining teaches an LLM how to predict the next token given preceding context drawn from massive corpora.  Although this pretraining yields impressive zero‑shot abilities, it does not guarantee alignment between what the model produces and what users ultimately require.  When faced with complex multi‑step problems—such as proving mathematical statements or writing correct programs—the best next‑token prediction may still lead down unproductive reasoning paths because no direct feedback penalizes costly mistakes early in a long generation trace.

Reinforcement learning supplies precisely that missing feedback loop.  By defining a scalar return \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) associated with every completed query–response pair \((x,y)\), one can shape the distribution over entire output sequences rather than merely over individual tokens.  The empirical evidence presented in recent studies shows dramatic gains when applying large‑scale reinforcement learning: models trained under such procedures succeed on benchmarks that require sustained logical deduction (e.g., AIME’24), program synthesis under stringent correctness criteria (e.g., LiveCodeBench), and competitive coding performance measured by Elo ratings (e.g., CodeForces).  

Crucially, extending reinforcement learning from modest‑size models toward trillion‑parameter experts raises new practical challenges.  Existing algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) already reduce reliance on an auxiliary value network—a major memory burden—but exhibit severe stability problems when applied at extreme scale [[grpo]].  Empirically observed “catastrophic collapse’’ manifests when long response sequences combine high‑variance gradient estimates with aggressive clipping mechanisms designed originally for token‑wise updates.  The instability grows especially acute for mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) architectures whose sparse gating induces volatile activation patterns across successive policy updates; standard token‐level corrections become unreliable because experts activated under an old policy may differ dramatically after even a single gradient step.



In short, while reinforcement learning opens up pathways toward ever more capable text generators,
its successful deployment at scale demands algorithms whose mathematical foundations respect two simple principles:
firstly that importance weights should reflect true distributional changes between old and new policies;
and secondly that those weights ought to operate at a granularity matching the granularity at which rewards are assigned—that is,
at the **sequence level** rather than at isolated token steps.


## Autoregressive language models as stochastic policies  

An autoregressive language model trained on a corpus learns a conditional distribution over tokens given any prefix whatsoever.  
When we parameterize such a model by \(\theta\) we interpret its generative behavior directly as a **policy**,
which we denote \(\pi_{\!\theta}\).  
Formally,
\[
\pi_{\!\theta}(y\,|\,x)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}
    \pi_{\!\theta}\bigl(y_t\,\big|\,
        x,\,
        y_{<t}
    \bigr),
\qquad
|y|
=
\text{length of }y .
\]
Here \(x\) denotes an input query drawn from some dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), while \(y=(y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})\) denotes a sampled continuation produced token by token according to \(\pi_{\!\theta}\).  

Because each conditional factor \(\pi_{\!\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})\) is itself a probability distribution,
sampling repeatedly yields different continuations even when presented with identical prompts;
this stochasticity precisely matches the formalism required by most reinforcement‑learning algorithms,
in which an agent samples actions according to its current policy before receiving feedback.


In practice training proceeds iteratively:
at iteration \(k\) one fixes an **old** set of parameters \(\theta_{\text{old}}\),
samples many tuples 
\(x\rsim \mathcal{D},\, 
    y_1,\dots , y_G\rsim 
        \pi_{\!\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\,|\,x)\)
where \(G\geqslant 2\) denotes how many *responses* are drawn per prompt,
and then uses these *rollouts* together with learned rewards 
to improve upon \(\theta_{\text{old}}\).
The distinction between old (\(\pi_{\!\theta_{\text{old}}}\)) 
and new (\(\pi_\theta\)) policies is central:
reinforcement‑learning updates are intrinsically *off‑policy*—
the data were generated under one distribution whereas gradients are computed under another—
and therefore principled correction mechanisms based on *importance sampling* become indispensable.


## Reward functions for generated text  

The participant who evaluates each candidate response during training is called a **verifier**,
and its output serves exactly as the scalar **reward** 
\(r : \mathcal{X}\times \mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]\),
where \(\mathcal X\) denotes queries,
and \(\mathcal Y\) denotes all feasible completions.
In concrete deployments this verifier may summarize several desiderata:
linguistic fluency persists automatically through likelihood maximization;
more task–specific attributes such as factual **correctness**, instruction following **helpfulness**, 
or adherence to safety constraints translate into higher values assigned when those properties hold,
and lower values otherwise.
Thus every pair $(x,y)$ receives a real number $r(x,y)$ quantifying its overall utility according
to whatever combination of criteria has been encoded into $r$.
The bounded interval $[0,1]$ simplifies subsequent normalizations
and ensures numerical stability when expectations are estimated.



Rewards are never used directly inside log‑likelihood terms;
rather they act through *advantage* estimates,

## High‑level overview of the RLHF pipeline  

Combining the elements above yields an iterative loop typically called **Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback** (RLHF).  
Each iteration proceeds through four tightly coupled stages:

**(i) Generation under an old policy**  
Given a batch of prompts sampled uniformly from $\mathcal D$,
the current checkpoint $\theta_{\text old}$ produces multiple completions per prompt:
$\displaystyle 
    \bigl( {x},\, y_1,\dots , y_G\bigr)
    \;\sim\
    {\mathcal D}\times
        {\bigl[\;\pi_{\!\theta_{\text old}}(\cdot |x)\;\bigr]}^{G}.
$
This step leverages highly parallel hardware,
and modern pipelines often partition these rollouts into several mini‑batches so that gradient descent can be performed multiple times per set of rollouts without re‑sampling.


**(ii) Evaluation via a verifier**  
Each sampled continuation $y_i$ receives a scalar score
$r(x,y_i)$ computed by an independently trained verification network.
Because $r$ maps into $[0,1]$, it serves directly as a proxy for human preference;
benchmark results reported later (e.g., Pass@k scores on systematic test suites)
are precisely aggregations over these verifier judgments.


**(iii) Advantage computation**  
For every prompt’s response set we calculate an *advantage*
as shown above:
the centered‐and‐scaled difference between each individual reward 
and its empirical mean over all $G$ candidates.
This construction removes absolute magnitude variations across queries —
only relative ordering matters —
which stabilizes training especially when absolute scores differ wildly among prompts.

With advantages already computed,
an objective function determines how parameters should change.

# Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models  

Reinforcement learning has become an indispensable ingredient when trying ­to push very large language models far beyond what pure supervised pre‑training can offer.  By letting a model receive scalar feedback after generating whole answers—and then adjusting its parameters so that higher­scoring answers become more probable—the model learns behaviours such as multi­step logical deduction, precise code synthesis or faithful factual answering that would otherwise remain elusive.  The remainder of this chapter explains why these ideas matter at scale, how autoregressive generative models naturally act as stochastic policies suitable for reinforcement learning, what kind of reward signals are employed during training, how all components fit together in today’s “RL with Human Feedback’’ (RLHF) workflow, and finally collects the principal symbols and terminology used throughout the book.



## Why reinforcement learning is essential for scaling LLM capabilities  

Large autogressive language models acquire most basic linguistic knowledge while being trained only to predict next tokens in massive corpora.  This next‑token prediction objective alone does not guarantee alignment between what a model says and what users ultimately desire—for example whether a generated algebraic proof actually reaches the right conclusion or whether synthesized code compiles without error.  

When developers augment pure pretraining with **reinforcement learning**, every completed interaction $(x,y)$ between a user prompt $x$ and a generated response $y$ receives a scalar “reward’’ $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.  
That scalar encodes properties valued by downstream users—such as factual correctness, helpfulness toward user intent or safety compliance—and therefore provides direct supervision on *entire* sequences rather than merely on individual next‐token predictions.  

Empirical observations documented in recent work confirm dramatic gains once such feedback loops are installed:   
models trained under reinforcement signaling achieve state‑of‑the‑art performance on benchmarks requiring deep reasoning (e.g., AIME’24 mathematics problems), systematic program synthesis (e.g., LiveCodeBench pass@k metrics), and competitive coding skill assessment (e.g., Codeforces Elo rating). These achievements demonstrate clearly that reinforcement signals enable **capability scaling** far beyond what supervised data alone permits.  

When we move toward ever larger parameter counts—including sparsely gated Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures—the stability requirements become stricter.  Prior algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimisation (**GRPO**) already remove dependence on auxiliary value networks—which otherwise increase memory footprints dramatically—but nevertheless suffer catastrophic instability when applied at extreme scales or when handling long generations (> 200 tokens).  Practitioners have observed “model collapse’’ whereby tiny perturbations introduced during off‑policy correction snowball into unrecoverable degradation even after checkpoint restoration or hyperparameter retuning.



The root cause identified by subsequent analysis lies in **how** off‑policy corrections are performed mathematically: GRPO applies tokenwise importance weights built from single draws out of next–token distributions instead of genuine multi–sample expectations required by classic importance sampling theory (§ 4 below). Because every weight only reflects one sample rather than correcting an entire distributional shift, variance accumulates dramatically along long sequences and performs poorly once clipping mechanisms restrict extreme ratios—a protective trick originally invented for proximal optimisation yet rendered counterproductive here.



In summary,

* Reinforcement signals let us translate abstract desiderata (“answer correctly”, “be helpful”) into concrete optimisation objectives applied directly onto whole generations;
* Scaling those signals up demands careful algorithmic design because naïve off‑policy corrections become unstable when sequences grow long or MoE routing changes abruptly;
* Recognising these failure modes motivates newer approaches that respect fundamental statistical principles—most notably assigning **sequence‑level** importance ratios aligned with sequencewise rewards.

The root cause identified by subsequent analysis lies in **how** off-policy corrections are performed mathematically: GRPE applies tokenwise importance weights built from single draws out of next-token distributions instead of genuine multi-sample expectations required by classic importance sampling theory (§ 4 below). Because every weight only reflects one sample rather than correcting an entire distributional shift,_variance accumulates dramatically along long sequences_and performs poorly once clipping mechanisms restrict extreme ratios—a protective trick originally invented for proximal optimisation yet rendered counterproductive here.



In summary,

* Reinforcement signals let us translate abstract desiderata (“answer correctly”, “be helpful”) into concrete optimisation objectives applied directly onto whole generations;
* Scaling those signals up demands careful algorithmic design because naïve off-policy corrections become unstable when sequences grow long or MoE routing changes abruptly;
* Recognising these failure modes motivates newer approaches that respect fundamental statistical principles—most notably assigning **sequence-level** importance ratios aligned with sequencewise rewards.

Foundations: Notation, Policy Gradient Methods, and Proximal Policy Optimization  

# Formal Definition of a Policy π<sub>θ</sub>(x→y) and Query‑Response Notation  

In reinforcement‑learning (RL) applied to language generation we view an autoregressive language model as a stochastic **policy**.  A policy is a probability distribution over possible output sequences **y** conditioned on an input query **x**; the parameters of this distribution are denoted by θ and written π<sub>θ</sub>.  Concretely, if **y** consists of |y| tokens (y₁,…,y<sub>|y|</sub>) then  

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}
\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{t}\mid x,\ y_{<t}\bigr),
\]

where π<sub>θ</sub>(yₜ∣x,y₍₍ₜ₎₎) is the conditional probability assigned by the model to token yₜ given the query x and all previously generated tokens y₍<t₎ (the “prefix”).  The set of all queries used during training or evaluation is denoted by 𝔇.  For any particular pair (x,y) we may evaluate a *verifier* function  

\[
r(x,y)\in[0,1],
\]

which maps the pair to a scalar reward indicating how well‑behaved or useful that response is—for example whether it solves a math problem correctly or adheres to desired stylistic guidelines.  

Treating generation as an RL problem allows us to improve θ so that high‑reward responses become more likely under π<sub>θ</sub>.  This viewpoint makes explicit two distinct objects:  
* The **policy**, i.e., the probability‑distribution family π<sub>θ</sub>.  
* The **reward**, i.e., r(x,y), which supplies learning signals after each complete response has been produced.  

These definitions constitute our basic notation throughout.  

# Expectation Under a Behavior Distribution vs Target Distribution  

When training we rarely have direct access to samples drawn from the *current* policy π<sub>θ</sub> because we wish to reuse data generated earlier by an *old* version of the model—denoted π<sub>θ_old</sub>.  The old policy serves as a **behavior distribution** (also called a “sampling” distribution), while π<sub>θ</sub> is the **target distribution** whose expectations we ultimately want to maximize:

\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\,x\sim\mathcal D,\ y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,].
\]

Because samples come from π<sub>θ_old</sub>, we invoke **importance sampling** to rewrite expectations over π<sub>θ</sub> in terms of those over π<sub>θ_old</sub>.  The fundamental identity used throughout this work (see § 3) is  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
\Bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(f(z)\) can be any measurable function—in our case it could be \(r(x,y)\) or its variance‑reduced surrogate.  Equation (1) tells us that if we weight each sampled datum by an **importance ratio**  

\[
\rho(z)=\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)},
\]

the weighted average over behavior samples yields an unbiased estimate of an expectation under the target distribution.  In practice achieving low variance requires many independent samples \(N\gg1\); otherwise random fluctuations in individual ratios can dominate learning signals—a point emphasized repeatedly when discussing instability in token‑level methods such as GRPO (§ 3).

# Derivation of the REINFORCE Gradient Estimator  

The canonical way to obtain an unbiased stochastic gradient of \(J(\theta)\) leverages two elementary tricks: (i) rewriting \(J(\theta)\) as an expectation under π<sub>θ</sub>, and (ii) applying the **log‑derivative identity**  

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\,\log \pi_{\theta}(y|x)
=
\tfrac{\nabla_{\theta}\,\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}.
\tag{2}
\]

Starting from  

\[
J(\theta)=
\E_{\,x\sim \mathcal D,\ y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}
       [\,r(x,y)\,],
\]

we differentiate under the expectation sign (justified because r does not depend on θ):

\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\E_{\,x,\ y}
     [\,r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x)\,].
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) is precisely the *REINFORCE* gradient estimator introduced by Williams (1992).  It shows that if we sample a query‑response pair (x,y) from π<sub>θ</sub>, compute its reward r(x,y), and multiply it by ∇θ log π<sub>θ</sub>(y∣x), then averaging such terms yields an unbiased estimate of ∇θJ(θ).

Because raw rewards often possess high variance across episodes—especially when rewards are sparse or binary—the estimator can be sharpened by subtracting any baseline b(x) that does not depend on y:

\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta)
=
\E_{\,x,\ y}
     [(r(x,y)-b(x))
      \,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x)].
\tag{4}
\]

Choosing b(x)=V(x), where V denotes an estimate of expected return given x (the **value function**), yields what practitioners call an *advantage* term

\[
A(x,y)=r(x,y)-V(x),
\]

which typically exhibits lower variance than raw rewards alone.  This idea lies at the heart of modern actor‑critic methods—including Proximal Policy Optimization—where V is learned by a separate neural network (“value model”) sharing roughly the same capacity as the policy itself (§ 5).

# Proximal Policy Optimization Objective Formulation With Clipping  

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) augments REINFORCE with two stabilizing ingredients:

1. **Clipping** bounds how far any single update can move probabilities relative to their values under an older “reference” policy π<sub>θ_old</sub>.  
2. **Advantage normalization** replaces raw advantages with estimates derived from a learned critic network.

For language generation tasks we treat each token independently when forming gradients because rewards are provided only after completing an entire sequence.  Accordingly define at token position t   

\[
w_t(\theta)
=
\dfrac{
      \pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,\ y_{<t})
    }{
      \pi_{\!{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{old}}}}\bigl(y_t\,|\,x,\ y_{<t}\bigr)
    },
%
~~
A_t=\widehat A_t
%
,
\\[6pt]
%
s_t^\pm = 
    {\rm clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\bigr).
}
    
The overall PPO surrogate objective summed over tokens reads

\[
%
\mathcal J_{\!\rm PPO}(\theta)
=
\E_{
   x\simeq{\cal D},\
   y\simeq \pi_{{}^{!}{old}}(\cdot | x)}
   {
   {1 \over |y|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
   \min
   {
      w_t(\theta)\,\widehat A_t,
      ~~ s_t^\pm\, \widehat A_t
   }
   }.
   % 
   \tag{5}
%
\]

The inner ``min'' implements *clipping*: should \(w_t(\theta)\) stray outside ``[1−ε, 1+ε]'', its contribution gets replaced by that produced at exactly one boundary location (depending on sign(ĤA)).  Consequently any single sample cannot push probabilities arbitrarily far away from those recorded under π<sub>θ_old</sub>, thereby enforcing a **proximal region** around the old policy during optimization—a practical proxy for more expensive trust‑region methods.

The factor \(1/|y|\) normalizes over response length so sequences with differing token counts contribute comparably to loss values—a design choice that aids stability when dealing with variable‑length outputs common in language modeling tasks.

PPO thus retains the unbiasedness property inherent in Eq.(3)-(4), while its clipped surrogate helps mitigate detrimental large updates caused by noisy advantage estimates—a crucial consideration when dealing with high‑dimensional policies such as large language models.



# Role and Challenges of Value Function Estimation in PPO  

The term $\widehat A_t$ appearing above represents an *advantage estimate*. In standard implementations this estimate derives from an auxiliary **value model**, often denoted $V_\phi$ where φ are parameters distinct from θ:

```
ẐA_t ≈ r(x , y ) − V_φ( x , y_<t ).
```

For token‐wise updates one needs V̂ at each prefix $y_{<t}$ because rewards are supplied only after completing full generations; therefore $V_φ$ must predict future returns conditioned on arbitrary partial sequences—a demanding requirement indeed mentioned explicitly in § 2:

> “the core challenge of PPO … lies in its heavy reliance on the value model…the value model usually has a similar size to the policy model.”

Consequently two intertwined problems arise:

* **Memory & compute burden:** Replicating a near‑full‑size transformer just for V_φ effectively doubles GPU memory usage per worker while simultaneously increasing arithmetic work per forward/backward pass.
* **Statistical reliability:** Even assuming sufficient capacity, learning accurate predictions for *all possible prefixes* becomes progressively harder as response length grows—and errors directly translate into biased advantage estimates which can destabilize updates despite clipping safeguards.

When scaling up – e.g., moving from tens-of-millions parameters toward tens-of‑billions – maintaining two equally massive networks quickly exceeds practical hardware limits or forces severe reductions in batch size or sequence length . Moreover real‐world datasets dispatch heterogeneous queries stimulating diverse decoding trajectories threaded through long contexts; mastering robust value estimation across such breadth remains difficult despite sophisticated training tricks.



# Why PPO Becomes Burdensome for Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs), especially those reaching billions (or even trillions) of parameters, inherit several properties making vanilla PPO untenable:

1. **Duplicated Model Size:** Since both actor (policy π_θ ) and critic (V_φ ) occupy comparable memory footprints, employing them simultaneously strains GPU memory pools dramatically – sometimes requiring multi‑GPU sharding even when inference alone would fit comfortably on a single device.
2. **Token‐Level Computation Explosion:** Evaluating advantages entails constructing per‑token logits twice—once through π_θ and once through V_φ for every sampled prefix $y_{<t}$. For long responses ($|y|$ often exceeding hundreds if tasks demand deep reasoning), this multiplies compute expense roughly proportional to |y|.
3. **Variance Growth With Sequence Length:** The stochastic nature inherent in Eq.(5)’s sample average accumulates across tokens; more tokens imply larger cumulative variance unless offset by dramatically larger minibatches—which again raises memory demands.
4. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** As noted throughout § 2 “the algorithmic effectiveness hinges on reliability … ensuring its scalability … presents an even greater challenge.” Choosing ε appropriately becomes difficult when likelihood ratios vary wildly across billions­​of‐parameter policies exploring long tails.
5. **Infrastructure Overheads:** Practical pipelines frequently partition massive rollout batches into several minibatches so GPU utilization stays high during training—a process described explicitly in § 3 (“partitioning … inevitably introduces an off‑policy learning setting”). This off‑policy nature necessitates clipping mechanisms like those shown above; however their protective effect diminishes if underlying importance weights suffer high variance—in other words exactly what occurs when using token‑wise ratios wₜ(θ).

Collectively these constraints limit training efficiency and aggravate stability concerns described early in § 1 (“current state-of-the-art RL algorithms … exhibit severe stability issues… resulting in catastrophic model collapse”). This motivates subsequent research directions—including algorithms such as GRPO which attempt exploiting groupwise relative advantages without requiring V_φ , and ultimately GSPO which elects sequence­‑level importance ratios rather than token­‑wise ones—to overcome PPO’s scalability bottleneck while preserving reliable RL improvement signals.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Design and Shortcomings  

# Motivation behind removing the value network  

Reinforcement learning with language models traditionally follows the paradigm of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). PPO relies on two intertwined components: a **policy network** that generates text and a **value network** that predicts expected future reward (the “advantage”) for every possible continuation. In practice, training very large language models—often exceeding tens of billions of parameters—makes it cumbersome to keep an additional value model that is comparable in size to the policy itself. The memory footprint doubles, the compute cost roughly doubles, and any inconsistency between policy and value networks can destabilize learning. Moreover, obtaining reliable token‑level value estimates becomes increasingly difficult as responses grow longer and tasks become more intricate; errors in the value model directly propagate into noisy gradient updates.  

The designers of **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (GRPO) therefore set out to eliminate the explicit value network altogether. Instead of estimating an absolute expected return via a separate model, GRPO extracts an *relative* signal from a *group* of responses generated for the same query. By normalizing rewards within each group—subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation—GRPO obtains an advantage estimate that is automatically centered around zero and scaled appropriately. This circumvented need for any auxiliary regression target while preserving the essential role of advantages in policy gradient methods: guiding updates toward higher‑reward responses without overshooting because of large raw reward magnitudes.  

Beyond memory savings, this design simplifies engineering pipelines: there is no necessity to train two coupled neural networks in lockstep or to keep them synchronized during distributed rollouts. Consequently, researchers hoped that GRPO would retain PPO’s robustness while being far more scalable to modern large language models (LLMs).  

# Definition of a response group per query  

The cornerstone of GRPO is the notion of a **response group** associated with each query $x$ drawn from the data distribution $\mathcal D$. For a fixed query $x$, GRPO instructs the current *old* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ to sample **$G$ distinct completions**:
\[
\{\,y_i\,\}_{i=1}^{G}\;\sim\;\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\bigl(\,\cdot\,|\,x\bigr).
\]
Each $y_i$ is a complete token sequence whose length we denote by $|y_i|$. The entire batch thus consists of many groups sampled independently; each group supplies both diversity (different completions) and a built‑in baseline for relative evaluation.  

By gathering several candidates per query we achieve two goals simultaneously. First, we obtain multiple independent estimates of reward for essentially identical *task conditions*—the only variable being which tokens were generated. Second, we can treat these candidates as samples from an *empirical distribution* induced by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ over completions for $x$. This empirical distribution becomes the reference against which we later compute relative advantages (see next section). In practice, typical values of $G$ range from three to ten depending on compute budget; larger groups provide smoother baselines but incur higher sampling cost during rollout generation.  

It is important to note that while sampling occurs from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, gradients are computed with respect to the *new* policy parameters $\theta$. This off‑policy setting necessitates mechanisms—most notably importance weighting—to correct for distribution shift between old and new policies during learning.  

# Group‑based advantage computation using mean‑std normalization  

Once rewards have been assigned to each response by a verifier function $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$, GRPO converts raw scores into **relative advantages** via simple statistical normalization across the group:
\[
\widehat A_i 
      \;=\;
      \frac{
            r(x,y_i)
            \;-\;
            \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\,\{\,r(x,y_j)\,\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
          }
          {
            \operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\,\{\,r(x,y_j)\,\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)
          }.
\tag{1}
\]
Every token within response $y_i$ inherits exactly this same scalar $\widehat A_i$, i.e.
$\widehat A_{i,t} = \widehat A_i$ for all positions $t$.  
Intuitively, this transformation accomplishes three things:

1. **Centering:** Subtracting the mean removes any global bias in reward scale; if all candidates received uniformly high scores (or uniformly low scores), their advantages become zero and no gradient signal is produced—a desirable property because it prevents pointless updates when the task does not differentiate between candidates.
2. **Scaling:** Dividing by the standard deviation rescales rewards so that typical advantages lie roughly within $[-1,1]$. This stabilizes learning rates across groups whose raw reward variance may fluctuate dramatically.
3. **Relative judgment:** By comparing each candidate against its peers rather than against an absolute benchmark, GRPO directly promotes *rank ordering*: higher‑reward responses get positive advantages while lower‑reward ones obtain negative values.

Because these advantages are computed *per group*, they do not depend on any learned value estimator nor any external baseline such as running averages across batches. Their statistical nature makes them cheap to compute yet effective at emphasizing useful differences among sampled completions.  

# Token‑level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ used in GRPO  

While advantages encapsulate “how much better or worse” a response feels relative to its peers, estimates must also be corrected for **policy shift** between old and new parameters when performing gradient ascent. In PPO this correction takes the form of an **importance weight**:
\[
w_t(\theta)
    = 
    \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}.
\]
GRPO adopts exactly this *token‑level* ratio but indexes it by both response $i$ and position $t$:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
    =
    \frac{
        \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
        }
        {
        \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
        }.
\tag{2}
\]
The rationale mirrors PPO’s original derivation: if one could draw many samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ at every time step, weighting each sample by $w_t(\theta)$ would yield an unbiased estimate of expectations under $\pi_\theta$. By attaching this weight to each log‐probability term in the gradient estimator,
GRPO multiplies every token’s contribution by its own measure of how likely the new policy would have chosen that exact token relative to its old incarnation:
\[
\nabla_\theta
%
=
%
\mathbb E_{{x,\;\mathbf y}}
   \Biggl[
     \frac 1 G
     \sum_{i=1}^{G}
     \frac 1 {|y_i|}
     \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     w_{i,t}(\theta)\;\widehat A_i
     \nabla_\theta
       \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
   \Biggr].
% 
\tag{3}
\]
In practice GRPO further mitigates extreme weight values through *clipping*: 
$\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon ,\, 1+\varepsilon)$,
ensuring that overly off‑policy samples cannot dominate updates—a safeguard inherited directly from PPO’s surrogate loss formulation.

Thus, even though GRPO discards an explicit value network, it still adheres closely to PPO’s core machinery by retaining tokenwise importance weighting combined with clipped surrogates—all multiplied by group‐normalized advantages derived without any additional model.  

# $\ell$‑wise versus whole‑sequence objectives  

A subtle yet profound design decision in GRPO concerns **the granularity at which off‑policy correction is applied**. The term “$\ell$‑wise” refers precisely to applying importance weighting at each token position individually (where $\ell$ denotes sequence length). In this setting:

- Rewards are assigned **once per full sequence**, reflecting end‐to‐end task performance.
- Importance ratios are computed **per token**, i.e., $\ell$ separate factors are multiplied into the loss for a sequence of length $\ell$.
- Each token’s gradient contribution is scaled differently according to its local ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.

In contrast, a **whole‐sequence objective** would attach a single importance factor derived from *the full likelihood* of generating an entire response under new vs.\ old policies:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
  \Bigl(
      {\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}/
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
  \Bigr)^{\,1/|y_i|}
  = 
  \exp
    {\Bigl(
       {1\over |y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \log w_{i,t}(\theta)
     \Bigr)} .
\tag{4}
\]
Such sequence‐level ratios treat all tokens inside a response identically after length normalization—a concept later embodied in Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). It aligns cleanly with sequence rewards because both quantities now speak about entire trajectories rather than individual atomic actions.

GRPO deliberately opted for an $\ell$‑wise formulation because it stays faithful to classical PPO derivations where policies are updated incrementally after every action selection step (\emph{i.e.,} after each generated token). Moreover, fine‐grained weighting seemed attractive theoretically: if some tokens become dramatically less probable under the new policy while others become more probable, scaling them separately could preserve detailed information about where exactly policy changes matter most.

However, this choice introduced hidden tensions:

- **Reward–weight mismatch:** The reward signal does not vary across tokens—it pertains only to the final answer quality—yet tensors are multiplied by potentially very different weights per position.
- **Variance amplification:** Each weight $w_{i,t}$ contains stochasticity from sampling one particular token from its categorical distribution; chaining many such stochastic factors across tens or hundreds of positions yields high variance estimators.
- **Clipping interaction:** Since clipping acts on individual ratios independently, even modest deviations at just a few positions can cause those tokens' contributions to be suppressed entirely while other tokens remain fully active – thereby distorting credit assignment between successes/failures along a trajectory.

Thus while mathematically sound under certain i.i.d assumptions about independent token draws, applying an $\ell$‑wise correction under realistic language generation settings turns out far riskier than initially anticipated.

# Observed catastrophic collapse on long responses  

Empirical investigations revealed that applying GRPO’s tokenwise importance correction becomes increasingly unstable as two factors grow:

1. **Model scale:** Larger language models possess richer parametric capacity but also exhibit sharper probability distributions over next tokens due partly to better learned representations.
2. **Response length:** Many RL finetuning tasks involve multi‐sentence answers or code blocks extending beyond several hundred tokens—in such regimes one necessarily multiplies hundreds of per‐token importance weights together (implicitly through averaging across timesteps).

When either factor intensifies,
the product \( w_{i,t}(\theta)^{~?}\) or sum \( s_i(\theta)= (\prod_t w_{i,t})^{1/|y_i|}\) accrues substantial stochastic fluctuation even before clipping is applied. The variance observed during training can dominate gradient estimates so severely that updates push parameters into degenerate regions where likelihoods collapse drastically—that is, all generated tokens become uniformly low probability and rewards cease improving (`catastrophic collapse`). 

Practically researchers observed phenomena such as:

- Sudden spikes in loss together with near–zero average reward.
- Once collapsed weights were encountered their effect persisted even after reverting checkpoints or reducing learning rates.
- Adjustments on hyperparameters—tightening clipping bounds ($\varepsilon$), lowering batch sizes, redefining reward functions—failed repeatedly unless fundamental changes were made to how off‑policy corrections were handled.
  
The root cause can be traced back conceptually via importance sampling theory presented earlier: proper correction requires averaging over many samples drawn from the behavior distribution before applying weights—GRPO’s reliance on single-token samples violates this principle at every step along lengthy sequences --- causing an accumulation of bias amplified by clipping logic that “locks” high variance tokens out prematurely yet leaves noisy gradients free elsewhere.

Thus while GRPO successfully abolished dependence on an external value net in modest settings (short prompts + small models), scaling it up exposes an intrinsic flaw tied directly to mismatched granularity between reward attribution (global) and adjustment factor (local).

**Importance Sampling Theory in Off‑Policy Reinforcement Learning**

# Importance Sampling Identity and Proof Sketch  

The elementary task of off‑policy reinforcement learning is to estimate an expectation under a *target* distribution while having available only samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution.  The mathematical foundation for this task is the **importance sampling identity**  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}}\!
\left[
\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
\right].
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) follows directly from a change‑of‑measure argument.  Write the expectation under $\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}$ as an integral (or sum) over all possible outcomes $z$:  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\sum_{z} \pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)\,f(z).
\]  

Multiplying and dividing inside the sum by $\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)$ yields  

\[
\sum_{z}\Bigl[\underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}}_{w(z)}\,\underbrace{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}_{p(z)}\,f(z)\Bigr]
=
\sum_{z}p(z)\,w(z)\,f(z)
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[w(z)\,f(z)],
\]  

where $w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}$ is called the **importance weight**.  Hence the right‑hand side of (1) equals exactly the left‑hand side, establishing unbiasedness: if one could average $w(z)f(z)$ over all possible draws $z$ drawn according to $\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}$, one would recover precisely $\mathbb E_{\,\pi_\mathrm {tar}}\![f]$.  

In practice an analyst possesses only a finite collection of samples drawn from $\pi_\mathrm {beh}$; therefore $(1)$ inspires a Monte‑Carlo estimator

\[
\widehat{\mu}
=
\frac1N
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
w(z_n)\,
f(z_n),
\qquad z_n \stackrel{i.i.d}{\sim}\!\pi_\mathrm {beh},
\tag{2}
\]  

which converges almost surely to $\mathbb E_{\,\pi_\mathrm {tar}}\![f]$ as $N$ grows because of the law of large numbers (discussed next).

# Behavior Distribution versus Target Distribution Roles  

In Eq. (1), $\pi_\mathrm {beh}$ plays the role of *sampling distribution*.  It determines which data points are actually observed during rollouts or demonstrations—e.g., responses generated by an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text {old }}$.  The *target* distribution $\pi_\mathrm {tar}$ encodes what probability mass we would like our estimate to reflect—typically those arising under a new policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized.  

Because reinforcement learning iteratively improves policies, one never has direct access to fresh samples from $\pi_\theta$ while computing gradients for $\theta$.  By treating $\pi_\theta$ as $\pi_\text {tar}$ and using previously collected rollouts sampled under $\pi_{\theta_\text {old }}$, we retain an offline learning signal while staying unbiased provided we correctly re‑weight each observation by its importance weight $w(\cdot)= {\displaystyle \frac{\pi_\theta(\cdot)}{\;\;\;\,\pi_{\theta_\text {old }}(\cdot)}}$.  

Thus “behavior” refers concretely to **the policy at which data were generated**, whereas “target” refers **to the current policy whose improvement objective we wish to evaluate**.

# Importance Weight Definition  

The scalar factor correcting for distributional mismatch is called **the importance weight**,

\[
w(z)\;\triangleq
\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm {tar}}\!\bigl(z\bigr)}
      {\;\;\;\,\;\;\;\,\displaystyle 
       \pi_{\mathrm {beh}}\!\bigl(z\bigr)}.
      \tag{3}
\]  

When $w(z)>1$, outcome $z$ has become more probable under the target policy than under the behavior policy; conversely $w(z)<1$ signals that $z$ has been de‑emphasized relative to its former probability mass.  In policy gradient methods that employ clipping---for instance PPO---the raw weight $w(\cdot)$ often appears inside a piecewise function 

\[
\tau(w)=
    \min
    \Bigl(
        w\,A,
        \
        \operatorname {clip}(w,\,
               1-\varepsilon,\,
               1+\varepsilon )A
    \Bigr),
    \tag{4}
\]  

where $A$ denotes an advantage estimate and $\varepsilon>0$ constrains how far off‑policy samples may influence parameter updates.  This construction rests squarely upon Eq. (3).

# Law of Large Numbers Requirement $N \gg 1$

By virtue of either strong or weak forms of law–of–large–numbers arguments (or equivalently central-limit scaling considerations), **the estimator becomes unbiased asymptotically**, with standard error scaling roughly as 

$\sigma_w / {\sqrt N}$ where $\sigma_w^2 = {\rm Var}_{\, z~π_{beh}}\!\bigl[ w(z) f(z)\bigr].$
This reasoning clarifies why reinforcement‑learning practice routinely aggregates many trajectories into large mini‑batches before forming an update step—a practical expression of $N\gg 1.$

# Variance Blowup When Weights Are Computed From Single Samples  

Although Eq. (1) guarantees unbiasedness irrespective of $N$, practical estimation suffers dramatically if $N$ is too small relative to fluctuations in $w(\cdot) $.  
If only **one** observation per “distribution” were employed—the situation encountered when each token’s next‑token conditional probability is treated individually—the estimator reduces to

\[
w(y_t)
    = 
    {\displaystyle 
     \frac{
           πθ(y_t|x,y_{<t})
           }
           {
           πθold(y_t|x,y_{<t})
           } },
     %   star step ...
     % process...

which corresponds precisely to GRPO’s token‑level weighting term $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ introduced in Eq.~(2).  
Because each ratio involves *a single draw* from its own categorical distribution over vocabulary tokens,
the denominator may become extremely small while its numerator stays bounded away from zero,
so $w(y_t)$ can explode arbitrarily large or vanish arbitrarily close to zero.
Consequently its variance 

$\operatorname {Var}\!\bigl(w(y_t) A\bigr)$

may become unbounded even when rewards are bounded ($r(x,y)\in[0,1]$, thus advantage magnitudes remain modest).
When many tokens appear inside long responses—as typical for modern language models—these high‑variance ratios multiply along sequences,
amplifying gradient noise at every update step.
Moreover clipping
invariantly throttles extreme ratios yet preserves their *biasing effect*: even modest deviations near $(1±ε)$ are multiplied exponentially across positions leading easily toward model collapse.
Empirically this phenomenon manifested in GRPO experiments as catastrophic performance breakdowns after relatively few optimisation steps,
and attempts at hyperparameter tuning failed because variance was rooted fundamentally in applying Eq. (3) at too granular a unit (single tokens).  
Hence **a misapplication of importance weights at token level violates**
the essential large‐sample requirement articulated earlier,
and produces “variance blowup” – an instability confirmed by repeated empirical failures reported throughout Sec.~3 (*Motivation*).



# Illustrative Examples From Bandits To Sequential Decision Making  

To consolidate intuition about why averaging across many draws mitigates variance,
consider first a **single‐armed stochastic bandit** setting.
Suppose an algorithm chooses action $a$, receives scalar reward $R$, yet wishes retrospectively evaluate how another stochastic policy would have performed.
If every pull yields exactly one scalar outcome,
the unbiased estimator reduces merely to evaluating 

$
R\times%
\dfrac{{π}_{{new}}\!(a)}
{{π}_{{old}}\!(a)},
$

which depends on drawing *exactly one* reward per action—a notoriously noisy gauge because ${π}_{{old}}\!(a)$ can be arbitrarily tiny.
Increasing reliability demands pulling each arm repeatedly (i.e., larger $N$), thereby smoothing out fluctuations via Eq.~(2).

Now map this perspective onto **sequential decision making**, specifically autoregressive language generation.
A response $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ arises through successive conditionals:
$\displaystyle π_θ(y|x)=
     ∏^{T}_{t=1}
     π_θ(y_t|x,y_{<t}) .
$
If one treats every conditional independently—as did GRPO—each position contributes a factor identical in spirit
to drawing once from its own categorical “arm.”
Because distinct logits are sampled independently per position,
the product-of‑ratios form

$
∏^{T}_{t=1}
\dfrac{
   π_θ(y_t|x,y_{<t})
}{
   π_θ^{old}(y_t|x,y_{<t})
}
$

acquires multiplicative noise detrimental especially when $T$ is large.
In contrast, **Group Sequence Policy Optimization** adopts
the **sequence‑level** weight

$
s_i(\theta)=%
      \Bigl(
           {\displaystyle 
            {
            π_θ(y_i|x)
            }\over{

            π^{old}_θ(y_i|x)}}%
       {
      }\Bigr)^{
        {1}/{|y_i|}
        }
$
which mathematically equals taking *the geometric mean* across token ratios.
Equivalently,

$
s_i(\theta)=%
       e^{
       {
            {%
                (1/|y_i|)
                }\!
                {{
                  ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                 log {
                  π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})/
                  π^{old}_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) }}
                } }
       }
$

Effectively this computes **one average log‐ratio** per entire response.
By aggregating information across all tokens *before* applying clipping,
GSPO restores precisely what Eq.~(2)’s law of large numbers demands:
each response acts as one high‐dimensional sample whose importance weight has reduced variability compared with its component token weights.
Consequently gradient estimates inherit lower variance,
as illustrated empirically by dramatically fewer clipped tokens (Fig.~3), yet maintain superior learning efficiency—a direct validation of core importance‐sampling theory applied at compatible granularity.


---  
Through these sections we have connected three central learning messages:

* Importance sampling supplies an exact unbiased correction so long as sufficient independent draws support each correction factor.
* Misusing Eq.~(3) at too fine a granularity—specifically at individual token levels—breaches this statistical principle, inflating estimator variance and destabilising training.
* Aligning the unit of reward (normally assigned per complete response) with the unit for off‐policy correction (entire sequence likelihood) restores statistical soundness, which underpins Group Sequence Policy Optimization’s empirical success over its predecessor GRPO.

These insights scaffold principled design choices for future off‑policy reinforcement learning algorithms targeting ever larger language models or other sequential decision problems.
