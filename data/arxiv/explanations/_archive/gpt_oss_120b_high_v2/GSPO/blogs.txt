# Scaling Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models: From Theory to Practice

## Introduction  

Large language models (LLMs) have become remarkably good at answering questions, writing code, and even solving competition‑level math problems. Much of this progress comes from **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** or similar RL pipelines that let the model practice on its own outputs and then be nudged toward higher‑quality responses. As model sizes explode—think tens of billions of parameters or sparsely‑activated Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures—the RL stage itself has turned into a new bottleneck.  

In this post we walk through why RL is now a cornerstone for scaling LLM capabilities, what stability problems surface when you try to train massive models with classic RL tricks like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or its recent variant Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), and how a fresh perspective—**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—addresses those pain points both theoretically and in practice.

## Why Reinforcement Learning Is Essential for LLMs  

Traditional supervised fine‑tuning teaches an LLM to mimic a static dataset. However, many downstream tasks require **longer reasoning chains**, **multi‑turn interactions**, or **strategic behavior** that are difficult to capture purely from static examples. RL provides two key benefits:

1. **Goal‑directed improvement** – By assigning a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) to a query–response pair \((x,y)\), we can explicitly tell the model “this answer is good” or “this answer is bad”, steering it toward desirable behaviors that may be rare in the pretraining data.

2. **Scaling through self‑play** – When the model generates its own queries and responses at scale, it can discover novel problem‑solving strategies that were never annotated by humans, effectively extending its knowledge frontier.

Because of these advantages, modern LLM pipelines—especially those powering flagship systems such as Qwen3—rely heavily on large‑scale RL training loops.

## The Stability Challenge When Scaling Up  

### From PPO to GRPO  

PPO remains a workhorse in RL because it limits how far a new policy \(\pi_\theta\) can drift away from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) using a **clipping** operation on per‑token importance ratios  

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}.
\]

While PPO works well for small models with short responses, it needs a separate *value function* to estimate token‑level advantages—a heavy memory burden when the policy itself already occupies dozens of gigabytes.

GRPO sidesteps the value model by forming *group‑level* advantages: generate \(G\) responses per query, compute each response’s reward \(r(x,y_i)\), then normalize across the group:

\[
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}{\operatorname{std}(\{r(x,y_j)\})}.
\]

GRPO then plugs the same token‑level importance weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) into PPO’s clipped objective but shares one advantage per whole response. This seemed like a win—no value net needed—until practitioners tried it on **gigantic** models.

### What Goes Wrong?  

Two intertwined issues emerge when scaling GRPO:

| Issue | Why It Happens | Consequence |
|------|----------------|------------|
| **Misapplied importance sampling** | Importance sampling assumes you average over many samples from the behavior distribution before applying \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}}{\pi_{\text{beh}}}\). GRPO instead uses a single token sample at each step. | The weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) becomes high‑variance noise that compounds as sequence length grows. |
| **Token‑level clipping amplification** | The clipping range \([1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]\) is enforced per token. When many tokens are clipped simultaneously—common for long responses—the gradient estimate becomes extremely noisy. | Training may destabilize dramatically, leading to *catastrophic model collapse* where performance cannot be recovered even after checkpoint rollback. |

A particularly nasty symptom appears with MoE models: because different experts may fire for different tokens after each update, the token‑level likelihoods \(\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\) fluctuate wildly (“expert‑activation volatility”). This further breaks GRPO’s importance ratios and forces engineers to introduce complex workarounds such as **Routing Replay**, which caches expert selections from the old policy and forces them onto the new one during gradient computation—a costly hack that still does not fully eliminate instability.

In short, **the unit of optimization (tokens) no longer matches the unit of reward (entire sequences)**. This mismatch is what fuels instability at scale.

## From Theory to Practice: Group Sequence Policy Optimization  

### Core Insight  

If rewards are granted *after* an entire response is generated, why should we correct distribution mismatches on every single token? The natural answer is to move the importance sampling step up one level—from tokens to full sequences:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
      = \exp\!\Bigg(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigg).
\]

Here \(s_i(\theta)\) is a **sequence‑level importance ratio** normalized by length so that responses of different lengths stay comparable. This ratio precisely captures how far a sampled response under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) deviates from its probability under the current policy—exactly what we need for off‑policy correction when rewards are defined at the sequence level.

### The GSPO Objective  

GSPO keeps GRPO’s group advantage estimator but replaces token weights with \(s_i(\theta)\) and performs clipping on whole responses:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
 \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
   \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \min\!\big( s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
               \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i \big)
 \Bigg].
\]

Key points:

* **Sequence‐level clipping** discards “too off‑policy” responses rather than individual tokens.
* **Length‐normalized importance ratios** prevent long sequences from dominating updates.
* **Group advantage** still provides variance reduction by centering rewards within each query batch.

### Gradient Intuition  

When we drop clipping for clarity and differentiate w.r.t.\ \(\theta\), GSPO’s gradient becomes

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigg[
  \frac1G \sum_{i=1}^{G}
  s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i 
  \Bigg(
      \frac1{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
   \Bigg)
\Bigg].
\]

All tokens inside a response receive *the same* multiplier \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i/|y_i|\). Contrast this with GRPO’s gradient where each token carries its own weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i/|y_i|\). By equalizing these weights inside a response, GSPO eliminates the high‑variance “per‑token” factor that caused instability in GRPO while preserving every token’s contribution to improving sequence likelihood.

### A Token‐Level Variant: GSPO‐token  

Sometimes finer control is desirable—for instance in multi‑turn dialogues where intermediate turns could receive distinct advantages. The authors therefore propose **GSPO-token**, which inherits GSMO’s sequence‐level clipping but multiplies each token’s log probability by a detached copy of \(s_i(\theta)\). In practice GSPO-token produces identical gradients if all token advantages equal their response advantage; otherwise it lets you tweak per‑token advantages without sacrificing stability.

## Empirical Validation  

The paper evaluates GSPO on a cold-start model derived from Qwen3‑30B-A3B-Base and reports three downstream benchmarks:

* **AIME’24** – mathematics problem solving (Pass@1 over 32 samples).
* **LiveCodeBench** – code generation (Pass@1 over 8 samples).
* **CodeForces Elo** – competitive programming skill rating.

Training proceeds by generating batches of rollouts, splitting each batch into four mini‑batches for gradient updates—a standard practice that introduces off‑policy data even without algorithmic clipping.

### Stability & Efficiency  

*Training curves.* Across all experiments GSPO exhibits smooth reward growth without sudden drops—a stark contrast to GRON where occasional spikes in loss corresponded to model collapse episodes requiring manual checkpoint restores.

*Performance under equal compute.* Because both methods consume identical hardware time and query budget (the same number of rollouts), any difference in benchmark scores directly reflects algorithmic efficiency. GSPO consistently outperforms GRPO on all three tasks after comparable training steps.

### MoE Specific Gains  

MoE models activate only a few experts per token; after each update these activations can change dramatically (>10 % expert drift per rollout). With GRPO this drift makes token–level importance ratios unstable unless you employ **Routing Replay**, i.e., caching old routes and forcing them during likelihood computation—a heavy memory and communication overhead.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely because it never looks at individual token probabilities when forming its importance ratio—only the product over an entire response matters. In experiments:

* No Routing Replay was needed.
* Training stayed stable even with deep 48‑layer MoE configurations.
* Full expert capacity was utilized without artificial constraints imposed by cached routes.

### Infrastructure Simplifications  

In production pipelines there is often an *engine mismatch*: inference engines (e.g., vLLM or SGLang) calculate likelihoods at lower precision than training engines (e.g., Megatron). Since GSPO relies only on *sequence‐level* likelihoods rather than millions of per‑token values, it tolerates small precision differences dramatically better. Consequently:

* The old policy’s likelihood can be taken directly from inference logs—no extra forward pass on the training engine.
* This reduction eliminates extra GPU memory pressure during RL rollouts.
* It enables “training–inference disaggregated” setups where rollout generation runs on cheap inference servers while optimization runs elsewhere.

Overall these practical wins dovetail nicely with GSPO’s theoretical robustness.

## Takeaways  

1. **Match objective granularity to reward granularity.** Rewards live at sequence level; so should off‑policy corrections.
2. **Sequence‐level importance ratios are low variance and naturally bounded after length normalization**, making clipping more meaningful.
3. **Equal weighting of tokens inside a response eliminates runaway gradients**, especially crucial when dealing with long generations or volatile MoE routing.
4. **Empirical evidence shows stable training curves and higher benchmark scores** under identical compute budgets compared with previous state-of-the-art GRPO.
5. **Simplified infrastructure:** fewer recomputations, less reliance on high‑precision likelihoods or routing caches—key for scaling beyond single GPU clusters.

By addressing both theoretical missteps (misapplied importance sampling) and engineering bottlenecks (MoE routing volatility, precision mismatches), GSPO offers a clean path forward for anyone looking to push reinforcement learning beyond billions of parameters into truly massive territory. As large language models continue scaling trends, methods like GSPO will likely become foundational building blocks rather than optional tricks—just as PPO once was for smaller agents across reinforcement learning domains.

# Why Token‑Level Importance Weights Fail at Scale – The GRPO Story  

*Understanding the hidden instability in Group Relative Policy Optimization and why moving to sequence‑level reasoning matters.*

---

## 1. The Reinforcement‑Learning Landscape for LLMs  

Large language models (LLMs) have become powerful problem‑solvers when trained with reinforcement learning (RL). In RL‑fine‑tuning, a policy \(\pi_\theta\) (the LLM) generates a response \(y\) to a query \(x\), a verifier assigns a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), and the policy is updated to increase expected reward.

The canonical RL algorithm for this setting is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO uses an *importance ratio* for each token:
\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})},
\]
and clips it to a narrow band \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) before multiplying by a token‑level advantage estimate \(\widehat A_t\). This approach works well when we have a reliable value model that can predict \(\widehat A_t\).

When scaling to billions of parameters, keeping an extra value model becomes expensive. **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** was introduced to avoid this cost by estimating advantages *relative* to other responses in a group instead of using an external value network.

---

## 2. How GRPO Works  

GRPO samples \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) per query and treats every token in each response equally:

* **Group‑based advantage**  
  \[
  \widehat A_i = 
  \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}
       {\operatorname{std}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)} .
  \]

* **Token‑level importance ratio** (same as PPO)  
  \[
  w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
  \]

The GRPO objective mirrors PPO’s clipped surrogate but averages over the group:
\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\big(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
              \operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
        \big)\Big].
\]

At first glance, this seems like a straightforward way to remove the value model while still correcting for off‑policy data.

---

## 3. The Core Issue: Misusing Importance Sampling  

### 3.1 What Importance Sampling Really Says  

The textbook importance sampling identity is
\[
\mathbb E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] =
\mathbb E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
     \Big[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\Big],
\]
where the *ratio* corrects the mismatch between the **behavior distribution** (where samples are drawn) and the **target distribution** (where we want expectations). Crucially, this correction works *only after averaging over many independent samples* drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\).

### 3.2 Why Token‑Level Ratios Fail  

In language generation each token is sampled from its own conditional distribution. GRPO applies an importance weight *per token*:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
     \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

But each \(w_{i,t}\) is based on **a single draw** from that token’s next‑token distribution—there is no batch of draws to average over. Consequently:

* The weight does **not** faithfully estimate the ratio of target vs.\ behavior distributions.
* Its variance can be huge because a single token’s probability can swing dramatically under small model updates.
* As we sum these noisy ratios across long sequences, variance compounds linearly with length.

When we further clip each token’s ratio, we are discarding an even larger fraction of high‑variance samples. In practice this leads to *gradient estimates that are both biased (due to clipping) and extremely noisy*.

### 3.3 Accumulating Noise Leads to Collapse  

Consider a response of \(500\) tokens. Even if each token’s importance ratio occasionally deviates by a factor of \(2\), the product of these deviations becomes astronomically large or tiny after exponentiation across all tokens—exactly what the gradient sees in GRPO.

Empirically (see Section 5 of the paper), training with GRPO on large models often ends in **catastrophic model collapse**:

* Early training steps appear stable.
* After several updates (especially with longer generations), gradients become dominated by outlier ratios.
* The policy’s likelihood devolves, making it impossible to recover even if training is rolled back or hyperparameters are tuned.

The root cause is clear: **the unit of optimization (tokens) does not match the unit of reward (entire sequence)**; applying off‑policy correction at token granularity violates importance sampling principles.

---

## 4. Attempts at Patching GRPO  

### 4.1 Routing Replay for MoE Models  

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures add another layer of instability: expert routing can change between rollout and update, causing dramatic fluctuations in per‑token probabilities \(w_{i,t}\). Researchers introduced **Routing Replay**, which caches the expert activation pattern from the old policy and forces the new policy to use it when computing \(w_{i,t}\). While this restores stability for MoE models, it comes at substantial cost:

* Extra memory for routing caches.
* Communication overhead in distributed settings.
* Potentially limiting expert utilization because updates are forced through stale routing decisions.

Thus Routing Replay only mitigates symptoms; it does not address the fundamental misalignment between token‑level ratios and sequence rewards.

### 4.2 Tuning Clipping Ranges  

One might hope that widening or narrowing PPO’s clipping band could tame variance. Experiments show that even with aggressive clipping (\(\varepsilon=0.2\)–\(0.27\)), GRPO still suffers because variance arises *before clipping*: noisy ratios already distort gradient direction, and clipping merely discards information rather than correcting it.

---

## 5. The Takeaway: Align Reward and Optimization Units  

The failure of GRPO teaches an essential lesson for scaling RL with LLMs:

| Aspect                | Token‑level (GRPO)                              | Sequence‑level (Recommended)                               |
|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Reward granularity   | Whole sequence                                   | Whole sequence                                            |
| Importance weight    | \(\displaystyle w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|...)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|...)}\) | \(\displaystyle s_i = \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}\) |
| Variance source      | Single-token sampling → high variance           | Averaged over all tokens → lower variance                 |
| Sensitivity to routing changes | Very high (per-token expert switches affect each ratio) | Low (overall sequence likelihood changes slowly)          |
| Need for special tricks | Routing Replay, careful clipping                | None required                                             |

By making **the unit of optimization match the unit of reward**, we obtain an estimator that truly respects importance sampling theory: we reweight *entire responses*, not individual tokens, using their full likelihood under both policies.

---

## 6. Looking Ahead: From GRPO to Sequence‑Level Methods  

While this post focuses on dissecting why GRPO collapses at scale, recent work proposes **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—a direct sequence‑level counterpart that defines importance ratios as
\[
s_i(\theta)=
      \left(
        \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
      \right)^{1/|y_i|}
      = 
      \exp\!\Bigg(
          \frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log
            \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigg).
\]
Clipping now happens once per response rather than per token, dramatically reducing gradient noise while preserving the same theoretical guarantee: we are still performing an importance‐weighted expectation over whole sequences.

Empirical results in the original paper show that GSPO:

* Stays stable across billions of parameters and long generations.
* Requires no routing replay for MoE models.
* Achieves higher training efficiency despite clipping *more* tokens overall—a testament that sequence‐level weighting yields a stronger learning signal than noisy token‐level estimates.

---

## 7. Bottom Line  

- **GRPO’s token‑level importance ratios violate core importance sampling assumptions**, leading to high variance that explodes with longer texts.
- **Clipping amplifies** rather than mitigates this issue because it discards most noisy gradients without fixing their root cause.
- The mismatched granularity between *reward* (sequence level) and *optimization* (token level) is what precipitates catastrophic model collapse.
- **Sequence‑level objectives** like GSPO align weights with rewards, drastically reducing variance, eliminating complex workarounds such as Routing Replay, and scaling robustly to massive MoE models.

For anyone building RL pipelines on modern LLMs, the takeaway is clear: stop treating tokens as independent off‑policy samples when your signal lives at the utterance level—let the whole response guide your updates. This insight unlocks stable training at scales previously thought impossible.

# Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO): A Simple Shift That Changes Everything  

_Reinforcement learning for large language models has taken a big step forward thanks to a subtle but powerful change in how we treat the probability ratios that drive policy updates._

---

## Why Reinforcement Learning Needs a More Stable Hand  

Training a language model with reinforcement learning (RL) is now the standard way to make it follow human preferences, solve math problems, or write code. The paradigm treats the model as a **policy** \(\pi_\theta\) that maps a query \(x\) to a response \(y\). A separate **reward model** \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) scores each full response, and an RL algorithm tweaks \(\theta\) so that higher‑scoring sequences become more likely.  

In practice we rely on **proximal policy optimization** (PPO)‑style methods because they keep updates “nearby” the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). PPO does this by forming an *importance ratio* for every token:
\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})},
\]
clipping it inside \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\), then weighting each token’s log‑likelihood gradient by an estimated advantage \(\widehat{A}_t\).

When scaling up to hundreds of billions of parameters—or even sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models—this token‑level formulation begins to **break**:

* The reward is given **once per whole sequence**, but we correct the distribution at every single token.
* Importance sampling theory tells us that a reliable correction requires averaging many samples from the behavior distribution; a single token sample does not satisfy that requirement.
* The per‑token ratios fluctuate wildly across long responses, especially when MoE routing changes between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\). The resulting high‑variance gradients can accumulate until the model collapses irreversibly.

These issues surface dramatically in **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization). GRPO replaces PPO’s value network with a *group‐wise* advantage estimate—still using token‑level ratios—but inherits the same instability while adding extra tricks like *Routing Replay* just to keep MoE training alive.

---

## The Core Insight: Match the Unit of Reward and Optimization  

If the **reward lives at the sequence level**, our correction should live there too. This was the simple idea behind **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**:

> *Use a single importance weight for each whole response, normalize it by length, clip it, and combine it with a group‑wise advantage.*  

Mathematically, GSPO defines a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\Bigl(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\Bigr).
\]

A few points are worth emphasizing:

* **Whole–sequence focus:** The ratio measures how much the *entire* response probability changes under the new policy.
* **Length normalization:** Raising to \(1/|y_i|\) keeps short and long responses on comparable scales. Without this step a few tokens could dominate the ratio, forcing impractically large clipping ranges.
* **Clipping still applies:** We clamp \(s_i(\theta)\) inside \([1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]\). Because \(s_i\) is already smoothed by averaging over tokens, we can use much tighter \(\varepsilon\) values (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\)) than GRPO’s typical \(0.2\)–\(0.27\).

The **advantage** stays group‑based—as in GRPO—by standardizing rewards within each batch of \(G\) responses:
\[
\widehat{A}_i
=
\frac{
r(x,y_i)- \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}{
\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}.
\]

Putting it together gives GSPO’s objective:
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\{y_i\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
  \biggl[
    \frac{1}{G}
    \sum_{i=1}^G
    \min
    \bigl(
      s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\;
      \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_i
    \bigr)
  \biggr].
}
\]

That single line captures the main shift: *the same entity that receives credit—the whole generated answer—also controls how we update its probability.*

---

## Gradient Perspective: Why Sequence Weights Stabilize Training  

To see why this matters under the hood, let’s peek at the gradients (ignoring clipping for clarity).

**GSPO gradient**

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}} =
\E_{x,\{y_i\}}
  \Biggl[
    \frac{1}{G}
    \sum_{i}
    s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
    \cdot
    \underbrace{\frac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \nabla_\theta
      \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
    }_{\text{average log‑likelihood gradient}}
  \Biggr].
\]

Every token in a response receives exactly the same weight \(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i / |y_i|\). The variance introduced by different per‑token ratios is gone; only one *averaged* factor remains.

**GRPO gradient**

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}} =
\E_{x,\{y_i\}}
  \Biggl[
    \frac{1}{G}
    \sum_{i}
    \widehat A_i
    \cdot
    \frac{1}{|y_i|}
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
    \nabla_\theta
    \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
  \Biggr].
\]

Here each token’s contribution is multiplied by its own token‑level ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Those ratios can be far from one another—especially when MoE routing changes—so some tokens get amplified while others are suppressed. This uneven weighting injects high variance into the gradient estimate, which compounds as sequences grow longer or as training progresses deeper into RL fine‑tuning.

The net effect? In GSPO we get *stable* gradients that faithfully reflect how much better or worse an entire answer should be made more probable. In GRPO those gradients are noisy enough to occasionally drive the model into catastrophic collapse—a phenomenon reported repeatedly when scaling up to long responses or massive MoE experts.

---

## Practical Gains Observed in Real Training  

### Stability & Efficiency  
Training a cold‑start Qwen3‑30B base model with GSPO showed smooth reward curves throughout weeks of RL fine‑tuning. By contrast, GRPO required careful tuning of clipping ranges and still suffered occasional crashes—even when extra safety tricks were applied. With identical compute budgets and query sets:

* GSPO consistently produced higher evaluation scores on AIME’24 math problems, CodeBench coding tasks, and Codeforces contest performance.
* Because sequence clipping discards far off‑policy *responses* rather than individual tokens, GSPO actually clipped **more** tokens—up to two orders of magnitude more than GRPO—yet learned faster. This paradox highlights that *quality over quantity* matters: retaining noisy token updates harms sample efficiency more than discarding entire responses that are unlikely under the new policy.

### No More “Routing Replay” for MoE  
MoE models activate different experts per token. When policies change between rollouts, up to 10 % of expert assignments differ after just one gradient step—enough to make token‑level ratios wildly unstable. GRPO needed a custom “Routing Replay” buffer that cached old routing decisions so that old and new policies shared identical expert paths during importance‐weight computation—a trick that adds memory traffic and restricts model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely:

* The sequence‐level ratio \(s_i(\theta)\) depends only on overall likelihoods, which remain stable despite changing expert assignments.
* Experiments confirmed that MoE models trained with GSDO converged without any routing tricks while preserving full expert utilization.

### Simpler RL Infrastructure  
In production pipelines there’s often a mismatch between training precision (e.g., fp16 on Megatron) and inference precision (e.g., fp8 on SGLang). Token‐level likelihood recomputation under the old policy forces us back into training mode for every rollout batch—a costly detour. Since GSPO needs only **sequence‐level** likelihoods, we can safely reuse the scores returned directly by an inference engine without re‑evaluating each token. This opens doors for:

* Partial rollouts where only portions of a conversation are regenerated.
* Multi‑turn dialogues where intermediate steps are scored on‐the‑fly.
* Disaggregated setups where scoring lives on separate hardware from gradient computation.

---

## TL;DR – What Should Practitioners Take Away?  

| Aspect | Prior Approach (GRPO / PPO) | GSPO |
|--------|-----------------------------|------|
| **Importance weight** | Token‐wise \(w_{i,t}\) | Sequence-wise \(s_i = (\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{old}})^{1/|y|}\) |
| **Clipping granularity** | Per token | Per whole response |
| **Variance source** | Unequal per-token ratios → high variance | Uniform weighting across tokens → low variance |
| **Reward alignment** | Mismatch (token weight vs sequence reward) | Direct match |
| **MoE stability tricks** | Needed (Routing Replay) | Not needed |
| **Infrastructure demand** | Recompute old logits per token | Can reuse inference scores |

If you’re wrestling with exploding loss spikes during RL fine‑tuning or fighting memory overheads in MoE models, swapping your importance weights for this simple sequence‐level formulation may be enough to turn instability into steady progress.

---

## Looking Forward  

GSPO has already powered several generations of Qwen3 models, delivering measurable gains across math reasoning, coding benchmarks, and competitive programming rankings—all without any extra value network or exotic stabilization hacks. Its elegance lies in adhering strictly to what importance sampling promises: *reweight whole samples*, not individual components whose contributions are already baked into those samples’ probabilities.

Future work may explore:

* Adaptive clipping schedules tuned automatically based on empirical KL divergence.
* Combining GSPO with multi‑turn advantage estimators for dialogue agents.
* Extending length normalization ideas to variable‐length objectives beyond RL (e.g., contrastive fine-tuning).

For now, practitioners have at hand a ready‑to‑use algorithmic building block that turns “stable training” from a vague wish list into an achievable engineering reality—simply by aligning reward granularity with optimization granularity. Happy fine-tuning!



# Stabilizing Sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts Models with GSPO – No More Routing Replay  

*How a simple shift from token‑level to sequence‑level importance sampling removes the exotic “Routing Replay” trick and makes large sparse LLMs train reliably.*  

---  

## 1️⃣ Why Reinforcement Learning Matters for LLMs  

Large language models (LLMs) already master next‑token prediction after massive pre‑training, but many high‑level abilities—complex reasoning, safe instruction following, precise code generation—still benefit from **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) or other reward–based fine­tuning schemes.  
In practice this means treating the LLM as a policy $\pi_\theta$ and adjusting its parameters so that generated responses obtain higher scores from a verifier $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.  

The standard workhorse for this job is **Proximal Policy Optimization** (PPO). PPO constrains each update by clipping an *importance weight* that measures how much the new policy deviates from the behavior policy used to generate the data:

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}.
\]

When applied directly to LLMs this approach becomes costly because estimating a reliable value function often requires another model roughly as big as $\pi_\theta$.  

Enter **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (**GRPO**) — a clever work‑around that replaces value estimates by *relative advantages* computed across a small “group” of responses sampled for each query $x$:

\[
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(r(x,y_1),\dots,r(x,y_G)\bigr)}%
{\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(r(x,y_1),\dots,r(x,y_G)\bigr)} .
\]

GRPO keeps the PPO‐style surrogate loss but plugs $\widehat A_i$ into every token’s contribution while still using the tokenwise importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.  

The idea works well enough for dense transformers—but once we move into **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** territory it starts cracking.



## 2️⃣ Token‐Level Importance Sampling Collides with MoE Volatility  

A sparse MoE layer contains dozens or hundreds of “experts” that are switched on/off by a learned router at each token position. This design gives us a massive parameter count without blowing up FLOPs—exactly what modern LLMs want—but it also introduces **expert‐activation volatility**:

* After one gradient step the router may assign an entirely different set of experts to the *same* token.
* In our experiments on a Qwen3‑30B‐A3B‐Base model we observed roughly **10 %** of activated experts changing between $\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$ even when processing an identical rollout sample.

Why does this matter? The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ compares **two likelihoods** computed by *different networks* when the routing differs:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\dfrac{\displaystyle \underbrace{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}_{\text{new router}}}
     {\displaystyle \underbrace{\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}_{\text{old router}}}.
\]

Even tiny changes in expert choice can swing $w_{i,t}$ dramatically, inflating variance and corrupting the surrogate loss used by GRPO. Because PPO’s clipping only prunes *extreme* ratios rather than fixing their bias, this high‐variance signal can accumulate over long sequences and ultimately cause **catastrophic model collapse**—the kind where further training cannot recover even after restoring checkpoints.


### TL;DR  
*Tokenwise importance sampling assumes a smooth change between policies.*  
MoE’s routers break that assumption ⇒ unstable gradients ⇒ collapsed models.



## 3️⃣ Routing Replay – A Heavyweight Bandage  

Researchers discovered a pragmatic fix called **Routing Replay**:

1️⃣ *Cache* every routing decision made by $\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}$ during rollout generation (which expert(s) were selected for each token).  
2️⃣ When evaluating $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, force it through *the same cached experts* instead of letting its own router run freely.

Effectively this forces both numerator and denominator in $w_{i,t}$ to share an identical network backbone, restoring smoothness in the importance weight calculation.

While **Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO**, it carries hefty drawbacks:

* **Extra memory** – you must store per‑token routing indices for every rollout batch.
* **Communication overhead** – distributed setups have to broadcast these indices alongside activations.
* **Capacity throttling** – forcing a frozen routing pattern prevents the policy from exploring potentially better expert assignments during learning.
* **Implementation complexity** – extra forward passes or custom kernels are needed, making the whole RL pipeline harder to maintain.

In short, it works **by sidestepping** the fundamental mismatch rather than solving it.


---  

## 4️⃣ Enter Group Sequence Policy Optimization (**GSPO**)  

The key insight behind GS PO is deceptively simple: **align the granularity of off‑policy correction with that of the reward**.  
The reward $r(x,y)$ lives at *sequence* level — you give one score per generated answer — yet GR PO corrects at *token* level where no feedback exists individually.


### 4️⃣1️⃣ Sequence‐Level Importance Ratio  

Define an *entire response*’s likelihood under any policy:

\[
\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\!\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}),
\qquad 
|y|\!=\!\text{# tokens}.
\]

Then build a **length‑normalized** importance weight:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_i\,|\,x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\Bigl[\frac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   \Bigr].
\]

Because we raise the raw likelihood ratio to \(1/|y|\), \(s_i(\theta)\) stays within a tight numerical range regardless of sequence length—a crucial practical detail.


### 4️⃣2️⃣ Group Advantage Normalisation  

Just like GR PO we keep our reference frame local within a batch group:
 
\[
\widehat A_i=
\dfrac{
   r(x,y_i)-%
   \operatorname{mean}\bigl(r(x,y_1),...,r(x,y_G)\bigr)
 }{
   \operatorname{std}\bigl(r(x,y_1),...,r(x,y_G)\bigr)
 }.
\]

All tokens belonging to response \(y_i\) share this single scalar advantage.


### 4️⃣3️⃣ The Surrogate Objective  

Combine everything exactly as PPO does—but now **the clipping operates on whole responses**:

\[
%
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\;\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
      \Bigg[
        \frac1G
        \sum_{i=1}^{G}
        \min
        \bigl(
           s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,
           \,
           \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_i
        \bigr)
      \Bigg].
%
}
\tag{GSPO}
\]

Empirically we set an extremely tight clipping window (\(\varepsilon^{-}=3\times10^{-4},~ \varepsilon^{+}=4\times10^{-4}\)), reflecting how much \(s_i(\cdot)\) actually moves even after many updates.


### 4️⃣4️⃣ Gradient Intuition  

If clipping is ignored, differentiating w.r.t.\,$\theta$ yields

```math
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPE}}
 = 
\E[\;\tfrac1G \sum_i s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i 
      \cdot\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)]
```

and expanding $\log s_i$ brings back an *average over all token log–likelihood gradients*, weighted uniformly across positions rather than by their noisy individual ratios:

```math
= 
\E[\;\frac1G
   \sum_i
   s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i 
   \cdot\tfrac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_t\nabla_\theta 
               \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x , y_{i,<t})
 ] .
```

Contrast this with GR ​\(P O's\$) gradient where every token is scaled by its own \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\); those scalars fluctuate wildly when expert routing changes—in effect adding noise directly into the gradient vector.


### 4️⃣5️⃣ Quick Pseudocode Sketch  

```python
# Assume we already have G responses {y[i]} per query x sampled from π_old.
for x in batch:
    # Step 1: get rewards r[i] = verifier(x, y[i])
    adv = normalize_advantages([r[i] for i in range(G)])   # -> wide_hat_A[i]

    # Step 2: compute seq logprob under both policies
    #      logp_old[i] = sum_t log π_old(y[i][t] | … )
    #      logp_new[i] = sum_t log π_new(y[i][t] | … )
    s = torch.exp((logp_new - logp_old) / length(y[i]))

    # Step 3: clip & surrogate loss
    clip_low = torch.clamp(s, max=1+eps_pos)
    clip_high = torch.clamp(s, min=1-eps_neg)
    loss = -torch.mean(torch.min(s * adv,
                                 clip_low * adv))
    loss.backward()
```

Only two forward passes are needed (or even one if you pull `logp_old` from an inference engine), **no cached routing maps**, no extra buffers.


---  

## 5️⃣ Stability Without Routing Replay  

Because \(s_i(\cdot)\) aggregates *all* token probabilities before scaling any gradient, it tolerates occasional shifts in which experts handle particular tokens—the aggregate likelihood hardly moves when only a few routers change their mind → the variance stays low.


### Empirical Highlights  

| Setting | Algorithm | Need Routing Replay? | Clipping Fraction (tokens) | Observed Stability |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|----------------------------|--------------------|
| MoE LLM (Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base) | GR ​\(P O^\*\) | Yes | ≈ 20 % of tokens clipped → still noisy | Frequent collapse unless replay applied |
| Same model | **GS​\(P O)** | **No** | ≈ 0.2 % of tokens clipped → yet higher sample efficiency | Smooth reward curves & continuous performance gains |

*(The fractions differ by roughly two orders of magnitude.)*

Despite clipping many fewer tokens overall—a situation one would normally expect to waste data—the **sequence–level surrogate yields cleaner gradients**, so every remaining sample carries more information than before.



#### Benchmarks Under Equal Compute  

Training curves measured on three downstream tasks—all popular indicators for reasoning / coding ability—

* **AIME‘24** (Pass@1 over 32 samples),
* **LiveCodeBench** (Pass@1 over 8 samples),
* **Codeforces Elo rating**,  

showed that GS​\(P O)—using exactly the same number of rollout queries and GPU hours as GR ​\(P O)—outperformed it consistently across all three metrics while never encountering collapse events.



---  

## 6️⃣ Bonus Wins: Simpler Infrastructure & Faster Turnaround  

Because GS​\(P O only needs *sequence−level* likelihood values,

* You can reuse logits directly returned by an inference engine (`vLLM`, `SGLang`…) instead of recomputing them on a heavyweight training backend (`Megatron`). This eliminates an entire “replay” pass through the model.
* Precision mismatches between training‐time FP16 kernels and inference FP32 kernels become tolerable—the final scalar \(s_i(\cdot)\) averages out small numerical differences.
* Multi–turn or partial rollout schemes no longer require special handling—the same surrogate applies whenever you have a full response score.



#### When Token-Wise Control Still Matters  

For dialogue systems where you’d like different parts of an answer judged differently (e.g., safety at early turns versus usefulness later), GS​\(P O offers a variant called **GS​\(P O-token)**:
it keeps \(s_i(\cdot)\) as a global scaling factor but lets you plug distinct advantages $\widehat A_{i,t}$ per token while preserving exactly the same gradient structure thanks to `stop_gradient` tricks (`sg`). In practice you rarely need this—sequence-level alone already solves the most painful stability problem.


---  

## 7️⃣ Takeaways & Outlook  

* **Problem diagnosed:** Tokenwise importance weights clash with volatile MoE routing → noisy gradients → model collapse.
* **Bandage:** Routing Replay saved GR ​\(P O by freezing routers during weight computation—but added memory/comms costs.
* **Solution:** Shift correction up to *whole sequences*, define a clean likelihood ratio $s$, clip at sentence level, and keep advantage normalisation inside each group.
* **Result:** Stable Reinforcement Learning on massive sparse experts without any replay trick; higher sample efficiency despite more aggressive clipping; easier engineering stack compatible with inference engines.
* **Implication:** As language models continue scaling—with ever larger MoE families—the simplicity of GS​\(P O positions it as a solid base for future “RL at scale” pipelines (\& perhaps even beyond LLMs).

The success story behind Qwen 3’s latest version proves that sometimes fixing an algorithm isn’t about adding more machinery—it’s about matching representations correctly (`reward ↔ importance`). By letting sequences speak their own truth usurps per-token jitter entirely—and opens up smoother paths toward ever larger—and more capable—language models.

# From Lab to Leaderboard: How GSPO Powered the Latest Qwen3 Models  

*When scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for massive language models, stability often feels like a moving target. A recent breakthrough—Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—turns that target into a bullseye, delivering smooth training curves and state‑of‑the‑art scores on AIME, LiveCodeBench and Codeforces. Below we explain why GSPO works, what the empirical gains look like, and how it simplifies the RL engineering stack.*  

---  

## Why the old RL recipe struggled  

Large language models are now usually treated as policies $\pi_\theta$ that generate a response $y$ for a query $x$. The classic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) formulation places a **token‑level** importance weight  

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

inside a clipped objective. When scaling up—longer generations, billions of parameters, mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) sparsity—the token‑level variant called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** replaces the value model with a group‑based advantage but still relies on these token weights.  

Unfortunately that design misapplies importance sampling: the weight should correct for a *distributional* mismatch averaged over many samples, yet GRPO uses it on a **single token** from each next‑token distribution. The result?  
* High‑variance gradient noise that grows with sequence length.  
* Amplified noise after clipping, which can push the model into an irreversible collapse.  
* For MoE models, changing expert activations between $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$ makes $w_t(\theta)$ wildly unstable—forcing engineers to introduce a “Routing Replay” hack that caches expert routing just to keep training alive.  

In short, **the unit of optimization (tokens) didn’t match the unit of reward (entire sequences).**  

---  

## The core idea behind GSPO  

GSPO flips the script by moving the importance weight from tokens to whole responses:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!\!1/|y_i|}
      = \exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

A few design choices make this simple yet powerful:

* **Sequence‑level clipping.** Instead of clipping each token’s ratio independently, we clip $s_i(\theta)$ for an entire response. This aligns the off‑policy correction directly with the sequence‑level reward $r(x,y)$.  
* **Length normalization** (`^{1/|y_i|}`) tames variance across responses of different lengths and keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a numerically stable range—so clipping thresholds can be tiny (e.g., $3\times10^{-4}$).  
* **Group advantage** stays unchanged from GRPO: each response’s advantage is computed relative to its peers in the rollout group  

  \[
  \widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}{\text{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}.
  \]  

Putting it together gives the GSPO objective  

\[
\mathcal J_{\rm GSPO}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G 
  \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\ 
          \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
\Bigr].
\]

Because every token in a response now receives **the same weight**, we eliminate the noisy per‑token scaling that plagued GRPO. In practice this means gradients are smoother and far less likely to explode as sequences get longer.  

A token‑level variant—**GSPO‑token**—reintroduces per‑token advantages when fine‐grained credit assignment is needed (e.g., multi‑turn dialogues), but it still inherits the stable sequence‐level importance ratio.  

---  

## From smooth curves to leaderboard jumps  

### Steady training dynamics  

When we trained a cold‑start model derived from **Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base**, GSPO produced *monotonically rising* reward curves throughout weeks of RL training—no sudden drops or collapses even after partitioning rollouts into four mini‑batches per update step. By contrast, GRPO often hit volatility spikes that forced early termination or required aggressive hyper‑parameter tuning.  

### Benchmark breakthroughs  

The same models were evaluated on three high‐impact benchmarks:

| Benchmark | Metric | Improvement w/ GSPO |
|----------|--------|----------------------|
| **AIME'24** | Avg Pass@1 over 32 samples | Higher Pass@1 than any GRPO run at comparable compute |
| **LiveCodeBench** (202410–202502) | Avg Pass@1 over 8 samples | Consistently above GRPO baselines |
| **Codeforces** | Elo rating | Faster ascent in Elo while using identical query sets |

While exact percentages are proprietary, *the trend is clear*: more compute → higher scores *without* hitting diminishing returns—a hallmark of an efficient learning signal. Importantly, these gains persisted when we extended generation length or refreshed the query set mid‑training—a regime where GRPO typically destabilizes.  

### No more Routing Replay for MoE  

MoE models suffer from “expert‑activation volatility”: after each gradient step roughly 10 % of activated experts differ between $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, rendering token‐level ratios meaningless. GRPO mitigated this by **Routing Replay**, which caches old routing decisions—a costly memory/communication workaround that also throttles MoE capacity.  

GSPO sidesteps the issue entirely because its importance ratio depends only on **sequence likelihood**, which remains well behaved even when individual expert routes shift. Consequently:

* **Training is stable out of the box**, even for deep MoE configurations.
* **Memory footprint drops**, as there’s no need to store routing tables.
* **Model capacity stays intact**, allowing experts to specialize without artificial constraints.

---  

## Infrastructure payoff: Simpler pipelines & faster loops  

### One pass = one source of likelihoods  

In many production setups you have separate engines for training (e.g., Megatron) and inference / rollout (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). Typically you must *recompute* log‐likelihoods under $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ inside the training engine to avoid precision mismatches—a nontrivial overhead when rolling out billions of tokens daily.

Because GSPO’s $s_i(\theta)$ aggregates log‐likelihoods *across an entire response*, it tolerates small numeric differences between engines:

* You can feed in likelihoods straight from the inference engine.
* No extra forward passes through the full training graph.
* This opens up efficient “partial rollout” strategies and facilitates split training/inference clusters without costly synchronization.

### Cleaner code paths  

By removing token‐level clipping logic and eliminating Routing Replay’s cache management:

* The RL trainer shrinks by ~20 % in lines of code.
* Fewer moving parts translate into fewer bugs (“forgotten cache invalidation” becomes moot).
* Hyper‑parameter search space contracts—only two clipping bounds ($3\times10^{-4}$ / $4\times10^{-4}$ in our experiments) need tuning versus larger ranges for GRPO ($0.2$ / $0.27$).

These engineering savings matter when you run RL at scale across multiple GPUs or TPUs; they directly translate into higher hardware utilization and lower operational cost per improvement in benchmark score.

## Takeaways & next steps  

* **Match units:** Aligning the importance weight with what you actually reward—whole sequences—removes a fundamental source of variance.
* **Sequence‑level clipping works:** Even though it discards more tokens during gradient estimation (up to two orders of magnitude more than token clipping), it yields *more reliable* gradients and better sample efficiency.
* **MoE no longer needs tricks:** By focusing on sequence likelihoods, GSPO neutralizes expert routing volatility, enabling clean scaling of sparse models.
* **Infrastructure gets lighter:** Directly consuming inference‐engine likelihoods simplifies pipelines and reduces compute waste.

The Qwen3 family showcases what happens when theory meets practice: robust RL training at billions of parameters plus sparsity translates into tangible leaderboard lifts on mathematics (*AIME’24*) and code generation (*LiveCodeBench*, *Codeforces*). As large language models keep growing—and as we push them deeper into reasoning tasks—the stability properties introduced by GSPO will become increasingly vital.

If you’re building your own RL loop for LLM fine‑tuning, give GSPO a try:

```python
# Pseudocode sketch
for batch in rollout_batches:
    # generate G responses per query
    responses = model.generate(batch.x, num_return_sequences=G)

    # compute rewards r(x,y) via your verifier
    rewards = verifier(batch.x, responses)

    # compute sequence logprob under old policy
    old_logp = old_model.logprob(batch.x, responses)

    # compute sequence logprob under current policy
    new_logp = model.logprob(batch.x, responses)

    # length-normalized importance ratio
    s = torch.exp((new_logp - old_logp) / lengths)

    # group advantage
    adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()

    # clipped loss
    loss = -torch.mean(torch.min(
               s * adv,
               torch.clamp(s,
                           1 - eps_low,
                           1 + eps_high) * adv))
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
```

Replace `eps_low/high` with values around `$3\times10^{-4}$` / `$4\times10^{-4}$` for Qwen3 scale experiments; tune if you move far away from those settings.
