\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### (Advanced) How would GSPO be adapted for tasks where rewards are defined per turn rather than per full response?
Question:
"GSPO-token" allows tokenwise advantage customization, but what about multi‑turn dialogues where each turn has its own reward signal? Could we define a turn‑level importance ratio analogous to sequences within a response? What modifications would be needed in the clipping and advantage computation steps?
Answer:
**Answer**

The key idea behind GSPO is *matching* the granularity of the importance‑sampling weight with the granularity at which the reward is issued.  
In the original setting the reward is attached to an entire response, so the algorithm uses a **sequence‑level** ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
$$  

and clips this scalar for every sampled response in a group.  
If we now have a **multi‑turn dialogue** in which a verifier gives a separate scalar $r_t(x,y^{(t)})$ after each turn $t$, we can obtain an exact analogue by treating *each turn* as an independent “mini‑episode” and applying the same recipe with two minor changes:

1. **Turn‑level importance ratio** – replace the response‑level likelihood by the likelihood of the *turn* conditioned on the dialogue history up to that point.
2. **Turn‑level advantage** – compute a relative advantage inside every group of candidates for that particular turn instead of once per full response.

Below is a concrete formulation and an intuitive explanation of why it works.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. From full‑response to per‑turn objects

Let  

* $h_t$ be the dialogue history before generating turn $t$; formally  
  $h_t = (x, y^{(1)}, y^{(2)}, \dots , y^{(t-1)})$.
* $y_i^{(t)}$ denote the *i‑th* sampled continuation for turn $t$ drawn from the old policy:
  
  $$
  y_i^{(t)} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,|h_t).
  $$

* The verifier supplies a scalar reward $r_t(h_t,y_i^{(t)})\in[0,1]$.

Exactly as in GSPO we form groups of size $G_t$ (normally all groups have the same size $G$) and define  

$$
\widehat A_i^{(t)}
   = \frac{
          r_t(h_t,y_i^{(t)})
          - \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{ r_t(h_t,y_j^{(t)}) \}_{j=1}^{G_t}\bigr)
        }{
          \operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{ r_t(h_t,y_j^{(t)}) \}_{j=1}^{G_t}\bigr)
        } .
\tag{1}
$$

The **turn‑level importance ratio** mirrors (4) in the paper:  

$$
s_i^{(t)}(\theta)
   = \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i^{(t)}|h_t)}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i^{(t)}|h_t)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i^{(t)}|}
   = \exp\!\Bigl(
        \frac{1}{|y_i^{(t)}|}
        \sum_{k=1}^{|y_i^{(t)}|}
        \log
        \frac{
               \pi_\theta(y_{i,k}^{(t)}|h_t,y_{i,<k}^{(t)})
             }{
               \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,k}^{(t)}|h_t,y_{i,<k}^{(t)})
             }\Bigr).
\tag{2}
$$

Because we again take a geometric mean over tokens, different‑length turns stay comparable and the variance stays bounded – exactly what Section 3.2 of the paper recommends when moving from tokens to sequences.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Turn‑level objective

With (1)–(2) we write a **turn‑wise GSPO** objective that simply adds together all turns in a dialogue:  

$$
\boxed{
   {\mathcal J}_{\text{GSPO‑turn}}(\theta)
   = 
   {\mathbb E}_{\,h_1,\dots,h_T}
   {\Biggl[
      \sum_{t=1}^{T}
      {\frac{1}{G_t}}
      {\sum_{i=1}^{G_t}}
      {\min}
      {\bigl(
          s_i^{(t)}(\theta)\,\widehat A_i^{(t)},
          ~~ 
          {\rm clip}\!\bigl(s_i^{(t)}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,
                                   1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,
          \widehat A_i^{(t)}
     \bigr)}
   {\Biggr]} .
}
\tag{3}
$$

All symbols have exactly the same meaning as in Eq.\,(5) of the paper; only “response” has been replaced by “turn”.  

*The clipping* remains unchanged except that its threshold now applies to *each* turn’s scalar ratio $s_i^{(t)}(\theta)$. ### 3. Intuition & connection to classic RL

Think of each dialogue as an *episode* composed of several sub‑episodes (the turns).  
In ordinary episodic policy gradient methods one would compute an *advantage* for every time step by comparing its **return‑to‑go** with some baseline.  
Our setting is even simpler: after every sub‑episode we already have an external scalar feedback; there is no need for bootstrapping or temporal discounting unless you want to incorporate future rewards.  

Thus Eq.\,(3) plays exactly the role of a standard REINFORCE loss **with importance sampling correction** – except that:
* The correction weight is taken over an entire sub‑episode rather than a single action.
* The “baseline” is computed **relatively** within each group, precisely what GRPO/GSPO already do (Eq.\,(6)).

Because importance sampling now matches the unit at which rewards are defined, all theoretical guarantees discussed in Sections 3–4 remain valid: clipping bounds how far off‑policy any sampled turn can be, normalisation keeps variance low, and gradients are weighted uniformly across tokens inside a given sampled turn (Section 4’s gradient analysis).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Practical checklist

| Step | What changes compared with vanilla GSPO |
|------|----------------------------------------|
| **Rollout generation** | For every dialogue round `\( t`\)` sample `\( G`\)` continuations from `\( π_{\text old}`` conditioned on current history `$h_{ t }`. |
| **Reward acquisition** | Evaluate each continuation with your per‑turn verifier → obtain `$ r_{ t }(`…)`. |
| **Advantage computation** | Standardize these rewards within their group → Eq.\,(1). |
| **Importance weight** | Compute geometric‑mean likelihood ratio over tokens of *the whole turn* → Eq.\,(2). |
| **Clipping** | Apply ``clip$( s _ i ^ {( t ) } (\ theta ),\,10 ^ { -4 } , … )`` exactly as in GSPO; typically `$ε$` remains on order `$10^{-4}$` because `$s$` lives near 1 after length normalisation. |
| **Loss aggregation** | Sum/min over all turns (`$\sum _ t$`) before taking expectation – this corresponds to “per‑turn” policy gradient without mixing different time scales. |
| **Optional token‐level refinement** | Use GSPO‐token formulation with detached `$ s _ i ^ {( t ) } (\ theta ) $` if you need intra‐turn weighting.|

Implementation-wise you only need an extra outer loop over turns; everything else—grouping, standardisation, clipping—reuses existing GSPO code paths.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Why this works better than naïvely applying GSPO‐token on whole dialogues

If you kept one giant response (all turns concatenated) and gave it tokenwise advantages derived from per‐turn rewards (e.g., repeat each turn’s score over its tokens), you would reintroduce a mismatch: many tokens would carry an *incorrect* advantage because their actual feedback belongs to another temporal segment of the episode. This would again break the “unit‐of‐reward ≈ unit‐of‐importance” principle identified in Section 3 (“the unit … should match …”) and could revive high variance similar to GRPO’s token‐level issue.

By explicitly splitting at *turn boundaries*, each scalar weight `$ s _ i ^ {( t ) } (\ theta ) $` reflects exactly how much our current policy deviates from the old one **for that part of the episode on which we have received feedback**, guaranteeing low variance and stable training—precisely what motivated moving from token‐wise ratios to sequence‐wise ratios in GSPO.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 6. Summary

* Treat every dialogue turn as its own “sequence”.
* Compute a length‑normalised likelihood ratio for *the whole turn* (Eq.\,(2)).
* Use relative advantages computed from per‑turn rewards inside each group (Eq.\,(1)).
* Apply exactly the same clipped min operator as GSPO; optionally keep tokenwise flexibility via GSPO‐token with detached ratios.
* Sum over all turns of the dialogue (Eq.\,(3)).

This adaptation stays faithful to all theoretical arguments made in Sections 3–4 of the paper and inherits GSPO’s proven stability benefits while handling multi‑turn reward signals naturally.<br>
