### How does mini‑batch partitioning introduce an off‑policy setting, and how does sequence‑level clipping mitigate its effects?
Question:
During RL training, rollout batches are split into multiple mini‑batches for gradient updates. This means samples are used after several parameter updates have already occurred, making them off‑policy. In PPO/GRPO this is handled by clipping per token. In GSPO we clip per response via \(s_i(\theta)\. How does this clipping bound the bias introduced by using stale samples across mini‑batches?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Splitting a rollout batch into several gradient‑update mini‑batches means that after the first update the parameters have already moved from the policy $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{old}}}$ that generated all the responses. The remaining mini‑batches therefore contain *stale* samples – they are drawn from an old behavior distribution while the optimiser is trying to improve a newer target distribution $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. In PPO and its relatives this mismatch is handled by **trust‑region clipping**: an importance‑weight term is multiplied by the advantage, but every weight larger than $1+\varepsilon$ or smaller than $1-\varepsilon$ is truncated before it contributes to the gradient.  

GSPO replaces the per‑token weight used in GRPO with a **sequence‑level importance ratio**
$$
s_i(\boldsymbol{\theta})
=
\Biggl(
    \frac{\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigl(y_i\,|\,x\bigr)}
         {\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{old}}}\bigl(y_i\,|\,x\bigr)}
   \Biggr)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp!\Biggl(
     \frac{1}{|y_i|}
     \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log%
        \frac%
          {\displaystyle%
           \pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,
               x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}%
          {\displaystyle%
           \pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{old}}}
             \bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,
               x,y_{i,<t}}\bigr)}%
        }\Biggr),
$$

Why does this matter more than token–level clipping?
GRPO’s token–wise importance weight  

$
w _ { i , t } ({ θ })
=
\dfrac{
    π _ { θ } ( y _ { i , t } |

}{
    π _ { θ _ {\mathrm { old }} }
       ( y _ { i , t } |
          x , y _ { i , < t })

is applied separately at every position.
Because a single response consists of many tokens,
even modest changes in a few token probabilities multiply together over long sequences,
producing wildly varying products $\prod_t w _ { i , t }$. After applying per–token clipping these extreme products still appear many times (the paper observes two orders of magnitude more tokens being clipped in GRPO), so most gradients are computed from heavily trimmed weights while only a tiny fraction survives unchanged.
The net result is an estimator with large variance and an uncontrolled bias that grows with sequence length – precisely what caused “catastrophic model collapse” in large–scale training.
