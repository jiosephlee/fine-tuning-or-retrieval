Chapter 4: **Importance Sampling Theory in Off‑Policy Reinforcement Learning**

# Importance Sampling Identity and Proof Sketch  

The elementary task of off‑policy reinforcement learning is to estimate an expectation under a *target* distribution while having available only samples drawn from a *behavior* distribution.  The mathematical foundation for this task is the **importance sampling identity**  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}}\!\bigl[f(z)\bigr]
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}}\!
\left[
\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
\right].
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) follows directly from a change‑of‑measure argument.  Write the expectation under $\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}$ as an integral (or sum) over all possible outcomes $z$:  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\sum_{z} \pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)\,f(z).
\]  

Multiplying and dividing inside the sum by $\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)$ yields  

\[
\sum_{z}\Bigl[\underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}}_{w(z)}\,\underbrace{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}_{p(z)}\,f(z)\Bigr]
=
\sum_{z}p(z)\,w(z)\,f(z)
=
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[w(z)\,f(z)],
\]  

where $w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}(z)}$ is called the **importance weight**.  Hence the right‑hand side of (1) equals exactly the left‑hand side, establishing unbiasedness: if one could average $w(z)f(z)$ over all possible draws $z$ drawn according to $\pi_{\mathrm{beh}}$, one would recover precisely $\mathbb E_{\,\pi_\mathrm {tar}}\![f]$.  

In practice an analyst possesses only a finite collection of samples drawn from $\pi_\mathrm {beh}$; therefore $(1)$ inspires a Monte‑Carlo estimator

\[
\widehat{\mu}
=
\frac1N
\sum_{n=1}^{N}
w(z_n)\,
f(z_n),
\qquad z_n \stackrel{i.i.d}{\sim}\!\pi_\mathrm {beh},
\tag{2}
\]  

which converges almost surely to $\mathbb E_{\,\pi_\mathrm {tar}}\![f]$ as $N$ grows because of the law of large numbers (discussed next).

# Behavior Distribution versus Target Distribution Roles  

In Eq. (1), $\pi_\mathrm {beh}$ plays the role of *sampling distribution*.  It determines which data points are actually observed during rollouts or demonstrations—e.g., responses generated by an older policy $\pi_{\theta_\text {old }}$.  The *target* distribution $\pi_\mathrm {tar}$ encodes what probability mass we would like our estimate to reflect—typically those arising under a new policy $\pi_\theta$ being optimized.  

Because reinforcement learning iteratively improves policies, one never has direct access to fresh samples from $\pi_\theta$ while computing gradients for $\theta$.  By treating $\pi_\theta$ as $\pi_\text {tar}$ and using previously collected rollouts sampled under $\pi_{\theta_\text {old }}$, we retain an offline learning signal while staying unbiased provided we correctly re‑weight each observation by its importance weight $w(\cdot)= {\displaystyle \frac{\pi_\theta(\cdot)}{\;\;\;\,\pi_{\theta_\text {old }}(\cdot)}}$.  

Thus “behavior” refers concretely to **the policy at which data were generated**, whereas “target” refers **to the current policy whose improvement objective we wish to evaluate**.

# Importance Weight Definition  

The scalar factor correcting for distributional mismatch is called **the importance weight**,

\[
w(z)\;\triangleq
\dfrac{\pi_{\mathrm {tar}}\!\bigl(z\bigr)}
      {\;\;\;\,\;\;\;\,\displaystyle 
       \pi_{\mathrm {beh}}\!\bigl(z\bigr)}.
      \tag{3}
\]  

When $w(z)>1$, outcome $z$ has become more probable under the target policy than under the behavior policy; conversely $w(z)<1$ signals that $z$ has been de‑emphasized relative to its former probability mass.  In policy gradient methods that employ clipping---for instance PPO---the raw weight $w(\cdot)$ often appears inside a piecewise function 

\[
\tau(w)=
    \min
    \Bigl(
        w\,A,
        \
        \operatorname {clip}(w,\,
               1-\varepsilon,\,
               1+\varepsilon )A
    \Bigr),
    \tag{4}
\]  

where $A$ denotes an advantage estimate and $\varepsilon>0$ constrains how far off‑policy samples may influence parameter updates.  This construction rests squarely upon Eq. (3).

By virtue of either strong or weak forms of law–of–large–numbers arguments (or equivalently central-limit scaling considerations), **the estimator becomes unbiased asymptotically**, with standard error scaling roughly as 

$\sigma_w / {\sqrt N}$ where $\sigma_w^2 = {\rm Var}_{\, z~π_{beh}}\!\bigl[ w(z) f(z)\bigr].$
This reasoning clarifies why reinforcement‑learning practice routinely aggregates many trajectories into large mini‑batches before forming an update step—a practical expression of $N\gg 1.$

# Variance Blowup When Weights Are Computed From Single Samples  

Although Eq. (1) guarantees unbiasedness irrespective of $N$, practical estimation suffers dramatically if $N$ is too small relative to fluctuations in $w(\cdot) $.  
If only **one** observation per “distribution” were employed—the situation encountered when each token’s next‑token conditional probability is treated individually—the estimator reduces to

\[
w(y_t)
    = 
    {\displaystyle 
     \frac{
           πθ(y_t|x,y_{<t})
           }
           {
           πθold(y_t|x,y_{<t})
           } },
     %   star step ...
     % process...

which corresponds precisely to GRPO’s token‑level weighting term $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ introduced in Eq.~(2).  
Because each ratio involves *a single draw* from its own categorical distribution over vocabulary tokens,
the denominator may become extremely small while its numerator stays bounded away from zero,
so $w(y_t)$ can explode arbitrarily large or vanish arbitrarily close to zero.
Consequently its variance 

$\operatorname {Var}\!\bigl(w(y_t) A\bigr)$

may become unbounded even when rewards are bounded ($r(x,y)\in[0,1]$, thus advantage magnitudes remain modest).
When many tokens appear inside long responses—as typical for modern language models—these high‑variance ratios multiply along sequences,
amplifying gradient noise at every update step.
Moreover clipping
invariantly throttles extreme ratios yet preserves their *biasing effect*: even modest deviations near $(1±ε)$ are multiplied exponentially across positions leading easily toward model collapse.
Empirically this phenomenon manifested in GRPO experiments as catastrophic performance breakdowns after relatively few optimisation steps,
and attempts at hyperparameter tuning failed because variance was rooted fundamentally in applying Eq. (3) at too granular a unit (single tokens).  
Hence **a misapplication of importance weights at token level violates**
the essential large‐sample requirement articulated earlier,
and produces “variance blowup” – an instability confirmed by repeated empirical failures reported throughout Sec.~3 (*Motivation*).



# Illustrative Examples From Bandits To Sequential Decision Making  

To consolidate intuition about why averaging across many draws mitigates variance,
consider first a **single‐armed stochastic bandit** setting.
Suppose an algorithm chooses action $a$, receives scalar reward $R$, yet wishes retrospectively evaluate how another stochastic policy would have performed.
If every pull yields exactly one scalar outcome,
the unbiased estimator reduces merely to evaluating 

$
R\times%
\dfrac{{π}_{{new}}\!(a)}
{{π}_{{old}}\!(a)},
$

which depends on drawing *exactly one* reward per action—a notoriously noisy gauge because ${π}_{{old}}\!(a)$ can be arbitrarily tiny.
Increasing reliability demands pulling each arm repeatedly (i.e., larger $N$), thereby smoothing out fluctuations via Eq.~(2).

Now map this perspective onto **sequential decision making**, specifically autoregressive language generation.
A response $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ arises through successive conditionals:
$\displaystyle π_θ(y|x)=
     ∏^{T}_{t=1}
     π_θ(y_t|x,y_{<t}) .
$
If one treats every conditional independently—as did GRPO—each position contributes a factor identical in spirit
to drawing once from its own categorical “arm.”
Because distinct logits are sampled independently per position,
the product-of‑ratios form

$
∏^{T}_{t=1}
\dfrac{
   π_θ(y_t|x,y_{<t})
}{
   π_θ^{old}(y_t|x,y_{<t})
}
$

acquires multiplicative noise detrimental especially when $T$ is large.
In contrast, **Group Sequence Policy Optimization** adopts
the **sequence‑level** weight

$
s_i(\theta)=%
      \Bigl(
           {\displaystyle 
            {
            π_θ(y_i|x)
            }\over{

            π^{old}_θ(y_i|x)}}%
       {
      }\Bigr)^{
        {1}/{|y_i|}
        }
$
which mathematically equals taking *the geometric mean* across token ratios.
Equivalently,

$
s_i(\theta)=%
       e^{
       {
            {%
                (1/|y_i|)
                }\!
                {{
                  ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                 log {
                  π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})/
                  π^{old}_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) }}
                } }
       }
$

Effectively this computes **one average log‐ratio** per entire response.
By aggregating information across all tokens *before* applying clipping,
GSPO restores precisely what Eq.~(2)’s law of large numbers demands:
each response acts as one high‐dimensional sample whose importance weight has reduced variability compared with its component token weights.
Consequently gradient estimates inherit lower variance,
as illustrated empirically by dramatically fewer clipped tokens (Fig.~3), yet maintain superior learning efficiency—a direct validation of core importance‐sampling theory applied at compatible granularity.


---  
Through these sections we have connected three central learning messages:

* Importance sampling supplies an exact unbiased correction so long as sufficient independent draws support each correction factor.
* Misusing Eq.~(3) at too fine a granularity—specifically at individual token levels—breaches this statistical principle, inflating estimator variance and destabilising training.
* Aligning the unit of reward (normally assigned per complete response) with the unit for off‐policy correction (entire sequence likelihood) restores statistical soundness, which underpins Group Sequence Policy Optimization’s empirical success over its predecessor GRPO.

These insights scaffold principled design choices for future off‑policy reinforcement learning algorithms targeting ever larger language models or other sequential decision problems.
