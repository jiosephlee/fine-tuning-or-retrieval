\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Why Token‑Level Importance Weights Fail at Scale – The GRPO Story  

*Understanding the hidden instability in Group Relative Policy Optimization and why moving to sequence‑level reasoning matters.*

---

## 1. The Reinforcement‑Learning Landscape for LLMs  

Large language models (LLMs) have become powerful problem‑solvers when trained with reinforcement learning (RL). In RL‑fine‑tuning, a policy \(\pi_\theta\) (the LLM) generates a response \(y\) to a query \(x\), a verifier assigns a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\), and the policy is updated to increase expected reward.

The canonical RL algorithm for this setting is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**. PPO uses an *importance ratio* for each token:
\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})},
\]
and clips it to a narrow band \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) before multiplying by a token‑level advantage estimate \(\widehat A_t\). This approach works well when we have a reliable value model that can predict \(\widehat A_t\).

When scaling to billions of parameters, keeping an extra value model becomes expensive. **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** was introduced to avoid this cost by estimating advantages *relative* to other responses in a group instead of using an external value network.

---

## 2. How GRPO Works  

GRPO samples \(G\) responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) per query and treats every token in each response equally:

* **Group‑based advantage**  
  \[
  \widehat A_i = 
  \frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)}
       {\operatorname{std}\!\big(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\big)} .
  \]

* **Token‑level importance ratio** (same as PPO)  
  \[
  w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
  \]

The GRPO objective mirrors PPO’s clipped surrogate but averages over the group:
\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min\!\big(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,
              \operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
        \big)\Big].
\]

At first glance, this seems like a straightforward way to remove the value model while still correcting for off‑policy data.

---

## 3. The Core Issue: Misusing Importance Sampling  

### 3.1 What Importance Sampling Really Says  

The textbook importance sampling identity is
\[
\mathbb E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] =
\mathbb E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
     \Big[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\Big],
\]
where the *ratio* corrects the mismatch between the **behavior distribution** (where samples are drawn) and the **target distribution** (where we want expectations). Crucially, this correction works *only after averaging over many independent samples* drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\).

### 3.2 Why Token‑Level Ratios Fail  

In language generation each token is sampled from its own conditional distribution. GRPO applies an importance weight *per token*:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
     \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

But each \(w_{i,t}\) is based on **a single draw** from that token’s next‑token distribution—there is no batch of draws to average over. Consequently:

* The weight does **not** faithfully estimate the ratio of target vs.\ behavior distributions.
* Its variance can be huge because a single token’s probability can swing dramatically under small model updates.
* As we sum these noisy ratios across long sequences, variance compounds linearly with length.

When we further clip each token’s ratio, we are discarding an even larger fraction of high‑variance samples. In practice this leads to *gradient estimates that are both biased (due to clipping) and extremely noisy*.

### 3.3 Accumulating Noise Leads to Collapse  

Consider a response of \(500\) tokens. Even if each token’s importance ratio occasionally deviates by a factor of \(2\), the product of these deviations becomes astronomically large or tiny after exponentiation across all tokens—exactly what the gradient sees in GRPO.

Empirically (see Section 5 of the paper), training with GRPO on large models often ends in **catastrophic model collapse**:

* Early training steps appear stable.
* After several updates (especially with longer generations), gradients become dominated by outlier ratios.
* The policy’s likelihood devolves, making it impossible to recover even if training is rolled back or hyperparameters are tuned.

The root cause is clear: **the unit of optimization (tokens) does not match the unit of reward (entire sequence)**; applying off‑policy correction at token granularity violates importance sampling principles.

---

## 4. Attempts at Patching GRPO  

### 4.1 Routing Replay for MoE Models  

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures add another layer of instability: expert routing can change between rollout and update, causing dramatic fluctuations in per‑token probabilities \(w_{i,t}\). Researchers introduced **Routing Replay**, which caches the expert activation pattern from the old policy and forces the new policy to use it when computing \(w_{i,t}\). While this restores stability for MoE models, it comes at substantial cost:

* Extra memory for routing caches.
* Communication overhead in distributed settings.
* Potentially limiting expert utilization because updates are forced through stale routing decisions.

Thus Routing Replay only mitigates symptoms; it does not address the fundamental misalignment between token‑level ratios and sequence rewards.

### 4.2 Tuning Clipping Ranges  

One might hope that widening or narrowing PPO’s clipping band could tame variance. Experiments show that even with aggressive clipping (\(\varepsilon=0.2\)–\(0.27\)), GRPO still suffers because variance arises *before clipping*: noisy ratios already distort gradient direction, and clipping merely discards information rather than correcting it.

---

## 5. The Takeaway: Align Reward and Optimization Units  

The failure of GRPO teaches an essential lesson for scaling RL with LLMs:

| Aspect                | Token‑level (GRPO)                              | Sequence‑level (Recommended)                               |
|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Reward granularity   | Whole sequence                                   | Whole sequence                                            |
| Importance weight    | \(\displaystyle w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|...)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|...)}\) | \(\displaystyle s_i = \Big(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\Big)^{1/|y_i|}\) |
| Variance source      | Single-token sampling → high variance           | Averaged over all tokens → lower variance                 |
| Sensitivity to routing changes | Very high (per-token expert switches affect each ratio) | Low (overall sequence likelihood changes slowly)          |
| Need for special tricks | Routing Replay, careful clipping                | None required                                             |

By making **the unit of optimization match the unit of reward**, we obtain an estimator that truly respects importance sampling theory: we reweight *entire responses*, not individual tokens, using their full likelihood under both policies.

---

## 6. Looking Ahead: From GRPO to Sequence‑Level Methods  

While this post focuses on dissecting why GRPO collapses at scale, recent work proposes **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—a direct sequence‑level counterpart that defines importance ratios as
\[
s_i(\theta)=
      \left(
        \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
      \right)^{1/|y_i|}
      = 
      \exp\!\Bigg(
          \frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log
            \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
        \Bigg).
\]
Clipping now happens once per response rather than per token, dramatically reducing gradient noise while preserving the same theoretical guarantee: we are still performing an importance‐weighted expectation over whole sequences.

Empirical results in the original paper show that GSPO:

* Stays stable across billions of parameters and long generations.
* Requires no routing replay for MoE models.
* Achieves higher training efficiency despite clipping *more* tokens overall—a testament that sequence‐level weighting yields a stronger learning signal than noisy token‐level estimates.

---

## 7. Bottom Line  

- **GRPO’s token‑level importance ratios violate core importance sampling assumptions**, leading to high variance that explodes with longer texts.
- **Clipping amplifies** rather than mitigates this issue because it discards most noisy gradients without fixing their root cause.
- The mismatched granularity between *reward* (sequence level) and *optimization* (token level) is what precipitates catastrophic model collapse.
- **Sequence‑level objectives** like GSPO align weights with rewards, drastically reducing variance, eliminating complex workarounds such as Routing Replay, and scaling robustly to massive MoE models.

For anyone building RL pipelines on modern LLMs, the takeaway is clear: stop treating tokens as independent off‑policy samples when your signal lives at the utterance level—let the whole response guide your updates. This insight unlocks stable training at scales previously thought impossible.
