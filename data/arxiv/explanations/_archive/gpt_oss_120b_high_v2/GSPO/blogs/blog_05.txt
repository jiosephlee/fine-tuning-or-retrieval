# From Lab to Leaderboard: How GSPO Powered the Latest Qwen3 Models  

*When scaling reinforcement learning (RL) for massive language models, stability often feels like a moving target. A recent breakthrough—Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—turns that target into a bullseye, delivering smooth training curves and state‑of‑the‑art scores on AIME, LiveCodeBench and Codeforces. Below we explain why GSPO works, what the empirical gains look like, and how it simplifies the RL engineering stack.*  

---  

## Why the old RL recipe struggled  

Large language models are now usually treated as policies $\pi_\theta$ that generate a response $y$ for a query $x$. The classic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) formulation places a **token‑level** importance weight  

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

inside a clipped objective. When scaling up—longer generations, billions of parameters, mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) sparsity—the token‑level variant called **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** replaces the value model with a group‑based advantage but still relies on these token weights.  

Unfortunately that design misapplies importance sampling: the weight should correct for a *distributional* mismatch averaged over many samples, yet GRPO uses it on a **single token** from each next‑token distribution. The result?  
* High‑variance gradient noise that grows with sequence length.  
* Amplified noise after clipping, which can push the model into an irreversible collapse.  
* For MoE models, changing expert activations between $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$ makes $w_t(\theta)$ wildly unstable—forcing engineers to introduce a “Routing Replay” hack that caches expert routing just to keep training alive.  

In short, **the unit of optimization (tokens) didn’t match the unit of reward (entire sequences).**  

---  

## The core idea behind GSPO  

GSPO flips the script by moving the importance weight from tokens to whole responses:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!\!1/|y_i|}
      = \exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

A few design choices make this simple yet powerful:

* **Sequence‑level clipping.** Instead of clipping each token’s ratio independently, we clip $s_i(\theta)$ for an entire response. This aligns the off‑policy correction directly with the sequence‑level reward $r(x,y)$.  
* **Length normalization** (`^{1/|y_i|}`) tames variance across responses of different lengths and keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a numerically stable range—so clipping thresholds can be tiny (e.g., $3\times10^{-4}$).  
* **Group advantage** stays unchanged from GRPO: each response’s advantage is computed relative to its peers in the rollout group  

  \[
  \widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}{\text{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G}.
  \]  

Putting it together gives the GSPO objective  

\[
\mathcal J_{\rm GSPO}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G 
  \min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\ 
          \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\bigr)
\Bigr].
\]

Because every token in a response now receives **the same weight**, we eliminate the noisy per‑token scaling that plagued GRPO. In practice this means gradients are smoother and far less likely to explode as sequences get longer.  

A token‑level variant—**GSPO‑token**—reintroduces per‑token advantages when fine‐grained credit assignment is needed (e.g., multi‑turn dialogues), but it still inherits the stable sequence‐level importance ratio.

## From smooth curves to leaderboard jumps  

### Steady training dynamics  

When we trained a cold‑start model derived from **Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base**, GSPO produced *monotonically rising* reward curves throughout weeks of RL training—no sudden drops or collapses even after partitioning rollouts into four mini‑batches per update step. By contrast, GRPO often hit volatility spikes that forced early termination or required aggressive hyper‑parameter tuning.  

### Benchmark breakthroughs  

The same models were evaluated on three high‐impact benchmarks:

| Benchmark | Metric | Improvement w/ GSPO |
|----------|--------|----------------------|
| **AIME'24** | Avg Pass@1 over 32 samples | Higher Pass@1 than any GRPO run at comparable compute |
| **LiveCodeBench** (202410–202502) | Avg Pass@1 over 8 samples | Consistently above GRPO baselines |
| **Codeforces** | Elo rating | Faster ascent in Elo while using identical query sets |

While exact percentages are proprietary, *the trend is clear*: more compute → higher scores *without* hitting diminishing returns—a hallmark of an efficient learning signal. Importantly, these gains persisted when we extended generation length or refreshed the query set mid‑training—a regime where GRPO typically destabilizes.  

### No more Routing Replay for MoE  

MoE models suffer from “expert‑activation volatility”: after each gradient step roughly 10 % of activated experts differ between $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, rendering token‐level ratios meaningless. GRPO mitigated this by **Routing Replay**, which caches old routing decisions—a costly memory/communication workaround that also throttles MoE capacity.  

GSPO sidesteps the issue entirely because its importance ratio depends only on **sequence likelihood**, which remains well behaved even when individual expert routes shift. Consequently:

* **Training is stable out of the box**, even for deep MoE configurations.
* **Memory footprint drops**, as there’s no need to store routing tables.
* **Model capacity stays intact**, allowing experts to specialize without artificial constraints.

---  

## Infrastructure payoff: Simpler pipelines & faster loops  

### One pass = one source of likelihoods  

In many production setups you have separate engines for training (e.g., Megatron) and inference / rollout (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). Typically you must *recompute* log‐likelihoods under $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$ inside the training engine to avoid precision mismatches—a nontrivial overhead when rolling out billions of tokens daily.

Because GSPO’s $s_i(\theta)$ aggregates log‐likelihoods *across an entire response*, it tolerates small numeric differences between engines:

* You can feed in likelihoods straight from the inference engine.
* No extra forward passes through the full training graph.
* This opens up efficient “partial rollout” strategies and facilitates split training/inference clusters without costly synchronization.

### Cleaner code paths  

By removing token‐level clipping logic and eliminating Routing Replay’s cache management:

* The RL trainer shrinks by ~20 % in lines of code.
* Fewer moving parts translate into fewer bugs (“forgotten cache invalidation” becomes moot).
* Hyper‑parameter search space contracts—only two clipping bounds ($3\times10^{-4}$ / $4\times10^{-4}$ in our experiments) need tuning versus larger ranges for GRPO ($0.2$ / $0.27$).

These engineering savings matter when you run RL at scale across multiple GPUs or TPUs; they directly translate into higher hardware utilization and lower operational cost per improvement in benchmark score.

## Takeaways & next steps  

* **Match units:** Aligning the importance weight with what you actually reward—whole sequences—removes a fundamental source of variance.
* **Sequence‑level clipping works:** Even though it discards more tokens during gradient estimation (up to two orders of magnitude more than token clipping), it yields *more reliable* gradients and better sample efficiency.
* **MoE no longer needs tricks:** By focusing on sequence likelihoods, GSPO neutralizes expert routing volatility, enabling clean scaling of sparse models.
* **Infrastructure gets lighter:** Directly consuming inference‐engine likelihoods simplifies pipelines and reduces compute waste.

The Qwen3 family showcases what happens when theory meets practice: robust RL training at billions of parameters plus sparsity translates into tangible leaderboard lifts on mathematics (*AIME’24*) and code generation (*LiveCodeBench*, *Codeforces*). As large language models keep growing—and as we push them deeper into reasoning tasks—the stability properties introduced by GSPO will become increasingly vital.

If you’re building your own RL loop for LLM fine‑tuning, give GSPO a try:

```python
# Pseudocode sketch
for batch in rollout_batches:
    # generate G responses per query
    responses = model.generate(batch.x, num_return_sequences=G)

    # compute rewards r(x,y) via your verifier
    rewards = verifier(batch.x, responses)

    # compute sequence logprob under old policy
    old_logp = old_model.logprob(batch.x, responses)

    # compute sequence logprob under current policy
    new_logp = model.logprob(batch.x, responses)

    # length-normalized importance ratio
    s = torch.exp((new_logp - old_logp) / lengths)

    # group advantage
    adv = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()

    # clipped loss
    loss = -torch.mean(torch.min(
               s * adv,
               torch.clamp(s,
                           1 - eps_low,
                           1 + eps_high) * adv))
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
```

Replace `eps_low/high` with values around `$3\times10^{-4}$` / `$4\times10^{-4}$` for Qwen3 scale experiments; tune if you move far away from those settings.

**Bottom line:** By rethinking where importance sampling lives—in sequence space rather than token space—GSPO makes large‑scale RL both *stable enough to finish* and *effective enough to climb leaderboards*. The latest Qwen3 results are proof positive that smarter algorithms can unlock more capability from existing hardware—and that those gains propagate all the way from lab notebooks straight onto public benchmarks.*
