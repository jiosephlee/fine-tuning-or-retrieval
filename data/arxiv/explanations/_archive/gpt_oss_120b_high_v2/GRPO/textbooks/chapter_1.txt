\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: Motivation and Overview of DeepSeekMath  

# Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Challenging for Language Models  

Mathematical problem solving differs fundamentally from ordinary natural‑language generation because it demands strict adherence to logical structure, precise symbol manipulation, and often multi‑step derivations that must remain internally consistent. Unlike narrative text, a single arithmetic mistake or an algebraic misstep invalidates an entire answer, so errors are highly visible and unforgiving. Moreover, many problems require the model to interleave symbolic computation with linguistic explanation—a capability that traditional large language models (LLMs) have never been explicitly trained for at scale. The training data that most LLMs receive consist largely of informal prose or source code fragments lacking the dense hierarchical dependencies typical of textbook proofs or competition‑level contest problems such as those found in the MATH benchmark \citep{MATH}. Consequently, even very large proprietary models sometimes struggle when asked to generate step‑by‑step solutions without external tools or verification mechanisms.



# State of the Art Closed‑Source Models Versus Open‑Source Baselines  

Recent closed‑source systems such as GPT‑4 \citep{gpt4} and Gemini‑Ultra \citep{gemini} have demonstrated impressive quantitative‐reasoning abilities across a range of academic benchmarks, achieving accuracies far above those reported for publicly available models. For example, these systems comfortably exceed a fifty‑percent success rate on competition‐level datasets like MATH, while most open‑source competitors remain well below this threshold despite employing similar architectural designs (e.g., transformer decoders with billions of parameters).  

Open‑source baselines—including generalist LLMs such as Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} and specialized mathematics models built on proof‐oriented corpora—have historically lagged behind closed models by large margins (often more than ten absolute percentage points). Even when these smaller models are further refined through mathematics‐focused pre–training or instruction tuning, their performances still fall short compared with proprietary solutions that benefit from massive compute budgets and undisclosed data pipelines.\  
This persistent gap motivates a systematic investigation into whether open resources—especially publicly crawled web material—can be leveraged more effectively to close the disparity.



# Key Research Questions Addressed by DeepSeekMath  

The DeepSeekMath project was designed around several concrete questions raised by the observed performance gap:

1️⃣ **Can publicly available web pages supply enough high‑quality mathematical content to enable strong reasoning without resorting to proprietary corpora?**  
2️⃣ **If a large mathematics–specific corpus can be assembled, what is its impact relative to scaling model size alone?** In other words, does a modestly sized model trained on superior data rival much larger closed models?  
3️⃣ **Does prior exposure to source code confer advantages for pure mathematical reasoning (including tool–free solutions) as well as program­–aided problem solving?**  
4️⃣ **What role do scholarly archives such as arXiv play in strengthening quantitative reasoning when mixed with other sources?**  
5️⃣ **Beyond supervised instruction tuning, can reinforcement learning (RL) further sharpen mathematical capabilities while remaining computationally affordable?**  
6️⃣ **If an RL algorithm can be made memory efficient, does it still deliver measurable gains over strong instruction–tuned baselines?**  

By explicitly targeting these inquiries, DeepSeekMath seeks both practical progress—producing a high‑performing open model—and methodological insight that can guide future research.



# High‑Level Architecture: Pre‑Training → Instruction Tuning → Reinforcement Learning  

The overall training pipeline consists of three successive stages that progressively enrich the model’s mathematical competence while preserving its general language proficiency.

### 1 Pre‑Training on a Massive Mathematics Corpus  

The first stage extends the existing code‐oriented foundation model **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 7B** \citep{deepseek-coder}. Using an iterative filtering pipeline built around a fastText classifier \citep{joulin2016fasttext}, approximately **120 billion tokens** were harvested from Common Crawl pages identified as mathematically rich. The classifier was bootstrapped with OpenWebMath instances \citep{openwebmath}, refined through four rounds of domain analysis and manual URL annotation, ultimately yielding **35.5 million** distinct web pages spanning both English and Chinese sources.\  

During continued pre–training the token mix comprised roughly sixty percent mathematics from the newly constructed **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, four percent specialized AlgebraicStack code snippets, ten percent arXiv material (which later proved marginally useful), twenty percent GitHub code tokens, and a final ten percent generic natural‐language crawl data.\  
Training employed AdamW optimization with a peak learning rate near \(4.2\times10^{-4}\) and a batch size of ten million tokens per step under a four‑kilobyte context window—mirroring settings that proved effective for prior DeepSeek LLMs.\  

Empirically, this approach enabled a **7 billion parameter** model (“DeepSeekMath‑Base”) to achieve quantitative‐reasoning scores comparable with the closed‐source Minerva 540 B model \citep{minerva}, confirming that data quality can compensate dramatically for parameter count.


### 2 Instruction Tuning With Structured Reasoning Formats  

Having acquired strong raw knowledge, the second phase refines how the model articulates solutions through supervised fine–tuning (“SFT”). A curated instruction dataset containing **776 k** examples was assembled across English and Chinese curricula.<br>Each example pairs a problem statement with one—or multiple—solution representations drawn from three paradigms:

* **Chain–of–Thought (CoT)** prompting encourages explicit verbal reasoning steps.<br>
* **Program–of–Thought (PoT)** framing requires generating executable Python fragments that leverage libraries such as *math* or *sympy*.<br>
* **Tool–Integrated Reasoning** combines natural language explanations with calls to external utilities following the ToRA paradigm \citep{tora}.  

The SFT stage was brief yet effective: batches were concatenated up to \(4\,\text{k}\) tokens per sequence and optimized at a constant learning rate of \(5\times10^{-5}\) over five hundred gradient steps.\  
After this step the resulting model (**DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B**) outperformed every other open source system at similar scale on both tool‐free CoT evaluations and tool‐augmented PoT benchmarks—in particular surpassing existing state‐of‐the‐art openly released models on eight diversified English/Chinese test suites.


### 3 Reinforcement Learning Using Group Relative Policy Optimization  

The final refinement adopts reinforcement learning aimed specifically at boosting answer correctness beyond what supervision alone provides.<br>Traditional actor–critic methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) demand an auxiliary value network (“critic”) that predicts expected returns for every token—a heavy memory burden when applied to Large Language Models.\<br>To circumvent this cost while still harnessing comparative feedback, DeepSeekMath introduces **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**.\  

In GRPO each question \(q\) spawns a *group* of \(G\) sampled completions \(\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\) drawn from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). A learned reward model \(r_{\varphi}\) assigns scalar scores \(r_i=r_{\varphi}(q,o_i)\). Rather than relying on an external baseline estimator,\footnote{The classic PPO advantage \(A_t = r_t + \gamma V_{\psi}(s_{t+1})-V_{\psi}(s_t)\) requires a separate value function \(V_{\psi}\).} GRPO computes a *group-relative advantage* by normalizing each reward against its group mean:\[
   \hat A_i = \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)} .
\]These normalized advantages replace tokenwise advantage estimates in PPO’s surrogate objective while an explicit KL penalty between policy and reference (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)) is added directly to the loss rather than embedded into per‑token rewards.\<br>Formally,
\[
   \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
   \mathbb E_{q,\{o_i\}}\!\left[
      \frac1G
      \sum_{i=1}^{G}
        \frac1{|o_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}
        \!\Bigl(
          \min\!\Bigl[\!\frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}\,\hat A_{i,t},
                 {\rm clip}\bigl(\cdot\bigr)\hat A_{i,t}
          \Bigr]
          -\,\beta\,D_{\rm KL}[\pi_\theta||\pi_{\rm ref}]
        \!\Bigr)
    \right] .
\]Here \(D_{\rm KL}\) denotes an unbiased estimator
\(D_{\rm KL}= {\pi_{\rm ref}}/{\pi_\theta}
   - \log({\pi_{\rm ref}}/{\pi_\theta}) -1,\)
which guarantees positivity without requiring separate sampling.\  
By removing the critic network altogether GRPO reduces GPU memory consumption dramatically while still delivering robust policy updates based purely on *relative* feedback among sampled outputs.\  

Training employed only chain-of-thought formatted questions drawn from GSM8K and MATH SFT data (\(\approx144\,k\) distinct prompts), generating groups of \(64\) completions per question.\  The policy’s learning rate was set at \(1\times10^{-6}\), KL coefficient at \(0.{04}\), and optimization proceeded via single updates after each exploration batch—a design that mirrors an “online” reinforcement loop.\  
After RL refinement (**DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B**) achieved notable improvements over its SFT predecessor: GSM8K accuracy rose from \(82.{9}\%\) (\(≈87?\)—see original numbers) up to \(88.{2}\%\), while competition-level MATH accuracy climbed from \(46.{8}\%\) before RL to $\displaystyle 51.{7}\,\%\,$ after GRPO finetuning—a gain realized despite training only on GSM8K/MATH style supervision.<br>Additionally applying *self-consistency*—aggregating answers over sixty-four sampled completions—further elevated MATH performance to roughly $\displaystyle 60.{9}\,\%\,$.


# Summary of Main Contributions  

DeepSeekMath delivers two intertwined advances that together narrow—and arguably invert—the long‐standing superiority gap between open‐source LLMs and their closed counterparts in academic mathematics:

## Math Pre‑Training at Scale  

* By constructing an exhaustive mathematics mining pipeline anchored in fastText classification over Common Crawl data, researchers demonstrated that publicly reachable web material contains ample high‐quality mathematical exposition—far exceeding previously used collections such as Minerva’s web subset or OpenWebMath both in size (sevenfold larger than Minerva’s corpus) and linguistic diversity (substantial Chinese content).  
* Training a relatively small base model (**DeepSeekMath Base 7B**) on this curated corpus enabled it to match or surpass much larger closed systems (e.g., Minerva 540 B), establishing that careful data curation can outweigh sheer parameter magnitude for structured reasoning tasks.<br>  
* Experiments revealed that initializing mathematics training from a code specialist foundation markedly improves subsequent problem solving—both when tools are permitted (program-of-thought) and when pure textual derivations are required—providing empirical support for longstanding hypotheses about code pretraining enhancing logical inference capabilities.<br>  
* Conversely, adding extensive scientific papers from arXiv contributed little—or even slightly degraded—performance across all evaluated mathematical benchmarks, tempering assumptions about indiscriminate inclusion of scholarly PDFs during domain adaptation.


## Exploration and Analysis of Reinforcement Learning  

* The introduction of **Group Relative Policy Optimization** supplies an efficient alternative to conventional PPO by eliminating any auxiliary critic network and instead exploiting intra‑group reward normalization as baseline estimation—a strategy proven to halve memory requirements while preserving—or even enhancing—policy improvement dynamics.<br>  
* Empirical evaluation confirmed that GRPO delivers sizable uplifts over strong instruction-tuned baselines both *in domain* (e.g., GSM8K improvement from $\displaystyle82.{9}\,\%\,$ → $\displaystyle88.{2}\,\%\,$) and *out of domain* (e.g., CMATH rise from $\displaystyle84.{6}\,\%\,$ → $\displaystyle88.{8}\,\%\,$). Moreover self-consistency postprocessing pushes MATH accuracy beyond sixty percent without expanding model size.<br>  
* By situating GRPO alongside related methods such as Rejection Sampling Fine–Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), standard PPO, and newer online/offline variants within a unified gradient formulation,
the authors illuminate common underlying mechanisms—namely choices around data sources (online vs.~offline sampling), reward modeling strategies (rule-based versus learned), and algorithmic gradient coefficients—and systematically dissect their impact through ablations covering outcome versus process supervision as well as single-turn versus iterative RL loops.<br>  
* Finally, through controlled experiments assessing Pass@K versus Majority@K metrics it was shown that reinforcement learning predominantly sharpens result distribution robustness; i.e., it amplifies correct answers among top samples rather than fundamentally altering underlying reasoning ability—a nuanced insight guiding future RL design considerations.


Collectively these contributions demonstrate that judiciously engineered web mining combined with lightweight yet powerful reinforcement learning can elevate modestly sized open models into competitive performers on demanding academic mathematics benchmarks—a milestone unlikely achievable through scaling alone.



--- 

In sum, DeepSeekMath advances both *data engineering*—by unlocking vast hidden reservoirs of web mathematics—and *algorithmic efficiency*, via Group Relative Policy Optimization—to bring public language models into proximity with proprietary leaders such as GPT‑4 or Gemini Ultra across core number‐theoretic, algebraic, calculus‑based—and increasingly multilingual—reasoning tasks.
