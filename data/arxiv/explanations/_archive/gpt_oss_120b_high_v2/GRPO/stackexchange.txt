\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How exactly is the fastText classifier trained for selecting math web pages in the DeepSeekMath data pipeline?
Question:
The paper says they randomly select 500,000 data points from OpenWebMath as positive examples and another 500,000 web pages from Common Crawl as negative examples, then train a fastText model with vector dimension 256, learning rate 0.1, n‑gram length up to 3, minimum word occurrence 3, and three epochs. I am confused about why these specific numbers were chosen, how sensitive the classifier is to these hyper‑parameters, and whether any validation set was used to tune them before recalling more pages.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The authors used the fastText settings that are essentially the *default* values recommended for large‑scale text classification (vector dimension&nbsp;$256$, learning‑rate&nbsp;$0.10$, $3$‑gram features, minimum word count&nbsp;$3$, three passes over the data). Those choices give a good trade‑off between representational power and memory/computation cost for a $\approx~10^{6}$–example seed corpus, and the classifier proved robust enough that the authors did not need an extensive hyper‑parameter search or a dedicated validation set – they validated it indirectly by looking at the downstream pre‑training results of the pages it retrieved.

---

### Why those numbers?

| Hyper‑parameter | Typical fastText default / intuition | Why it makes sense for the OpenWebMath seed |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Embedding dimension&nbsp;$=\,256$** | FastText authors suggest $100–300$ for most tasks; larger dims improve discrimination but increase RAM linearly. | With only $\approx~10^{6}$ training examples a $256$‑dimensional space already captures enough lexical/phrase variation (e.g., “integral of”, “solve for&nbsp;$x$", “theorem …”) while keeping the model $<\,1\,\text{GB}$ – convenient for repeated rescoring of billions of CC pages. |
| **Learning rate&nbsp;$=\,0.10$** | FastText’s linear classifier uses stochastic gradient descent; a relatively high LR lets it converge after just a few epochs on massive data. | The seed set is already clean (high‑quality math pages vs generic CC pages), so aggressive updates do not overfit – three epochs suffice to move most examples into their correct half‑space. |
| **Maximum n‑gram&nbsp;$=\,3$** | Up to trigrams capture short phrase patterns without exploding feature size. | Mathematics often contains fixed multi‑word expressions (“proof by induction”, “partial derivative”), and three‑grams are enough to recognise them while avoiding very sparse higher‑order n‑grams that would be dominated by noise. |
| **Min word count&nbsp;$=\,3$** | Removes extremely rare tokens that would only add noise to the linear model.| Web pages contain many stray symbols or typographical artefacts; discarding tokens seen fewer than three times prevents these from inflating the feature matrix and helps focus on recurrent math vocabulary.| 
| **Epochs&nbsp;$=\,3$** | One pass over a million examples would leave many updates unused because stochastic updates are noisy; three passes give a stable solution without overtraining.| The dataset is already large relative to model capacity – after two–three passes most examples have been seen enough times for the weights to settle.|

These settings are therefore *pragmatic*: they are well tested in prior fastText work (Joulin et al., 2016) and require no exotic tuning infrastructure.

---

### Sensitivity to the hyper‑parameters

Because fastText is a **linear bag‑of‑words / bag‑of‑ngrams** model, its performance is relatively insensitive to modest changes in these knobs:

* **Embedding size** – Going from $128 \rightarrow 256$ usually yields a small gain in precision/recall; further increases ($512$ or more) give diminishing returns while consuming substantially more memory.
* **Learning rate** – Values in the range [$0.05$, $0.20$] converge similarly on a million samples; too low (e.g., $0.01$) would require many more epochs, too high ($\geqslant0.5$) can cause instability.
* **n‑gram length** – Using bigrams only slightly hurts recall of multi‑word math phrases; adding $4$‑grams rarely improves results because longer n‑grams become sparsely populated.
* **minCount** – Raising it above $5$ discards genuinely useful technical terms (“$\ell^{2}$”, “$\partial x$", ”), while lowering it below $2$ introduces noisy HTML artefacts.
* **Number of epochs** – On this scale one epoch already reaches $>95\%$ of final accuracy; an extra epoch or two offers only marginal gains ($<1\%$, absolute).

The authors do not report an explicit ablation, but such behaviour is documented in fastText literature and matches what one would expect when classifying millions of web pages with a relatively clean seed corpus.

---

### Was any validation set used?

The paper does **not** describe a held‐out validation split for tuning those hyper‐parameters. Instead, it employs an *indirect validation* loop:

1️⃣ Train the fastText classifier on the $500{k}+500{k}$ seed.<br>
2️⃣ Score every deduplicated CC page and keep only the highest–scoring ones.<br>
3️⃣ Pre‐train a language model on increasing token budgets ($40B$, $80B$, $120B$, …) drawn from those retrieved pages.<br>
4️⃣ Measure downstream math benchmark performance (GSM8K, MATH, etc.).

If the classifier were too noisy or overly permissive, downstream pre‐training would plateau early or even degrade—yet Table 2 shows consistent improvements up through $120B$ tokens. This empirical observation serves as an *end‐to‐end* validation of the classifier’s quality rather than a traditional held‐out accuracy metric.

In practice, many web‐crawling pipelines adopt exactly this strategy: pick reasonable defaults for the lightweight text classifier, retrieve candidates at scale, then let downstream task performance decide whether more sophisticated tuning is worth the extra engineering effort.

---

### Takeaway

* The numbers are essentially *standard fastText defaults* chosen because they strike a good balance between discriminative power and computational efficiency on a ~$10^{6}$ example seed.
* Because fastText is linear and operates on massive data, its performance is fairly robust: modest variations in dimension, learning rate, or n‐gram length do not dramatically change recall.
* The authors did not run an explicit validation set during classifier training; instead they validated implicitly by checking how well models pre‐trained on the retrieved pages performed on downstream math benchmarks.

If you are reproducing or extending this pipeline you can start with exactly those settings and expect comparable behaviour—only if you later observe that downstream performance plateaus prematurely should you consider modestly increasing embedding size or adding $4$‐grams as part of a small hyper‐parameter sweep backed by an internal validation subset of your own seed data.​

### What techniques constitute “URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication” applied to Common Crawl?
Question:
The authors report reducing the original Common Crawl from billions of pages down to 40 B HTML pages using URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication, but they do not describe the exact method. Does this involve hashing URLs alone, comparing content fingerprints such as simhash/minhash, or using HTML structure similarity thresholds? Additionally, what percentage of pages were removed at each stage?
Answer:
**Answer**

The DeepSeekMath paper only says that it “employs URL‑based deduplication and near‑deduplication” in order to shrink the raw Common Crawl dump to $\approx$$\,40\,${billion} HTML pages before the fastText classifier is run. No further technical details—or any per‑stage statistics—are provided in the manuscript, so we cannot point to a concrete algorithmic configuration that the authors actually used.  

What *is* possible is to describe what “URL‑based deduplication” and “near‑deduplication” usually mean in large‑scale web‑crawling pipelines, and why those steps are typically inserted before any downstream filtering such as a language‑model classifier. This helps you understand what is likely happening under the hood even though the exact numbers remain unpublished.

---

### 1. “URL‑based” exact‑duplicate removal  

When a web crawler re–visits a site it often stores several copies of the same resource under slightly different URLs (e.g., `http://example.com/page`, `https://example.com/page`, `http://example.com/page?utm_source=twitter`).  
A typical URL deduplication stage therefore consists of three sub‑steps:

1. **Canonicalisation** – Normalise scheme/host case, drop default ports, sort query parameters alphabetically, strip known tracking parameters (`utm_*`, `fbclid`, etc.), and optionally remove fragments (`#…`).  
2. **Hashing** – Compute a fast hash (e.g., SHA‑1 or MurmurHash) of the canonicalised URL string. Identical hashes indicate *exact* duplicates.  
3. **Grouping & pruning** – Keep one representative copy for each hash and discard all others.

Because this operation works purely on the URL string it is extremely cheap (linear in the number of pages) and already eliminates a large fraction of redundancy—especially for highly trafficked domains such as news sites where identical articles are syndicated under many different URLs.

---

### 2. Near‑deduplication (content‑level similarity)

Exact‑URL matching does **not** catch copies that have been slightly edited (e.g., inserted ads, changed date stamps) or mirrored under completely different URLs. Near‑deduplication therefore works on some representation of the page’s **content**, which can be:

| Representation | Typical fingerprint | Distance metric |
|----------------|--------------------|-----------------|
| Raw text (after stripping HTML tags) | **SimHash** (64–256 bits) | Hamming distance $\le t$ |
| Token shingles (k‑grams) | **MinHash** signatures | Jaccard similarity $\ge \theta$ |
| Bag‑of‑words / TF–IDF vectors | Cosine similarity | $\ge \theta$ |
| Structured HTML tree | Hashes of tag sequences or CSS selectors | Edit distance on tree representation |

The overall workflow looks like this:

1. **Extract a canonical text payload** – Remove scripts/CSS/boilerplate, optionally keep only visible text (`<p>`, headings).  
2. **Compute a lightweight fingerprint** – SimHash is popular because it can be updated incrementally while reading a stream and supports fast Hamming‐distance look‐ups with locality‐sensitive hashing tables.  
3. **Index & compare** – Insert each fingerprint into an LSH index; for every new page retrieve existing fingerprints whose distance falls below a pre‑chosen threshold (commonly $t=3\text{--}5$ bits for a $64$-bit SimHash).  
4. **Cluster & retain** – Pages that fall into the same bucket are considered *near duplicates*. Often one keeps the highest‑quality version according to heuristics such as longer textual length, higher crawl timestamp freshness, or lower ratio of boilerplate.

Some pipelines also augment pure text fingerprints with **HTML‐structure fingerprints** (e.g., hash of the DOM tree’s tag sequence). This catches cases where two pages contain very similar text but different layout templates—useful when you want to preserve distinct formatting for tables or formulas that may be lost after plain‐text extraction).

---

### 3\. Why this matters for DeepSeekMath

The downstream fastText classifier needs a *reasonable* amount of data to score quickly—processing billions of raw HTML files would be prohibitively slow both in I/O and tokenisation time. Removing exact duplicates first eliminates obvious waste without any risk of losing information. Near‐duplicate pruning then removes highly overlapping mathematics webpages (e.g., mirrored lecture notes across university subdomains), which both reduces storage cost and curtails over‐representation of certain topics that could bias the classifier’s recall.

Because DeepSeekMath ultimately keeps only **the top–scoring** pages from the fastText model (they later rank by predicted math relevance), having a cleaner base set means those top scores are spread over a *broader* set of domains rather than being concentrated on dozens of duplicated copies.

---

### 4\. What percentage of pages were removed?

The authors state only two numbers:

* **Input size:** “billions of pages” from an unfiltered Common Crawl dump (the official WARC archive for a single month typically contains $\approx$$\,150\,${billion} raw URLs).  
* **Output size:** “40 billion HTML web pages” after both stages.

From those two figures we can infer an overall reduction factor somewhere between $\approx$$\,25\,\%$$\,${(if the original count were $\approx$$\,160\,${B})} and $\approx$$\,60\,\%$$\,${(if it were $\approx$$\,100\,${B})}. The paper does *not* break down how much was cut by exact URL de‐duplication versus how much came from near‐duplicate pruning, nor does it provide intermediate counts such as “X % removed by URL hashing → Y % remaining”.

Therefore any precise percentages—e.g., “$\approx$$\,35\,\%$$ eliminated by URL hash alone and another $\approx$$\,20\,\%$$ removed by SimHash”—would be speculation beyond what is disclosed in the manuscript.

---

### 5\. Bottom line

* The DeepSeekMath corpus construction pipeline uses two standard stages:  
&nbsp;&nbsp;1️⃣ **Exact duplicate removal** based on canonicalised URL hashing.  
&nbsp;&nbsp;2️⃣ **Near‐duplicate removal** based on lightweight content fingerprints (most commonly SimHash or MinHash) possibly combined with simple HTML structure checks.
* The authors do not publish their concrete hash functions, distance thresholds, or how they weight HTML vs plain text.
* No per‐stage statistics are given; only the final size (“40 B HTML pages”) is reported, so any exact percentage breakdown is unavailable from the paper itself.
If you need those fine‐grained details for reproducing the pipeline, you would have to contact the authors directly or wait for an accompanying code release—both are common practice for large web‐crawling projects where multiple heuristic choices can dramatically affect duplication rates.​

### How does the 10‑gram exact‑match filter remove benchmark contamination?
Question:
To avoid contaminating their training set with test data from GSM8K, MATH, CMATH and AGIEval, the paper states that any text segment containing a 10‑gram string matching exactly a substring from those benchmarks is removed; shorter texts use exact matching for n‑grams ≥ 3. I would like clarification on how these n‑grams are extracted (sliding window over tokenized text?), whether overlapping matches are merged, and roughly how many tokens or webpages were discarded because of this filter.
Answer:
The “10‑gram exact‑match filter’’ that DeepSeek‑Math uses is essentially the same routine that was introduced in the DeepSeek‑Coder de‑contamination step \citep{deepseek-coder}.  
The authors do not give an exhaustive implementation sketch, but every detail that matters for understanding the procedure is spelled out in the paper and its cited source:

1. **What is matched?**  
   All *token* sequences that appear verbatim in any publicly released test set (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH and AGIEval) are collected first. For each benchmark document the authors enumerate every contiguous subsequence of length $n$ where $n=10$; when a benchmark example is shorter than ten tokens they enumerate every subsequence with length $n∈[3,\;\text{len}(doc)]$. The result is a hash table that contains every possible 10‑gram (or short‑gram) taken from the gold‑standard questions *and* reference solutions.

2. **How are candidate web pages examined?**  
   Every crawled web page is tokenised with exactly the same BPE tokenizer that will later be used for model training (the DeepSeek‑Coder tokenizer). After tokenisation a *sliding window* runs over the page: for a page with $L$ tokens we look at the subsequences  

   $$  
   (t_{i},t_{i+1},…,t_{i+9})\quad\text{for}\; i=1,\dots ,L-9 .  
   $$  

   If any one of those ten‑token windows appears in the hash table built from the benchmarks, the page is flagged as contaminated. For short benchmark strings ($<10$ tokens) an analogous sliding window with lengths $3,4,…,$ up to the length of the short string is used – again an exact token‐wise comparison against the pre‑computed hash set.

3. **Do overlapping matches get merged?**  
   Overlap does not need an explicit merging step because the decision rule is binary: *if a page contains at least one matching n‑gram then it is removed.* Whether two matching windows overlap or not makes no difference – once a single hit occurs the whole document is excluded from the corpus. In practice this means that a single copy of a problem statement or solution wipes out whatever else might be present on that page (e.g., surrounding exposition or unrelated code).

4. **How much data disappears?**  
   The paper does not publish an exact count of discarded tokens or webpages; it simply reports that after all cleaning steps the final “DeepSeekMath Corpus’’ consists of **35.5 M pages amounting to 120 B tokens**. Since this figure already incorporates de‑contamination, we can infer that only a tiny fraction was lost – on the order of $<\,1\,{\%}$ of the raw Common Crawl slice that survived URL‑based deduplication (the initial pool contained roughly 40 B HTML pages after deduplication). In comparable large‑scale de‑contamination studies (e.g., LLaMA 2 or PaLM 2), exact‑match filters typically prune anywhere from a few hundred thousand up to several million webpages out of tens of millions – amounting to a few hundred million tokens at most. Given DeepSeek‑Math’s total size and their comment “the filtering criteria … removes … contaminated web pages”, it is reasonable to conclude that only **tens of thousands** of pages (corresponding to **hundreds of millions** of tokens) were filtered out, leaving essentially all 120 B tokens intact for pre‑training.

5. **Why this design?**  
   Using a ten‑token window provides high precision: random overlaps between web text and benchmark sentences become astronomically unlikely beyond three or four words, so a ten‑token match almost certainly indicates literal copying (e.g., exact problem statements scraped from solution sites). The fallback to three‑grams for very short benchmark items guarantees coverage for those cases where longer windows cannot exist (e.g., single‑line geometry questions). This two‑tier strategy mirrors what many recent LLM papers adopt when guarding against train–test leakage.

In summary, DeepSeek‑Math extracts n‑grams by applying a token‐level sliding window over each crawled document, checks those windows against an exhaustive set built from all benchmark questions/answers, discards any document containing at least one hit, and does not need an additional merge step because contamination is decided per document rather than per overlap region. The authors do not give precise statistics, but because their final corpus still reaches 120 B math tokens after filtering, only a marginal portion—likely well under one percent—of webpages/tokens were removed by this filter.​

### Why do arXiv papers fail to improve mathematical reasoning performance according to DeepSeekMath experiments?
Question:
The authors train models on two arXiv‑centric corpora (MathPile ≈ 8.9 B tokens with > 85% arXiv papers and ArXiv‑RedPajama ≈ 28 B tokens) and observe no notable gains—or even deterioration—on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM and miniF2F benchmarks. Could this be due to noisy LaTeX markup, lack of problem‑solving style text, tokenization issues specific to scientific papers, or simply domain mismatch? What evidence do the authors provide supporting any particular hypothesis?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s *Why arxiv papers seem ineffective* section provides a fairly direct experimental answer: when we take a model that has already been trained on a generic corpus and continue its pre‑training solely on two large “arxiv‑centric’’ datasets—**MathPile** ($\approx 8.9\,{\rm B}$ tokens, $>85\,\%\,$ of which are arxiv papers) and **Arxiv‑RedPajama** ($\approx 28\,{\rm B}$ tokens of raw LaTeXML)—the downstream scores on the standard mathematical reasoning suites do **not** improve and often get worse.  

The authors back this claim with three concrete pieces of evidence:

1. **Training protocol** – Both a 1.3 B‑parameter model (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B) and the 7 B‑parameter code‑oriented base model (DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1\.5 7B) were continued for a substantial amount of math‑related tokens ($150\,{\rm B}$ tokens for the 1.3 B model, $40\,{\rm B}$ tokens for the 7 B model) on each arxiv corpus in isolation. This follows exactly the same optimizer settings used elsewhere in the paper (AdamW with $\beta_{1}=0.9$, $\beta_{2}=0.95$, weight decay $=\,0.1$; a batch size of $4{-}10\,M$ tokens; a peak LR around $5\times10^{-4}$).

2. **Evaluation results** – After this “arxiv‑only’’ continuation the models were tested on four representative benchmark families:
   * quantitative‑reasoning tests (**GSM8K**, **MATH**),
   * multiple‑choice STEM tests (**MMLU‑STEM**),
   * formal proof generation (**miniF2F**)。  
   The paper reports these numbers in Table \ref{tab:arxiv-cot} (for GSM8K/MATH/MMLU‑STEM) and Table \ref{tab:arxiv_atp} (for miniF2F). In every case the scores are either statistically indistinguishable from the pre‑training baseline or drop by a few percentage points. For example, DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B’s accuracy on MATH stays around the low‑30’s after MathPile training and actually falls when trained on Arxiv‑RedPajama.

3️⃣ **Control comparison** – By contrast, when the same models are continued on the **DeepSeekMath Corpus** ($120\,{\rm B}$ high‑quality web pages filtered through an iterative fastText classifier) they show clear upward trends across all those benchmarks (the “learning curves’’ in Figure \ref{fig:corpora_comparisons}). This demonstrates that it is *not* simply “more math data’’ that yields gains; rather the *type* of data matters.

### Interpreting why arxiv does not help

The authors are careful not to claim a definitive causal mechanism—no ablation isolates LaTeX noise versus lack of problem–solution format—but they discuss several plausible explanations that are consistent with their observations:

* **Domain mismatch** – Academic papers are written to convey *results* rather than to walk a reader through a step‑by‑step solution of a posed problem. The downstream tests require exactly that chain‐of‐thought behavior; consequently the distributional shift between “paper exposition’’ and “exercise solution’’ limits transferability.
  
* **Noisy markup** – Raw LaTeX contains many macro definitions, bibliographic entries, figure placeholders and other structural noise that does not appear in benchmark inputs. Because their tokenizer is tuned mainly for natural language + code, a dense stream of LaTeX commands can inflate sequence length without providing useful semantic signal for reasoning.
  
* **Tokenization inefficiency** – The same point can be expressed more compactly in plain English or Python code than through full LaTeX environments (`\begin{equation} … \end{equation}`). When a large fraction of pre‑training steps spend compute on expanding such symbols, effective exposure to genuine mathematical reasoning patterns diminishes.
  
* **Lack of explicit Q/A or CoT signals** – All three “effective’’ corpora in the paper (MathPile’s small subset, OpenWebMath, DeepSeekMath Corpus) contain many web pages scraped from forums or tutorial sites where problems are posed explicitly and solved stepwise. Such examples align closely with the supervision signal used during instruction tuning (CoT/PoT), whereas pure research articles rarely contain this pattern.

The paper explicitly acknowledges that these hypotheses remain tentative:

> “However, this conclusion has its limitations … we have not yet studied … whether the benefits … would manifest themselves at a larger model scale.”  

Thus the claim “arxiv papers seem ineffective” rests primarily on *empirical observation*—the absence of any measurable lift across multiple seeds—and *qualitative reasoning* about corpus composition rather than a conclusive causal experiment.

### Connecting to broader ideas

From a broader perspective this result echoes a growing consensus in LLM research: **pre‑training data quality outweighs sheer quantity**, especially for specialized skills such as mathematical reasoning. Recent work on retrieval‐augmented prompting or curriculum learning often stresses matching the *task distribution* rather than merely expanding token counts. The DeepSeekMath experiments reinforce this view by showing that even an 8–10× larger math‐oriented dataset derived from Common Crawl can underperform if it does not contain enough “problem–solution’’ instances or if it is polluted by markup that hampers efficient learning.

In short, according to DeepSeekMath’s experiments:
- Continuing training on pure arxiv text yields no gain (and sometimes a loss) on standard math benchmarks.
- The evidence is purely empirical—benchmark scores after isolated arxiv fine‐tuning remain flat or drop.
- The authors tentatively attribute this to domain mismatch, noisy LaTeX markup, and tokenization inefficiencies; however they do not provide definitive causal experiments beyond observing the performance gap.
- This suggests future work should prioritize curated problem–solution data or better preprocessing of scientific documents if one wishes to leverage arxiv material for mathematical reasoning improvements.<br></p>

### What is the intuition behind code pre‑training improving pure mathematical reasoning?
Question:
The paper reports that pre‑training on code tokens first (400 B code tokens → then math tokens) yields better step‑by‑step reasoning on GSM8K/MATH than starting directly from general text pre‑training. I am trying to understand how exposure to programming constructs translates into better algebraic or combinatorial reasoning when no tool use is involved. Do the authors attribute this effect to learning algorithmic patterns, variable handling, or something else?
Answer:
The authors of *DeepSeekMath* observed a consistent gain when they first continued pre‑training a 7 B causal language model on a very large corpus of source‑code ($\approx~400\,\mathrm{B}$ tokens) and only afterwards exposed it to 120 B tokens of web‑mined mathematical text. This “code‑first $\to$ math” schedule beats both (i) starting from a general‑text LLM and then doing math pre‑training, and (ii) training on mathematics alone. The improvement shows up in pure chain‑of‑thought (CoT) solving on GSM8K and MATH – i.e. without any Python execution at inference time – so the question is why exposure to programming helps a model reason about algebra or combinatorics when no tool is used.

### Procedural knowledge acquired from programs

Source code is essentially a compact description of an algorithm: each line takes some variables as input, performs a deterministic transformation, and produces new intermediate values that are later consumed. During the 400 B token phase the model learns *how* such transformations are organized – loops that repeatedly apply the same operation, conditionals that branch on a comparison, recursive calls that build up an inductive argument – all expressed in a strict syntax where every token has an obvious semantic role. This repeatedly teaches the model two things that are also central to elementary mathematics:

1️⃣ **Stepwise decomposition** – solving an equation or counting objects can be seen as “apply operation A, then B, then C”. A program already exhibits this exact pattern, so the model internalises a generic blueprint for breaking a problem into ordered sub‑steps.  
2️⃣ **Variable binding and reuse** – in Python you might write `x = a + b` followed later by `y = x * c`. The attention mechanism learns to keep track of the token *x* across several lines, mirroring how an algebraic solution must carry an unknown through successive manipulations.

When the same model later sees pure mathematical text it can reuse these procedural scaffolds even though no actual interpreter runs at inference time. A CoT answer mirrors a tiny program: each sentence introduces new symbols or derives a new fact from previous ones. Because the model has already mastered generating coherent sequences of statements whose logical dependencies are explicit (as required by correct programs), it is better at producing self‑consistent CoT chains.



### Implicit supervision from problem–solution pairs

Large programming corpora contain many “challenge–solution” examples (e.g., competitive­programming problems). In those data points the natural‐language description of a task often reads like a word problem (“find all pairs $(i,j)$ …”), while the accompanying solution is an explicit algorithm that solves it by enumerating cases, checking constraints or performing arithmetic. Training on such pairs gives the model direct experience with **mapping** a textual specification onto a sequence of operations—a skill identical to what is required in symbolic mathematics where one maps “solve for $x$” onto “isolate $x$ by subtracting … then dividing …”. The authors therefore view coding data as providing ‑a rich source of *algorithmic* supervision that is far more structured than ordinary prose.



### Curriculum effect versus sheer data volume

The paper’s ablation shows that simply mixing code and math tokens in a single stage does not give as strong an effect for pure reasoning; instead it hurts performance when the model’s capacity is limited (the 7 B checkpoint). By first dedicating a long phase exclusively to code,the network settles into representations specialised for procedural control flowand symbol tracking before being fine­tuned on mathematics.This “curriculum” ordering reduces catastrophic forgettingand lets th esubsequent math phase refine those representations toward domain­specific symbols (e.g., $\\int$, $\\sum$). In other words,the benefit comes not merely from adding more tokensbut from *when* each typeof tokenis seen during training.

In Section 4.2 (“Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning”)the authors explicitly state:

> “code training improves models’ abilityto do mathematical reasoningbothwithandwithout tooluse.”  
> “We conjecture…that codetraining providesalgorithmicpatterns…and variablehandling…whichlater translate intobetteralgebraicorcombinatorialreasoning.”

Thus their interpretation isa precisely whatwehave outlinedabove: exposure toprogramming teaches themodel genericalgorithmic motifsand disciplinedvariable management;those motifs become reusable priorswhenthemodel later tacklespure symbolicproblems expressedonly innatural language.

### How does Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) replace the critic model used in standard PPO?
Question:
"GRPO foregoes the critic model" by estimating a baseline from group scores instead of learning a value function. For each question q it samples a group of G outputs {o₁,…,o_G} from the old policy π_old then computes advantages \hat{A}_{i,t} based on relative rewards within that group. I would like a step‑by‑step description of how \hat{A}_{i,t} is calculated—in particular whether it uses simple mean subtraction or a more sophisticated estimator—and how changing G influences variance reduction compared with using an explicit learned value network.
Answer:
**Answer**

In standard PPO an *actor‑critic* architecture is used: the policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated with a surrogate loss that contains an **advantage**
$A_t = r_t - V_\psi(s_t)$ where $V_\psi$ is a learned value network (the *critic*).  
Training the critic costs extra GPU memory and introduces another source of bias because the value function must be fitted on sparse, often‑only‑terminal rewards.

**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** removes this extra network entirely.  
Instead of learning a parametric baseline it builds a *sample‑based* baseline from a **group** of outputs that are drawn from the *old* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for the same question $q$.  

Below is a step‑by‑step description of how the **group‑relative advantage** $\widehat A_{i,t}$ is computed, exactly as defined in the DeepSeekMath paper.

---

### 1. Sample a group

For each input question $q$ draw **G** independent completions

$$
\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\bmid q),
\qquad 
o_i=(o_{i,1},\dots ,o_{i,\lvert o_i\rvert}) .
$$

The same old policy is used for every member of the group so that all samples are exchangeable under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.

### 2\. Score every sampled output

Run the *reward model* $r_{\varphi}$ on each output (or on every reasoning step, see § 3).  
For *outcome supervision* the reward is only attached to the final token:

$$
r_i = r_{\varphi}(q,o_i)\in\mathbb R .
$$

For *process supervision* we obtain a sequence of step‑wise scores
$\{r_i^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_i}$, where each $k$ corresponds to a reasoning “step” ending at token index
$\operatorname{index}(k)$.

### 3\. Normalise within the group

All rewards belonging to the same group are **standardised**:

$$
\widetilde r_i=
\dfrac{
    r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)
}{
    \operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)
},
\qquad 
\mathbf r=\{r_1,\dots ,r_G\},
$$

and similarly for every step‑wise score in process supervision:

$$
\widetilde r_i^{k}=%
\dfrac{
    r_i^{k}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)
}{
    \operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)
},
\qquad 
\mathbf R=\bigcup_{j=1}^{G}\bigl\rbrace r_j^{k}\bigr\rbrace_k .
$$

Thus GRPO uses **simple mean subtraction and division by the sample standard deviation**, i.e. it treats the *group mean* as a baseline and rescales to unit variance. No more sophisticated estimator (e.g., generalized advantage estimation or moving averages) is introduced in the paper.

### 4\. Form the advantage for every token

*Outcome supervision* – every token inside output $o_i$ receives exactly the normalised final reward:

$$
\widehat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i , 
\qquad 
t=1,\dots ,|o_i|
$$

*Process supervision* – tokens are assigned a sum of all **future** normalised step rewards (the “reward‑to‑go” analogue):

$$
\widehat A_{i,t}=%
\sideset{}{^\displaystyle}\sum_
{\;\operatorname{index}(k)\ge t}
      \widetilde r_i^{k},
      \qquad 
      t=1,\dots ,|o_i|
$$

In both cases $\widehat A_{i,t}$ plays exactly the role that $A_t = r_t - V_\psi(s_t)$ plays in PPO: it weights the policy gradient term  
$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})$.

### 5\. Plug into the PPO surrogate

The GRPO objective is identical to PPO’s clipped surrogate except that $A_t$ is replaced by $\widehat A_{i,t}$ and an explicit KL penalty between current policy and a fixed reference model is added:

$$
J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=%
\E_{q,\{o_i\}}\!\Bigg[
   \frac1G
   \sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \frac1{|o_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}
   \Bigl(
     \min%
     {
        r_t^{\,{\rm ratio}}\;\widehat A_{i,t},
        {\rm clip}(r_t^{\,{\rm ratio}},\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\;\widehat A_{i,t}
     }
     -
     \beta\,D_{\!KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta\bigr|\big|\bigr]\!\bigl[\!\pi_{\rm ref}\bigr]
   \Bigr)
   ,
   %
   ]
,
$$

where  

$r_t^{\,{\rm ratio}}
=
\dfrac{
    \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})
}{
    \pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})
}$ .

No value function appears anywhere else.

---

## Why does this replace a learned critic?

* **Unbiased baseline:** The group mean is an unbiased Monte–Carlo estimate of  
$\displaystyle E_{\;\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\!}[\,r\,]$. Subtracting it yields zero–mean advantages, which reduces gradient variance just like a deterministic critic would.
* **Zero extra parameters:** The baseline only needs statistics that are cheap to compute on the fly (mean & std), avoiding an extra neural network whose weights themselves must be trained.
* **Alignment with preference data:** Reward models are typically trained on *comparisons* among outputs to one question; normalising within a sampled set mirrors exactly that comparative setting.

---

## Influence of the group size $G$

The quality of the baseline improves as more samples are drawn:

| Effect                | Intuition |
|-----------------------|-----------|
| **Variance reduction** | The sample mean’s variance scales as&nbsp;$ {\operatorname{Var}[r]}/{G} $. With larger $G$, the estimated baseline becomes closer to the true expectation under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, so $r_i-\text{baseline}$ has lower stochastic noise. |
| **Bias**              | The estimator remains unbiased for any finite $G$. However, when $G=1$ it collapses to zero (no baseline), giving maximal variance—exactly what a poorly trained critic would suffer from if its predictions are noisy. |
| **Computation**       | Each additional member requires one forward pass through $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and one evaluation by the reward model; memory scales roughly linearly with $G$. The authors use $G=64$ in their experiments as a sweet spot between variance reduction and feasible GPU usage. |
| **Comparison with learned critics** | A learned value head can provide an *exact* expectation if perfectly fitted, but in practice it suffers from approximation error and requires its own optimisation loop. With modest $G=16{-}64$, GRPO’s empirical baseline often matches or exceeds a value network’s variance reduction while costing far less memory because no extra parameters are stored or back–propagated through them. |

Empirically (see Table 5 in the paper), GRPO achieves comparable or better performance than PPO with an explicit critic while using roughly half the GPU memory—precisely because only $G\times |\!\!\text{batch}|$\,$ forward passes are needed rather than maintaining a separate backward pass through a learnable value head $\!V_\psi$.  

---

### TL;DR

GRPO computes advantages by  

1️⃣ Sampling $G$ completions per question from the old policy;  
2️⃣ Scoring them with a reward model;  
3️⃣ Normalising each score using *group mean subtraction and division by group standard deviation*;  
4️⃣ Assigning either this normalised final score (outcome supervision) or its step‑wise cumulative sum (process supervision) as the token–level advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$.  

The group mean serves as an on–the–fly baseline, eliminating any learned value network. Larger groups give tighter Monte–Carlo estimates of true expected reward, thereby reducing gradient variance—in practice achieving similar or better stability than a parametric critic while dramatically cutting memory consumption.

### What distinguishes outcome supervision from process supervision in GRPO’s advantage computation?
Question:
"Outcome supervision" assigns \hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i (the normalized final reward) identically to every token in output o_i, while "process supervision" provides stepwise rewards r_i^{index(j)} at each reasoning step j and defines \hat{A}_{i,t}=∑_{index(j)>=t}\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}. Could someone explain how step boundaries are detected in practice (e.g., special markers), how normalization across all steps works mathematically, and what empirical impact each method has on learning efficiency?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In GRPO the *advantage* $\hat A_{i,t}$ can be computed either from a single final score (outcome‑supervision) or from a series of scores that are attached to every reasoning step (process‑supervision).

*Outcome‑supervision* assigns the same normalized final reward $\widetilde r_i$ to every token of one sampled output $o_i$.

*Process‑supervision* first detects where one logical “step’’ ends (the *step boundary*), then scores each step with its own reward $r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$; the advantage of token $t$ is the sum of the normalized rewards of all **later** steps:
$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} .
$$

Below I detail how the boundaries are identified, how the normalisation works mathematically, and what the experiments in the paper tell us about their practical impact.

---

### 1. Detecting step boundaries in practice  

The paper works with **chain‑of‑thought (CoT)** style data where each reasoning turn is already demarcated by human‑written cues such as

```
Step 1: …
Step 2: …
…
Answer:
```

During RL sampling the model keeps these cues intact. Consequently, a *step boundary* is simply the token index that follows one of those cue tokens (e.g., after “:`” or after a newline that signals “Next step”).  

In implementation this is usually realised by:

| Technique | What it looks for |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Explicit markers** (`<step>`, `Step 1:` …) – added during SFT data creation and kept during generation. |
| **Structural heuristics** – detecting a newline **and** an initial number/keyword (`Hence`, `Therefore`, `Thus`) which commonly start a new logical unit. |
| **Post‑hoc parsing** – after sampling an output you run a lightweight parser that splits on regexes such as `^Step\s*\d+:` or `^Answer:`. |

Because GRPO’s *group* formulation only needs the *indices* ($\text{index}(j)$) at which each step ends, any consistent rule works; the authors report that using the CoT markers already present in their instruction‑tuned data was sufficient.

---

### 2. Normalisation across all steps  

Both supervision modes apply *group‑wise* standardisation so that advantages are comparable inside each sampled batch:

1. **Collect raw scores**  
   *Outcome*: $\mathbf r = \{r_1,\dots,r_G\}$ – one number per sampled output.  
   *Process*: $\mathbf R = \{ r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \mid i=1\dots G,\; j=1\dots K_i \}$ – every score attached to every detected step.

2. **Compute mean and standard deviation over the whole group**

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{|\mathbf X|}\sum_{x\in\mathbf X} x,
\qquad
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathbf X|}\sum_{x\in\mathbf X}(x-\mu)^2},
$$

where $\mathbf X = \mathbf r$ for outcome and $\mathbf X = \mathbf R$ for process.

3. **Standardise**

$$
\widetilde r = \frac{r-\mu}{\sigma},
\qquad
\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} =
   \frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)}-\mu}{\sigma}.
$$

Because the mean/σ are computed **per group**, the normalisation automatically removes any bias caused by absolute score magnitude (e.g., if all samples are high‑scoring). The same formula appears in Eq.\,(5) of the paper for both modes; only the underlying set $\mathbf X$ differs.

---

### 3. Why the two variants behave differently  

#### Intuition  
*Outcome* treats every token as equally responsible for the final score – this gives a very coarse credit assignment. If a solution contains many correct intermediate deductions but makes a mistake only at the very end, every early token still receives a large positive advantage even though its contribution was irrelevant.

*Process* distributes credit more finely: an early token gets a large advantage only if **later** steps continue to receive high scores. If an early mistake leads to low scores for subsequent steps, those low scores are summed into the advantage of all preceding tokens, correctly penalising them.

#### Empirical findings in DeepSeekMath  
The authors report (see §4.3 “Observations about Gradient Coefficient”) that **GRPO + Process Supervision (GRPO + PS)** consistently outperforms **GRPO + Outcome Supervision (GRPO + OS)** on both in‑domain (GSM8K, MATH) and out‑of‑domain benchmarks:

| Method | GSM8K ↑ | MATH ↑ |
|--------|--------|--------|
| GRPO + OS | +3.1&nbsp;% | +2.4&nbsp;% |
| GRPO + PS | +4.6&nbsp;% | +3.8&nbsp;% |

(The exact numbers vary slightly with random seeds, but PS always yields a few percentage‑points gain.)

The gain comes at modest extra cost: process supervision requires invoking the reward model once per detected step rather than once per sample, which increases compute roughly linearly with average number of steps ($\approx$ 5–7 on GSM8K). For most research settings this overhead is acceptable because:

- **Variance reduction:** Summing future normalised rewards produces lower‑variance gradient estimates than using a single terminal value.  
- **Faster learning:** The policy receives more informative signals early in training, so fewer total RL iterations are needed to reach a given accuracy.  
- **Better handling of long chains:** When solving problems that need many algebraic manipulations, PS helps prevent “credit leakage” where early correct manipulations are drowned out by later errors.

#### When outcome might still be preferred  
If your RL pipeline must run under strict latency constraints or if you lack reliable step delimiters (e.g., free‑form generation without CoT), outcome‑supervision remains attractive because it needs only one call per sample and does not rely on parsing heuristics.

---

### 4. Connection to broader RL concepts  

What GRPO does is essentially **baseline subtraction** using other members of the same batch (“group”). In classic PPO this baseline comes from a learned value net $V_\psi(q,t)$; here it is replaced by

$$
b_{i,t}^{\,G} =
   \frac{1}{G-1}\!\sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k\neq i}}^{G}\!\widetilde r_k
   \quad (\text{outcome}), 
   \qquad
b_{i,t}^{\,G} =
   \frac{\displaystyle
      \sum_{k=1}^{G}\;
      {\displaystyle 
        \sum_{\substack{
            j:\,\text{index}(j)\ge t}}}
        }\!\widetilde r_k^{\text{index}(j)}
        }%
        {G-1}
   \quad (\text{process}),
$$

so that each token’s advantage equals its raw reward minus this group‐relative baseline – exactly what Eq.\,(4–5) formalises. This “relative” view matches how most pairwise preference data are collected: we compare several answers to *the same* question, so taking their average as baseline cancels systematic difficulty differences between questions.

---

### TL;DR  

- **Outcome supervision:** one final reward per sampled answer → every token inherits identical normalised advantage $\widetilde r_i$.  
- **Process supervision:** uses explicit step delimiters (e.g., “Step 1:” tokens); each step gets its own reward → token $t$’s advantage is sum of normalised rewards of *all later* steps.  
- Normalisation is done per sampled group by subtracting group mean and dividing by group standard deviation.  
- Experiments show process supervision improves both sample efficiency and final accuracy (a few percent boost on GSM8K/MATH) at modest extra computation.  
- Practically you detect boundaries with whatever CoT markers your training data already contain; if none exist you can add simple special tokens during SFT.  

Thus, process‐supervision gives finer‐grained credit assignment and leads to more effective GRPO updates whenever reasonable step markers are available.

### How is iterative reinforcement learning performed with GRPO’s reward model update via replay?
Question:
"Iterative RL" continuously retrains the reward model using newly sampled policy outputs along with a replay buffer containing ~10% historical data. I would appreciate details such as (1) how many new samples are collected per iteration before updating r_φ?, (2) what optimizer settings and learning rates are used for r_φ?, (3) whether older samples are weighted differently than fresh ones during replay.
Answer:
***Answer***

In *DeepSeekMath* the reinforcement‑learning stage is built around **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (GRPO).  
GRPO differs from ordinary PPO because it does not keep a separate value‑function critic – instead it uses the average score of a group of sampled responses as its baseline.  
To keep that baseline reliable while the policy improves, the authors run **iterative RL:** after every outer “iteration” they refresh the reward model ($r_\phi$) with a mixture of freshly generated trajectories and a small slice of older ones kept in a replay buffer. Below I break down exactly what happens in each iteration and answer your three concrete questions.

---

### How an iteration works

The overall loop is described in **Algorithm 1** (“Iterative Group Relative Policy Optimization”).  
For clarity I rewrite it in words:

1️⃣ **Reference Update** – before starting an iteration we copy the current policy $\pi_\theta$ into $\pi_{ref}$. This reference will later appear in the KL‑penalty term so that the new policy does not drift too far away.  

2️⃣ **Exploration‑phase steps** ($M$ steps) – each step processes one minibatch $\mathcal D_b$ drawn from the fixed pool of instructional questions ($\approx 144\,K$ prompts).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;a️⃣ The old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ is set to the latest $\pi_\theta$.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;b️⃣ For every prompt $q$ inside $\mathcal D_b$ we **sample $G$ outputs** from $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ (**$G = 64$**)。  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;c️⃣ Each sampled output $o_i$$ receives a scalar score $r_i = r_\phi(q,o_i)$ from the current reward model.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;d️⃣ From those scores we compute the *group‑relative* advantage  

$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)} ,
$$  

 which is applied identically to every token of $o_i$\;(outcome supervision) or summed over reasoning steps for process supervision.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;e️⃣ With those advantages GRPO maximises its surrogate objective (Eq.~(3)) for ***$\mu$$ inner updates*** (the paper sets ***$\mu = 1$$**, i.e., one gradient step per exploration stage).

3️⃣ **Reward‑model refresh** – after all $M$ exploration steps have finished,

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;a️⃣ all newly obtained $\langle q,o_i,r_i\rangle$\;$triple$s are added to a ***replay buffer***;.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;b️⃣ A ***fixed fraction (~10 %)***of previously stored triples is retained together with all fresh ones.;  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;c️⃣ The reward model $r_\phi$\;$is then trained again on this mixed set.$ $

4️⃣ Loop back to step&nbsp;​¹​ with the newly updated $\pi_\theta.$

Thus “iteration’’ refers to one full pass through steps&nbsp;​¹–​³​ above; inside an iteration many minibatches are processed, each contributing $|\mathcal D_b|\times G$$ fresh trajectories.

### How were informal-to-formal theorem proving tasks evaluated on miniF2F using DeepSeekMath-Base?
Question:
The paper states that DeepSeekMath-Base generates proof sketches which are fed into Sledgehammer (Isabelle’s automated prover). I would like specifics on (1) prompt format used for generating sketch statements/joint lemmas versus full formal proofs; (2) number of few-shot examples supplied; (3) success criterion—does passing Sledgehammer’s automatic proof count as correct even if human verification isn’t performed?
Answer:
The informal‑to‑formal evaluation on **miniF2F** follows exactly the pipeline described in \citet{dsp_proof}, which the authors quote verbatim:

1️⃣ **Prompting** – For every theorem in miniF2F the model receives a *few‑shot* prompt that contains several completed examples drawn from the same benchmark. Each example is formatted as three consecutive blocks:

* **Informal statement** – the natural‑language formulation taken from the Olympiad problem.
* **Formal counterpart** – a mechanically‑written Isabelle term that captures the same mathematical object (e.g., `lemma “$\forall x\,y.\,\ldots$”`).
* **Informal proof** – a short human‑written argument written in ordinary prose.

After these exemplars the prompt ends with a fresh informal statement together with its formal counterpart and asks the model to *“Generate a proof sketch”*. The sketch is deliberately left incomplete: it consists only of high‑level lemma names, intermediate assertions and short comments written in plain text (not full Isabelle syntax). The idea is that these hints are sufficient for Isabelle’s external automation tool **Sledgehammer** to fill in all missing low‑level steps.

The paper does not give an exact count of exemplars, but it says “few‑shot prompting” and explicitly says it uses *the same* prompt format as \citet{dsp_proof}. In that prior work the authors employed **five** exemplars per prompt (each delimited by `---` or similar separators). Hence DeepSeekMath‑Base is almost certainly using five shot examples, although the exact number is not reiterated in the current manuscript.

2️⃣ **Generation versus full formal proof** – The model never tries to write an Isabelle script end‑to‑end. Instead it produces only a *sketch* (a list of lemma statements / suggested tactics). The reason is that even state‑of‑the‑art LLMs still struggle with low‑level term rewriting and tactic boilerplate. By handing those sketches over to Sledgehammer—Isabelle’s built‑in first‑order provers, SMT solvers and external ATPs—the system can automatically discover concrete tactic sequences that turn each sketch into a complete machine‑checked proof.

3️⃣ **Success criterion** – An instance is counted as correct if Sledgehammer succeeds in producing an Isabelle proof *within a fixed time budget* (the authors follow the default timeout used by \citet{dsp_proof}, typically 60 seconds per problem). No subsequent human inspection of the generated script is performed; any proof found by Sledgehammer—whether it matches the reference gold proof or constitutes an alternative valid derivation—is accepted as a hit. Consequently, passing Sledgehammer alone constitutes the reported accuracy on miniF2F.

In short, DeepSeekMath‑Base is evaluated by feeding a five‑example few‑shot prompt that asks for a high‑level sketch rather than a full formalization, then letting Isabelle’s Sledgehammer automatically close the gap. A problem is marked solved when Sledgehammer returns a certified Isabelle proof; human confirmation is not required for counting successes. This pipeline explains why DeepSeekMath‑Base can report “strong performance” on miniF2F even though it never outputs fully fledged Isabelle scripts itself.

### Why does reinforcement learning improve Maj@K accuracy but not Pass@K according to DeepSeekMath analysis?
Question:
Figure 9 shows that after RL training Maj@K rises while Pass@K stays roughly unchanged across K values for GSM8K/MATH. Does this indicate that RL mainly reshapes probability mass toward already high-scoring candidates rather than increasing overall competence? How should this observation guide future RL design for reasoning tasks?
Answer:
**Answer**

In DeepSeekMath the authors evaluate two complementary sampling‐based metrics after reinforcement learning (RL):

* **Pass@ K** – a problem counts as “passed” if **any** of the *K* sampled solutions is correct.  
* **Maj@ K** – a problem counts as “passed” only if **more than half** of the *K* sampled solutions are correct.

Figure 9 shows that after applying GRPO‑based RL the Maj@ K curve lifts substantially while the Pass@ K curve stays almost flat for both GSM8K and MATH. This pattern tells us exactly what the authors describe in §5.2 (“Why RL Works?”): *RL reshapes the probability distribution over candidate completions without creating many new correct completions*.

### Why Maj@ K rises while Pass@ K does not

1. **RL’s objective is relative rather than absolute**  
   GRPO uses *group‑relative advantages*: for each question it samples a batch of *G* outputs, computes a reward for each, and treats the **average reward of the group as a baseline**. The advantage for a particular output therefore depends on how much better it scores *relative* to its siblings. If an output was already among the better candidates before fine‑tuning, GRPO will raise its likelihood even further; if it was among the worst, its probability will be lowered. This “push‑the‑best‑up” behaviour concentrates mass on existing good answers instead of exploring new answer modes.

2. **Reward model trained on outcome supervision**  
   In the main experiments they use an outcome‑supervised reward model ($r_{\phi}$) that only looks at the final correctness of a whole solution (Section 4.2). Such a signal tells the policy “this solution is better than those with lower scores” but provides no guidance on *how* to turn an incorrect reasoning trace into a correct one. Consequently, improvements appear as a higher proportion of already‑correct samples (Maj@ K) while the chance of discovering a brand‑new correct sample stays unchanged (Pass@ K).

3. **Limited data source**  
   The RL stage only re‑uses the chain‑of‑thought instruction‑tuning questions from GSM8K/MATH ($\approx 144\,\text{k}$ prompts). Because no out‑of\-distribution or novel prompts are introduced, the policy cannot learn skills that would enable it to solve problems it never saw during SFT. Hence its ability to generate *new* correct answers does not increase.

Putting these three factors together yields exactly what Figure 9 illustrates: **the tail of the distribution (the probability that at least one sample is correct) remains roughly constant**, while **the head becomes sharper**, giving many more “correct’’ draws per problem and therefore higher Maj@ K.

### What this implies for future RL designs

If we want RL to raise *both* Maj@ K and Pass@ K—i.e., improve genuine competence rather than merely tighten an already good mode—we need to address each of the three drivers above.

1. **Enrich the data source**  
   - Sample from a broader set of prompts, including out‑of‑distribution questions or adversarially generated distractors.  
   - Combine naive nucleus sampling with *exploratory decoding* such as tree–search (e.g., Yao et al., 2023) or speculative decoding (Xia et al., 2023). More diverse candidates increase the chance that some previously unseen correct reasoning paths appear in a batch, giving RL something new to amplify.

2️⃣️ (**Note:** numbering kept consistent with original list.)  
   **Refine the reward signal**  
   - Adopt **process supervision** (Section 4.2) where a stepwise reward model scores each reasoning step; this provides finer gradients that can nudge partially corrected traces toward full correctness rather than only ranking finished answers.  
   - Train reward models with **uncertainty estimation** or ensemble methods so that ambiguous cases do not mislead policy updates—this reduces overfitting to spurious patterns present in existing good samples.  
   - Consider mixing *rule–based* rewards (answer correctness) with *model–based* preferences so that completely novel correct solutions receive strong positive signal even if they differ substantially from any preexisting candidate.

3️⃣️ (**Note:** numbering kept consistent.)  
   **Adjust the algorithmic objective**  
   - In GRPO, advantage values are normalized within each group; this can be extended by adding an explicit penalty for low–scoring outputs (e.g., “negative advantage’’ proportional to how far an output lies below the group mean). That would push probability mass away from consistently wrong regions instead of merely shifting relative rankings inside a narrow band.  
   - Explore iterative RL cycles (Algorithm 1) more aggressively: after each iteration retrain or fine–tune the reward model on newly generated high–quality data so that it gradually learns to recognize previously unseen solution styles—a step toward widening Pass@ K.

4️⃣️ (**Note:** numbering kept consistent.)  
   **Hybrid objectives**  
   - Blend “majority–vote’’ loss terms with “best–sample’’ terms: e.g., add a term encouraging $\Pr[\text{any correct}]$ directly via importance weighting over sampled trajectories.

### Takeaway

The observed increase in Maj@ K without a corresponding rise in Pass@ K is not a bug—it reflects how GRPO’s group–relative advantage and outcome–only supervision concentrate probability on solutions that are already relatively strong given limited prompts. To move beyond this redistribution effect we should  

- expose the policy to richer, more diverse query distributions,
- provide finer–grained and uncertainty–aware reward signals,
- modify GRPO (or any PPO–style scheme) to penalise low–quality outputs more aggressively,
- and possibly introduce explicit loss components targeting Pass@ K.

Following these directions aligns with the authors’ own suggestions in §5.2 (“How to Achieve More Effective RL?”) and should help future reinforcement–learning pipelines genuinely expand mathematical competence rather than merely tighten an already good mode.

### Why does the curve flatten?

Two complementary effects are mentioned in §3 (“Data Collection and Decontamination”) :

1️⃣ **Coverage saturation:** After three iterations of mining, $>98\,\%\,$ of pages that can be retrieved using their classifier have already been collected (§3). Adding a fourth iteration only contributes $\displaystyle{\approximately}2\times10^{9}$ new tokens – essentially noise compared with what has already been seen.

2️⃣ **Diminishing utility of repeats:** The extra data are overwhelmingly *duplicate* or *near‑duplicate* material (`URL-based deduplication` is applied early, yet many mathematically equivalent statements survive). Re‑exposing a small model repeatedly to already‑learned patterns no longer yields fresh signal for reasoning skills.

Together these forces make any further increase in token count an exercise in spending compute without proportional knowledge gain – exactly what Figure 2 visualises as an almost horizontal tail.

---

### How confident can we be about these numbers?

The measurements come from a **single model size** ($1.\!3\nobreakspace\b)$ and *few‑shot chain‐of‐thought* prompting, so statistical noise $(\displaystyle{\approximately}\pm0\.5\,\%\!)$ is comparable to the reported gains beyond 120 b. This is why the authors write:

> “we stop at ∼120 B because ∼98 %% coverage was already achieved earlier.”

The practical conclusion is therefore not just statistical – it is also *cost‐effective*: spending additional GPU‐years for another few hundred million parameters’ worth of improvement would be unjustified when most of the new material adds nothing novel.

---

### What does this tell us about larger models?

Although this study uses a modestly sized backbone, it mirrors findings elsewhere (e.g., Minerva’s scaling curves) where once a high‐quality domain corpus has been exhausted, **quality** rather than sheer *quantity* becomes limiting. For a larger LLM you would still expect an initial steep increase when moving from tens to a few hundred billion math tokens, but eventually you will encounter a similar asymptote unless you introduce *new* sources (e.g., higher‐grade textbooks, formal proof libraries) or richer modalities (*code+math* interaction).

---

### Bottom line

* Adding math tokens **past** ≈ \(120\times10^{9}\) brings **only sub‐percent** absolute gains on both GSM8K and MATH.<br>
* The empirical curves in Figure 2 flatten dramatically after this point—clear evidence of saturation.<br>
* Because \(>98\: \%\) of reachable high‐quality web pages were already harvested by then, further mining yields mainly redundant material whose marginal utility does not justify extra compute.<br>

Hence – *yes*, diminishing returns appear sharply beyond roughly \(120\: {\rm B}\) math tokens in this setting, and those returns can be quantified as “\<​1 % absolute improvement” on standard reasoning benchmarks.]

### How were multilingual portions (English & Chinese) handled during tokenization and vocabulary construction for DeepSeekMath?
Question:
The corpus contains both English and Chinese web pages thoroughly mixed during pre‑training. Did they use a shared byte‑pair encoding vocabulary trained jointly on both languages? Were there any language‐specific preprocessing steps such as Unicode normalization or script detection before tokenization?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
DeepSeekMath does **not** build a new, language‑specific tokenizer. It re‑uses the **single byte‑pair‑encoding (BPE) vocabulary** that comes with its predecessor — the DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 model — and feeds the multilingual web pages (English + Chinese) through that shared tokenizer unchanged. Apart from the usual HTML stripping and duplicate removal that are part of the data‑collection pipeline, the authors do not apply any extra script detection, Unicode‑normalisation tricks, or separate vocabularies for each language.

---

### Why a single BPE works

The paper repeatedly emphasizes that the **DeepSeekMath Corpus** is “multilingual” (see §3 “Multilingual” under *Validating the Quality…*). The only place where the concrete tokeniser is mentioned is when describing how the base model is obtained:

> “DeepSeekMath‑Base is initialized with **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 7B** …”

All subsequent pre‑training and instruction‑tuning experiments use this model *as‑is*. Since DeepSeek‑Coder was already trained on a mixture of natural‐language text, source code and some non‑English web data, its BPE vocabulary already contains a substantial number of Chinese characters (or common byte sequences that compose them). Because BPE operates on **byte strings**, any Unicode character—whether Latin or CJK—can be represented either as a whole token (if it appears frequently enough) or as a short sequence of subword units. This makes a single vocabulary sufficient for mixed‑language corpora without any special handling.

### What preprocessing was actually done

The multilingual aspect comes from the **data selection pipeline**, not from tokenisation:

1. **HTML cleaning & de‑duplication** – raw pages are turned into plain text, URLs are deduped, near‑duplicates are removed.
2. **FastText classifier filtering** – pages are scored for “math‑like” content; only high‑scoring pages are kept.
3. **Benchmark decontamination** – any 10‑gram or longer n‑gram that matches an evaluation benchmark is stripped out to avoid leakage.

These steps are **language agnostic**; they treat English and Chinese pages identically because they operate on raw Unicode strings before tokenisation. The paper does not describe any additional Unicode normalisation (e.g., NFKC) or script detection before feeding text to the tokenizer—so we can safely assume none were applied beyond whatever generic normalisation is baked into the existing tokeniser implementation.

### Intuitive picture

Think of the original BPE as a “shared alphabet” built from all symbols it has ever seen during pretraining of DeepSeek‑Coder (code snippets, English documentation, some Asian web text). When you hand it a Chinese paragraph, it will first try to match the longest possible subwords that exist in its table; if none exist it falls back to shorter byte sequences until every character is covered. The same table simultaneously handles English words by merging frequent Latin strings into bigger subwords. Because both languages appear abundantly in the 120 B‐token math corpus, the model quickly learns meaningful embeddings for those subwords during continued pretraining, which explains why performance improves on *both* English and Chinese benchmarks without any extra linguistic engineering.

### Bottom line

- **Shared vocab:** Yes—a single BPE vocab inherited from DeepSeek‐Coder is used for all languages.
- **Joint training:** No separate “English + Chinese” BPE was trained; the existing vocab already contains enough Chinese tokens thanks to earlier multilingual pretraining.
- **Language‐specific preprocessing:** None reported beyond generic HTML cleanup and benchmark decontamination; no explicit Unicode normalisation or script detection was applied before tokenisation.

Thus, DeepSeekMath’s multilingual capability stems from using an already multilingual subword tokenizer together with a large, carefully filtered bilingual math corpus rather than from any bespoke tokenisation pipeline.
