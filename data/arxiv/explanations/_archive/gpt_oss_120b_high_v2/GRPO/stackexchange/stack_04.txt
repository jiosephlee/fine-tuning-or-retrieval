\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why do arXiv papers fail to improve mathematical reasoning performance according to DeepSeekMath experiments?
Question:
The authors train models on two arXiv‑centric corpora (MathPile ≈ 8.9 B tokens with > 85% arXiv papers and ArXiv‑RedPajama ≈ 28 B tokens) and observe no notable gains—or even deterioration—on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM and miniF2F benchmarks. Could this be due to noisy LaTeX markup, lack of problem‑solving style text, tokenization issues specific to scientific papers, or simply domain mismatch? What evidence do the authors provide supporting any particular hypothesis?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s *Why arxiv papers seem ineffective* section provides a fairly direct experimental answer: when we take a model that has already been trained on a generic corpus and continue its pre‑training solely on two large “arxiv‑centric’’ datasets—**MathPile** ($\approx 8.9\,{\rm B}$ tokens, $>85\,\%\,$ of which are arxiv papers) and **Arxiv‑RedPajama** ($\approx 28\,{\rm B}$ tokens of raw LaTeXML)—the downstream scores on the standard mathematical reasoning suites do **not** improve and often get worse.  

The authors back this claim with three concrete pieces of evidence:

1. **Training protocol** – Both a 1.3 B‑parameter model (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B) and the 7 B‑parameter code‑oriented base model (DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1\.5 7B) were continued for a substantial amount of math‑related tokens ($150\,{\rm B}$ tokens for the 1.3 B model, $40\,{\rm B}$ tokens for the 7 B model) on each arxiv corpus in isolation. This follows exactly the same optimizer settings used elsewhere in the paper (AdamW with $\beta_{1}=0.9$, $\beta_{2}=0.95$, weight decay $=\,0.1$; a batch size of $4{-}10\,M$ tokens; a peak LR around $5\times10^{-4}$).

2. **Evaluation results** – After this “arxiv‑only’’ continuation the models were tested on four representative benchmark families:
   * quantitative‑reasoning tests (**GSM8K**, **MATH**),
   * multiple‑choice STEM tests (**MMLU‑STEM**),
   * formal proof generation (**miniF2F**)。  
   The paper reports these numbers in Table \ref{tab:arxiv-cot} (for GSM8K/MATH/MMLU‑STEM) and Table \ref{tab:arxiv_atp} (for miniF2F). In every case the scores are either statistically indistinguishable from the pre‑training baseline or drop by a few percentage points. For example, DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B’s accuracy on MATH stays around the low‑30’s after MathPile training and actually falls when trained on Arxiv‑RedPajama.

3️⃣ **Control comparison** – By contrast, when the same models are continued on the **DeepSeekMath Corpus** ($120\,{\rm B}$ high‑quality web pages filtered through an iterative fastText classifier) they show clear upward trends across all those benchmarks (the “learning curves’’ in Figure \ref{fig:corpora_comparisons}). This demonstrates that it is *not* simply “more math data’’ that yields gains; rather the *type* of data matters.

### Interpreting why arxiv does not help

The authors are careful not to claim a definitive causal mechanism—no ablation isolates LaTeX noise versus lack of problem–solution format—but they discuss several plausible explanations that are consistent with their observations:

* **Domain mismatch** – Academic papers are written to convey *results* rather than to walk a reader through a step‑by‑step solution of a posed problem. The downstream tests require exactly that chain‐of‐thought behavior; consequently the distributional shift between “paper exposition’’ and “exercise solution’’ limits transferability.
  
* **Noisy markup** – Raw LaTeX contains many macro definitions, bibliographic entries, figure placeholders and other structural noise that does not appear in benchmark inputs. Because their tokenizer is tuned mainly for natural language + code, a dense stream of LaTeX commands can inflate sequence length without providing useful semantic signal for reasoning.
  
* **Tokenization inefficiency** – The same point can be expressed more compactly in plain English or Python code than through full LaTeX environments (`\begin{equation} … \end{equation}`). When a large fraction of pre‑training steps spend compute on expanding such symbols, effective exposure to genuine mathematical reasoning patterns diminishes.
  
* **Lack of explicit Q/A or CoT signals** – All three “effective’’ corpora in the paper (MathPile’s small subset, OpenWebMath, DeepSeekMath Corpus) contain many web pages scraped from forums or tutorial sites where problems are posed explicitly and solved stepwise. Such examples align closely with the supervision signal used during instruction tuning (CoT/PoT), whereas pure research articles rarely contain this pattern.

The paper explicitly acknowledges that these hypotheses remain tentative:

> “However, this conclusion has its limitations … we have not yet studied … whether the benefits … would manifest themselves at a larger model scale.”  

Thus the claim “arxiv papers seem ineffective” rests primarily on *empirical observation*—the absence of any measurable lift across multiple seeds—and *qualitative reasoning* about corpus composition rather than a conclusive causal experiment.

### Connecting to broader ideas

From a broader perspective this result echoes a growing consensus in LLM research: **pre‑training data quality outweighs sheer quantity**, especially for specialized skills such as mathematical reasoning. Recent work on retrieval‐augmented prompting or curriculum learning often stresses matching the *task distribution* rather than merely expanding token counts. The DeepSeekMath experiments reinforce this view by showing that even an 8–10× larger math‐oriented dataset derived from Common Crawl can underperform if it does not contain enough “problem–solution’’ instances or if it is polluted by markup that hampers efficient learning.

In short, according to DeepSeekMath’s experiments:
- Continuing training on pure arxiv text yields no gain (and sometimes a loss) on standard math benchmarks.
- The evidence is purely empirical—benchmark scores after isolated arxiv fine‐tuning remain flat or drop.
- The authors tentatively attribute this to domain mismatch, noisy LaTeX markup, and tokenization inefficiencies; however they do not provide definitive causal experiments beyond observing the performance gap.
- This suggests future work should prioritize curated problem–solution data or better preprocessing of scientific documents if one wishes to leverage arxiv material for mathematical reasoning improvements.<br></p>
