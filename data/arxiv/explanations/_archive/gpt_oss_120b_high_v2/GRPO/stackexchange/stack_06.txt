\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) replace the critic model used in standard PPO?
Question:
"GRPO foregoes the critic model" by estimating a baseline from group scores instead of learning a value function. For each question q it samples a group of G outputs {o₁,…,o_G} from the old policy π_old then computes advantages \hat{A}_{i,t} based on relative rewards within that group. I would like a step‑by‑step description of how \hat{A}_{i,t} is calculated—in particular whether it uses simple mean subtraction or a more sophisticated estimator—and how changing G influences variance reduction compared with using an explicit learned value network.
Answer:
**Answer**

In standard PPO an *actor‑critic* architecture is used: the policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated with a surrogate loss that contains an **advantage**
$A_t = r_t - V_\psi(s_t)$ where $V_\psi$ is a learned value network (the *critic*).  
Training the critic costs extra GPU memory and introduces another source of bias because the value function must be fitted on sparse, often‑only‑terminal rewards.

**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** removes this extra network entirely.  
Instead of learning a parametric baseline it builds a *sample‑based* baseline from a **group** of outputs that are drawn from the *old* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for the same question $q$.  

Below is a step‑by‑step description of how the **group‑relative advantage** $\widehat A_{i,t}$ is computed, exactly as defined in the DeepSeekMath paper.

---

### 1. Sample a group

For each input question $q$ draw **G** independent completions

$$
\{o_1,\dots ,o_G\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\bmid q),
\qquad 
o_i=(o_{i,1},\dots ,o_{i,\lvert o_i\rvert}) .
$$

The same old policy is used for every member of the group so that all samples are exchangeable under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.

### 2\. Score every sampled output

Run the *reward model* $r_{\varphi}$ on each output (or on every reasoning step, see § 3).  
For *outcome supervision* the reward is only attached to the final token:

$$
r_i = r_{\varphi}(q,o_i)\in\mathbb R .
$$

For *process supervision* we obtain a sequence of step‑wise scores
$\{r_i^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_i}$, where each $k$ corresponds to a reasoning “step” ending at token index
$\operatorname{index}(k)$.

### 3\. Normalise within the group

All rewards belonging to the same group are **standardised**:

$$
\widetilde r_i=
\dfrac{
    r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)
}{
    \operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)
},
\qquad 
\mathbf r=\{r_1,\dots ,r_G\},
$$

and similarly for every step‑wise score in process supervision:

$$
\widetilde r_i^{k}=%
\dfrac{
    r_i^{k}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)
}{
    \operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)
},
\qquad 
\mathbf R=\bigcup_{j=1}^{G}\bigl\rbrace r_j^{k}\bigr\rbrace_k .
$$

Thus GRPO uses **simple mean subtraction and division by the sample standard deviation**, i.e. it treats the *group mean* as a baseline and rescales to unit variance. No more sophisticated estimator (e.g., generalized advantage estimation or moving averages) is introduced in the paper.

### 4\. Form the advantage for every token

*Outcome supervision* – every token inside output $o_i$ receives exactly the normalised final reward:

$$
\widehat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i , 
\qquad 
t=1,\dots ,|o_i|
$$

*Process supervision* – tokens are assigned a sum of all **future** normalised step rewards (the “reward‑to‑go” analogue):

$$
\widehat A_{i,t}=%
\sideset{}{^\displaystyle}\sum_
{\;\operatorname{index}(k)\ge t}
      \widetilde r_i^{k},
      \qquad 
      t=1,\dots ,|o_i|
$$

In both cases $\widehat A_{i,t}$ plays exactly the role that $A_t = r_t - V_\psi(s_t)$ plays in PPO: it weights the policy gradient term  
$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})$.

### 5\. Plug into the PPO surrogate

The GRPO objective is identical to PPO’s clipped surrogate except that $A_t$ is replaced by $\widehat A_{i,t}$ and an explicit KL penalty between current policy and a fixed reference model is added:

$$
J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=%
\E_{q,\{o_i\}}\!\Bigg[
   \frac1G
   \sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \frac1{|o_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|}
   \Bigl(
     \min%
     {
        r_t^{\,{\rm ratio}}\;\widehat A_{i,t},
        {\rm clip}(r_t^{\,{\rm ratio}},\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\;\widehat A_{i,t}
     }
     -
     \beta\,D_{\!KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta\bigr|\big|\bigr]\!\bigl[\!\pi_{\rm ref}\bigr]
   \Bigr)
   ,
   %
   ]
,
$$

where  

$r_t^{\,{\rm ratio}}
=
\dfrac{
    \pi_\theta(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})
}{
    \pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(o_{i,t}\mid q,o_{i,<t})
}$ .

No value function appears anywhere else.

---

## Why does this replace a learned critic?

* **Unbiased baseline:** The group mean is an unbiased Monte–Carlo estimate of  
$\displaystyle E_{\;\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\!}[\,r\,]$. Subtracting it yields zero–mean advantages, which reduces gradient variance just like a deterministic critic would.
* **Zero extra parameters:** The baseline only needs statistics that are cheap to compute on the fly (mean & std), avoiding an extra neural network whose weights themselves must be trained.
* **Alignment with preference data:** Reward models are typically trained on *comparisons* among outputs to one question; normalising within a sampled set mirrors exactly that comparative setting.

---

## Influence of the group size $G$

The quality of the baseline improves as more samples are drawn:

| Effect                | Intuition |
|-----------------------|-----------|
| **Variance reduction** | The sample mean’s variance scales as&nbsp;$ {\operatorname{Var}[r]}/{G} $. With larger $G$, the estimated baseline becomes closer to the true expectation under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, so $r_i-\text{baseline}$ has lower stochastic noise. |
| **Bias**              | The estimator remains unbiased for any finite $G$. However, when $G=1$ it collapses to zero (no baseline), giving maximal variance—exactly what a poorly trained critic would suffer from if its predictions are noisy. |
| **Computation**       | Each additional member requires one forward pass through $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and one evaluation by the reward model; memory scales roughly linearly with $G$. The authors use $G=64$ in their experiments as a sweet spot between variance reduction and feasible GPU usage. |
| **Comparison with learned critics** | A learned value head can provide an *exact* expectation if perfectly fitted, but in practice it suffers from approximation error and requires its own optimisation loop. With modest $G=16{-}64$, GRPO’s empirical baseline often matches or exceeds a value network’s variance reduction while costing far less memory because no extra parameters are stored or back–propagated through them. |

Empirically (see Table 5 in the paper), GRPO achieves comparable or better performance than PPO with an explicit critic while using roughly half the GPU memory—precisely because only $G\times |\!\!\text{batch}|$\,$ forward passes are needed rather than maintaining a separate backward pass through a learnable value head $\!V_\psi$.  

---

### TL;DR

GRPO computes advantages by  

1️⃣ Sampling $G$ completions per question from the old policy;  
2️⃣ Scoring them with a reward model;  
3️⃣ Normalising each score using *group mean subtraction and division by group standard deviation*;  
4️⃣ Assigning either this normalised final score (outcome supervision) or its step‑wise cumulative sum (process supervision) as the token–level advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$.  

The group mean serves as an on–the–fly baseline, eliminating any learned value network. Larger groups give tighter Monte–Carlo estimates of true expected reward, thereby reducing gradient variance—in practice achieving similar or better stability than a parametric critic while dramatically cutting memory consumption.
