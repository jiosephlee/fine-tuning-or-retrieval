\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### In the unified paradigm equation (13), what form does Gradient Coefficient GC_𝓐 take for DPO compared with PPO/GRPO?
Question:
The authors express every method’s gradient as E[(q,o)] GC_𝓐(q,o,t,… )∇θlogπθ(o_t|…). For DPO they mention pairwise preference loss—but I cannot locate an explicit expression for GC_DPO(q,o,…). Could someone derive GC_DPO mathematically and contrast it with GC_PPO which involves clipped probability ratios times advantage?
Answer:
In Sec.~6 the authors cast every fine‑tuning / RL method into the “unified‑paradigm’’ form  

$$
\nabla_{\!\theta}\,\mathcal J_{\!\mathcal A}(\theta)
=\;\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal D}\!
   \Biggl[\,
      \frac1{|o|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
            {\color{blue}{GC_{\!\mathcal A}(q,o,t,\pi_{\!rf})}}\;
            \nabla_{\!\theta}\!\log\,\pi_{\!\theta}\!\bigl(o_{t}\mid q,o_{<t}\bigr)
   \Biggr]\,.%
$$  
(the term \(GC_{\!\mathcal A}\) is what we call the **gradient coefficient** – it tells us how strong an update should be applied at each token based on the training signal that method $\mathcal A$ supplies.)

Below we write down \(GC_{\!\mathcal A}\) explicitly for **Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO) and compare it with the coefficients that appear in **PPO** (and its variant **GR‑PO**).

$\displaystyle z={Δ}_r ^{/* */}+(\,[L⁺−L⁻]/β)$.

### 2 How does this differ from PPO / GR‑P​O ?

For PPO (and consequently GR‑P​O), after sampling an output    
$o=\langle o_1,…,o_T\rangle,$   
the surrogate loss reads  

$$             
J_{{PPO}}\!=!
\Esp{
	     \
		   {
		      {\rm min }
		      {
		          R_tA_t,\

		                     1-\varepsilon ,

		   }
	       },
	       ~~R_t=
	      {
	          	           	   	          $
	          ⁡{}
	           }
	       }                           
.$$

Taking derivatives gives directly  

```latex    
GC_{PPO}(q,o,t)=       
     {
           {\rm min }\!
           {
                R_tA_t,\
                {{clip(R_t,\,1-\varepsilon ,\: 1+\varepsilon)}A_t }\!.
           }
     }
```


with two important ingredients absent from D‑P​O:

* **Advantage \(A_t.\)**   
 \(A_t=r_t-V(s_t)\) comes either from outcome–supervision (“final reward’’)  
 or process–supervision (“step reward’’). It varies *per* time step,
 reflecting how much future return each token contributes.
* **Clipping** The probability ratio    
 \(R_t=\exp(\logπ_θ-\logπ_{old})"
is clipped inside  
 ${[\,1-\varepsilon ,\, 1+\varepsilon ]}$  
 to prevent excessively large updates.
* **Explicit baseline/value function** PPO needs an auxiliary critic   
 \(Vψ(s)\); GR‑P​O replaces this critic by a *group–average* baseline,
but still retains an advantage term.
* **Sign convention** Both methods treat every sampled trajectory uniformly –
there’s no separate “negative’’ example whose gradients receive opposite sign.


By contrast,
D‑P​O’s coefficient is symmetric:
it multiplies tokens belonging to $𝑜⁺$ by $+\sigma(-z)/β$
and those belonging to $𝑜¯$ by $-\sigma(-z)/β.$  
Thus a single sampled *pair* supplies both positive reinforcement  
(and negative penalisation) simultaneously.
There is no clipping because the sigmoid already bounds its magnitude between 0 and ½;
the “advantage’’ role is played by ${Δ}_r^𝘲}$ via its appearance inside \(z.\)

### 3 Summary – intuition  

* **PPO / GR‑P​O:** updates are proportional to *probability ratio × advantage*,  
capped by clipping so that large ratios do not dominate.
The advantage itself comes from an external value estimate;
its sign can be arbitrary depending on whether future reward increases or decreases after a particular token.
All tokens belonging to one trajectory receive *different* weights because $A_t$
changes along time steps.
* **D‑P​O:** updates are proportional simply to a *sigmoid of a margin*
that mixes reward differences with the current log–probability gap between two candidate completions.
The weight is identical across all positions of a given completion,
and opposite signs appear automatically for preferred versus rejected samples.
No separate value network or clipping operation appears –
stability stems from soft saturation of~$\sigma$
combined with temperature~$\beta$.  
Consequently you can view D‑P​O as “pairwise‑policy‑gradient’’ whose gradient coefficient collapses exactly onto Eq.~$(17)$.


Both families fit neatly into Eq.~$(13)$: they merely instantiate different functions   
\(GC_{\!\mathcal A}(\cdot).\)

Understanding these functions clarifies why methods such as GR‑P​O,
which keep an explicit advantage term but replace costly critics by group baselines,
behave similarly to PPO yet consume far less memory,
while D‑P​O enjoys an even more lightweight formulation at the price of relying on pairwise preference data rather than an explicit scalar advantage per timestep.
