\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is iterative reinforcement learning performed with GRPO’s reward model update via replay?
Question:
"Iterative RL" continuously retrains the reward model using newly sampled policy outputs along with a replay buffer containing ~10% historical data. I would appreciate details such as (1) how many new samples are collected per iteration before updating r_φ?, (2) what optimizer settings and learning rates are used for r_φ?, (3) whether older samples are weighted differently than fresh ones during replay.
Answer:
***Answer***

In *DeepSeekMath* the reinforcement‑learning stage is built around **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (GRPO).  
GRPO differs from ordinary PPO because it does not keep a separate value‑function critic – instead it uses the average score of a group of sampled responses as its baseline.  
To keep that baseline reliable while the policy improves, the authors run **iterative RL:** after every outer “iteration” they refresh the reward model ($r_\phi$) with a mixture of freshly generated trajectories and a small slice of older ones kept in a replay buffer. Below I break down exactly what happens in each iteration and answer your three concrete questions.

---

### How an iteration works

The overall loop is described in **Algorithm 1** (“Iterative Group Relative Policy Optimization”).  
For clarity I rewrite it in words:

1️⃣ **Reference Update** – before starting an iteration we copy the current policy $\pi_\theta$ into $\pi_{ref}$. This reference will later appear in the KL‑penalty term so that the new policy does not drift too far away.  

2️⃣ **Exploration‑phase steps** ($M$ steps) – each step processes one minibatch $\mathcal D_b$ drawn from the fixed pool of instructional questions ($\approx 144\,K$ prompts).

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;a️⃣ The old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ is set to the latest $\pi_\theta$.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;b️⃣ For every prompt $q$ inside $\mathcal D_b$ we **sample $G$ outputs** from $\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}$ (**$G = 64$**)。  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;c️⃣ Each sampled output $o_i$$ receives a scalar score $r_i = r_\phi(q,o_i)$ from the current reward model.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;d️⃣ From those scores we compute the *group‑relative* advantage  

$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)} ,
$$  

 which is applied identically to every token of $o_i$\;(outcome supervision) or summed over reasoning steps for process supervision.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;e️⃣ With those advantages GRPO maximises its surrogate objective (Eq.~(3)) for ***$\mu$$ inner updates*** (the paper sets ***$\mu = 1$$**, i.e., one gradient step per exploration stage).

3️⃣ **Reward‑model refresh** – after all $M$ exploration steps have finished,

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;a️⃣ all newly obtained $\langle q,o_i,r_i\rangle$\;$triple$s are added to a ***replay buffer***;.  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;b️⃣ A ***fixed fraction (~10 %)***of previously stored triples is retained together with all fresh ones.;  

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;c️⃣ The reward model $r_\phi$\;$is then trained again on this mixed set.$ $

4️⃣ Loop back to step&nbsp;​¹​ with the newly updated $\pi_\theta.$

Thus “iteration’’ refers to one full pass through steps&nbsp;​¹–​³​ above; inside an iteration many minibatches are processed, each contributing $|\mathcal D_b|\times G$$ fresh trajectories.

---

### (1) How many new samples are collected before updating $r_\phi$

The only hyperparameter governing sample quantity is $G$.  
For every prompt that appears in a minibatch we generate ***64*** completions, so after processing one minibatch we obtain  

$$
\text {new samples}= (\#\hbox {prompts in }\mathcal D_b)\times G .
$$  

The authors fix ***the token‑wise minibatch size at 1024 tokens*** and keep this setting throughout RL training (§ “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL”).  
Consequently an entire outer iteration gathers roughly  

$$
(148\times10^{3}\;\hbox {prompts})\times G
\simeq
9\times10^{6}
$$  

new \<question , output〉 pairs before $r_\phi$\;$is refreshed once.\footnote{
In practice this amount is split across many smaller batches defined by
the token budget,
but conceptually all those pairs constitute “the new data’’.
}

---

### (2) Optimiser settings and learning rates used for $r_\phi$

The paper gives only two explicit numbers for the reward‐model optimiser:

| Component | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Optimiser | **AdamW** (the same optimiser used elsewhere in the work) |
| Learning rate | ***\(2\times10^{-5}\)*** |

All other AdamW hyperparameters follow those employed for pretraining and SFT  

$$
\beta_{1}=0\.9,\qquad 
\beta_{2}=0\.95,\qquad 
weight\!\,_decay=0\.1 .
$$  

No learning–rate schedule is mentioned for $r_\phi$, so it appears that a constant LR of \(2\times10^{-5}\)$​is used throughout every iterative refresh.

---

### (3) Are older replay entries weighted differently?

No special weighting scheme is described.  
When constructing th­e replay set for­a refresh,

*approximately* \(~10 %\)\,$of historically stored examples are simply mixed back together with ​all freshly generated examples,
and this combined set​ is fed ​to AdamW ​without any additional scaling factors.\footnote{
Fresh trajectories and retained old trajectories receive ***equal loss weight*** during reward‐model fine‐tuning.
}

The distinction lies solely ​in frequency :**old examples constitute ~~\(~10 %\)\,$while recent ones dominate (>\(~90 %\)).²**

Because uniform sampling already gives older data far less presence than fresh data,
there Is ​no explicit “age‐based’’ weighting beyond that proportion.

### Why this design matters – an intuitive view

*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\*\**/

_In ordinary PPO_, you maintain a separate value network that tries to predict expected return at every time step． For LLMs this becomes costly because you would have to estimate a value for thousands of tokens—even though most tasks only give you an end‐of‐sequence score．  

_GRPO sidesteps⁠that cost⁠by computing a single scalar baseline—the average score⁞inside⁞each⁞group⁞—so no extra network needs   be trained． ​
