\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Digging Gold from the Wild Web – Building the 120 B Token DeepSeekMath Corpus  

Mathematical reasoning remains one of the toughest hurdles for large language models (LLMs).    Open‑source models lag far behind closed giants such as GPT‑4 or Gemini‑Ultra, despite recent breakthroughs in quantitative reasoning benchmarks like **MATH** and **GSM8K**.  
The authors of *DeepSeekMath* discovered that one decisive ingredient was **data** – specifically a massive, high‑quality collection of mathematics‑rich web pages extracted from the public Common Crawl archive.  
Below is a step‑by‑step walkthrough of how they mined “raw” web data into a clean **120 billion token** multilingual math corpus that powers DeepSeekMath‑7B, a model that rivals proprietary systems on competition‐level math tasks.

---

## Why a dedicated math corpus matters  

LLMs learn patterns from massive text streams; when those streams contain rich mathematical exposition—definitions, proofs, problem statements—the model can internalize symbolic reasoning steps much more effectively than from generic prose alone.  
Previous public math datasets such as **OpenWebMath** (~13 billion tokens) or **Proof‑Pile‑2** are valuable but relatively small compared with what closed models ingest during their proprietary pre‑training stages.   

To bridge this gap without resorting to closed data sources, the DeepSeek team set out to answer two questions:

1️⃣ **Can publicly available web material supply enough high‑quality math content?**  
2️⃣ **If so, how do we retrieve it efficiently while keeping it clean and unbiased?**  

The answer turned out to be yes—and it required an *iterative* pipeline built around a lightweight text classifier called **fastText**.

---

## From seed to scraper – training the first fastText model  

### Selecting a seed  
The journey began with **OpenWebMath**, a curated collection of high‐quality mathematical web texts (~13 GB). This collection served two purposes:

* It provides positive examples that truly reflect “math‐y” language.
* It defines the vocabulary and stylistic cues that a classifier should learn to recognize elsewhere in the wild crawl.

### FastText basics (in plain language)  
fastText is a simple yet powerful linear classifier that converts every piece of text into a bag of character n‑grams (substrings). By training on enough labeled examples it learns which n‑grams are indicative of mathematics versus ordinary prose.  

### Training details (straight from the paper)

| Hyperparameter | Value |
|----------------|-------|
| Vector dimension | 256 |
| Learning rate | 0.1 |
| Max n‑gram length | 3 |
| Minimum word frequency | 3 |
| Training epochs | 3 |

They used **500 k** random samples from OpenWebMath as *positive* instances and another **500 k** random Common Crawl pages as *negative* examples. The open‐source fastText library (`https://fasttext.cc`) was then run with the settings above until convergence.

### Early filtering – deduplication before classification  

Common Crawl contains billions of HTML snapshots, many duplicated across mirrors or different timestamps. To avoid counting identical content repeatedly—and to keep memory usage reasonable—the team performed two layers of removal:

* **URL‐based deduplication:** identical URLs were collapsed into one entry.
* **Near‑deduplication:** similarity heuristics detected near‐identical pages even when URLs differed slightly (e.g., adding tracking parameters).

After this aggressive pruning only around **40 billion** HTML documents remained—a manageable pool for scoring with fastText.

---

## First pass over Common Crawl  

Running the newly trained classifier over those **40 billion** documents yields a score per page reflecting “mathness”. The authors then ranked all pages by this score and kept only the highest ones. To decide how many top pages should be retained they performed quick pre‑training experiments at several token thresholds (**40 B**, **80 B**, **120 B**, **160 B**) and observed downstream performance gains across several math benchmarks (see Table X in the paper).  

The first iteration settled on keeping roughly ***the top chunk*** that accumulated around **40 billion tokens** worth of text—a solid starting point but clearly incomplete because many mathematical sites were still missing.

---

## Expanding coverage – iterative mining cycles  

### Why iterate?  
A single classifier trained only on OpenWebMath misses many legitimate math sites whose style differs significantly—for example forums or university lecture notes whose phrasing never appeared in OpenWebMath’s limited vocabulary.  

### The iterative loop  

For each subsequent round they performed three key actions:

1️⃣ **Domain analysis:** The entire reduced Common Crawl was split into *domains* (all URLs sharing the same base host). For every domain they computed what fraction of its pages had already been selected in previous iterations. Domains where ***>10 %*** of pages were flagged as math were marked “math‐related”.

2️⃣ **Human annotation of URLs:** Within those promising domains workers manually inspected typical URL patterns pointing specifically to mathematical content—for instance `mathoverflow.net/questions/*` or `*.edu/course/linear_algebra/*`. These URLs were added back into the *positive* seed set.

3️⃣ **Retraining fastText:** With an enriched set of positive examples covering more stylistic diversity, a fresh fastText model was trained again using exactly the same hyperparameters as before.

After retraining they rescored all remaining uncollected webpages and once again kept the highest-scoring ones according to a new token budget (e.g., extending toward an additional ~40–80 billion token window).

They repeated this process four times total:

| Iteration | New positive sources added | Approximate extra tokens collected |
|-----------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| 1 | Seed only → OpenWebMath | ≈40 B |
| 2 | Manually annotated domain URLs | ≈30–40 B |
| 3 | Further domain refinement | ≈30–40 B |
| 4 | Minor residual cleaning (≈2%) | negligible |

By the **fourth pass**, only about ***2 %*** new material remained uncollected—a clear sign that diminishing returns had set in (the authors noted “nearly 98 % already captured in iteration 3”). They therefore stopped mining at this point.

### Final statistics  

* Total distinct webpages: **35.5 million**  
* Total token count: **≈120 billion tokens**  
* Language mix: predominantly English plus a substantial Chinese component (the pipeline never filtered out non‐English pages).

The resulting resource is called the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, roughly ***seven times larger*** than Minerva’s math‐web slice and ***nine times larger*** than OpenWebMath’s public release—making it one of the most extensive publicly available mathematics corpora today.

---

## Cleaning up – decontamination against benchmark leakage  

A crucial prerequisite for any fair evaluation is ensuring that training data does *not* contain exact excerpts from test sets such as GSM8K, MATH or their Chinese counterparts CMATH / AGIEval.  
If leaked solutions remained inside pre‑training data they would artificially inflate scores (“cheating”).

The authors applied an extremely strict n‑gram filter:

* Scan every candidate page for any contiguous string of length *10* words (**10‑gram**) that appears verbatim in any benchmark reference file.
* If such a match exists—in either English or Chinese—the whole page segment containing it is removed.
* For shorter benchmark excerpts (<10 words but ≥3 grams), an exact match also triggers removal.

Because even short exact overlaps could give away answers for some problems, this conservative approach virtually guarantees zero contamination while preserving most genuine mathematical exposition untouched.

---

## Multilingual bonus – why Chinese shows up  

Most prior math corpora focus exclusively on English sources because early large‐scale LLMs were primarily trained on English internet dumps.  
The DeepSeek pipeline deliberately left non‐English material untouched during filtering and classification steps; consequently large swaths of Chinese educational portals (\*.cn sites hosting K–12 math tutorials) survived through all iterations.

When fine‑tuned models were later evaluated on Chinese benchmarks such as **CMATH**, **Gaokao-MathCloze**, or **Gaokao-MathQA**, they consistently outperformed models trained only on English corpora—and sometimes even surpassed them when those baselines suffered slight degradation due to linguistic mismatch. This multilingual advantage demonstrates an often-overlooked side effect: an *open* selection pipeline can automatically harvest high-quality resources across languages without needing separate language‐specific crawlers.

---

## Quality validation – does more data really help?  

To confirm that sheer size wasn’t masking low quality, the researchers ran controlled pre‑training experiments using a modest ***1.3 billion parameter*** backbone (`DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3 B`). Each variant was trained for exactly ***150 billion tokens*** on one corpus only:

* `DeepSeek-LLM` + MathPile (~8.9 B tokens)
* `DeepSeek-LLM` + OpenWebMath (~13.6 B)
* `DeepSeek-LLM` + Proof-Pile‑2 (~42 B)
* `DeepSeek-LLM` + DeepSeekMath Corpus (~120 B)

All models were evaluated under few-shot *chain-of-thought* prompting across eight diverse mathematics benchmarks—including GSM8K (grade school), MATH (competition level), SAT style items, OCW courses and both English & Chinese multiple-choice tests.

### What showed up?

| Metric | MathPile | OpenWebMath | Proof-Pile‑2 | DeepSeekMath |
|--------|----------|-------------|--------------|--------------|
| Average accuracy across eight benchmarks | Baseline₁ | Baseline₂ | Baseline₃ | **+~5–10% absolute over others** |
| Learning curve slope (accuracy vs #tokens) | Quickly plateaus after ~50 B | Similar early saturation | Early gains then flatten | Steady rise even beyond 100 B |

Key observations reported by the authors:

* The DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently delivered higher scores *across all eight tasks*, confirming its superior average quality.
* Its learning curve remained upward well past \(50\) billion token exposure—a regime where other corpora had already hit diminishing returns.
* Multilingual evaluation revealed tangible improvements on Chinese tasks; by contrast purely English datasets sometimes hurt performance there due to distributional shift introduced during fine-tuning.

Together these findings validate that meticulous crawler engineering plus iterative classifier refinement does more than just inflate numbers—it actually supplies richer mathematical signals useful for downstream reasoning tasks.

## Putting it all together – From raw crawl to stronger LLM  

Below is an abbreviated “recipe” distilled from the paper’s methodology:

1️⃣ **Start with a small high-quality seed** (`OpenWebMath`).  
2️⃣ **Train a fastText binary classifier** using balanced positive/negative samples; keep low resource footprints (`dim=256`, `lr=0.1`, `epochs=3`).  
3️⃣ **Deduplicate massive raw crawl** via URL hashing + near‑duplicate detection → ~40 billion documents left.  
4️⃣ **Score every remaining page**, rank by probability score, select top candidate pool based on desired token budget (`~40–120 billion`).  
5️⃣ **Iterate:**  
   * Group uncollected URLs by domain → flag domains whose collection ratio exceeds `10 %`.  
   * Manually label representative URL patterns inside flagged domains → add them back into positives → retrain classifier → re-score remaining pool → expand candidate pool further.  
6️⃣ **Repeat step&nbsp;5 until marginal gain drops (<2%)**, which typically occurs after three–four rounds (`≈35½M` total unique URLs).  
7️⃣ **Perform strict n‑gram contamination filtering** against all evaluation suites ➜ guarantee zero leakage into test sets.  
8️⃣ **Finalize:** Assemble filtered text into a single training file → yield \(\approx120\) billion clean mathematics tokens spanning multiple languages.

By following these steps the authors built not just any math dataset but arguably *the gold standard* for open research: massive enough to drive competitive performance yet clean enough not to compromise benchmark integrity.

## Takeaways & future directions  

* **Simple classifiers can scale:** Despite its simplicity relative to modern transformer classifiers, fastText proved sufficient when coupled with systematic human curation at domain level—showcasing an effective low-cost alternative for other niche domains (e.g., bioinformatics literature or legal statutes).
* **Iterative enrichment beats one-shot mining:** The biggest boost came after expanding positive examples beyond the initial seed via domain analysis—a reminder that “seed bias” must be addressed early when mining specialized content.
* **Deduplication matters more than you think:** Removing duplicate copies prevented inflating token counts without adding new knowledge—and reduced storage/computation costs dramatically.
* **Multilingual inclusion should be default:** By not restricting language during crawling we unintentionally harvested valuable Chinese educational material—a win for worldwide research equity.
* **Bench­mark hygiene protects progress:** The rigorous n‑gram filter avoided accidental overfitting onto test sets—a practice all future large-scale crawls should emulate before publishing pretrained checkpoints.
* **Data quality still outranks sheer size:** Even though DeepSeekMath’s corpus dwarfs previous public collections by factors up to nine, its superiority stems largely from cleaner signal—not merely volume—as evidenced by steeper learning curves and cross-lingual gains.

The authors envision extending this pipeline further—perhaps integrating more sophisticated similarity measures or semi-supervised labeling—to capture even richer mathematical contexts such as informal theorem statements or proof sketches that currently reside behind paywalls or LaTeX-heavy PDFs rather than plain HTML text.



---


### TL;DR  

Through an iterative fastText–driven retrieval loop over Common Crawl—paired with aggressive deduplication, manual URL annotation per domain and strict n‑gram decontamination—the team assembled a multilingual mathematics corpus exceeding _120 billion_ tokens while keeping contamination at zero level. Pretraining experiments proved its superior quality compared with older public collections, ultimately enabling DeepSeekMath​⁽⁷ᴮ⁾ to hit >50% accuracy on competition‐level MATH without external tools—a milestone previously unreachable by open models.

The ingredients are cheap; the payoff can be world‐class reasoning capabilities without needing closed corporate datasets.
