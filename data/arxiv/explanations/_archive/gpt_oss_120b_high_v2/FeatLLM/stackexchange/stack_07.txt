\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why is there no bias term in the linear model used to combine binary rule features?
Question:
Equation (2) computes logits as a dot product between non‑negative weights and binary rule vectors with no bias added before softmax. Would adding a bias term improve calibration or allow handling cases where none of the generated rules fire?
Answer:
In **FeatLLM** the class‑specific logit is defined as  

$$
\operatorname{logit}_{k}^{i}= \max (\mathbf w_{k},0)\;\cdot\;\mathbf z_{k}^{i},
\qquad 
\mathbf p^{i}= \operatorname{softmax}\bigl([\operatorname{logit}_{1}^{i},\dots ,\operatorname{logit}_{c}^{i}]\bigr),
\tag{2}
$$

where $\mathbf z_{k}^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{R}$ records which of the $R$ rules for class $k$ are satisfied by sample $i$, and $\mathbf w_{k}$ are learned weights that are projected onto the non‑negative orthant by the element‑wise $\max(\cdot ,0)$.
The authors deliberately omit an additive bias term for three inter‑related reasons that follow from their design goals:

1. **Monotonic contribution of each rule** –  
   The binary feature $\mathbf z_{k}^{i}$ simply says “the rule fires” (value 1) or “does not fire” (value 0). By forcing every weight to be non‑negative,
   any satisfied rule can *only increase* the logit of its own class.  
   A conventional bias would act as a global offset that could *decrease* a logit even when a rule fires, breaking this clean “positive evidence only” interpretation that makes the model easy to inspect (“this rule contributed +0.8”).  

2. **Implicit handling of the “no‑rule” case** –  
   If none of the $R$ rules fire for a sample then $\mathbf z_{k}^{i}=\mathbf 0$ for every $k$, so all logits equal zero:
   $$
   \operatorname{logit}_{k}^{i}=0,\;\forall k .
   $$
   The soft‑max of a zero vector is the *uniform* distribution,
   i.e.~$p_{k}=1/c$.  
   This is precisely what we would expect under complete ignorance (maximum‑entropy prior). Adding a bias would tilt this uniform baseline toward one or more classes even though there is no evidence from any generated rule, which is undesirable in a low‑shot setting where such tilting would have to be learned from very few examples and could easily overfit.

3. **Regularisation / data‑efficiency considerations** –  
   In the few‑shot regime only a handful of labelled points are available for fitting $\mathbf w_{k}$. Introducing an extra scalar per class (the usual intercept) adds $c$ parameters that have almost no observational support. Empirically it tends to increase variance without providing much benefit because any systematic class prior can already be expressed by an *always‑true* rule whose weight acts exactly like an intercept. In practice one may prepend such an “intercept feature” (a column of ones) to $\mathbf z_{k}^{i}$; since its weight is constrained to be non‑negative it plays exactly the role of a calibrated bias while keeping the monotonic interpretation intact.

Because of these points the authors kept Eq.~(2) “bias‑free”.  

**Would adding an unrestricted bias improve calibration?**  
Potentially yes, if the true class prior is far from uniform and there are enough labeled samples to estimate it reliably. In standard logistic regression an intercept often improves both discrimination and calibration by allowing the decision surface to shift away from zero. However, with only 4–8 shots per task—as used throughout the paper—there is insufficient signal to separate genuine prior information from random noise, and any learned intercept would likely degrade calibration rather than help it.

**Alternative ways to encode a prior without violating the design**  
* Include an explicit constant rule (“always true”) among the generated rules; its weight becomes an intercept while still obeying $\max(\cdot ,0)$.  
* Post‑hoc calibrate the output probabilities (e.g., temperature scaling or Platt scaling) on whatever validation data are available; this does not interfere with the training dynamics of $\mathbf w_{k}$.

In summary, the omission of a bias term is intentional: it preserves a simple, positively‑weighted additive interpretation of each LLM‑generated rule, yields a sensible uniform baseline when no rule fires, and avoids introducing poorly‑determined parameters in an extreme low‑shot regime. If one wishes to incorporate class priors explicitly, doing so by adding an “always‑true” binary feature respects both the methodological spirit and the mathematical constraints imposed by Eq.~(2).
