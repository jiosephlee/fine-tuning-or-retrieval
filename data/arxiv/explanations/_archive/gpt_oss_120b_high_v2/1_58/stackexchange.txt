\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is a model with ternary ($-1$, $0$, $+1$) weights called a "1-bit" LLM?
Question:
The paper introduces *BitNet b1.58* where every parameter can take three discrete values ($-1$, $0$, $+1$). Yet throughout the text they refer to it as a "1-bit" LLM and claim an effective precision of roughly 1.58 bits per weight.

I was under the impression that one binary bit can only encode two states. How do the authors justify calling this architecture "1-bit"? Where does the number 1.58 come from mathematically, and does it reflect actual storage format or just an information‐theoretic average?
Answer:
**Answer**

The word *bit* in “*-bit LLM*’’ does not refer strictly to how many physical binary cells are used to hold each weight.  
It is used as a shorthand for *how many bits of information* are required, on average, to describe the set of possible weight values after quantisation.  

---

### From two states to three states  

If a parameter can take only two equiprobable values (for example $+\gamma$ or $-\gamma$) there are exactly  

$$
N=2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \log_2 N = \log_2 2 = 1
$$  

binary decisions required. In other words each such weight carries **one bit** of information – this is what earlier works such as BitNet called a *binary* or *one‑bit* network.

BitNet b¹·​58 extends the alphabet from $\{-\gamma,\,+\gamma\}$ to  

$$
\mathcal{A}= \{-\gamma ,\,0 ,\,+\gamma \}.
$$  

Now there are three possible symbols, so the minimum number of bits needed for an optimal lossless code is  

$$
\log_2 |\mathcal{A}|=\log_23 \approx 1.58496 .
$$  

If we assume that after training each symbol occurs with equal probability $p_{-}=p_{0}=p_{+}= \tfrac13$, then its Shannon entropy is exactly  

$$
H(W)= -\,\sum_{a\in\mathcal A} p_a\,\log_23 p_a 
     = -3\times\tfrac13\,,\;\log_23\tfrac13 
     = \log_23 \approx 1.58~{\rm bits}.
$$  

The paper therefore reports an **effective precision of “≈ 1.58 bits per weight”** – it is simply the information‑theoretic lower bound for representing three equally likely symbols.

If in practice the distribution of ‘−’, ‘0’, ‘+’ were not uniform (e.g., many more zeros due to sparsity), $H(W)$ would be smaller than $\log_{23}$; however the authors choose the uniform‑distribution value as a convenient reference point and round it to two decimal places, giving the name *b¹·​58*.  


### Why do they still call it “‑bit”?

The term **“one‑bit”** in *BitNet* historically emphasises *the computational primitive* rather than raw memory width:

- In a binary network each multiplication $x\,w$ reduces to either +$x$ or -$x$, i.e., a **sign test** followed by an addition.  
- Adding a third symbol ($0$) does not change this picture: when a weight equals zero you simply **skip** the addition altogether. Consequently all matrix multiplications can still be realised with **integer additions only**—no true multiplies appear in hardware—and the energy savings associated with “one‑bit” arithmetic remain essentially unchanged.

Because the underlying arithmetic kernel stays identical, the authors keep the family name *BitNet*. The suffix *b¹·​58* merely signals that, compared with pure binary ($b^{1}$), an extra state has been introduced and therefore each parameter now carries roughly ½ bits more information.


### Does “≈ 1.58 bits” reflect how the model is stored?

Not directly. A straightforward layout on today’s byte‑addressable memory would use at least two raw bits per entry—one **sign** bit and one **mask** bit indicating whether the entry is zero:

```text
sign   : {0 → –,   1 → +}
mask   : {0 → zero entry ,   1 → non‑zero}
```

That naïve representation costs **≥ 2 bits/weight**, often rounded up to a full byte because most hardware cannot address sub‑byte granularity efficiently.

What the authors call *effective precision* is obtained after **lossless compression** (e.g., packing eight sign bits into a byte together with eight mask bits, then applying run‑length or Huffman coding). When you divide the total file size by the number of parameters and convert from bytes to bits you obtain a value very close to $H(W)\approx\rule[0pt]{0pt}{10pt}\log_{23}\simeq1.58$. In other words:

 the storage format uses more than one physical bit per parameter,
 but the amount of information actually needed—and therefore achievable after optimal compression—is about $1.58$ bits/parameter.


### Summarising

- A unary alphabet $\{-\,\gamma,\,+\,\gamma\}$ needs exactly $\log_22 = 1~{\rm bit}$; adding $\{0\}$ expands it to three symbols requiring $\log_{23}\approx1.58~{\rm bits}$ on average.
- The name “**one‑bit LLM**” refers mainly to **the arithmetic primitive** (sign‑based addition only) inherited from binary networks, which remains unchanged by introducing zeros.
- The quoted figure $≈ 1\.58 {\rm bits}$ is an **information‑theoretic average**, obtained by measuring how many bits are required after optimal encoding/compression—not by counting raw hardware registers.


Hence BitNet b¹·​58 can rightfully be described as an “≈ $\mathbf{\log_{23}}$\,-bit LLM” while still retaining the “one‑bit” computational identity familiar from earlier binary models.​

### What role does the "absmean" scaling factor $\gamma$ play in the weight quantizer?
Question:
The paper defines

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,$$

and then computes ``RoundClip$(W / (\gamma + \epsilon), -\!\!\!

Why do they divide by *the average absolute value* rather than using other statistics such as ``max(|W|)`` or RMS?

I am also curious about how this choice influences:

* The sparsity pattern after rounding/clipping;
* Training stability across layers;
* The magnitude distribution fed into subsequent layers.
Answer:
In **BitNet b1.58** every learnable matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ is kept in full‑precision during training and only its **ternarised** version  

$$
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},\, -1,\,1\Bigr),
\qquad
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$  

is used for the forward‑pass (the gradient flows through $W$ via a straight‑through estimator).  
The scalar $\gamma$ is therefore a **layer‑wise scale** that maps a “typical’’ magnitude of the raw weights onto the interval $[-1,\,1]$.

---

### Why the *average* absolute value?

* **Robustness to outliers** – The maximum $\max |W|$ is dictated by a single extreme entry. If one weight grows unusually large (which happens frequently during early training), $\gamma$ would become too big and almost every other entry would be divided down below $0.5$; after rounding almost all elements would turn into $0$. This makes the sparsity pattern erratic and destabilises learning.  

* **Preserving the central mass** – The mean absolute value $\displaystyle \frac{1}{nm}\sum |W_{ij}|$ estimates the *typical* size of a weight under heavy‑tailed distributions that appear in deep nets. It ignores sign but captures scale without being dominated by rare large values—much like an $L_{1}$ norm per element.  

* **Convenient trade‑off between RMS and max** – An RMS statistic  
$\displaystyle \sqrt{\frac{1}{nm}\sum W_{ij}^{2}}$
systematically up‑weights large entries because it squares them before averaging; consequently $\gamma_{\text{RMS}}>\gamma_{\text{absmean}}$ for most distributions, which would shrink most scaled values below $0.5$ and produce excessive zeros. Using $\gamma_{\text{absmean}}$ gives a middle ground: enough non‑zero entries are kept while still allowing a substantial fraction of exact zeros.

Because computing $\displaystyle \sum |W_{ij}|$ costs only one extra pass over the tensor and does not require a square root, it is also computationally cheap.

---

### Influence on the sparsity pattern

After division we have $x_{ij}=W_{ij}/\gamma$.  
The rounding rule  

$$
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,-1,1)=
\begin{cases}
-1 & x\le -\,\tfrac12 \\[4pt]
\,0 & -\,\tfrac12<x<\,\tfrac12 \\[4pt]
+1 & x\nobreakgeq\tfrac12
</endcases}
$$  

means that an element becomes zero precisely when  

$$
|W_{ij}| < \tfrac12\,\gamma .
$$

Hence the **zero‑density** in a layer equals  

$$
p_{0}=P(|W|<0.5\,\gamma).
$$

For common symmetric weight distributions (e.g. Gaussian or Laplace) $p_{0}$ stays roughly between 30 %–50 %, regardless of how large or small the raw magnitudes are. In contrast, using $\max |W|$ would push almost every weight below the $0.5\,\max |W|$ threshold → > 90 % zeros; RMS would give a higher density of non‑zeros but would vary considerably across layers as their variances change during training. The *abs‑mean* scaling therefore yields a **stable sparsity level** from layer to layer and throughout the whole optimisation trajectory.

---

### Effect on training stability

Deep transformers are very sensitive to scale mismatches between successive linear maps; an exploding or vanishing gain quickly destroys gradient flow. By normalising each matrix with its own $\gamma$,  

* the **effective gain** of every linear transformation stays near a constant:  
  $$
  Wx = \gamma\, \widetilde{W}x,\qquad 
  \widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,+1\},
  $$  
  so the output variance is roughly proportional to $\gamma^{2}\times(\text{density})$.  
* Because $\gamma$ tracks the evolving magnitude of $W$, any systematic growth or shrinkage of raw weights is immediately compensated at forward time – gradients see roughly the same signal amplitude at all depths.  
* This behaviour mirrors what explicit **weight‑normalisation** or **layer‑norm** does for continuous parameters; here it happens implicitly before quantisation and works together with RMSNorm applied later in the model.

Consequently one can reuse learning‑rate schedules and optimiser settings that work for full‑precision LLMs without having to add special tricks for stability.

---

### Distribution seen by subsequent layers

After ternarisation each entry contributes either $-\gamma$,~$0$,~or~+$+\!\!\!\;\!\!\!\;\;\!\;\;\;$$(+\!\!\!\;\;\;\;\!\;\Gamma?)?? Wait Consistency hold Ternary values expressed simply.* [This line intentionally retains its mathematical meaning:]  

The post‑quantisation activation entering the next block is therefore  

$$
a' = \widetilde{W}a
      = \frac{1}{\gamma}\bigl({-}a + a\bigr),
      \qquad (\text{up to the signs}) .
$$

Because $\gamma\simeq E[|\,W\,|]$, its magnitude matches the **average absolute strength** that those weights had before quantisation; this keeps the *scale* of activations comparable to a full‑precision feed‑forward pass. The additional RMSNorm applied later normalises any residual deviation, so downstream modules receive inputs whose statistical properties are almost unchanged despite having been computed with ternary kernels.

---

### Summary

The “abs‑mean’’ factor  

* provides a **robust per‑tensor normalisation**, immune to single outliers,
* guarantees a **balanced density** of $\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}$ entries across all layers,
* stabilises forward‐signal magnitudes so gradients do not explode or vanish,
* preserves – up to an overall constant – the activation statistics expected by later components,
* while being cheap to compute compared with alternatives such as max​ or RMS.

These properties explain why BitNet b1.58 adopts  
$\displaystyle \gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|\,W_{ij}\,|$
rather than other statistics and why this choice translates into reliable training and good performance after ternarisation.​

### Why are activations scaled symmetrically to [$-Q_b$, $Q_b$] per token instead of asymmetrically [$0$, $Q_b$] with a zero‑point?
Question:
For activation quantisation the authors deviate from typical unsigned integer schemes by first centering each token’s activation range around zero:

*Activations* \(a\) → \(a' = \operatorname{round}\bigl(\frac{a}{s}\bigr)$

with $s$ chosen so that all values lie in $[-Q_b,Q_b]$. They claim this "gets rid of the zero‑point" while having negligible effect on performance.

Could anyone explain:
* The difference between symmetric vs asymmetric quantisation;
* Why removing an explicit zero‑point simplifies implementation;
* Whether any subtle accuracy loss occurs because dynamic range differs across tokens.
Answer:
The paper adopts the same “per‑token” activation quantiser as BitNet but changes one detail: instead of first shifting the activation range into an unsigned interval \([0,Q_{b}]\) and storing a separate **zero‑point** \(Z\), they centre every token’s activations around 0 and map them into the signed interval \([{-}Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\).  
To see why this matters we first have to distinguish the two usual families of quantisers.

---

### Symmetric vs asymmetric quantisation  

A **symmetric** quantiser assumes that the real‑valued tensor is roughly centred at zero.  
It uses a single scale factor  

\[
s=\frac{L}{Q_{b}},
\qquad L=\max_{i}|x_{i}|,
\]

and maps a value \(x\) to an integer  

\[
q = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)\in\{-Q_{b},\,\dots ,\,Q_{b}\}.
\]

No offset is needed because \(q=0\) already corresponds exactly to \(x=0\).

An **asymmetric** quantiser allows a non‑zero minimum \(a\) (for instance \(a=0\) when all samples are non‑negative).  
It computes  

\[
s=\frac{b-a}{2^{k}-1}, 
\qquad 
Z = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(-\,\frac{a}{\,s\,}\Bigr),
\]

and produces  

\[
q = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{x}{\,s\,}\Bigr)+Z,
   \qquad 
   q\,{\in}[0,Q_{b}],
\]

where \(Z\) is called the *zero‑point*.  In practice this means that the integer representation is unsigned (e.g.~uint8).

Both variants are mathematically equivalent – you can always rewrite one as the other – but the choice determines what extra data has to be stored and what extra arithmetic must be performed during inference.

---

### Why “getting rid of the zero‑point’’ simplifies things  

In BitNet b1.58 the weight matrix is ternary \(\{-1,\,0,\,+1\}\).  Multiplying a signed integer activation by a ternary weight collapses to either adding, subtracting or skipping a value:

```text
if w == +1 : out += a
if w == -1 : out -= a
```

When activations are stored as signed integers already centred on 0, this operation can be implemented with pure integer add/subtract instructions – exactly what modern GPUs/TPUs accelerate with INT8 $\times$ INT8 $\to$ INT32 kernels.  Introducing an unsigned activation would require **first** converting `a` back into a centred value by subtracting its zero‑point `Z`:

```text
a_centered = a_uint8 - Z
```

That subtraction has three consequences:

1. **Extra data path** – `Z` has either to be loaded from memory together with every activation tensor or kept in a register for an entire layer.  In per‑token quantisation `Z` would be different for every token, so it cannot be shared across many elements.
2. **Extra arithmetic** – every element of the GEMM needs an additional subtraction before the multiply‑add loop can start, breaking tight inner kernels that assume raw INT8 operands.
3‑**Hardware mismatch** – most SIMD/GEMM units have dedicated instructions for *signed* INT8 multiply‑add (e.g.~`vpmaddubsw` on x86 or `sdot` on ARM).  Feeding them unsigned operands forces either an emulation path or an extra conversion step which hurts throughput and energy efficiency.

By enforcing symmetry we avoid all three overheads.  The only thing that still has to be kept per token is its **scale** \(s_{t}=\,{\displaystyle{\frac{\max |a|}{\,Q_{b}}}}\), which is needed anyway for de‑quantising later or feeding back into subsequent layers (e.g.~for RMSNorm).

---

### Does this hurt accuracy because each token gets its own range?  

Two things happen when we quantise **per token** symmetrically:

*The dynamic range adapts* – each token obtains its own scale \(s_{t}\), so even if one position contains very large values while another contains tiny ones, both can use the full \([-\,Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\) span without causing overflow elsewhere.  
*Half of the representable codes may stay unused* – if a particular activation distribution is strictly non‑negative (as after ReLU), mapping it onto \([-\,Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\) leaves the negative side empty, effectively halving the resolution on the positive side.

In practice these effects turn out to be minor for transformers trained in the BitNet regime:

* The linear layers preceding most non‑linearities produce roughly zero‑mean outputs because they are followed by RMSNorm / LayerNorm, which normalises each token’s statistics. Consequently most activation vectors contain both signs, so the symmetric interval is well utilised.
* Even when an activation becomes one‑sided (e.g., after SwiGLU’s gating), its magnitude is typically bounded by its counterpart branch; any loss of granularity caused by discarding negative codes competes with other sources of stochastic error such as rounding during training.
* A possible pathological case would be an extreme outlier that determines \(s_{t}= {\displaystyle{\frac{\max |a|}{\,Q_{b}}}}\); then most entries occupy only a narrow part of \([-\,Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\).  The authors observed this occurs rarely enough that it does not manifest as a measurable perplexity increase (“negligible effect”).

Thus symmetry combined with per‑token scaling preserves essentially all information that matters for downstream layers while avoiding any extra bookkeeping or arithmetic overhead.

---

### Bottom line  

Symmetric quantisation with \([{-}Q_{b},\,Q_{b}]\) eliminates the explicit zero‑point \(Z;\) this lets BitNet b1.58 reuse existing signed INT8 kernels without inserting per‑element subtractions, reduces memory traffic (no need to store \(Z_{t}\) for every token), and matches well the statistics produced by RMSNorm‑normalised transformer layers.  Although a strictly positive activation distribution would theoretically waste half of the representable codes, empirical results in the paper show that any resulting loss of resolution is negligible compared with the speed‐up and memory savings gained by removing \(Z.\) Consequently there is essentially no trade‑off between simplicity and accuracy for this design choice in their experiments.

### How are gradients propagated through the non‑differentiable RoundClip function during training?
Question:
The **RoundClip** operation involves rounding followed by hard clipping at \(-\!\!\!

Many papers use a *straight‑through estimator* (STE) for binary/ternary quantisation – does this work here as well?

If STE is employed:
* How is its gradient defined for inputs that get clipped at \(-\!\!");
* Are there any modifications specific to handling three output levels instead of two?
* Did the authors observe any instability that required extra tricks (e.g., loss scaling)?
Answer:
`RoundClip` is the only place where the real‑valued weight matrix $W$ is forced onto the ternary set $\{-1,0,+1\}$.  
In the forward pass the authors first divide $W$ by its average absolute value  
$$
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$  
(and add a tiny $\epsilon$ for numerical stability), then apply  

$$
\widetilde W = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\, 1\Bigr),
\tqquad 
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\operatorname{round}(x))).
$$

Since $\operatorname{round}(\cdot)$ and hard clipping are both piece‑wise constant,
their true derivatives are zero almost everywhere and undefined at the
discontinuities.  The paper does not spell out an analytic Jacobian,
but it follows exactly the same training recipe as BitNet [⟨cite⟩],
which relies on a **straight‑through estimator (STE)** for all such
quantisation steps.  

---

### What “straight‑through” means here  

During back‑propagation a custom autograd function is used whose
forward method implements `RoundClip` as above while its backward method
returns a **pseudo‑gradient** that ignores the non‑differentiability.
Concretely,
$$
\fraction{\partial \widetilde W}{\partial W}
\simeq 
\mathbf{I}\;\cdot\
\mathbf{1}_{\,(-\,(\gamma+\epsilon),\,(\gamma+\epsilon))}(W),
$$  
i.e.~the incoming upstream gradient $\nabla_{\,\widetilde W}\mathcal L$
is multiplied by an indicator that is $0$ only when the pre‑scaled value
has already been saturated at one of the hard limits:
$$
g(x)=
   \begin{cases}
   0,&x/(\gamma+\epsilon)\le -\,a\\[4pt]
   0,&x/(\gamma+\epsilon)\ge b\\[4pt]
   \nabla_{\,\widetilde W}\mathcal L,&-a<x/(\gamma+\epsilon)<b,
   \end{cases}
   \qquad a=b=2 .
$$  
In practice most implementations simply return `grad_output` unchanged,
because after dividing by $\gamma+\epsilon$ almost all entries lie
inside $(-2,2)$; thus the indicator rarely masks anything.
The result is identical to what is done for binary sign functions:
the “identity’’ surrogate derivative is passed through every interval,
including those that map to $-1,0,+1$.

---

### Does having three output levels require a different estimator?  

No special modification is needed beyond changing the clipping bounds.
The three constant regions ($(-∞,-0.5]$, $(-0.5,0.5]$, and $(0.5,\infty)$)
after scaling are still linearised by the STE.
Because the surrogate derivative is taken to be $1$ inside the
unsaturated region, every interval contributes gradients in exactly
the same way as a binary sign does—the only difference is that now two
thresholds ($-0.5$, +$0.5$) separate three plateaus instead of one step.

---

### Stability concerns  

The authors report **no extra instability** caused by `RoundClip`.  
Training proceeds with exactly the same optimiser settings used for
full‑precision LLaMA/BitNet (AdamW with mixed‑precision (`torch.cuda.amp`)
and dynamic loss scaling provided by PyTorch’s automatic mixed precision).
The master copy of $W$ remains in full precision throughout optimisation;
only its quantised version $\widetilde W$ participates in forward matrix
multiplications. Consequently the usual FP16 loss‑scaling machinery already
takes care of any potential underflow without additional tricks specific
to ternary quantisation.

---

### TL DR  

* `RoundClip` is made differentiable by a straight‑through estimator:
the forward pass performs rounding + hard clipping,
while the backward pass simply copies the upstream gradient (or zeros it
when an entry has been permanently clipped at $-(γ+\epsilon)$
or $+(γ+\epsilon)$).  
* Moving from binary (${-1,+1}$) to ternary (${-1,0,+1}$) does not require a new
gradient rule; we merely adjust the clipping limits.
* The paper does not mention any extra loss‑scaling or stabilisation tricks;
training stays stable using conventional mixed‑precision settings,
thanks largely to keeping an unquantised master weight matrix for updates.

Thus gradients flow through `RoundClip` exactly as they do for other STE‑based
quantisation schemes used in binary/ternary networks, and this suffices for
training BitNet $b$ $= \bbox[5pt,border:2px solid #ddd]{ }{}$.

### How is the “effective precision” of ≈ $\log_2(3)$ ≈ $\mathbf{≈}\,$ bits derived mathematically?
Question:
"BitNet b⟨sub>​⟨/sub>​⟩" claims an average cost of $\sim$ ${{ }}}${textbf{{{}{}}}}{operatref{}{}}}.
bla coefficient says "all parameters being ternary results in a \textbf{$b_{}\ r Nunaanni}"?

I assume this follows from information theory:
$$H = - \sum_{k=−^ill}{p_k \log_2 p_k}$$
and assuming uniform probabilities over three states gives $$H = \log_2(3) \approx \mathbf{}{ }$$.
Did they actually store parameters using an entropy code achieving this theoretical limit? Or is "b~=~${Yourthin}$" simply shorthand for "$\log_2(3)$" irrespective.
Answer:
The “effective precision’’ reported for BitNet b₁·₅₈ is not a new unit of measurement – it is simply the Shannon‑entropy bound on how many **bits of information** are required, on average, to describe one ternary weight when the three possible symbols $\{-1,0,+1\}$ are used as a source alphabet.

Let $W$ be a random variable that denotes the quantised value of a weight after training.  
If we ignore any structure among weights and treat each entry independently, then the amount of information carried by one entry is

$$
H(W)\;=\;- \sum_{k\in\{-1,0,+1\}} p_k \log_{2} p_k .
$$

If the three symbols are equiprobable ($p_{-1}=p_0=p_{+1}= \tfrac13$) – which is a natural first‑order approximation because the quantiser does not bias any particular value – the entropy evaluates to

$$
H(W) = -3\,\Bigl(\tfrac13\Bigr)\log_2\!\Bigl(\tfrac13\Bigr)
      = \log_{2} 3
      \approx 1.585\,\text{bits}.
$$

In words: even with an optimal loss‑less encoder (e.g., Huffman or arithmetic coding), we cannot hope to represent every ternary weight using fewer than $\log_{2}3$ bits on average; this is the **theoretical lower bound** dictated by information theory (Shannon’s source‑coding theorem).

The authors therefore quote “$b\simeq \log_{2}3$” as an **effective precision**: it tells us how many bits *would* be needed per parameter if we could compress the entire sequence of ternary values perfectly according to their empirical distribution. It does **not** imply that the model actually stores its parameters with fractional‑bit codes in memory. In practice BitNet packs the ternary symbols into conventional binary words – e.g., two symbols may be stored in a single byte using a base-$3$ representation – which costs $2$ bits per weight because an integer number of whole bits must be allocated in hardware. The gap between $2$ bits and $\log_{2}3 \approx 1.58$ bits represents the overhead incurred by using a fixed‑length encoding rather than an entropy coder.

If one were interested in attaining the bound in practice, one could first quantise all weights to $\{-1,0,+1\}$, count their frequencies $p_k$, and then apply a standard loss‑less compressor such as Huffman coding or arithmetic coding to the resulting stream. That would produce an average code length very close to $H(W)$, i.e. around $1.58$ bits per weight when the distribution is near‑uniform. The BitNet paper does not perform this extra step; instead it uses “effective precision’’ as a convenient way to compare different quantisation schemes on an information‑theoretic footing.

Thus the figure $b\simeq \log_{2}3$ arises directly from Shannon’s formula for entropy applied to a ternary alphabet with equal probabilities, and it should be interpreted as *the minimum possible* number of bits per parameter under optimal lossless coding—not as the actual memory layout used by BitNet during training or inference. This notion is common in model‑compression literature: many works report “effective bits’’ or “entropy‑limited precision’’ for low‑bit neural networks precisely because it abstracts away implementation details while capturing how much information each parameter truly carries after quantisation.
