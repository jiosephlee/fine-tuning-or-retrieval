\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What is exponential gating in xLSTM and why replace sigmoid gates?
Question:
I am reading Section~2 where the authors define i[t] = \exp(pre_i[t]) instead of using a sigmoid activation function sigma(.). The paper says exponential gates allow larger dynamic range and help revise stored information, but I am confused about two things: (1) What specific limitation of sigmoid gates motivates switching to an exponential form? (2) Since an exponential can grow very large very quickly, how do the authors prevent overflow or numerical instability when training large models? The text mentions a stabiliser state m[t], but I do not see clearly how it interacts with i[t] and f[t].
Answer:
**Exponential gating** is the replacement of the usual sigmoid‑activated input (and optionally forget) gates in an LSTM by *positive* gates obtained through an exponential non‑linearity

$$
i_{t}= \exp(\tilde i_{t}) ,\qquad 
f_{t}= \sigma(\tilde f}_{\,{ }{ }{\}})\;\text{or}\;\exp(\tilde f}_{\,{ }{ }{\}})
$$

The authors introduce this change in the scalar sLSTM (Eq.\,(3)) and propagate it unchanged to the matrix‑memory mLSTM (Eq.\,(7)).  
Below I explain why this modification is motivated, what limitation it overcomes, and how the model stays numerically stable during training.

---

### 1. Why not use a sigmoid any more?

In a standard LSTM each gate lies in $[0,1]$.  
Consequently the **input gate** can only *add* at most one copy of the candidate vector $z_{t}$ to the cell,
and the **forget gate** can only *attenuate* the old cell value because it multiplies $c_{\,{ }{ }{ }{ }}$_{ t-1}$ by a factor $\le 1$.  
When an LSTM stores a piece of information early on (large $i_k$) it cannot later increase its magnitude – any subsequent multiplication by a forget factor will only shrink it. This “inability to revise storage decisions’’ is explicitly illustrated in Fig.\ 1 of the paper on a nearest‑neighbour search task: once a value has been written into the cell it cannot be amplified again even if a more relevant datum appears later.

By allowing $i_t$ (and optionally $f_t$) to take **any positive value**, an exponential gate supplies a *dynamic range* that spans many orders of magnitude.  
If later evidence suggests that what has been written should be reinforced, an input gate with $i_t>1$ can increase the contribution of $z_t$; similarly an exponential forget gate can *erase* faster when $\exp(\tilde f_t)\ll 1$ or *preserve* more aggressively when $\exp(\tilde f_t)>1$. In other words, exponential gating turns the additive update

$$
c_t = f_t\,c_{\! t-1}+ i_t\,z_t
$$

into an operation where both terms may be **scaled up or down** without artificial caps. This extra flexibility makes it possible for the network to “revise’’ earlier storage choices – exactly what Figure 2 shows for sLSTM versus vanilla LSTM on synthetic formal‑language tasks.

Because an unbounded positive multiplier may explode numerically, we cannot simply replace sigmoids by exponentials without additional care. The authors therefore devise two complementary normalisation mechanisms.

---

### 2. Keeping the computation stable

#### (a) Normaliser state $n_t$

The sLSTM carries a second recurrence

$$
n_t = f_t\,n_{\! t-1}+ i_t,
$$

which accumulates exactly the same multiplicative factors that affect the cell but **without** multiplying by any candidate vector.  
When producing the hidden output they divide out this accumulated scale:

$$
\tilde h _{ t}= \frac{c_t}{n_t},\qquad
h _{ t}= o_t\,\tilde h _{ t}.
$$

Because both numerator and denominator are multiplied by identical products of $i$’s and $f$’s, any overall growth or decay cancels out – the hidden representation stays in a sensible numeric range even if individual gates become very large or very small. This trick mirrors what is done in attention mechanisms where exponentials are normalised by a softmax denominator.

#### (b) Stabiliser state $m_t$

A second safeguard tracks, on a logarithmic scale, **the largest factor** that has ever appeared up to time $t$:

$$
m_t= \max\!\bigl(\,\log(f_t)+m_{\! t-1},\,\log(i_t)\,\bigr).
$$

Intuitively, $\log(f_t)+m_{\! t-1}$ represents how much the existing scale would grow if we followed the forget path one step further,
whereas $\log(i_t)$ captures any fresh growth contributed directly by the new input gate.
The quantity $m_t$ therefore stores $\log(\text{max scale seen so far})$.

Using this record we re‑scale both gates before they enter the recurrences:

$$
i'_t= \exp\!\bigl(\log(i_t)-m_t\bigr)
      = \exp(\tilde i _{ t}-m_t),\\[4pt]
f'_t= \exp\!\bigl(\log(f_t)+m_{\! t-1}-m_t\bigr).
$$

Both $i'_ t$ and $f'_ t$ are guaranteed to lie in $(0,\;e^{\,|\,Δ|}]$, where $|Δ|$ is at most
the difference between two consecutive maxima; in practice this keeps them close to one.
Crucially, replacing $i_t,f_t$ everywhere in Eq.\,(3) by $i'_ t,f'_ t$ does **not change** either:
*the forward output* – because every occurrence of an exponential factor appears both in numerator
$c_T$ and denominator $n_T$, which are divided –
and *the gradient* – because differentiating through Eq.\,(5) yields exactly the same Jacobians as differentiating through Eq.\,(3).  
Thus training dynamics are mathematically identical while numerical values stay well‑behaved.

The same stabiliser equation is employed for mLSTM (see Eq.\,(9)), ensuring that matrix updates
$\mathbf C_T = f_T\,\mathbf C _{ T - 1 } + i_T\,\mathbf v _ T\,\mathbf k ^{\top}_ T$
receive bounded coefficients despite using exponentials.

---

### 3. Intuition and broader connections

Exponential gating transforms each gate into something resembling an *unnormalised softmax weight*: take raw logits $\tilde i _T$, apply $\exp(\cdot)$, then renormalise (by division through $n_T$). This idea appears throughout modern deep learning – e.g., attention uses precisely $\exp(QK^{ T })/\sum_j \exp(QK^{ T })$, and classic fast‑weight programmers treat update strengths as exponentials multiplied by outer products.
What is novel here is that while standard LSTMs restrict themselves to probabilities ($[0,1]$), xLSTM lets those weights become arbitrary positives, thereby giving recurrent units **the ability both to amplify existing memory contents and to overwrite them completely**.



### 4. Summary

*Sigmoid gates limit an LSTM’s capacity to revise what it has already stored because they never exceed one.* By switching to exponential activations for at least one of its multiplicative pathways (the input gate always, optionally also forget), xLSTM obtains an unrestricted positive scale that dramatically enlarges its dynamic range and enables later timesteps to boost or suppress earlier memories—a property required for tasks such as nearest‑neighbour search or rare‑token prediction.

*Exponential growth would normally cause overflow.* The authors neutralise this risk with two simple but powerful devices:

1. A **normaliser state** $n_T$, accumulated with exactly the same multiplicative factors as the cell; dividing $c_T/n_T$ removes any global magnitude.
2. A **stabiliser state** $m_T$ that records the running log‑scale maximum; before exponentiation each log‑gate is shifted by this quantity so that all effective gates stay close to one while preserving exact equivalence after division.

Together these mechanisms let xLSTM enjoy unbounded gating without numerical instability, which explains both empirical gains reported on synthetic formal‑language benchmarks and large‑scale language modelling experiments throughout Sections 2–4 of the paper.
