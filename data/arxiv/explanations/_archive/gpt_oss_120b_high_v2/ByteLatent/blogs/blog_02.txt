# From Bytes to Patches: The Secret Sauce Behind BLT’s Efficiency

Large language models have long leaned on **tokenizers** – hand‑crafted rules that group raw bytes into a fixed vocabulary of subword tokens.  
Tokenization works well enough, but it forces every token to receive exactly the same amount of compute, even though some tokens are trivial to predict while others are challenging.  
The **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** throws away this static vocabulary and lets the model decide **how** to group bytes on the fly. The result? A model that spends its FLOPs where they matter most and slashes inference cost by up to half while matching token‑based LLMs at scale.

Below we unpack BLT’s *dynamic patching* strategy—starting from naïve “one‑size‑fits‑all” patches and ending with an entropy‑driven scheme that adapts patch size to the data’s intrinsic difficulty.

---

## 1. What Is a Patch?

In BLT a **patch** is simply a contiguous chunk of bytes that will be processed as one unit by the *global* latent transformer (the heavyweight part of the model).  
Formally, let a byte sequence be  

\[
\mathbf{x}= \{x_1,\dots ,x_n\},
\]

and let \(f_p\) be a *patching function*.  
\(f_p\) produces a binary mask \(b_i\in\{0,1\}\) indicating whether \(x_i\) starts a new patch; consecutive “0”s belong to the same patch.  
Applying \(f_p\) yields  

\[
\mathbf{p}= \{p_1,\dots ,p_m\}, \qquad m < n,
\]

where each \(p_j\) is a variable‑length slice of \(\mathbf{x}\).

The **average patch size**  

\[
\bar{s}= \frac{n}{m}
\]

directly controls how many steps the global transformer takes—fewer patches → fewer expensive transformer layers → lower FLOPs per byte processed.

BLT therefore needs *a good way* to choose where those boundaries go. Three strategies have been explored:

| Strategy | How boundaries are chosen | Main advantage | Main drawback |
|----------|---------------------------|----------------|----------------|
| Strided (static) | Fixed stride \(K\) – every \(K^{th}\) byte starts a new patch | Simplicity & easy FLOP budgeting | No adaptivity; wasteful or under‑powered |
| Whitespace | New patch after any “space‑like” byte | Aligns with natural word boundaries | Ignores intra‑word difficulty & multilingual variation |
| Entropy (learned) | High *next‑byte entropy* signals a cut | Data‑driven adaptivity – more compute on hard parts | Requires a tiny auxiliary LM |

Let’s walk through each one in depth.

---

## 2. Strided Patching – The Baseline

The simplest option groups every \(K\) bytes together (e.g., \(K=4\) or \(K=6\)).  
This **static stride** was used in earlier byte‑level models such as MegaByte ([Yu et al., 2023]).

### Why it looks attractive
* Trivial implementation – just slice the stream every \(K\) positions.  
* Patch size can be set directly, giving an immediate handle on FLOP cost during training or inference.

### Where it falls short
* **Uniform compute allocation** – all patches invoke the global transformer regardless of how predictable their content is.  
* In predictable regions (e.g., long runs of whitespace or repetitive code) we waste capacity, while dense regions (mathematical formulas, rare names) receive no extra attention.  
* Identical words may be split differently depending on surrounding context, breaking consistency across examples.

Consequently, strided patches provide an **upper bound** on efficiency but leave most potential gains untapped.

---

## 3. Whitespace (“Space”) Patching – A Linguistic Heuristic

A modest improvement comes from cutting patches at *space-like* bytes—a rule proposed by SpaceByte ([Slagle et al., 2024]).  
A *space-like* byte is anything that isn’t part of a Latin character, digit, or UTF‑8 continuation byte; each resulting patch must contain at least one non–space character.

### What makes it better than pure striding?
* Whole words become single patches, so **hard predictions**—typically the first character after a space—receive their own transformer's attention step.  
* Consistent word‐level boundaries improve downstream performance compared with random splits from striding.

### Limitations
* Languages without explicit spaces (Chinese, Japanese) or domains with irregular delimiters break this heuristic.  
* The scheme still cannot vary **average** patch size beyond what naturally emerges from word length statistics—so we cannot explicitly trade off FLOPs versus model capacity as cleanly as we would like.

SpaceByte showed noticeable gains over static striding but still lagged behind state‑of‑the‑art tokenizers in large‑scale experiments.

---

## 4. Entropy Based Adaptive Patching – Letting Data Call the Shots

The most powerful ingredient in BLT is **entropy–driven dynamic patching**. Instead of heuristic rules, we let a *tiny byte‐level language model* estimate how uncertain each next–byte prediction is:

\[
H(x_i)\;=\;-\,\sum_{v\in \mathcal{V}} p_e(v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i})\,\log p_e(v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{<i}),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\mathcal{V}\) is the set of 256 possible byte values and \(p_e\) is the probability distribution output by an auxiliary LM trained on exactly the same data as BLT (typically ~100 M parameters).

Two pragmatic criteria turn these entropies into boundaries:

1️⃣ **Global Threshold** \( H(x_t)> \theta_g \)

2️⃣ **Approximate Monotonicity** \( H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r \)

The first marks any *abnormally high* entropy point as the start of a new patch. The second detects *local spikes*—instances where entropy rises sharply compared with its immediate predecessor—capturing situations where predictability drops inside an otherwise smooth segment.

### How it works in practice
* For each incoming byte we look only at its past context (`$\mathbf{x}_{<i}$`)—the entropy model never peeks ahead—so **patching remains incremental** (`\(f_p(\mathbf{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{<i}\)`), an essential property for generation.
* Low‐entropy stretches get merged into long patches; high‐entropy spikes become short ones, forcing an extra call to the global transformer precisely where predictions are hardest.
* By adjusting thresholds (\(\theta_g,\theta_r\)) we directly control average patch size—and therefore inference FLOPs—without changing any other architectural hyperparameters.

### Why this matters computationally

Recall that most FLOPs in BLT belong to the *global latent transformer*, which processes one step per **patch**, not per byte:

```
Total FLOPs per byte ≈ 
    FL_global / #patches   +   FL_local_encoder   +   FL_local_decoder
```

Increasing average `$\bar{s}$` reduces `#patches`, shrinking `FL_global`. Because short patches appear only around high entropy locations, we allocate extra computation *exactly* where needed while staying cheap elsewhere. Experiments show up to **50 % fewer inference FLOPs** compared with Llama 3 when using an average patch size of ~8 bytes—yet achieving comparable perplexity measured as bits‐per‐byte (BPB).

---

## 5. Putting It All Together: How Dynamic Patches Shape BLT’s Architecture

BLT consists of three interacting modules:

1️⃣ **Local Encoder** – tiny transformer(s) that read raw bytes and produce *latent* representations for each current patch via cross‐attention pooling.<br>
2️⃣ **Global Latent Transformer** – the heavyweight autoregressive core that operates on *patch embeddings*. It runs once per detected boundary.<br>
3️⃣ **Local Decoder** – mirrors the encoder but maps latent outputs back into raw bytes during generation.

Because only step 2 incurs substantial FLOPs, **adaptive patches become an efficient gatekeeper:** most characters flow through lightweight encoders/decoders unchanged; only “hard” regions trigger expensive global attention passes.

---

## 6. Empirical Highlights

| Experiment | Patch Scheme | Avg Patch Size | FLOP Savings vs Tokenizer | Relative Performance |
|------------|--------------|----------------|---------------------------|----------------------|
| Compute‑optimal scaling (1–8 B params) | Entropy (p≈4–5 B) | ~4.5 bytes | ≤ 30 % fewer inference FLOPs | Matches / slightly exceeds Llama 3 |
| Same FLOP budget, larger patches | Entropy (p≈6–8 bytes) | ~6–8 bytes | ≈ 50 % fewer inference FLOPs | Comparable accuracy after modest tradeoff |
| Fixed‐inference budget scaling | Entropy vs BPE tokenizer | variable via thresholds | Better scaling curves across all budgets | Overtakes BPE models once training exceeds ≈2–3× compute‐optimal regime |
| Robustness tests (noisy text) | Entropy (p≈4–5 bytes) | — | — | ≈ 8 pp higher accuracy than tokenized baseline across five noise patterns |

These results confirm two key insights:

1️⃣ **Dynamic padding beats static heuristics**—entropy-patching consistently outperforms both strided and whitespace schemes across training efficiency and downstream tasks.  
2️⃣ **Patch flexibility unlocks new scaling dimensions**—by enlarging average patches without sacrificing model capacity we can grow both model depth and width while staying within a fixed inference budget—a possibility unavailable under fixed vocabularies where larger tokens enlarge embedding tables instead of reducing steps.

---

## 7. Takeaways

- **Static striding** gives you control but wastes compute on easy regions and starves hard ones.  
- **Whitespace cuts** align better with human language units but remain brittle across languages and still offer limited control over computation budget.  
- **Entropy-driven adaptive cuts** let you *listen* to the data itself: high uncertainty → short patches → more compute; low uncertainty → long patches → cheap processing.  
- Because only high‑entropy spots trigger additional global transformer passes, overall FLOP usage drops dramatically while maintaining—or even improving—prediction quality and robustness to noise.  

In short, *the secret sauce behind BLU’s efficiency isn’t just “bytes instead of tokens”; it’s “smartly sized groups of bytes”.* By turning prediction uncertainty into an explicit layout decision, BLT reallocates its resources exactly where they’re needed most—a paradigm shift that could redefine how future LLMs handle raw text at scale.

---

## 8. Looking Ahead

BLT currently relies on a separately trained entropy model for boundary detection—a lightweight “oracle” built before pretraining begins. Future work may learn these boundaries end‑to‑end or tighten integration between encoder/decoder and boundary prediction (akin to hierarchical RNNs). Moreover, extending adaptive padding ideas beyond language—for example vision transformers processing pixel streams—could yield similar efficiency gains wherever raw data exhibits heterogeneous information density.
