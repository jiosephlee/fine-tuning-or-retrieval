\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### Why does BLT allocate most flops to the global latent transformer while treating local encoder/decoder as lightweight?
Question:
Figure 2 shows that most FLOPs are spent in \(\mathcal{G}\). Given that byte‑level processing occurs at every step, why aren't the local encoder/decoder FLOPs larger? Is it solely due to their shallower depth (\(l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}\)) or also because they operate on shorter windows? How does this design affect scaling when increasing patch size?
Answer:
In a Byte Latent Transformer the bulk of the arithmetic work is deliberately placed in the *global latent transformer* $\mathcal{G}$.  
The reason is two‑fold: (i) $\mathcal{G}$ works on a *coarser* representation (patches instead of bytes) but it does so with a much larger model (more layers and a wider hidden dimension), and (ii) the *frequency* with which $\mathcal{G}$ is invoked scales inversely with the average patch size.  

### Why the local encoder/decoder are cheap

The paper’s flop accounting (Sec.~5.2) makes this trade‑off explicit:

$$
\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\text{Transf.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}},\,l_{\mathcal{G}},\,m=n_{\text{ctx}}/n_{p},\,V=0)}{n_{p}}}_{\text{global}}
+\;
\underbrace{\text{Transf.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}},\,l_{\mathcal{E}},\,m=w_{\mathcal{E}},\,V=0)}_{\text{local encoder}}
+\;
\underbrace{\text{Transf.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}},\,l_{\mathcal{D}},\,m=w_{\mathcal{D}},\,V=256)}_{\text{local decoder}}
+\;\dots
$$

* **Depth and width** – The global block uses $l_{\mathcal G}$ layers with hidden size $h_{\mathcal G}$ that are comparable to a full‑size LLM (e.g. an 8 B‑parameter Llama‑3‑scale model). By contrast the encoder and decoder have $l_{\mathcal E}\!\ll l_{\mathcal G}$ and $l_{\mathcal D}\!\ll l_{\mathcal G}$, and their hidden dimensions $h_{\mathcal E},h_{\mathbb D}$ are chosen small enough that their feed‑forward network costs are negligible next to those of $\mathcal G$.

* **Windowed attention** – Both $\mathcal E$ and $\mathcal D$ attend only within a *fixed* local window ($w_{\!\!\!\;\;\!\;\!\;\;\,\;\;\;\mathbf{{E}}},w_{{D}};$ e.g. 512 bytes). This means their attention matrices have size $w\times w$ regardless of how long the whole document is, while $\mathcal G$’s attention operates on all patches that belong to the current context ($m = n_{\rm ctx}/n_{p}$). Because $w$ stays constant, the per‑byte cost of the locals does not grow when we make sequences longer.

* **Per‑byte versus per‑patch execution** – The encoder runs on **every** byte, but each step costs only a few matrix multiplies thanks to the tiny depth and dimensions mentioned above. The decoder similarly processes every output byte at low cost. In contrast $\c G$ runs **once per patch**; its computation is multiplied by $1/n_{p}$, where $n_{p}=n_{{\rm ctx}}/\textit{

patch‑size}. Even though it runs fewer times, each run carries hundreds of billions of FLOPs because it embodies the main modelling capacity.

Together these facts explain why Figure 2 shows that $\sim 80{-}90\,\%$ of FLOPs belong to $\c G$: the locals are deliberately shallow and confined to short windows, whereas $\c G$ is deep, wide and handles long‑range dependencies across patches.

### Effect of changing the average patch size

When we increase the average patch length from, say, 4 bytes to 8 bytes:

1. **Number of patches halves** ($n_{p}\downarrow$). In the flop formula this halves the denominator of the first term, cutting the total FLOPs spent by $\c G$ roughly in half.
2. **Local costs stay almost unchanged** – The encoder still processes every byte within its fixed window; its FLOP term does not contain $n_{p}$. The cross‑attention terms do contain a factor $k/n_{p}$, but because $k$ (the ratio of latent‑to‑byte dimension; e.g.~2) is tiny compared with $n_{p}$, these contributions remain negligible.
3. **Budget can be re‑allocated** – With fewer global steps we now have spare inference FLOPs. The authors exploit this by either *growing* $\c G$\,(adding layers or widening it) or by keeping $\c G$\,'s size constant while enjoying a lower overall flop budget (up to ≈50 % savings reported in Sec.~1).

This behaviour underlies Section 5.3’s “patches scale better than tokens”: larger patches reduce *global* compute linearly while preserving modelling power through a bigger $\c G$. The empirical scaling curves in Fig.~4 confirm that for a fixed inference budget models with bigger patches achieve steeper performance gains than token‑based baselines.

### Summary

- The **global latent transformer** dominates FLOPs because it is deep/wide *and* runs on every *patch*, which—although less frequent than byte‑wise steps—still represents a large chunk of work.
- The **local encoder/decoder** stay cheap by being shallow (`\(l_\ensureMathOperator{\mathrm E},\, l_\ensureMathOperator{\mathrm D}\)` small), using modest hidden sizes (`\(h_\ensureMathOperator{\mathrm E},\, h_\ensureMathOperator{\mathrm D}\)`), and restricting attention to short windows (`\(w_\ensureMathOperator{\mathrm E},\, w_\ensureMathOperator{\mathrm D}\)`).
- Consequently both shallowness *and* limited receptive fields contribute to their low flop share.
- When we enlarge patches, `$n_p$ drops → global FLOPs drop → we can either save compute or reinvest it into a larger `$\c G$` while keeping inference cost fixed.` This explains why longer patches “scale better than tokens” and why BLT can simultaneously grow model capacity and patch size without breaking an inference‐flop budget.

Thus Figure 2 reflects an intentional architectural decision: let cheap byte‑level modules handle encoding/decoding, reserve most computation for a powerful global transformer that captures long‐range structure across dynamically sized patches.
