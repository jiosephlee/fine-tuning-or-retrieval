\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### How are hash n‑gram embeddings constructed and why are they necessary?
Question:
In Section 3.1.1 the authors introduce hash embeddings for byte n‑grams of size 3–8 using RollPolyHash. How does this hashing avoid collisions that could degrade representation quality? Also, why are n‑gram embeddings beneficial beyond plain byte embeddings—what information do they provide to the local encoder?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BLT the local encoder does not rely only on a plain “one‑byte‑→‑vector’’ lookup.  
For every position $i$ it also injects information about the *short* context that  
precedes the byte.  This is done with *hash $n$-gram embeddings* (Section 3.1.1).

---

### How the hash $n$-gram embeddings are built  

For a given position $i$ we first enumerate all byte‑grams that end at that  
position and whose length lies in the set $\{3,4,5,6,7,8\}$:

$$
g_{i,n}= \{b_{i-n+1},\,b_{i-n+2},\,…,b_i\},
\qquad n∈\{3,\dots ,8\}.
$$

Each gram $g_{i,n}$ is fed through a *rolling polynomial hash*,  
$\operatorname{RollPolyHash}$, which computes an integer value deterministically  
from the byte sequence (the exact recurrence is given in Appendix A).  The  
hash is then reduced modulo the size of an embedding table $E^{\text{hash}}_n$:

$$
\operatorname{Idx}(g_{i,n}) = 
   \operatorname{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n})\bmod |E^{\text{hash}}_n|.
$$

The table $E^{\text{hash}}_n∈ℝ^{|E^{\\text{{hash}}}_n|×h_E}$  
contains learned vectors of dimension $h_E$.  The entry at  
$\operatorname{Idx}(g_{i,n})$ is looked up and added to the ordinary byte  
embedding $x_i∈ℝ^{h_E}$.  All six contributions are summed and then normalised:

$$
e_i = 
   \frac{x_i + \sum_{n=3}^{8} E^{\\text{{hash}}}_n(\operatorname{Idx}(g_{i,n}))}
        {1+6}.
$$

In practice BLT uses a single table per gram length with roughly  
$5\times10^5$ entries (the paper reports “500 000 hashes’’).  The same hash  
function is used at training and inference time, so every occurrence of a given  
$n$-gram maps to exactly the same embedding index.

---

### Why collisions do not hurt – the role of RollPolyHash  

A naïve hash could concentrate many different grams in a few buckets,  
causing systematic interference.  RollPolyHash mitigates this in three ways:

1. **Uniform spreading** – The polynomial accumulation (multiplying by a base  
   and adding the next byte) behaves like a random walk over $\mathbb Z$;  
   modulo reduction therefore yields a near‑uniform distribution across the  
   table slots.

2️⃣ **Large bucket count** – With half‑a‑million slots for each gram length,  
   even the worst‑case collision probability for an arbitrary gram is on the   
   order of $10^{-5}$ (by the birthday bound).  Empirically, Table 2 (a‑blation)   
   shows that increasing the table from 100 K to 500 K improves bits‑per‑byte   
   only marginally after ≈300 K entries, indicating that remaining collisions   
   are rare enough not to dominate performance.

3️⃣ **Additive combination across several lengths** – The final vector for a byte  
   is an average of seven terms (the raw byte plus six gram embeddings).  If two   
   distinct grams happen to collide in one table, their contributions are still   
   “diluted’’ by the other non‑colliding terms, so any single erroneous signal   
   has limited impact on downstream attention computations.

Because the hash function is deterministic there is no stochastic drift during   
training; the model can learn to compensate for occasional collisions just as it   
does for natural noise in data.

---

### What do $n$-gram embeddings buy us beyond a plain byte embedding?  

A lone byte embedding knows only *which* value appears at position $i$, but it  
contains no clue about **how** that byte participates in larger linguistic units.  
The summed $n$-gram vectors supply precisely that missing context:

* **Short‑range pattern encoding** – Bytes that frequently co‑occur (e.g. UTF‑8   
  continuation patterns, common ASCII digraphs such as “th”, “er”, or language‑specific   
  diacritic sequences) receive distinct combined vectors even though their last   
  byte may be identical.  This enables the encoder to differentiate “a” as part   
  of “cat” versus “a” as part of “data”.

* **Orthographic and sub‑word signals** – Bytes at word boundaries or after   
  spaces tend to start high‑entropy regions; grams spanning those boundaries capture   
  whether we are at a word start, inside a punctuation block, or inside a multi‑byte   
  code point.  Such cues are crucial for deciding where patches should be placed   
  and for producing clean patch representations.

* **Reduced burden on deeper layers** – By exposing these local motifs early,   
  the lightweight local encoder ($\\mathcal E$) can produce richer latent patches   
  with just one or two transformer layers.  Consequently fewer FLOPs are spent on   
  low‑level processing and more compute can be allocated to the global latent   
  transformer ($\\mathcal G$) where long‑range reasoning occurs.

* **Robustness to noise** – When bytes get corrupted (e.g., bit flips or case changes),   
  the surrounding gram embeddings still carry enough redundancy for the encoder   
  to recover plausible meanings, which aligns with BLT’s reported gains on noisy   
  benchmarks (Section 5).

In short, hash $n$-gram embeddings supply a compact but expressive summary of     
the recent byte neighbourhood without exploding memory (thanks to hashing).    
They enrich each token’s representation with short‑term syntactic and orthographic     
context, allowing BLT’s local encoder to build high‑quality patch vectors while     
keeping compute modest—a key ingredient behind BLT’s ability to match tokeniser­based     
models at scale.
