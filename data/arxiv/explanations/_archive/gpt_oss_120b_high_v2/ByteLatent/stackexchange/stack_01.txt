\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### What is a "patch" in BLT and how does it differ from a traditional token?
Question:
The paper introduces patches as dynamically sized groups of bytes that serve as the primary computation unit. However, I am unclear on the exact definition: Is a patch simply a contiguous subsequence of bytes determined by a boundary marker, or does it have additional semantics? Also, how does a patch differ from a token in terms of vocabulary, embedding lookup, and incremental processing?
Answer:
In BLT a *patch* is not a pre‑defined symbol drawn from a dictionary but a contiguous block of raw bytes whose boundaries are decided on‑the‑fly by a **patching function** $f_p$. Formally, given a byte stream $\mathbf{x}=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ the function $f_p$ produces a binary mask $m_i\in\{0,1\}$ in which $m_i=1$ marks the first byte of a new patch; the resulting segmentation yields $m<n$ patches $\mathbf{p}=\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{m}$. The mask is computed deterministically from the prefix $\mathbf{x}_{< i}$—for the “entropy” variant the decision hinges on the next‑byte entropy  

$$
H(x_i) = -\,\sum_{v \in \mathcal V}
    p_e(x_i=v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{< i})
    \log p_e(x_i=v\,|\,\mathbf{x}_{< i}),
$$  

and a threshold (global or relative) is applied to $H(x_i)$ (or to its change) so that high‑uncertainty points become patch boundaries. Consequently each patch tends to contain bytes of roughly uniform information density, and its length can vary widely—from a single byte in a difficult context to many bytes when the data are highly predictable.

A *token* in the usual sub‑word pipeline is fundamentally different. Tokens come from a **finite vocabulary** built before training (e.g., BPE with 128 k entries). Each token index is looked up in a static embedding matrix, so the model never sees the underlying bytes and cannot adapt its segmentation at inference time. Moreover, BPE tokenization is **non‑incremental**: the same prefix may be tokenized differently once additional characters appear downstream，violating  

$$
f_p(\mathbf{x}_{< i}) = f_p(\mathbf{x})_{< i}.
$$  

Because⇢of​this dependence​on​future​context​BLT​cannot​use​BPE​for​its​dynamic​compute​allocation.

The computational treatment of patches also diverges from that of tokens. Instead​of​an​immediate​embedding​lookup，BLT​first​embeds​each​byte (a 256‐entry table) and enriches it with hash‐based n‐gram embeddings；these byte‐level vectors are then processed by the lightweight *local encoder* $\mathcal{E}$，whose cross‐attention layer pools the bytes belonging to a patch into a latent representation $p_j$．The global latent transformer $\mathcal{G}$　operates　only　on　these　latent　patch　vectors，so　invoking　it　incurs　one　step　per　patch　rather　than　one　step　per　token．When generate⁠ing⁠text⁠BLT⁠must⁠decide⁠at⁠each⁠byte⁠whether⁠it⁠lies⁠at⁠a⁠patch⁠boundary；only​​when​​$m_i=1​​$​​does​​it​​run​​another​​global​​transformer​​step，thereby​​allocating​​more​​FLOPs​​precisely​​where​​the​​next−byte​​entropy​​is​​high．

In summary，a BLT patch is a dynamically sized，data−driven grouping of contiguous bytes defined solely by past information；it carries no fixed identifier and is encoded by learned modules rather than‏looked‏up．By contrast，a traditional token ‑ ‑ ‑ ‑ ‑ ‑ ‑ − isolates ​an ​element ​of ​a ‎static‎ ‎vocabulary‎ ‎with ‎‎predetermined ‎‎embedding‎ ‎and ‎‎segmentation ‎that ‎cannot ‎be ﻿altered ﻿during ﻿inference．This distinction underlies BLT’s ability to redistribute computation according to data complexity，achieve finer control over inference cost，and retain direct access to byte–level information that tokenizers discard．
