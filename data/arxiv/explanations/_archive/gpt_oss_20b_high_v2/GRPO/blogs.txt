# Harvesting Mathematical Knowledge From Millions Of Public Websites  

Mathematical reasoning is arguably one of the hardest problems that large language models (LLMs) face today – it requires a tight grasp of formal logic, algebraic manipulation, geometry intuition and even programming skills when tools are involved.  
The key bottleneck is data: how do you give an LLM enough *high-quality* mathematical text without poisoning it with evaluations you will later test against?  

DeepSeek Model’s team tackled this head‑on by turning an enormous slice of Common Crawl into a clean, multilingual mathematics corpus and then learning how to fine‑tune orders of magnitude faster than conventional Proximal Policy Optimization with a brand new algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Below is a distilled look at how this was achieved—and why it matters for the broader AI community.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

## 📚 Building a Massive Web‑Based Math Corpus

| Step | What It Means | Why It Helps |
|------|---------------|--------------|
| **Seed corpus → FastText classifier** | A tiny hand‑picked set called *OpenWebMat* acted as “positive” examples while half a million random CC pages were used as “negative” ones to kick‑start a FastText model (`dim=256`, *ngram=3*). | FastText gives you a cheap way of scoring every page “math‑likeness”, avoiding expensive language modeling during first pass selection.\n|
| **Four rounds of recall + human vetting** | Each round increased diversity (we added categories like “mathoverflow.net”), then re‑trained FastText on new positives & re‑classify again until virtually all high‑score pages were hit next time (`≈∅`). | By iteratively feeding back positives you gradually teach the system what *new* ways math appears online—think handling LaTeX snippets embedded in blogs or plain prose explanations.\n|
| **Dedup + Aware** | Used URL deduplication + near-deduplication tricks → reduced raw CC dump down to **40 billion** cleaned HTML files before scoring.\n| Only keep top \(G\) tokens per round → last pass kept **35 million** distinct webpages totaling **≈120 billion** tokens.\n\n### Resulting Scale🧪
> ‑more than eight times larger than Minerva’s web portion (>30 billion)  
> ‑almost nine times Minerva’s combined web+arXiv pile (~14 billion)  

This sheer size means each important sentence gets seen up front once *and* fails *checkpoints* across domain filters before being added—a massive switch‑on flag glow versus naïve sampling.

## 🚫 Clean Data = Higher Scores

Even if data looks right syntactically it can carry benchmark answers hidden inside “question–answer” sites—these contaminate downstream test sets.[¹]  

* **Exact‐match filter:** Remove any passage containing an exact \(10\)-gram overlap with any official benchmark text (GSM8K, CMATH,…).  
* **Granular fallbacks:** If potential contamination involves snippets shorter than \(10\) words yet longer than three, kill it outright too.\n\nResult?


*With the cleaned stream*, pre-trained models achieved better ground truth accuracy across eight maths benchmarks versus competing corpora such as *OpenWebMes*, *Proof‑Pile‑2* or *arrax*. While precise AUC measurements live in our Paper’s tables, every plot peaks higher when trained on our corpus—proof that quality trumps quantity alone.

## 📌 Reinforcement Learning That Saves Memory = More Math Power

### Why PPO stands behind most approach    

Common policy optimisation tokes history materialized via an extra neural value network held side‑by-side (`critics`). This adds:



1️⃣ Size bloat equal amplitude compared w/ policy network military  
2️⃣ A multi-step backward pass w/ Generalised Advantage Estimator → heavy GPU memory  

### Group Relative Policy Optimization – A Minimalist Switch  

Goal : Keep full PPO’s stability / clip tricks BUT cut off critic entirely.Zips inside few equations :

```latex
J_GRP(o)=E_q∑_group[ min(π_new/π_old ·ĤA ,clip(π_new/π_old,… )ĤA ) ] 
−β KL(π_new‖π_ref)
```

Where  

*- π_new*. ~current policy   
*- π_ref*. ~SFT snapshot​       « reference » overhead trades off into “do not drift too far” penalty   
*- ĤA*. ~advantage computed simply as normalized difference between each sample’s reward minus average reward inside its *group*

No value function → one less big tensor! Memory-savvy rendering fosters up to **three× speed-up** during policy updates vis POO.* 



### Bonus Details


✅ Group size = \(64\) outputs per question boosts statistical confidence without expensive double passes   ✅ Replay buffer mixes fresh games with old history ensures non‑degenerate critics mainly needed where reward fluctuations increase   ✅ We experiment with both outcome supervision (just last token uses reward model), process supervision refining rewards per logical step.



### Net Effect 

On identical instruction-tuned prompts:


| Task            | Before GRPO   | After GRPO |
|-----------------|-------------|------------|
| GSM8k — CoT     | ‐           | **≈88 %**   |
| MATH — CoT      | ‐           | ≈51–52 %   |
| Out-of-domain CMATH   | ‐        | ≈88–89 % |

These gains come solely from +14k–19k steps exploring diversified selections out-of-open-source field constellations—no extra labelled data beyond what existed already.

# Why Coding First Boosts Numerical Reasoning  

When an LLM starts its learning process by digesting huge amounts of source‑code rather than textbook prose, it ends up doing far better tricks at arithmetic tasks — everything from plain chain–of–thought questions shorted by manual calculations to fully programmatic solutions that invoke Python libraries such as `sympy`. Below I break down *why* that happens and what the concrete evidence looks like in practice.


--------------------------------------------------------------------

## The background story

Typical large language models toil through billions of tokens scraped from news sites, books, discussion forums and paid corpora before they ever see specialized mathematics material. Because “math” remains relatively sparse among these cosmic seas of language data, most LLMs still get stuck when they try to push through multi‑step gradeschool or college puzzles — even when their parameters live side-by-side with top closed models such as GPT‑4 or Gemini Ultra.”

Enter *DeepSeekMath*: instead of wasting precious capacity chasing conventional prose while hoping random pages contain algebraic symbols somewhere hidden away, the team around DeepSeekMathematics decided  
1️⃣ start with an already solid rich–coder backbone (**DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1 .5**, ≈7 B parameters trained solely on source-code)  
2️⃣ overlay it with an atlas–level collection of truly mathematical text pulled cleanly out of Common Crawl – *120 B *tokens*, pulled via an intensive fastText pipeline tuned against high quality reference sets like OpenWebMath.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## “Coding first” actually means more than just syntax

A coder’s playground lives full of four golden rules that coincide naturally with deductive thinking:
```
if   → choose more than one branch,
else → pick a fallback path,
for … loop     → repeat computation systematically,
return value     → finish a subproblem cleanly,
```
Each rule puts an explicit *flow chart* into the neural layers:
* conditionals teach pattern recognition under uncertainty,
* loops instill stable state updates,
* function calls encourage modular subproblem solving.



In practice this looks simply different decisions made right during token generation:
```text
Let x = ..., 
Then compute …
```
which mathematically corresponds roundly to looping over summands or controlling case analysis.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Two ways they mixed language and number  

| Strategy | Sequence | Outcome |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| **Two‑stage** | Code → Math | Big lift on tool–based solves (`Python`+`sympy`) after adding ≈150 B new maths tokens |
| **One–stage mix** | Simultaneous [400 B × code]+[150 B × math] | Keeps strong coding recall while smoothing forgetting — still produces larger boosts than dropping either source |

Both experiments are guided by::

* No architectural changes  
* Same hyper‑parameters apart from token budget  
* Downstream evaluation remains identical  


Therefore both approaches confirm *the recurrence of programming jargon actually assists pure deductive chains — even without any later automatically generated script.*


--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why does logical flow translate so well?

Below are three DSL patterns directly mapped onto popular numerical steps:

| DSL pattern | Example expression | Matching maths step |
|-------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Conditional branching (`if ... else`) | `.when(a > b)`     | Case split (“if `a>0`, treat otherwise”) |
| Loops (`for i=1…n`)            | `.iter()`         | Iterative summation / accumulation |
| Function calls (`func(x)`)      | `.sqrt()`         | Modular extraction — isolate helper formula |

When you read *“compute ∑x_i through loop”*, your thoughts automatically separate variables neatly into consistent passes — exactly what the LLM learned during massive source-code ingestion! Thus abstract algebraic identities become almost mechanical when seen aloud through token sequences shaped by earlier string-edit experiences.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

# GRPO Explained – A Lightweight Reinforcement Hook For Maths  

## Introduction  

Large language models (LLMs) have become proficient at many tasks, yet few‐shot mathematical reasoning remains a hard nut to crack for open‑source systems. Code‑centric instruction tables, chain‑of‑thought prompt engineering, or simple supervised fine‑tuning can get LLMs up against \emph{GSM8K} or \emph{MATH}, but their scores never quite match closed systems like GPT‑4 or Gemini Ultra – often hovering around sixty percent accuracy without tool help.  

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the natural next step after supervised fine‑tuning (SFT): tweak probability mass toward higher‑reward outputs so the model learns which reasoning paths actually solve problems instead of merely memorising correct answers.  
The most prevalent RL recipe in this space is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an actor–critic method familiar from OpenAI’s ``ChatGPT'' training pipeline.\footnote{OpenAI’s ChatGPT team employed PPO during alignment stages \citep{ouyang2022training}.} However, PPO requires an extra neural value network roughly the size of the policy itself – a serious memory burden when scaling up beyond a few hundred million parameters or when one wishes to run several epochs of fine‑tuning on GPUs without thousands of teraflops budgets.\n\nGroup Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), introduced by DeepSeekMath developers~\citep{deepseekmath}, removes this critic altogether while preserving – even boosting – performance on challenging math benchmarks.\n\n---

## The Need for a Critic‐Free Approach  

*PPO’s key mechanics:*  

1️⃣ Sample a set of outputs \(\{\mathbf{o}\}\) from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
2️⃣ Estimate a **value function** \(V_\psi\) that predicts expected total reward \(R_t = \sum_{k=t}^{|o|} r_k\) for every token step \(t\).  
3️⃣ Compute an **advantage** \(A_t = r_t - V_\psi(\cdot)\) to reduce variance before feeding it into the Policy Gradient update.\n\nBecause \(V_\psi\) runs over every token position and consumes nearly an equal share of parameters as \(\pi_{\theta}\), training already feels heavyweight.\n\nFor LLM inference where only the last token traditionally receives a reward—especially in long proof or reasoning chains—training an accurate token‑wise value network becomes tricky and rarely adds more signal than noisy estimates.\n\nGRPO sidesteps this by borrowing *the fact that rewards are computed against sampled answers* rather than each intermediate token.\n\n---

## What is GRPO?  

GRPO keeps each substep very similar to PPO except that it builds a **group baseline** instead of learning a separate value net.\n\n### The Core Idea  

For every question \(q\):

1️⃣ **Sample** a *group* of \(G\) different completions \(\bigl\{\,o_1,o_2,\dots,o_G\,\bigr\}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(O|q)\).  
2️⃣ Score every completion once with a pre‑trained *reward model* \(r_\varphi(q,o)\).  
3️⃣ **Normalize** these rewards inside their group:
   \[
   \tilde r_i = \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)} ,
   \]
   where \(\mathbf r=\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}\).  
   Thus the highest‐ranked answer gets positive advantage, lower ones negative—our *group relative baseline* replaces \(V_\psi\).  
4️⃣ Treat every token inside completion \(o_i\) as having advantage \(\hat A_{i,t} = \tilde r_i\)\--if we ask only for outcome supervision—or accumulate stepwise rewards if process supervision is used:\[
       \hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{steps}\ge t}\tilde r_i^{\text{step}}\!. 
   \]  
5️⃣ Run a **PPO‑style surrogate loss** where the ratio between new and old policies is clipped just like classic PPO:
   \[
   J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
     \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
      E_{t}\!\left[\min\!\Bigl(
           r_t^{\theta/\theta_{\text{old}}}\,\hat A_{i,t},
           {\rm clip}(r_t^{\theta/\theta_{\text{old}}},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_{i,t}
       \Bigr)- 
       \beta\, D_{\rm KL}(\pi_\theta||\pi_{\rm ref})\right].
   \]  
   Here \(D_{\rm KL}\) regularises drift toward previous policy w/out injecting it into reward computation—eliminating another enormous register needed by weighted KL terms in standard PPO.\n\nIn short, **no value network**, **no extra forward passes per token**, **no expensive attention over pseudo‑returns**—all modern acceleration gains come from easing backprop through independent outputs.\n\n---

## Step‑by‑Step Walkthrough  

Below is an operation sketch distilled into plain English–and literal code style—to help developers map together key steps if they wish to reproduce GRPO locally.\n\n```
for epoch in range(epochs):
    pi_ref = current_policy.clone()      # reference so we regularise later
    for batch_qs in message_loader:
        # Step 1: generate G completions per question
        gen_loopsamples = [
            generate(sample_prompt(q), g=G)
            for q in batch_qs      # G ≈ 64
        ]

        # Step 2: compute reward per completion
        rewards = [reward_model(q,o)
                   for q,o_list in zip(batch_qs,
                                       gen_loopsamples)
                   for o in o_list]

        # Step 3: normalize within each group → advantages
        advantages = normalize_group(rewards)

        # Step 4 & 5: update policy via clipped surrogate + kl penalty
        apply_grpo_update(current_policy,
                          advantages,
                          pi_ref,
                          ε=0.2,
                          β=0.04)
    # Optional — re‑train reward model with replayed data:
    update_reward_model(replay_buffer)
```\n\nNotice how each inner loop operates entirely per emulation of *independent* trajectories; no single optimiser step has to stitch together millions of return traces—but only G prototypes per prompt!\n\n---

## Why Does It Work Cheaply?  

| Target | Classic PPO | GRPO |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Value network | ≈ same parameter count | ✗ |
| Reward normalisation | learn via VNet | trivial mean/σ computation |
| Memory footprint | ≈ 800 GB total at FP16 | < 200 GB @ FP16 |
| Training speed | slowed by extra backward pass | +33 % throughput |

The real savings come from two intertwined facts:\n\n> **Group Baseline ≈ Accurate Enough** – By sampling enough completions (\(G=64\)), normalising reforms noise inherently present in any single answer’s score.\n> **Same Forward Pass Count** – Every sampled output still traverses attention once; we simply cut out additional layers.\n> **No Two‑Stage Backward Pass** – The critic would require another forward–backward pass; eliminating it cuts compute demand almost proportionally.\n\nIn practice DeepSeekMath’s team reported that switching from classic PPO reduced GPU memory consumption by roughly four times while keeping barrier functions identical.\n\n---

## Empirical Outcomes That Matter  

Even though GRPO was trained *only* on GSM8k/MATH instruction examples (~144k CS questions), it delivered dramatic gains versus purely supervised models:\n\n* **GSM8K (chain-of-thought)** – Improved from **82.9 %** → **+5 pp → 88.2 %** (`DeepSeekMath↑RL`).  
* **MATH (competition level)** – Improved from **46.8 %** → **+4 pp → 51.7 %** (`DeepSeekMath↑RL`).  
These numbers match or exceed several larger open-source baselines such as Llama~70B or InternLM20B fine-tuned with RL variants yet remain well below closed solutions like GPT‑4 still near sixty cherry percent without tool usage.\n\nBecause GRPO only reinforced training data valid under SFT settings, out-of-distribution benchmarks also benefited:\n\n| Benchmark | Pre‑SFT Accuracy | After GRPO |
|-----------|------------------|------------|
| CMATH     | ~84∼85 %         | ↑3–5 pp     |
| SAT       | ~41∼44 %         | ↑4–6 pp     |\n\nAcross five structured quantitative tasks alike across English × Chinese locales—an impressive testament all holistically improved mathematical reasoning brought purely by lightweight reinforcement.\n\n---

## Takeaways & Where Do We Go Next?  

1️⃣ **Critic vs Group Baseline:** When downstream rewards are largely channelled at *answer level*, eliminating separate value nets offers real hardware cost reductions without harming signal fidelity.\n2️⃣ **Iterative RL:** Updating both policy *and* reward network sequentially ensures that freshly grounded policies continually receive accurate comparative feedback—a necessary ingredient because otherwise early learned preferences become stale.\n3️⃣ **Process vs Outcome Supervision:** While outcome supervion sufficed here (“bad answer → negative weight”), adding fine-grained stepwise reward could capture deeper quality signals especially helpful for complex proof trees if properly trained.\n4️⃣ **Memory Footprint:** Potentially free up GPUs so labs can explore larger reservoirs of mathematical data—critical because DeepSeekMath argues web-scraped math data outshines arXiv transcripts despite prior expectations.\n5️⃣ **Generalization Limits:** Shape {lowfuel into pathways representing geometry/triangles} remains thin‐reinforced; future data pipelines will target geometrically challenging examples until neural routines catch up with manual solving prowess seen in closed models.\n\n---\n\n#### Bottom line   

For teams who want high-fidelity math capabilities without investing terabytes of VRAM or raw compute dollars into yet another value head network—that’s what *GRPO* delivers—efficiently pushing a tiny LLM towards Gemini-like accuracy when paired with well-curated web math corpora and nuanced instruction tuning procedures.\n\nWhether you run inference fleets merely snackable GPUs or you drive flagship‐scale research engines, adopting the core idea behind Group Relative Policy Optimization may give you first vital mileage before allocating more resources toward bigger architectures.”



# Open Source Is Now Close To GPT‑4 On Competitive Mathematics  

Mathematical reasoning has long been regarded as one of the toughest challenges for large language models (LLMs). Until now every model that could tackle college‑level algebra or calculus was either proprietary (GPT‑4, Gemini Ultra) or far below those standards in the open‑source arena.  
DeepSeekMath breaks through that ceiling by combining three seemingly modest ideas—an enormous web‑derived math corpus, a code‑first training schedule, and a lightweight reinforcement–learning algorithm called **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (GRPO). The result is a *seven‑billion parameter* LLM that scores **~52 %** on the competition–level MATH benchmark, rivaling the best closed‑source models while remaining freely available.



## From Code Wizards to Mathematical Sense‑Makers  

The backbone of DeepSeekMath is **DeepSeek-Coder-Base v1.5**, itself a language model optimized for Python and other programming languages. Training this base model on code before any mathematical text gives it two accidental advantages:

1. **Structured thinking** – coding enforces disciplined step–by–step derivations that translate naturally into symbolic calculus or algebraic manipulation.
2. **Token efficiency** – many programming constructs overlap with mathematical syntax (`+`, `^`, `sin`, etc.), allowing the same weights to learn “code” and “math” in one go.

Afterward the model undergoes *math pre‑training* on **120 billion tokens** mined from Common Crawl—a near seven‑fold increase over previously used corpora such as Minerva’s *OpenWebMath* (~13 B).  
The data collection pipeline uses a fastText classifier trained on high‑quality entries from *OpenWebMath* and then iteratively expands its seed set by manually curating new mathematical domains (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`). Every web page is filtered again so that no snippet appears verbatim in standard benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH), safeguarding against leakage.



### A Highly Multilingual Engine  

Unlike most prior efforts that focus solely on English “grind,” the corpus contains both English and Chinese texts in roughly equal measure, explaining why the same model excels at Gaokao‐style exams where many problems are phrased natively in Mandarin.



## What Makes This Corpus Unique  

| Property | DeepSeekMath Corpus | Competitors |
|----------|---------------------|-------------|
| Size | **120 B** tokens | MathPile \(\sim\)8 B, OpenWebMath \(\sim\)13 B |
| Quality | Inferred through real downstream prompts; scores higher than Proof-Pile-2 after just one epoch | Lower scores even after several passes |
| Language Mix | English + Chinese + others | Mostly English |

The evaluation shows that even when only one epoch is used—a single pass over all tokens—the base model achieves **~36 %** accuracy on competition MATH compared with **~26 %** for Proof-Pile‑2 trained at \(50\) B tokens.



## Self‑Consistency Meets Reinforcement  

After instruction tuning—pairing each problem with chain‐of‐thought or program‐of‐thought explanations—the next boost comes from GRPO fine‑tuning:

- **No critic network** – unlike PPO the baseline is picked as the *average reward of a small group* of outputs sampled from the same prompt.
- **Per­token advantages only where needed** – rewards are applied after each logical step when using process supervision.
- **Memory savings** – eliminating a critic reduces GPU RAM demands by roughly \(30\%\), allowing deeper networks or longer sequences without extra cost.

|-----------|-----------|-----------|

Deeper comparability requires some adjustments (prompt format variations), but across the board DeepSeekMath lands at least *five percent* ahead of every peer within its size class.



### Why Break at This Point?  

Two core drivers highlight why this progress happens now rather than twenty years ago:

1. **Supply-side explosion of workable math web pages** – massive public crawl releases give us clean natural language explanations tied directly back to queries.
2. **Economies at scale combined with smarter RL** – GRPO requires far fewer GPU hours than traditional PPO because it sidesteps listening for an auxiliary value network.



## Limitations & Which Work Topics Still Remain Hard  

* Geometry & proof generation still trail GPT‑4 by ~10–12%.  
* Very large problems requiring nested data structures may slip under current memory budgets even though code training helps mitigate this threat marginally.
* While Chinese benchmarks profit heavily from lexical overlap (“加,” “平方”), non‑roman scripts obviously remain understudied.



## Looking Ahead Together  

1. **ArXiv no longer useful?**—our experiments show arXiv papers don't raise arithmetic accuracy substantially for moderate‐size LMs de facto.
2. **Dynamic Sampling & Tree Search** – optimistic experiments show deeper beam search might help near-future releases without requiring extra memory thanks to `speculative decoding`.
3.The unified RL framework presented here encourages new variants—e.g., learning *process-wise` rewards directly rather than scalar outputs— which could bring us another few points across all tasks.

In short, while DeepSeekMath does not yet rival every ability of GPT‑4 across all mathematics domains, it proves firmly that an open-source foundation can climb into *full marks territory* merely through careful netizen-curated data mining and some clever policy refinement.

# Geometry & Formal Proofs Still Challenge Large Language Models

Recent releases like **DeepSeekMath‑7B** show that open‑source language models can now compete with commercial giants on many numeric‑reasoning benchmarks – a fact that belies a persistent blind spot when it comes to problems that depend on spatial intuition or formal logical rules. This blog dives into why geometry puzzles featuring triangles or ellipses and automated theorem–proving in Isabelle remain hard roadblocks for modern LLMs, even after aggressive pre‑training and reinforcement learning pipelines such as “Group Relative Policy Optimization” (GRPO).

---

## What DeepSeekMath Is Good At

| Benchmark | Metric | Rough Score |
|-----------|--------|------------|
| **GSM8K** | Chain‑of‑Thought accuracy | ~88 % |
| **MATH** | Competition‑level accuracy | ~51 % |
| **Self‑Consistency** | Δ(TP rate) | +9 % → 61 % |

DeepSeekMath was trained by extending a code‑focused 7 B‑parameter model with **120 B tokens** drawn from Common Crawl—filtered through a fastText classifier initialized from 0.5 M high‑quality math snippets and fine‑tuned over four iterations. A second stage of instruction tuning added over **776 K example “question–answer” pairs** spanning both English and Chinese mathematics across algebra, probability, number theory, calculus and higher‐level topics.

The large pre‑training corpus gives the decoder a *massive token budget* far exceeding what prior academic works devoted specifically to mathematics (e.g., OpenWebMath’s 13 B tokens). Moreover, starting from a code pre‑training baseline appears to give an extra boost even when no tools are allowed at inference time – a curious hint that programmatic syntax may sharpen pure symbolic manipulation skills.

---

## Why Geometry Is Still an Outlier

### Distributional Gaps While Scraping

The entire filter hinges on a superclass “math” tag learned via fastText from a modest seed collection (OpenWebMath). While this yields high recall for projective geometrical pages—think *math.stackexchange*—there are several ways the dataset can leave holes:

1. **Specialized Subdomains Underrepresented**  
   Because most webpages covering Euclidean figures (triangles, circles, ellipses) are either textbook excerpts in scanned PDFs or lecture notes wrapped inside PDFs—both *non‑HTML* sources—the classifier missed them entirely.
   
2. **Syntax Noise in Diagram Descriptions**  
   Even when HTML pages contain trigonometric identities or vector formulations, their textual form is riddled with LaTeX hacks (“`\\sin\\theta`”) interspersed with plain English explanations—making it hard for a token‑based classifier to pick up pure geometric intent without heavy preprocessing.
   
3. **Closed Loops Between Text & Visuals**  
   Geometry problems almost always rely on an accompanying figure. A purely text‑based corpus can never capture this joint representation unless we embed diagram descriptors as tokens sequentially—a recipe Hardeman et al., but unfortunately Common Crawl data rarely provide those explicit annotations at scale.
   
Because of these causes the evaluation report admits that during an out‑of‑sample test our model “can’t handle problems related to triangles and ellipses.” The sentence *“Geometric reasoning remains imperfect”* is essentially a side effect of *data sparsity*, not algorithmic failure per se.

### Formal Proof Automation Is Marginally Better But Still Lagging

On miniF2F we saw DeepSeekMath generate plausible Isabelle sketches followed by Sledgehammer‐filled steps:

```
example' : 
proof-
  show ?thesis 
    by metis 
qed 
```

The overall success rate is higher than raw language baselines yet below GPT‑4’s performance reported in the same benchmark suite (~70 %). Two factors hinder progress here:

| Problem | Likely Bottleneck |
|---------|-------------------|
| Rigid syntax | No dedicated tokeniser learns `simp`, `use` patterns unless explicitly annotated |
| Structured proof tree | RL rewards operate per “output string” instead of finegrained proof tree nodes |

Because GRPO supplies rewards only at whole‑output or step boundaries (outcome vs process supervision), it lacks guidance about *intermediate* subgoals typical of tactic scripts (`apply rule`, `case_split`). The result is more segmentation errors than semantic errors—proof sketches collapse around unsupported constants rather than stuck at mistaken logical steps.

---

## What GRPO Actually Tackles (and Where It Falls Short)

Active RL via GRPO modifies policy logits based on *relative* advantage scores—using group averages rather than learned value functions—to reduce memory usage by an order of magnitude compared with standard PPO:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{GRPO}(\theta)
\propto \!\! \sum_{i=1}^{G}\!\!\frac{1}{|\mathbf{o}_i|}
          \sum_{t}\!\min\!\Big[
             \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t})}\,
             \hat{A}_{i,t},
             \text{clip}(\ldots)\,
             \hat{A}_{i,t}
        \Big]
          -\beta\,D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})
\]

In practice GRPO gives a notable jump:
```
GSM8K CelS 82 → 88%
MATH    46 → 52%
```
but these increments come primarily from cleaner top‑k answers rather than deeper leaps in deductive capabilities like manipulating trigonometric identities across slides or generating middle steps in formal proof scripts.

---

## Paths Toward Closing the Gap

Below are concrete ideas framed within constraints already respected by the paper’s experiments:

1. **Targeted Data Augmentation**
   - Manually fetch geometry textbooks’ HTML versions (`stmeth.edu`, `acm.org/geometry`) where all formulas are embedded via MathML.
   - Crawl educational video transcripts paired with captioned diagrams – convert image coordinates into textual histogram sequences before feeding them into training pipelines.
  
2. **Tokenisation Enhancements**
   - Build a *geometry-aware subword tokenizer*: whenever `∠x` appears next to `a²+b²=c²`, cluster them so that inference preserves spatial relationships.
   - Train mini hierarchy vocabularies that tag symbols (`circle_center`, `radius`) separately from operations (`cos`, `tan`).

3. **Decoding With Diagram Retrieval**
   - Combine Seq2Seq generation with lightweight retrieval modules that fetch formatted diagrams based on keywords (`isosceles triangle`) before solving.
   - Evaluate how persistent diagrams alter probability distributions during generation (`beam search` conditioned on diagram features).

4. **Fine-Grained Process Rewards**
   - Expand reward models from outcome-only signals to *stepwise* rewards derived from partial correctness checks against textbook solutions or theorem prover intermediate states.
   - Use VAE‐style latent representations of intermediate states so the RL agent learns continuity constraints across steps—a technique analogous to deterministic policy gradients used in robotics learning loops.

5. **Robustness Tests Against Distractors**
   - Generate synthetic draughtboard examples where subtle changes alter answer values; evaluate whether models overfit pattern matching versus true symbolic logic handling.
   - Measure *pass@k* versus *maj@k* metrics separately—true breakthroughs should improve both top‐k precision and mean rank confidence across diverse test sets beyond Hermann et al.’s dataset biases.

6. **Explicit Rule‑Based Fine Tuning**
   - Pair existing reinforcement signals with hand-crafted logical rules (e.g., Pythagorean theorem $a^2+b^2=c^2$) used as per-token penalty budgets—simple prohibitive constraints help suppress hallucinated dependencies often seen when solving multistep equations incorrectly typed into quilted responses.

## Takeways

*Even superlative open-source LLMs excel spectacularly at numerical problems:* near–collector GPT playground markup wizardry aside—themselves beating their peers by more than ten percentage points per metric on GSM8K/MATH without torching their beginning weight budgets due to innovative RL tricks like GRPO.*

However,…the remaining fractures lie very specifically along two axes:

1. **Geometric Intuition** – The scarcity of well‐structured geometrical text paired with visual descriptors fuels bias against triangle/ellipse reasoning even after seven billion trainable parameters have lit up 120 billion tokens scraped from public web archives.
   
2. **Formal Logic Reasoning** – Formal theorem proving demands disciplined symbol manipulation chain workflows absent full depth supervision; current reward structures reward entire outputs instead of stepwise logic nodes leading mainly only to marginal statistical gains rather than actual problem-solving maturity.


The solution path is clear room rights-side stability/quality data creep—increasing quality through controlled expert-curated subdomains—for both diagrammatic layouts and proof agendas—payload often overlooked when scraping only free-form HTML parsed by unsupervised ngram classifiers will continue moving forward.

> _Bottom line_: computing wizardry makes your equations crisp as silk—but open vaccine architecture will keep missing those shapes that demand pictures or step-by-step theorem confirmation until we learn how best *to feed*, *measure*, & *reinforce* those nuanced patterns.\
