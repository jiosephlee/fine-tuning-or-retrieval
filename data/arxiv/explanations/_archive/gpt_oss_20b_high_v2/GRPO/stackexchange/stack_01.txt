\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the iterative fastText pipeline expand the size of the DeepSeekMath Corpus?
Question:
In Section 2.1 the authors describe an iterative process where an initial fastText model trained on OpenWebMath seed examples is used to recall candidate pages from Common Crawl, after which highly scored pages are added back into the seed corpus for a second round of training. I am unclear why repeating this cycle increases both diversity and quantity of mathematical content rather than merely re‑sampling already‑captured pages. Does adding manually annotated URLs from specific domains such as mathoverflow.net help change the feature distribution that fastText learns, or is there another mechanism that reduces near‑duplication?
Answer:
The “iterative fastText” loop in Section 2.1 is essentially a bootstrap‑and‑retrain cycle that keeps expanding the scope of what the classifier can recognise as *math* and at the same time prevents it from merely re‑sampling the same documents again.



---

### What the first round does

1. **Seed corpus** – OpenWebMath gives a handful of high‑quality math pages.  
2. **FastText training** – With half a million positives (the seeded text) and half a million negatives taken randomly from Common Crawl, a shallow supervised model is trained to output a probability that a page is math‑like.
3. **Recall & filtering** – The model scores every URL in a deduplicated Common Crawl snapshot (~40000000000 URLs).  
   Only the highest‑scoring tables are kept—usually the cut‑off is chosen so that about $40000000000$ tokens survive (~10 % of every domain).
4. **Deduplication** – Before any scores can be considered, identical or nearly identical documents have already been removed by URL–based as well as near‑duplicate hashing procedures taken out earlier when shrinking public Common Crawl to 40000000000 web pages.



After this pass you have a sizeable collection but it still contains large gaps: many legal math webpages were never scored highly enough because their vocabulary (LaTeX macros, uncommon symbols, or specialised terminology) was underrepresented in the positives used to train fastText.



---

### Why going again helps

When you run another round you **do two things** that both push the boundary:


#### 1. Add truly new positives
• Domains that had more than <em>0</em>.100 recovered in round <em>1</em> are flagged as “math‑related” (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`).  
• For each such domain you manually mark sub‑paths that almost certainly contain mathematics (`/questions`, `/blog`, `/faq`).  
• Every page linked under those paths **that was not already kept** in round <em>1</em> becomes part of the updated seed set.



Because these URLs were never seen before during training, their linguistic surface—and especially rare tokenised LaTeX fragments—introduces new features into fastText’s vocabulary and allows its n‑gram embeddings to cover new patterns (“equiv mod”, “sinc”, “$\mathbb{Z}$”).

#### 2. Retrain on an enlarged positive set
The next fastText instance now trains on **both** the original positives *and* all freshly added ones—roughly twice as many distinct documents and far richer token statistics.



This shifts its decision boundary toward:


* broader headline vocabulary (“holomorphic”, “Lefschetz”),  
* wider syntactic patterns (structured code blocks inside `<pre>` tags),  
* more varied formatting cues (MathJax scripts).

With this broadened feature space, when you query Common Crawl again many previously marginally–scored sites jump above threshold—ones whose headlines never appeared in round <em>1</em> at all—so you capture completely different portions of mathematical web space.



---

### How duplication is avoided

Because deduplication is performed **before** each recall batch:


* Any document already kept has its URL fingerprint removed from the candidate pool.  
* When new fastText scores are computed, only *unseen* URLs are evaluated &  	
* Even if two different domains happened to host identical content (common in mirrors or static blogs), fragment‑hashing removes them before they can be returned again.


Thus each iteration adds genuinely unseen material rather than just recombining what has already been remembered.



---

### Summary

Repeating the cycle enlarges both quantity and diversity because:


| Step                                 | Effect |
|--------------------------------------|--------|
| Manual domain links → fresh positives | Captures classes of sites absent in initial seed |
| Retraining fastText on larger & newer positives | Moves decision boundary toward unseen vocab & patterns |
| Score + deduplicate                    | Only pulls up yet-uncollected URLs |


Consequently after four iterations you end up with $35000000$ unique web pages ($120\times 109^{9}$ tokens). Each pass progressively widens what counts as “math,” pulling in corner cases rather than simply retracing your first steps.*
