Motivation Behind DeepSeekMath  

# Bridging performance gap while staying open‑source  
The most immediate driver behind DeepSeekMath was the observation that world‑class closed‑source language models—most notably GPT‑4 and Gemini Ultra—exhibit remarkable proficiency on rigorous mathematics benchmarks whereas publicly available alternatives lag far behind despite often matching or exceeding them in size or compute budget gaps remain wide even among recent large‑scale open‑source projects such as Llama‑2 70 B or InternLM 20 B [3–5].  
The motivation was therefore dual‑folded: first, eliminate this divide so researchers worldwide could experiment freely with state‑of‑the‑art reasoning capabilities; second, expose an exemplary framework that associates corpus engineering, specialized pre‐training, instruction tuning, and efficient reinforcement learning into a coherent pipeline for others desiring comparable outcomes without proprietary barriers.

To accomplish this goal three pillars were established explicitly—(i) create the largest ever curated arithmetic corpus extracted purely from public web data, (ii) demonstrate how prior exposure to programming functionally enhances generalizable problem solving even when architecture limits prevent full attunement between algebraic logic and decomposition strategies, and finally (iii) design an optimisation objective that discards heavy auxiliary components yet preserves their expressive benefits.*

These steps together produced an open‐source seven billion parameter system whose self‑contained chain‐of‐thought accuracy reaches **51 %** on competition‐level MATH—an achievement only slightly below GPT‑4’s performance—and surpasses all administrators possessing substantially larger modalities.*  

# Large‑scale high‑quality math corpus creation  
The core enabling resource was an extensively engineered mining pipeline directed toward Common Crawl dumps—a massive reservoir ordinarily dominated by noisy conversational media—but is punctuated by mathematically fluent articles verbatim extracted via a fastText classifier*.  

Initial seed signals came through OpenWebMath—a modest corpus rich in clean elementary textbooks and focused proofs—and represented positive samples throughout classification training ω priorization ℓ₁ = 0 and negatives derived sidewise from random Common Crawl downloads nominally devoid of algebraic structure*.  
Each second iteration incrementally broadened class boundaries through manual inspection acclaiming domains recognized explicitly during inference—for example browsing **mathoverflow.net**, extracting subsection URLs resembling `questions`, “answers”, “post”. These enriched seeds subsequently refreshed classifier parameters calibrated orthogonally over train epochs needing two passes over~300 million labeled snippets* (§3 “Data Collection”).  

With deduplicate heuristics cutting request duplication down markedly—a combination of URL hashing complemented by whole document hashing—the curated page set shrank cleanly into ~40 billion HTML entries ready for model scoring via fastText’s probabilistic compatibility score function *\(S(p)=w_0 + w_1·∑_i α_i·f_i(p)\)* where words encode stylized formulas*.*  
A validation procedure bounded through multi–round test set evaluations retains only top–ranked fractions saturating effective token budgets of ‑40 billion → ‑80 billion → ‑120 billion → ‑160 billion*, ultimately delivering **35 million pages equivalent_to 120 billion tokens**, vastly superseding previously available curated sets (*OpenWebMath* ≈ 13 billion).  

This corpus proved its mettle via systematic downstream investigations contrasting it against MathPile (*∼9 billion*) , Proof‐Pile‑2 (*≈42 billion* including arXiv emission), sparsely constructed Mixed corpora annotated previous works—all yielding consistently better pipeline metrics when tightly integrated during pre‑training evidencing higher semantic density across both English and Chinese contexts.*

# Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus  

Mathematical reasoning demands data that is dense with formal expressions,
rigorous deductions, and a wide variety of problem settings – plain web
pages rarely supply this richness at scale without careful filtering and
curation.  The DeepSeek team addressed this challenge by mining public
Common Crawl archives for mathematics‑heavy text through an iterative
pipeline incorporating supervised classification,
deduplication, domain‑guided enrichment,
benchmark decontamination,
and a focused multilingual analysis.  
The resulting “DeepSeekMark” corpus amounts to roughly **120 billion**
tokens extracted from **35 million** distinct webpages,
outstripping existing open‑source mathematical corpora by an order of
magnitude while preserving linguistic breadth beyond English alone.



---

## Seed corpus selection using OpenWebMath  

The starting point was **OpenWebMath**, an already-proven collection of
high‑quality mathematical web texts curated from subject forums,
tutorial sites and teaching portals.\footnote{\href{https://openwebmath.org}{openwebmath.org}}  
Its dense coverage spans algebraic manipulation, calculus proofs,
combinatorial arguments and more – exactly the sort of language patterns
that guide downstream models toward coherent deduction steps when prompted 
with chain‑of‑thought or program‑of‑thought formats.\par

Using OpenWebMath serves two purposes:

1. **Positive exemplars**– it supplies well‑structured sentences that
encourage a lightweight learner (such as FastText) to build internal vectors
aligned with equations (\(x^2+4xy+y^2=0\)), roster expansions (\(\sum_{i=1}^n i = n(n+1)/2\)), born‑by‑hook grammar (“for every \(x\)”), etc.;  
2. **Recall bridge**– it anchors our subsequent retrieval processes 
so that scarce mathematics signals become discoverable amid billions 
of unrelated webpages.\par



## FastText classifier training workflow  

Once positives were known, Unsurprisingly,
the task reduces to scanning billions of english‐origin webpages,
classifying each candidate page independently as *mathematics* or *not*.  
The authors employed **FastText**, an efficient supervised shallow‐network framework\footnote{\href{https://fasttext.cc}{fasttext.cc}}
based on linear classifiers over character/word n‑grams.\par

### Training set construction  
* **Positive examples** – Randomly select \$500\,000\$ snapshots from
OpenWebMath’s body text.\par  
* **Negative examples** – Randomly sample an equal number (\$500\,000\$) 
from raw Common Crawl dumps where no mathematics tag exists.\par

### Hyperparameters  
| Item | Value |
|------|-------|
| Vector dimension | \$256\$ |
| Learning rate | \$0.1\$ |
| Max word \(n\)-gram | \$3\$ |
| Min word occurrence | \$3\$ |
| Epochs | \$3\$ |

The resulting model learns compact word representations capturing
the frequent symbolic patterns (“$\frac{}{}$”, “$\forall$”) that denote
mathematical prose while ignoring purely natural‐language noise.



## Deduplication strategies applied to Common Crawl  

Common Crawl’s raw pool contains paired URLs covering near‑identical or repeating content – frivolity that would bias learning toward certain media forms.
The authors therefore applied two complementary dedupification stages:\par

1. **URL‑based hashing** – Normalize all URLs by stripping query parameters,
base domain paths are hashed into buckets so duplicate stories (e.g.,
multiple mirrors) collapse into single instances.\par  
2. **Near‑deduplication over page snapshots** – Apply cosine similarity over shingled TF–IDF vectors derived 
from each page’s surface tokens;
pairs exceeding a similarity threshold get merged,\par 

After pruning duplicates across roughly \(4\times10^{10}\) HTML records,
the remaining corpus contained approximately **40 billions** distinct documents,
which served as input population for subsequent FastText-based screening.



## Iterative enrichment loop via manual annotation  

The first FastText round yielded substantial new mathematical pages (\~20 % hit rate among candidates).
However visual inspection revealed systematic gaps –
certain specialized forums (e.g., *mathoverflow.net*) remained largely absent  
because their phrasing deviated slightly from OpenWebMath’s canonical “style”.
To address this limitation a concise differential loop was introduced.



### Domain assessment  
Each remaining page was grouped by its root domain “example.com”.  
If > 10 % of a domain’s seeds were already identified in Physical Computer's positive set,
the entire domain was flagged *potentially maths*.  
Domains such as `mathoverflow.net`, `planetmath.org`, 
`arxiv.org/abs`, etc.’s *question* directories gained attention.



### Manual URL curation  
Human curators examined sliding windows over frequently chosen domains,
labeling specific question feeds (`questions?id=`…) whose textual payloads clearly carried problem statements.\footnote{
Example URL panels inspected: https://mathoverflow.net/questions/222221 ,
https://greynote.com/math/linear-algebra/}



### Re-training step  
All newly annotated passages joined the positive tally;
FastText was re-trained exactly following the previous recipe,
after which it recalled far more nuanced tags—
for example hyperbolic geometry posts now scored high enough
to survive filtering even though their lexical makeup shifted away from verbatim OpenWebMath entries.



Repeating this enrichment cycle four times amassed roughly ***35 million***
unique mathematics webpages totaling about ***120 billion*** tokens—
approximately seven times larger than Minerva’s closest scrapes 
and nine times larger than OpenWebMath itself.



## Benchmark decontamination filtering rules  

Because evaluation sets (GSM8K, MATH‐torchling family) often appeared verbatim on public sites,

a strict **deterministic leaching** rubric was imposed:\par

* For every extracted webpage segment:
    * If any contiguous ten‑gram substring exactly matched any excerpt appearing anywhere inside one of the adults’ test prompts (**GSM8K**, **MATH**, **CMATH**, **AGIEval**),
      the entire segment was discarded;\par  
    * For snippets whose maximal length < 10 words yet comprised ≥ 3 grams found within test sets,
      exact match discard was also triggered.\par

This conservative pass guarantees none of the model’s knowledge during pre‑training accounted 
for future evaluation leakage—a critical condition given seeds were automatically harvested 
from internet contents likely overlapping existing datasets.

# Experimental Protocol & Hyperparameters  

In order to isolate the effect brought by the size and diversity of a source mathematical corpus,
the designers trained three independent language models that differed **only** in their data inputs.
The underlying architecture was an identical \(7\text{-parameter}\) decoder‑only transformer,
referred to as *DeepSeek‑LLM* \(7~\text{B}\), which had received prior exposure to broad‑coverage marginal
language signals generated during standard LLM pre‑training.
All other variables were thus controlled:
the tokenizer, parameter initialization scheme,
context window length (\(4\,\text{k}\) tokens), block‑size (`\(4~\text{m}\)`).

The learning schedule used AdamW \((\beta_{1}=0\.9,\;\beta_{2}=0\.95,\;\mathrm{weight\text{-}decay}=0\.1)\)
with an adaptive arithmetic progression.
After \(2\,000\) warmup steps the learning rate ramped linearly up to its peak value,
then decreased gradually according to  
\(L_{\max}\times[\,31\.6\%\text{},\,10\%\] ,
where \(L_{\max}=5\.3\times10^{-4}\).
This schedule was precisely replicated across all three runs.
For every datum set we maintained an identical batch size and non‑replacement sampling scheme,
thereby making token budget—not computational quirk—a sole differentiator among runs.
This experimentation safeguards against confounding factors such as hardware cache effects,
batch ordering bias or hidden curriculum version differences that could otherwise masquerade as domain advantages.



The three corpora compared were  

* **Proof‑Pile‑2**, comprising textual materials sourced mainly from arXiv plus structured codes extracted from MathOverflow;
* **OpenWebMath**, a curated subset harvested only from Common Crawl identified through heuristic rules;
* **DeepSeekMath**, produced using an iterative fastText classifier seeded by OpenWebMath cases,
followed by developer‑based filtering and human verification.
All three datasets were converted into clean token streams compatible with the tokenizer’s embedding table,
and any passage matching longest–\(10\)-gram snippets found among target evaluation benchmarks was removed  
to prevent leakage.



Since every run involved far more than one epoch over its particular collection,
the entire pre‑training trajectory could be recorded
in terms of cumulative minted token counts:
\(42~\text b,\;48~\text b,\;\ldots,\155~\text b\).
During these stages checkpoints were sampled regularly so that downstream performances could be plotted
against *token budget*. This procedure produced rigorous contrast curves over equal footing.

The selection guarantees an assessment spectrum—from short probabilistic evidence building up through exhaustive formal proofs—that directly reflects how rich curricular sources impact comprehension.

Accuracy scores were computed conventionally as proportion of correctly solved problems among those presented.



# Performance Comparison Against Existing Corpora  

The extensive grid imposed reveals one striking conclusion:
owing almost exclusively to *input diversification*, annotations derived from DeepSeekMath led robustly larger accuracy gains across all eight benchmarks
than did those obtained when training exclusively over Proof‑Pile‑2 or OpenWebMath.
In particular,

* Compared against Proof‑Pile‑2 every metric uplifted more than \(+12~\%\),
even though both corpora drew heavily upon arXiv sources;
  
* When contrasted versus OpenWebMath, which shares extraction pathways with common web,
the multipliers were typically \(+15~\%\) on English quantitative arenas;
  
* Most dramatically—in Chinese‐language navigation—the Difference exceeded \(+20~\%\),
a corroboration that multilingual coverage imbued sharper numeracy comprehension 
in non‐English formats rather than mere rote symbol memorization.



Beyond raw percentage points,
the dynamism embedded precisely in these victories is captured by how quickly gains plateaued.
While Proof‑Pile‑2’s accuracy curve stabilized well before surpassing forty‐billion processed tokens,
DeepSeekMath’s trajectory continued climbing until near its two-hundred-billion boundary,
implying its higher sample variety mitigated overfitting shortfalls inherently caused

# Pre‑Training DeepSeekMath–Base Model  

The DeepSeekMath‑Base model represents the first major milestone toward a strong open‑source language model whose raison d’être is solving mathematical problems with human‑level sophistication while preserving broader language understanding and programming skills.  
Below we walk through every crucial element that turns an ordinary decoder‑only Transformer into a powerful solver of equation sequences, a symbolic programmer capable of interacting seamlessly with Python libraries such as *sympy*, and a gentle collaborator in formal theorem proving tasks performed with Isabelle’s Sledgehammer routine.


# Model architecture foundation  

DeepSeekMath–Base inherits its architectural skeleton directly from **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1․5 7 B**, itself a decoder–only Transformer tuned heavily on programming corpora before receiving mathematical data.
This family shares five hallmark traits that distinguish it from conventional non-code LLMs:

* **Layer Stack:** A compact stack comprising roughly forty-five attention layers, each equipped with multi–head self‐attention (cold-headed heads vary between thirty-two and fifty) feeding forward networks featuring dimensionality four times larger than the hidden size.
* **Embedding Design:** Token embeddings are learned jointly over an alphabet fused from English ASCII characters together with Unicode blocks commonly seen in Chinese mathematics texts (e.g., CJK unified ideographs).
* **Position Encoding:** Instead of separate sinusoids one relies on ALiBi relative position biases amplified linearly over token distance — giving rise to better extrapolation properties useful when forced perhaps impossible sequences exceed trained maximum context lengths.
* **Parameter Scale:** With approximately seven billion trainable parameters the network occupies roughly four megabytes per million weights under FP16 precision — modest compared nevertheless to hundreds‐of‐terabyte scientific baselines.
* **Regularisation:** Dropout rates range between ten–twenty per cent applied right after attention projections guaranteeing noisy gradients that aid exploration during subsequent reinforcement stages.
