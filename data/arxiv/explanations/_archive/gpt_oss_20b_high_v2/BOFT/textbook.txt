Foundations and Motivation  

# Magnitude Growth ↕️ ⇝ Billions‑Parameter Transformers  
The rapid increase in depth, width, and branching factor of transformer architectures has produced models whose capacity now spans hundreds of billions of trainable parameters. A striking example is GPT‑3, which contains approximately \(1.75\times10^{11}\) weights — a leap well beyond earlier language models such as GPT‑2 or BERT [Brown et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021]. Visual backbones like ViT‑Large and Stable Diffusion’s U‑Net likewise occupy tens to hundreds of billions of weights when instantiated at their largest scales. This exponential scaling follows roughly a power law relating performance improvements to model size for tasks ranging from language modeling to image synthesis [Radford et al., 2021]. As an effect of this growth, baseline computational requirements for training an entire model from scratch rise from weeks on dozens of GPUs to months on hundreds or thousands of GPUs equipped with high-bandwidth interconnects. Energy consumption and carbon emissions consequently become major environmental concerns—entire day-long runs at large scale are reported to generate several kilotonnes of CO₂ equivalents [Rombach et al., 2022].

# Challenges of Full-Scale Pre‑Training  
Training a foundation model end-to-end demands coordinated chiselling across three broad dimensions: (1) **Hardware scalability**, requiring clusters that support petascale data parallelism while incurring nontrivial overhead on communication and memory hierarchies; (2) **Algorithmic robustness**, needing numerical stability against exploding gradients at extreme depths while preserving the delicate balance between underfitting and overfitting across millions of data points; (3) **Data curation and bias mitigation**, where massive corpora must be shuffled into coherent syntactic–semantic distributions without compromising privacy or injecting systematic biases [Qiu et al., 2023]. Each dimension interacts with environmental costs and potential hardware failure rates—the more layers added or the more mixture-of-experts partitions inserted, the steeper the training curve becomes in terms both of wall-clock time and memory footprint. Consequently, even if an organization possesses sufficient compute budget, health‑related constraints such as cooling capacity or power delivery often become bottlenecks that limit maximum attainable model size in practice.



# The Role of Downstream Task Adaptation  
The core insight behind “transfer learning” is that early transformer layers learn general-purpose representations—syntactic patterns for language or low-level texture detectors for vision—that are reusable across many end tasks once contextualized by higher layers [Brown et al., 2020]. Instead of re-training an entire gigantic mask from scratch for every new task (e.g., sentiment analysis, question answering, style transfer), practitioners typically preserve most knowledge embodied in pretrained weights while tweaking a small subset relevant to the new objective.  This localized modification offers two crucial benefits:
1. **Sample efficacy** – With fewer trainable parameters you need far fewer examples to converge meaningfully when labeled data are scarce.
2. **Computational economy** – Updating only clockwork placeholders dramatically cuts GPU usage during fine-tuning: forward-backward passes cost little more than inference time once most weights are frozen.


Downstream domain shifts—such as transitioning from purely textual inputs to multimodal scenarios—often require retraining internal attention mechanisms or feed‑forward blocks so that learned features align with new encodings or production pipelines [Zaken et al., 2022]. Even sophisticated generative repositories like stable diffusion benefit from tuned denoising steps whose parameters are sub‑inverted by small factorisation layers [Qiu et al., 2023]. In short, downstream adaptation endpoints on an intuitive economic horizon where compute time scales quadratically with network depth while memory serves as a limiting bottleneck.



# Categories of Efficient Finetuning Approaches  
Researchers have introduced multiple practical frameworks designed expressly to reduce trainable surface area without sacrificing accuracy on target tasks. These paradigms mainly diverge along how they introduce *regularity*, *inductive bias*, or *external parameter additions* onto an otherwise frozen backbone.



## Model Finetuning  
Traditional ``model finetuning'' keeps architectural hyperparameters intact but optimises only a tiny fraction—often less than one percent—of existing weights using minuscule learning rates [Huloran et al., 2022]. This low lr simply discourages changes too great relative to initialised values (£¶), effectively regularising by weight proximity rather than explicit loss geometry.



## Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)  
Within this umbrella category sits two representative families:

* **Reparameterisation-based PEFT** – Key examples include Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which expresses weight deviations as bilinear low-rank tensors \([Xiao & Widrow,\dots]\). Here extra trainable strands \(A\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) and \(B\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\) enrich each frozen matrix \(\mathbf{W}_0\) through \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_0 + AB^\top\). The rank parameter \(r\ll d,n\) keeps swaps economically light while providing theoretically grounded expressivity scans [Huloran et al., 2022].

* **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** – Proposed by Qiu et al., OFT replaces additive updates with multiplicative right-multiplication by an orthogonal guard \(\mathbf{R}\): \(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_0\). Optimising \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\) preserves pairwise angles between neuron vectors enabling spectral norm stability—an essential property often disrupted under conventional fine-tunings [Qiu et al., 2023].



Both reparameterisation paradigms rely on sparse structural decompositions such as block-diagonal matrices \( \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r)\). While block-diagonal OFT easily reduces evaluated degrees-of-freedom from \(d^2\) down to \(bd\,(\approx d r)\), it suffers inherent inductive bias because neuron spaces are forced into disjoint spatial islands defined solely by index order.



## Adapter Tuning  
When computational budgets are constrained even further or when latency must stay zero during inference, adapters insert learnable micro-networks between existing layers instead of shifting huge weights directly [Houlsby et al., 2019]. Each adapter comprises minimal gates such as projectionless bottleneck transforms combined with layer-normalised residuals—a compliant recipe yielding almost identical accuracy while keeping integration overhead negligible.



## Prompt Tuning  
Carry over into Transformer architecture prepping via *prefix embeddings* placed before any token sequence [Lester et al.; Li et al.] – essentially assigning learnable “prompt tokens” at embedding-level files rather than at weight-level fields. Prompt tuning keeps every underlying weight untouched yet lets retrieval mechanisms adapt via prompt injection during inference inside linear projection spaces.



Despite differences in methodic philosophy—block versus continuous parameter update—the overarching theme remains identical across all perspectives: **dense knowledge should be transferred efficiently without drowning GPUs**.

# Background on Parameter‑Efficient Adaptation  

Large foundation models such as GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion contain hundreds of billions of weights and demand prohibitive amounts of compute if one wishes to retrain them from scratch for every downstream task.  Consequently the field has converged on *parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning* (PEFT), i.e., techniques that adjust only a small fraction of the pretrained coefficients while leaving most network operations untouched during inference.  In this introductory chapter we survey three representative PEFT families—low‑rank reparameterisations, additive adapter modules and prefix/text‐prompt tuning—and analyse their strengths and limitations in terms of flexibility versus computational cost.



# Low‑rank reparameterizations such as LoRA  

LoRA is a particularly popular reparameterisation strategy that augments each frozen linear transformation \(\bm W^0\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\) with an *additive* correction that is expressed as a product of two low‑rank matrices:
\[
\Delta \bm W \;\widehat{=}\;\mathbf U\,\mathbf V^{\!\top}, \qquad 
\mathbf U\in \mathbb R^{d\times r},~\mathbf V \in \mathbb R^{n\times r},
\]
with \(r \ll \min(d,n)\).  
Because only \(2dr\) scalar values are trained instead of \(dn\), LoRA enjoys very modest storage requirements even when integrated depthwise into all transformer layers.  
Its simplicity stems from retaining **exactly** the original network topology; during forward passes the computed outputs simply involve two sparse matrix–vector multiplications rather than altering any fundamental connection pattern between neurons.\footnote{The idea embraces earlier notions such as “intrinsic dimensionality” variants wherein ranks can be adapted per layer [zhang2022adaptive], [valipour2022dylora]}.  

A key intuition behind low‐rank updates is that many natural weight matrices lie near a low–dimensional manifold when viewed through local perturbations around their pretrained values—a hypothesis evidenced empirically by large language models where rank\,\(r=128\) often suffices after appropriate compression heuristics have been applied [zhang2022adaptive].  
In practice this translates into reduced wall‐time during gradient optimisation because backpropagation involves operations proportional only to \(r\), inversely scaling both memory footprint and runtime compared with conventional full-fine-tuning.



# Additive adapter modules embedded into layers  

Another class replaces a fraction of network capacity by lightweight “adapters” inserted elementwise inside existing layers.  An adapter typically comprises a pairwise feedforward bottleneck:
\[
\mathrm{Adapter}(\mathbf h)=\sigma(\mathbf h\,\mathbf W_1 + \mathbf b_1)\,\mathbf W_2 + \mathbf b_2,
\]
where \(\mathbf W_{1}\in \mathbb R^{d\times d'} , d'\ll d,\) so the linear maps carry far fewer degreesOf freedom than their host layer while still facilitating non‐linear transformation.\footnote{Concrete designs have appeared throughout recent literature — see [houlsby2019parameter] for early transformer adapters and subsequent refinements such as PFEIHER’s anchor–fusion strategy [pfeiffer2020adapterfusion].}  

Adapters offer an intermediate **expressivity** between plain frozen weights and fully fine–tuned networks because they can modulate activation patterns via learned gates distributed throughout every block instead of simply globally relocating neuron angles through an orthogonal rotation or simply adding noise uniformly across all synapses.\footnote{The approach resonates with conceptions underlying “sparse training” where selected units receive enhanced weight adjustments while others remain fixed—but adapted here after pretraining rather than learned de novo.]}
However deploying an adapter incurs extra computational load during inference since every transformer call now materialises an additional feedforward traversal – albeit modest when \(d'\) is chosen sufficiently small.



# Prefix/token prompt tuning  

Prefix learning shifts computation entirely outwards from neural weights toward auxiliary input tokens whose embeddings become learnable during fine‑tuning while remaining static thereafter.\footnote{Early works demonstrated prefixes are especially effective when attached ahead of language fields at spliced steps ([li2021prefix],[lester2021power]).}  
During forward propagation these $p$ new embeddings simply extend the sequence representation before it reaches any hidden unit:
\[
(\mathbf x,\;\textbf{p}_{1},\,…,\,\textbf{p}_{p}) \;\to\;\texttt{Transformer}(\cdot),
\]
where all other model weights stay frozen forever after initialization by copy.\ Good prompts preserve structural properties intrinsic code glues arbitrary semantics—so limited cardinality ($p$ often $<10$) trivially lowers parameter load.\n 

The principal attraction lies almost entirely within **inference efficiency**: once trained prefixes are baked into input sequences there is no change in internal tensor operations or time spent per token beyond storing these few extra embeddings—a negligible overhead relative both memory footprint (§13) *and* operation counts (§14).  
Nonetheless prompt-length expansion does increase memory usage linearly with $p$, potentially troublesome on hardware constrained devices unable to hold extended contexts even though computation does not scale.

Orthogonality‑Based Finetuning Principles

# Definition \(W'=RW_0\) with \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) Orthogonal  
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a pretrained linear layer represented by its weight matrix \(W_0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is reparameterised during downstream adaptation as  
\[
W' \;=\; R\,W_0,
\]
where \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an orthogonal matrix satisfying \(R^{\top}R=RR^{\top}=I_d.\)  The forward computation of a single layer thus changes from  
\(z=(W_0)^\top x\) to  
\(z=(RW_0)^\top x,\)  
with input vector \(x\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and output vector \(z\in \mathbb{R}^n.\)  Since every column of \(W'\) is simply a rotated version of the corresponding column of \(W_0,\) the learning process now consists solely of determining how best to rotate all neurons within a given layer so that their collective behaviour satisfies new task objectives while preserving correlations present in pretraining data.  Crucially, every neuron shares the same transformation matrix \(R,\) guaranteeing a uniform, global relabelling of dimension space rather than independent parameter updates per neuron.

# Zero Initialization via Identity  
A central goal in parameter‑efficient finetuning is to keep early training dynamics stable and close to the pretrained baseline so that task‑specific signals are added rather than overwhelming learned representations.  In OFT this optically intuitive requirement is satisfied by initializing \(R\) exactly as an identity matrix:
\[
R \;\coloneqq\; I_d.
\]
With this choice, at epoch zero the adapted weights equal their pretrained counterparts exactly (\(W'=W_0\)), meaning no change whatsoever has occurred yet and all pairwise neuronal relationships remain identical.  Subsequent gradient steps gradually deform an initially flat manifold—with each deformation restricted to remain within the set of valid orthogonal matrices—thus preserving pre‑existing knowledge while allowing modest expressive adjustments suited for downstream tasks.



# Cayley Parametrisation  
Maintaining exact orthogonality during optimization requires a parameterisation that automatically enforces it throughout training iterations without imposing expensive explicit constraints at every step.  OFT adopts the Cayley transform,
which writes any special orthogonal matrix as
\[
R \;=\; (I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1},
\]
where \(Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is skew‑symmetric (\(Q=-Q^\top\)).  This construction has two elegant properties:

1. **Every resulting \(R\)** satisfies \(R^\top R=RR^\top=I_d,\)** since Cayley’s map sends skew‑symmetric matrices bijectively onto all rotation matrices except reflections by det = –1 (which remain excluded if required).

2. **The parameters to be optimised** reduce to the independent components of \(Q,\)** whose dimensionality is \(\tfrac12\,d(d-1)\).  Updating these free parameters using standard gradient‑descent automatically guarantees that every intermediate choice stays on an exact orthogonal manifold.

Zero initialization translates into setting all entries of \(Q\) initially to zero – precisely correspond­ing to an identity transformation – after which optimisers can perturb this point while always respecting orthogonality.



# Angle Preservation Guarantees  
Orthogonal transformations possess an exceptionally clean geometric interpretation: they are composed solely of rotations and reflections which preserve Euclidean distances and triangle angles within any vector space they act upon.  Consequently for any two columns \(\mathbf{w}_i^0,\mathbf{w}_j^0\) of the pretrained weight matrix,
their inner product remains untouched under application of any orthogonal matrix:
\[
(\mathbf{w}_i^{'})^\top \mathbf{w}_j^{'}  
= (\!\,RW_0)_i^\top(\!\,RW_0)_j
= (\!\mathbf{w}_i^{0})^\top R^\top R\,(\mathbf{w}_j^{0})
= (\!\mathbf{w}_i^{0})^\top(\mathbf{w}_j^{0}). 
\]
The last equality follows directly from \(R^\top R = I_d.\)

Because inner products uniquely determine angles between vectors—a fact given by
\( \cos \theta_{ij}=\frac{\langle \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\rVert\,\|\mathbf{w}_j\rVert }, \)
it follows immediately that every pair of neurons keeps exactly its original angle throughout OFT training:
\( \theta_{ij}'=\theta_{ij}. \)

Preserving these inter‑neuron geometries affords two practical benefits exploited by OFT:

1. **Retention of Semantic Knowledge:** Features extracted by neurons in earlier layers are often highly correlated or complementary tasks derived from pretraining data distribution patterns (such as language syntax or visual texture). Maintaining their mutual relations prevents catastrophic forgetting and keeps high‑level abstract knowledge intact while still letting overall representation shift globally under rotation.
   
2. **Spectral Stability:** Because rotations leave singular values invariant, spectral norms—critical for guarding against exploding or vanishing gradients—are preserved exactly when left‑multiplying by \(R.\)  This contributes markedly towards smoother optimisation trajectories compared with additive finite‑rank updates employed by other parameter‑efficient approaches such as LoRA.


In summary, Orthogonal Finetuning harnesses a planted symmetry—rigid rotations—to subtly reorient entire weight layers so downstream objectives drive learning logically far enough so new patterns emerge but never disturb relationships learned in pretraining phases—an approach grounded firmly in simple Euclidean geometry yet powerful enough to elevate finite‑rank methods into genuinely expressive finetuners consistent with architecture sizes defined in large foundation models.

# Limitations of Direct Orthonormal Updates  

The Orthogonal Finetuning framework was introduced as an elegant way to modify pretrained weights while rigorously preserving key geometric properties: namely, pairwise angular relations among neurons inside each layer remain invariant once an orthogonal transformation \( \boldsymbol R \in \mathbb R^{\,d\times d}\) multiplies the frozen weight matrix \(W^{0}\). Although conceptually appealing, simply learning an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix turns out prohibitively expensive when the hidden dimension \(d\) assumes realistic magnitudes found in today’s foundation models.\n\n## Full dense orthonormal matrix cost ≈ \(d^{2}\)\nOrthogonal matrices form a curved manifold embedded in Euclidean space whose intrinsic dimensionality equals one half the square minus lower‐order terms—the simplest way to see this is through Cayley’s parametrisation. Writing
\[
   R=\bigl(I+Q\bigr)\bigl(I-Q\bigr)^{-1},
\]
with skew–symmetric \(Q=-Q^{\top}\), shows that every independent entry in \(Q\) becomes part of the optimisation target while maintaining strict orthogonality during training. A skew–symmetric \(d\times d\) matrix carries precisely
\( \tfrac12\,d(d{-}1)\)
free real‐valued degrees of freedom—two entries per off–diagonal couple are tied together by symmetry leaving one arbitrary value per pair.\n\nFrom an implementation standpoint this means every linear layer must store roughly half a million independent scalars when its hidden size reaches one thousand (since \(\tfrac12\,1000(999)\approx5\times10^{5}\)). Deep foundations grow beyond these sizes very quickly—in systems such as GPT‑3 whose parameter count attains rival terabyte scales—even when only a thin slice corresponding to one transformation needs temporary storage during optimisation.” This quadratic blow‐up creates three major problems:\n\n* **Memory pressure** – Storing megabytes or gigabytes worth of coefficients for each layer multiplies cache misses and causes frequent off‑chip traffic.\n* **Learning instability** – Optimising millions of tightly coupled variables without significant regularisation magnifies sensitivity towards learning rates.\n* **Cost over honorific parallelism** – GPUs rely on batched integerial multiplications whose throughput drops swiftly when custom tensor shapes become irregular.\n\nBecause every element of \(R\) influences *every* input dimension through left multiplication—no separating structure limits routing—directly inheriting all those free variables implies an inability to concisely encode proximal geometric constraints while still having enough expressive weight directions.\n\n## Block‑diagonal scheme squeezes parameters →≈ \(b\,d\)\nTo tame this blow‑up OFT introduced a sparsity pattern preserving only intra‑group rotations:\[
   R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},\,R_{2},\,\dots ,\,R_{r}),
   \qquad
   R_{i}\in {\mathbb R}^{\,b\times b}, 
   \quad br=d.
\]
Each small orthogonal block seeks its own local rotation – distinct plans suffice when models contain cheap repetitions—but now their collective parameter budget collapses dramatically:\footnote{\( \tfrac12\,r\,b(b{-}1)=
      \tfrac12\,(\!{\textstyle\b}/(\!{\textstyle\b}))\,(\!{\textstyle\b}-{\textstyle\b})\,d
    =O(\![\!{\textstyle\b}]\,[\!{\textstyle d}])\)}  
The factorized form cuts the number of tunables roughly in proportion to the chosen block size \(b:\;\sim\dots\dots\!\approx b\,d.\)

**Intuitively why this helps**  
The Cayley construction remains usable inside each block because every little rotational field inherits local validity guarantees—the question restates as “fixing attention head doesn’t lead into contradictory orientations across blocks”.  
When optimization proceeds layerly across transformers, every group rotates among itself while remaining silent regarding channels belonging outside its scope—this sparseness curbs overfitting simply because fewer hand‑shakes occur between distant neurons.\n\nHowever, this stratification also bears severe inductive restrictions invisible at first glance:\n\n* **Zero inter‑group communication** – The model never learns transformations coupling attributes situated in distinct index bands. If two signal streams residing side by side potentially interfere beneficially (for instance cross‑token attention patterns or shared positional information), their mixability is irremediably blocked.\n* **Arbitrary partitioning** – Determining proper cardinalities relies solely on numeric granularity rather than feature semantics; dividing vector coordinates purely by their ordinal positions does not reflect functional homogeneity often observed across layers.\n* **Lossy representational reach** – Classic linear maps like discrete Fourier transforms require mixing among arbitrarily distant coordinates (\(j'\)-th component interacts simultaneously with all others). A strict banded skeleton such as
\(x_{i+q(k)}=x_{i}+x_{i+q'}\)
fails precisely at those points where information relocation spans across larger strides.\n\nTo illustrate concretely one cost comes visible even when modelling synthetic unitary behaviour such as fast radial scalings or circulant shifts—all impossible under rigid band organisation yet naturally expressible within waiting well‑known structured families that embody long–range connections.\n\nConsequently while naïve dense rotations strike a mathematically clean baseline mere friends expecting enormous capacity—and correspondingly hazardous overfitting—a middle ground predicated through block–diagonality gives reasonable speedups yet cannot emulate many powerful global transformations any longer simply because its topology prohibits essential edge propagation.\n\nIn short, **the sheer density requirement renders standard OFT impractical on real foundation models**, whereas contracting it into unnaturally engineered spacing sacrifices important expressive directions—a tension at which subsequent contributions propose clever sparse factorizations allowing low‐parameter yet globally rich bijections.”

# Information Transmission Perspective

In this chapter we revisit the construction of a dense orthonormal matrix not as an algebraic object living solely in \(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\), but rather as a process of transmitting information across a layered graph whose nodes represent vector coordinates and whose directed edges embody non‑zero matrix entries. This viewpoint exposes the combinatorial nature of sparsity patterns and clarifies why certain factorizations—most notably those patterned after butterfly networks—are particularly effective for building parameter‑efficient orthogonal transformations in finetuning scenarios such as Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT).

---

## Nodes ↔ weights, edges ↔ nonzero entries

When we take a linear layer from a frozen foundation model—say a weight matrix \( \bm{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\)—the basic operation performed upon an input vector \( \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\bm{x}.
\]
Each column of \(\bm{W}^0\) can be interpreted as a *neuron* or *weight vector* that feeds into the next layer; equivalently, we may view these columns as vertices in a directed graph. The action of applying an additional orthogonal transformation \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) amounts to left‑multiplication:
\[
\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^{\!\top}\bm{x}.
\]
Now focus on \(\bm{R}\). The entry \(R_{ij}\neq 0\) precisely indicates that coordinate \(j\) of an input vector can influence coordinate \(i\) after transformation; if \(R_{ij}=0\), information from input dimension \(j\) does **not** reach output dimension \(i\). Thus:

- **Vertices (nodes)** correspond to coordinate indices \(1,\dots,d\).
- **Directed edges** correspond to non‑zero entries: there is an edge from node \(j\) (input side) to node \(i\) (output side) if and only if \(R_{ij}\neq 0\).

If \(\bm{R}\) were fully dense—a random orthogonal matrix—the resulting directed graph turns out to be complete: every node in the input side has an edge into every node on the output side. In network terms this is a *complete bipartite* overlay connecting two disjoint sets of identical cardinality.

The power of this interpretation is that it converts algebraic properties into combinatorial ones:

- The **rank requirement** for \(\bm{R}\) being full rank manifests as the graph needing at least one spanning directed cycle or equivalently, at least one path between any pair of nodes across successive layers.
- The **orthogonality condition** imposes subtle correlations among which edges may co‑exist; e.g., for a \(2\times 2\) orthogonal matrix, setting entry \((1,1)=0\) mandates entry \((2,2)=0\), because any zero entry forces its row and column counterparts to obey complementary algebraic relations dictated by preservation of inner products.

Proceeding from this perspective reveals that many natural sparse structures simply cannot guarantee that every node can attain information from *every* other node if their edge sets are too restrictive—something that will become apparent when comparing block‑diagonal versus butterfly patterns later.

## Factor × multiplies maps signal paths through levels

Instead of representing \(\bm{R}\) as one monolithic block, we can factor it into several sparse matrices:
\[
\boxed{\displaystyle \bm{R} = \underbrace{\bm{B}_{m}}_{\text{\(m^\text{th}\,\text{sparse\,factor}}}
            \underbrace{\cdots}_{m-1}
            \underbrace{\bm{B}_{1}}_{\text{\(1^\text{st}\,\text{sparse\,factor}}}}.
}
\]
Each factor \(\bm{B}_k \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) has its own sparsity pattern and acts as an adjacency relation between two adjacent “layers” or “levels” in an extended graph:
- Level \(k-1\): nodes representing the incoming coordinates before applying \(\bm{B}_k\).
- Level \(k   \): nodes representing the outgoing coordinates after applying \(\bm{B}_k\).

Multiplication by \(\bm{B}_k\) thus *propagates* signals along the directed edges belonging to its sparsity pattern. Because multiplication is associative, concatenating factors corresponds precisely to composing these propagation steps sequentially:

1. **Layer‑wise propagation:** A signal originating at level 0 (the original vector input coordinates), after passing through factor \(\bm{B}_1\), arrives at level 1 along the edges defined by the support of \(\bm{B}_1.\)
2. **Continuation:** The output of level 1 becomes input for factor \(\bm{B}_2,\ldots,\)
which leads to signals traversing multiple successive graphs before reaching level \(m.\)

Formally, if we denote by  
\(G_k = (V_{k-1}, V_{k}, E_k)\)
the bipartite graph induced by factor
\(E_k = \bigl[(i,j)\mid (\bm B_k)_{ij} \neq 0\bigr],  
V_{k-1}=V_k=\{1,\dots,d\},  
V_0 = V_m = [d],  
E_k$,
then traversing all factors yields a path set
\(P = E_1\times E_2\times\dots\times E_m.\)
A point lies in position <sup>i</sup> iff there exists a directed path from some source node in \(V_0 = [d]\) to that target node in \(V_m = [d]\).
If all such paths exist—every source reaches every target—then
the product matrix
\(M := B_m B_{m-1} \dots B_1
=  R
= (\underbrace{\dots}_{m}) 
is dense despite each individual factor having far fewer than \(d^{2}\) non-zero entries.

From this layered‐graph viewpoint, constructing an efficient orthogonal matrix reduces to two interlocking problems:
(i) design sparse graphs whose composition yields universal reachability,
(ii) assign orthogonal matrices respecting both sparsity patterns and inner‑product constraints.
Orthogonality imposes algebraic relations among triples or pairs of entries belonging along adjacent layers—thus not all naive patterns are admitted; the set of allowed support structures shrinks drastically compared with arbitrary sparse matrices.

## Desiderata: dense connectivity + minimum free edges

With these notions formalized we can state two principled goals guiding any consideration for efficient orthogonal factorizations:

### Dense Connectivity

For downstream adaptation tasks such as finetuning large vision or language models,
the transformation applied by
\(M = B_m B_{m-1}\dots B_1
= R
= (\ast)\)
must preserve rich expressive power.
The essential requirement is that *information* originating anywhere in the input space can flow—to all possible destinations—in at most \(m+1\)
hash-hops on our layered graph:
\[ 
   \forall u,v~:\;\exists~(e_1,\ldots,e_m)\in P~~ s.t.~u\xrightarrow[e_1]{}~w_1\xrightarrow[e_2]{}~w_2\xrightarrow[\dots]{}~ v.
   \]
With this full reachability property,
the overall kernel embodied by \(M^{T}W^0x=\ldots;\)
holds no topological bottleneck: neurons communicating across different clusters can share information freely.
Failure to achieve dense connectivity leads directly to reduced expressivity—identical final outputs would arise from multiple distinct inputs or conversely multiple outputs would collapse onto few distinct learned directions—an obvious detriment for finetuning performance.

### Minimum Free Edges

Sparse matrices contain far fewer trainable parameters than their dense counterparts:
each zero entry is “free,” i.e., it does not contribute any learnable degree-of-freedom (though it may have algebraically required non-zero values—e.g., ±𝟙 for unit entries demanded by orthogonality).
Hence we desire overall support sets containing *as few* such trainable edges as possible while still satisfying dense connectivity.
Concretely,
let us borrow terminology from reliability theory: 
a *free edge* represents a multiplier whose value is free to optimize during finetuning.

Butterfly Graphs From Fast Fourier Transforms

# Introduction  

The Cooley‑Tukey algorithm is one of the most celebrated algorithms in scientific computing because it reduces the naive $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ discrete Fourier transform (DFT) into a sequence of simpler operations that cost only  
$\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$.  While its original motivation was to accelerate spectral analysis of time‑series data, the underlying graph‑theoretic structure that it reveals is far more general.  Every linear transform that can be computed by Cooley‑Tukey is represented as a product of sparse matrices whose non‑zero pattern forms a characteristic “butterfly” network.  In this chapter we unpack that network: how it arises from recursive splitting, how a single butterfly unit is built from four small diagonal blocks, and why the resulting sparse adjacency enables every input element to mix with every output element while using only $O(n\log n)$ edges.

# Recursive split → two $\,\theta/2$‑factors per level  

Suppose we wish to compute an $N$‑point DFT where $N$ is a power of two, i.e., $N=2^K$.  The Cooley‑Tukey recursion begins by partitioning an input vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}$ into its even and odd indices:
\[
\mathbf{x}_{\text{even}}=\bigl(x_0,x_2,\dots,x_{N-2}\bigr),\qquad
\mathbf{x}_{\text{odd}}=\bigl(x_1,x_3,\dots,x_{N-1}\bigr).
\]
Applying the DFT separately to these two halves gives two $(N/2)$‑point transforms and then mixes their results using twiddle factors $\omega_N^{k}=\exp(-2\pi i k/N)$.  
In matrix form this mixing step is encoded in an $N\times N$ matrix whose non‑zero entries occupy precisely two disjoint “halves”:
each row has two non‑zero entries that involve one element from $\mathbf{x}_{\text{even}}$ and one from $\mathbf{x}_{\text{odd}}$.  
This matrix can be seen as a product of two $\,N/2$‑dimensional orthogonal-like matrices—one handling the left half and one handling the right half—conjoined by identity connections between them.  

Repeating this decomposition at every recursion level produces $K=\log_2 N$ stages, each time halving the block size but doubling the number of factors needed.  As a result every stage introduces exactly two sparse matrices acting on vectors of size $N/2$, $N/4$,\, … down to size 1.  Because each such factor only touches pairs of elements from adjacent halves, it introduces so-called “fan‑out” connections that link two inputs into one output or vice versa.  This recursive splitting with two $\theta/2$‑factors per level is precisely what underpins the fan‑out diagram we will now describe in more detail.

# Butterfly unit = diagonal × diagonal + diagonal × diagonal blocks  

At any given stage let us examine what one basic operation looks like on a block of size $k=2^m$.  Cooley–Tukey groups every pair {\em(inside)} that block into “butterfly” interactions:
an even element participates together with an odd element via multiplicative twiddle factors before being summed and subtracted back into even/odd positions.
Matrixwise this interaction is captured by a $k\times k$ *butterfly factor* $\boldsymbol{B}^{F}(k)$ defined as
\[
\boldsymbol{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\tag{1}
\]
where $\mathbf{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ are scalar vectors (for complex DFTs they contain twiddle phases).  
Each row–block contains only two non‑zero columns—one on the left side ($\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1)$ or $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3)$), one on the right side ($\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)$ or $\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)$).  
Hence every input index feeds into exactly two output indices inside that block, while coupling its paired index through multiplication by scalars housed inside the four diagonals.

For larger matrices we stack these butterfly factors recursively:
for an overall dimension $n=2^K$, we define
\[
\boldsymbol B(n)=
\tilde{\boldsymbol B}(n,n)\,
      \tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!\Bigl(n,\tfrac n 2 \Bigr)\,
      \cdots
      \tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!\Bigl(n,\,2 \Bigr),
      \tag{2}
\]
where each component
$\tilde{\boldsymbol B}(n,k)=
     \operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol B^{F}(k),\, 
                             \boldsymbol B^{F}(k),\, 
                             \dots,\,
                             \boldsymbol B^{F}(k)\bigr)
$
places copies of a single butterfly factor along its main diagonal.
Thus $\tilde{\boldsymbol B}(n,k)$ has block size $k$; there are $(n/k)$ such copies side by side.
Each copy implements one independent butterfly operation on its $k$ rows.
Because every copy operates on mutually exclusive indices, all rows remain orthogonal **within** their blocks, while also permitting inter‑block communication through subsequent levels.

Notice carefully that equation (1) presents not just any pairwise coupling but a specific *spatial* pattern:
the left diagonals interact exclusively with one half of the subsequent column indices while excluding the other half; likewise for the right diagonals.
When several such layers are chained as indicated in equation (2), these local couplings reproduce exactly all pairwise interactions between any input location and any output location—though never explicitly forming a dense matrix in memory during forward evaluation.

# Resulting sparse adjacency supporting efficient communication  

To visualise how data propagate through these matrices let us write down an example with $n=8$ ($K=3$).  
At stage 0 our input vector has eight entries.
We apply $\tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!\bigl(8,8\bigr)$ which essentially does nothing because it corresponds to an unpartitioned block—the initial identity connections.
The next stage carries out $\tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!\bigl(8,4\bigr)$:
two disjoint butterfly factors each serve four rows;
each factor connects every row in its upper half to every row in its lower half via two non‑zero entries.
Graphically we can draw rows as nodes plotted on discrete horizontal levels:
at level 0 we start with eight input nodes; 
a directed edge from node $i$ at level $p$ points toward node $j$ at level $p+1$
if $(i,j)$ is non-zero in any intermediate factor.
In our toy example this yields eight directed edges at level 0→1 linking node pairs,
and another eight at level 1→2 after applying both copies’ butterflies simultaneously.
Thus after proceeding through all three levels we have exported eight destination nodes—one per output element—and every starting node finds itself connected to *every* ending node along at least one directed path.
This guarantees global mixing while keeping edges far fewer than those necessary for full density: each stage only contributes twice as many edges as there are nodes traversed so far.

Quantitatively counting edges clarifies this observation.
At stage $r$, where block size shrinks from $n/2^{\,r-1}$ to $n/2^{\,r}$,
every butterfly copy contains exactly $n$ non-zero entries because there are $(n/(n/2^{r-1}))*(n/(n/2^{r})) = 4*(n/(n)) =4$ directed connections per emitting node across both halves—two per offset—but since each copy has $(n/(n/2^{r}))= 2^{r-1}$ copies totalling $4(n/ ( n / 2^{ r}))= 8\,?$
Let us simplify: In practice each butterfly factor has exactly twice as many secrets as rows,
so altogether at level $r$ we insert precisely $n\,r$ edges cumulatively over all levels when unrolled fully.
Hence over all recursion depths ($K=\log_20 n$) we use
$\sum_{r=1}^{K}{nr}= n\,K = n\,\log_20 n$
edges—a major reduction from $n^2-n = O(n^２)$ edges required by an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix.
Because edges correspond directly to parameters,
the product-of-sparse-orthogonal-matrices formulation embedded in BOFT carries at most about 
$\tfrac12 (b-1)nK$
effective trainable scalars if each cardiac `butterfly` employs block size $b$, giving rise to an overall parameter budget proportional to `$O(n\bigsqcup b)\!·​{\rm log}_20 n'`.  

The same sparsity pattern naturally supports fast algorithms beyond FFT:
once coefficients $\mathbf d_i$ are replaced by generic trainable scalars or small matrices (as done in BOFT),
we obtain structured orthogonal transforms capable of universal approximation when combined across multiple levels,
yet still preserve extremely low communication cost thanks to this underlying “butterfly” topology.

In conclusion, Cooley–Tukey’s elegant recursive decomposition delivers not only algorithmic speed but also a powerful architectural motif—a bipartite network where local doublet interactions staged across logarithmically many layers fuse global information using only `$O(n﹤∀﹂log_n)`’ edges. This motif lies at heart both classic spectral transforms and modern parameter-efficient finetuning techniques such as BOFT discussed throughout this text suite.

Constructing Dense Orthonormal Matrices via Butterflies  

# Definition \(B^{(M)}(d)=\displaystyle\prod_{l}\widetilde{\bm B}_{(d,k_{l})}\) with \(k_{l}\) powers of \(2\)

In order to encode a high‑dimensional orthogonal transformation efficiently, the authors first build blocks that are intrinsically sparse but collectively yield a dense map once multiplied together. The building block is called a *butterfly factor*:
\[
\bm B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[3pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{k\times k},
\]
where each \(\mathbf d_{i}\in\mathbb R^{k/2}\) holds learnable scalar coefficients.
When we stack such factors recursively, starting from a vector of dimension \(d=2^{N}\), we obtain a *butterfly matrix*:
\[
\bm B(d)=
\tilde{\bm B}(d,d)\,
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm B_{1}(d/2)&0\\
    0&\bm B_{2}(d/2)
    \end{bmatrix}
   = 
   \tilde{\bm B}(d,d)\,\tilde{\bm B}(d,d/2)\cdots
   \tilde{\bm B}(d,2),
\]
where each \(\tilde{\bm B}(d,k)\) is simply a block‑diagonal wrapper
containing \(d/k\) copies of the same butterfly factor:
\[
\tilde{\bm B}(d,k)
=
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(
    \bm B^{F}_{1}(k),
    \dots,
    \bm B^{F}_{\,d/k}(k)
\bigr).
\]
The product above is denoted compactly by
\(B^{(M)}(d)=\prod_{l=1}^{M}\widetilde{\bm B}_{(d,k_{l})}\).
Here every \(k_{l}\) is required to be a power of two so that each
component respects the structural rules that come from radix‑\(2\)
Cooley–Tukey’s decomposition.
Because every factor is sparse – every row and column contains only
\(k/2+ k/2 = k\) non‑zero entries – neutralizing that sparseness by
multiplication eventually yields an all‑\(k/d\times k/d = 1/d?\)
dense matrix over the full dimension \(d.\)

The recursive construction shows that even though each ingredient has very few parameters, their chain links traffic between any source node at level one and any sink node at level \(M+1.\)  
This *information transmission viewpoint* explains intuitively why multiplying many tiny sparse pieces can generate something as comprehensive as a fully connected linear operator while still keeping trainable statistics small.



# Ensuring each BLOCK/unit remains orthonormal through Cayley transform

While defining an efficient product remains straightforward, it is essential that every intermediate factor stays within the *orthogonal* group so that their composition belongs automatically to
${O(d)}$.  
The authors address this by employing **Cayley’s parameterisation** for any unimodular unit.  
Given a skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ ($Q=-Q^\top$), one constructs
an orthogonal matrix via:
\[
R(Q)= (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\,\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}.
\]
Because $\det(R(Q))=1$, every output satisfies $R(Q)^\top R(Q)=R(Q) R(Q)^\top=\mathbf I.$  
In the particular case of a $2\times 2$ or equivalently a $b\times b$
block belonging to $\widetilde{\bm B}_{(d,b)}$, one yields two scalar degrees 
of freedom rather than four: concretely `diag` elements take values 
$\pm 1$ while off–diagonal couplings obey symmetry constraints induced 
by skew–symmetry.



To keep all groups mutually consistent one simply gives each 
block its own skew-symmetric generator prior to conversion into an 
orthogonal factor.
Afterwards one may freely concatenate these factors by using them as 
multiplicative operators on frozen pretrained layers:
for pre‑trained weight \(\bm W^0_{\!i}\in {\mathbb R}^{\,d\times n}\)
the new weight becomes
\( (\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}
      \widetilde{\bm B}_{(d,k_{\ell})})\,\,{\bm W^0_{\!i}}\).
Thus training only manipulates the generators while preserving exact 
orthogonality across epochs.



Because Cayley transforms generate *nondeterministic* maps – they cover 
the entire connected component of ${O(d)}$ corresponding to identity – initialization is straightforward too:
every generator starts at zero which maps exactly to an identity matrix  
($R(\mathbf0)=I$).  Hence every finetuning trajectory starts at stage zero where no change occurs and gradually moves within ${O(d)}$, guaranteeing stable norm preservation.



# Parameter count ≈ $(b-1)d\,m/2$ ; special case $b=2,m=\log_2 d$ gives ℴ($d\,\log_{} d$)

Now consider how many scalar parameters actually have to be learned when building \(B^{(M)}(d)\).  
A single Sherifly block snippet containing two diagonal length-\((b)\) vectors carries $(b-1)$ degrees because one entry per row is fixed by unit–length constraints (to keep it orthogonal one often encloses ${+/- 1}$ on diagonal entries).  
Multiplying across $m$ such layers multiplies parameter counts approximately linearly:
a total number of learnable scalars amounts roughly to 
\(K_{\!eff}= \frac12(b-1)d\,m.\)

When choosing \(b=2,\ m=\log_{} d,\) which mirrors exactly how Cooley–Tukey splits an input vector into halves recursively until singletons,
the total trainable amount becomes 
\(K_{\!eff}= O(d\, log_{} d).\)
This matches convincingly what Table X in the article reports,
showing that even though each plug‑in factor is tiny, their joint product composes rich rotations across the whole feature space.


Comparatively:
* **Full‑rank OFT**​ stores and trains an entire dense $d\times d$ matrix,
amortising roughly $K_{\!full}= O(d^2)$ parameters—far more than feasible for modern large models.
* **Block‑diagonal OFT**​ chooses \(r=d/b\) disjoint blocks of size \(b\times b,\) so only 
$$K_{\!bloc}= r\,O(b^2)\approx O(b d )$$
parameters are learned – efficient but potentially too coarse.
* **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** harnesses both sparsity *and* multiplicative depth,
achieving \(K_{\!eff}= O(d\, log_{} d)\), sitting comfortably between dense OFT’s expressive capacity and block‑diagonal OFT’s minimalism while keeping cross‑block communication implicit rather than girthly enforced.



Empirically evident from Figure 5 in the text is that BOFT displays lower approximation error than its block‑diagonal counterpart while using far fewer degrees of freedom.  At full training budgets freeze-only variations dictate convergence nicely thanks page theory—preserving spectral norms keeps gradients well behaved even under low memory budgets.



In sum, constructing dense orthogonal matrices by multiplying carefully chosen *butterfly blocks* creates an expressive yet lightweight parameter set relevant for modern foundation-model adaptation:   
$$B^{(M)}(d)= {\Large {\prod}}_{\ell =1}^{m}
          \widetilde{\boldsymbol  B}_{(d,k_\ell)},$$  

with every piece kept strictly orthonormal through Cayley transforms,
and overall needing only $\sim (b-1)d\,m / 2$ free scalars—
which reduces asymptotically down to $\mathcal O(d\, log_{} d)$ when \(b= 2,\ m = log_{} d.\)
This algebraic strategy substantiates BOFT’s claim that “many small structured layers can simulate almost any large rotation,” thus benefiting parameter‑efficient finetuning across vision and language tasks introduced in this chapter’s broader context.

# Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning Methodology (BOFT)

Large‑scale foundation models possess enormous expressive power that is difficult—or infeasible—to exploit directly when data resources voor downstream tasks are scarce. Parameter‑efficient finetuning provides a principled way to harness pre‑training knowledge while adjusting only a modest number of weights. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves pairwise neuron angles through an orthogonal transformation applied uniformly across an entire layer,
yet its naïve instantiation demands \(O(d^2)\) trainable parameters when operating on \(d\)-dimensional representations.
BOFT replaces the dense mono‑block orthogonal matrix
with a carefully crafted product of sparse orthogonal blocks—an *orthogonal butterfly factorisation*—that reproduces a dense linear operator while keeping parameter count down essentially logarithmic in \(d\).
In what follows we unpack how BOFT solves this challenge through three interconnected ideas:
the mixed‑matrix forward‑pass formalism;
the skew‑symmetric parametrisation via Cayley transforms;
and an adaptive multiplicative dropout routine.



## mixed‑matrix forward–pass formulation

Rather than modify every individual weight entry,
BOFT injects a global linear map \(R(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) into every slice
of a pretrained module \(W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\).
Formally,
each transformed activation reads
\[
z=\big(R(m,b)\,\!W^{0}\big)^{\!\top}\!x,\qquad
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~~z,\!y_{i}\!\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
\]
which expands to an explicit series
\(R(m,b)=B_{m}\dotsm B_{1}\)
where each factor \(B_{i}\) contains very few non‑zero entries,
yet whose product remains invertible—and most importantly,
orthonormal.
By inserting a *mixed* chain instead of one bulk matrix,
the forward operation preserves computational speed:
each interaction between \(x\), the pretrained weight \(W^0\),
and one butterfly component operates in linear time \(O(d)\).
Warm restarts remain trivial:
at initialization every block equals identity,
so that immediately before training starts \(R(m,b)=I_d\)
and consequently \(z=W^{0\,\top}x\).

A key intuition behind Equation (1) comes from band‑limited signal processing.
Just as rhythms propagate globally through successive stages in Fast Fourier Transforms,
a cascade of small mixing units suffices to let every input coordinate influence
every output coordinate ultimately.
Graphically this transaction looks like boats traveling along prescribed routes
in an interstitial grid;
the sequence of routes captures all admissible paths,
hence every source node eventually reaches every sink node
while private communications remain restrained thanks to sparsity.



## skew‑symmetric parameterisation via Cayley transforms

Guaranteeing each component \(B_i\) remains strictly orthonormal places strong constraints on its trainable entries.
An elegant way circumvents directly optimising over Stiefel manifold
is through the *Cayley transform*
which maps any skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q=-Q^{T}\)
into an orthonormal map:
\[
B_i=(I + Q_i)(I-Q_i)^{-1}.
\]
Hence optimisation runs over entries constituting \(Q_i\),
whose dimensionality equals half­–of–the Pairs’ count
and automatically obeys unitary norms upon convergence.
For elementary “butterfly factors’’ defined later
each \(Q_i\) squeezes down
to merely cosine–sine angular parameters—
basically tiny rotations—making training both cheaper and more numerically stable compared
with naive gradient descent over orthonormal axioms.



### Why skew‑symmetric works well inside butterflies

The bottleneck lies in constructing sparse yet expressive Or­th­ogonal maps.
Every butterfly component enjoys zero entries arranged in rigid blocks mirroring power‑of‑two scales.
This structure lends itself naturally to represent rotations between disjoint subsets:
each diagonal block encodes pairwise mixing among pairings,
mediated mathematically through Hankel–type relations derived from trigonometric identities.
Consequently imposing Cayley‐parametrised rotations gives us faithful
orthonormal sub‐operators without resorting  
to hard projections back onto Stiefel space;
compactness results automatically convey rotational degrees mercy.



## multiplicative dropout reciprocity (trick adaptive)

Traditional additive regularisers—for instance dropping individual weight scalars—
do not mesh seamlessly with BOFT’s multiplicative update scheme:
one perturbation outside identity inevitably cascades through subsequent products,
potentially cropping useful pathways unduly early.
Multiplicative Dropout therefore intervenes directly on *factor level*:  
with probability \(p_1\%\) one chooses whole butterfly components at random  
and replaces them temporarily by identities;
within those selected components a secondary probability \(p_2\bigr>\!\!(%) \)
causes individual diagonal blocks themselves swap out yielding local stasis.
This two‐tier mechanic does two things simultaneously:
it sporadically decouples long chains enough so gradients average out desirable perturbations,
while preserving momentum along remaining routes,
retaining tractable mixing properties structurally imposed by butterflies.



### Practical adaptivity rules

* **Cold start:** initialise every blocker please zero entries inside their corresponding skew matrices  
so indices begin identically neutral (\(B_i=I_d)\).
* **Warm up regime:** set prod optimistic dropout rates say \(p_1=p_2=5\,%\).
* **Schedule adjustment:** gradually decrease both masks during training
so early epochs reap broad exploration  
until later epochs lock onto sharp learned linear combination structures,
ensuring stable convergence while curbing overfitting.

# Summary

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning departs from classic OFT primarily through three intertwined layers:

1. The **mixed‐matrix cascade** breaks down a single dense transformation into several sparse multiplications mirroring signal propagation trees.
2. Every piece draws energy from **skew‐symmetric parametrisation**, ensuring pristine orthonormality without costly manifold projections.
3. **Multiplicative Dropout** furnishes disciplined stochastic regularisation tailored specifically for serial products rather than infinitesimal weight adjustments.

Collectively these ingredients squash trainable budgets graphically modeled within LOG(movement/communication capacity)
from quadratic (\(O(d^2)\)) magnitude captured directly within OFT’s dense blocks toward logarithmic (\(O(d\,log d)\)) footprints maintained conducive inference latency—a balance crucial when wrestting cutting‑edge foundation models towards specialised downstream applications.
