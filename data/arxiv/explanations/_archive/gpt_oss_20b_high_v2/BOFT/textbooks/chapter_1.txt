\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: Foundations and Motivation  

# Magnitude Growth ↕️ ⇝ Billions‑Parameter Transformers  
The rapid increase in depth, width, and branching factor of transformer architectures has produced models whose capacity now spans hundreds of billions of trainable parameters. A striking example is GPT‑3, which contains approximately \(1.75\times10^{11}\) weights — a leap well beyond earlier language models such as GPT‑2 or BERT [Brown et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021]. Visual backbones like ViT‑Large and Stable Diffusion’s U‑Net likewise occupy tens to hundreds of billions of weights when instantiated at their largest scales. This exponential scaling follows roughly a power law relating performance improvements to model size for tasks ranging from language modeling to image synthesis [Radford et al., 2021]. As an effect of this growth, baseline computational requirements for training an entire model from scratch rise from weeks on dozens of GPUs to months on hundreds or thousands of GPUs equipped with high-bandwidth interconnects. Energy consumption and carbon emissions consequently become major environmental concerns—entire day-long runs at large scale are reported to generate several kilotonnes of CO₂ equivalents [Rombach et al., 2022].

# Challenges of Full-Scale Pre‑Training  
Training a foundation model end-to-end demands coordinated chiselling across three broad dimensions: (1) **Hardware scalability**, requiring clusters that support petascale data parallelism while incurring nontrivial overhead on communication and memory hierarchies; (2) **Algorithmic robustness**, needing numerical stability against exploding gradients at extreme depths while preserving the delicate balance between underfitting and overfitting across millions of data points; (3) **Data curation and bias mitigation**, where massive corpora must be shuffled into coherent syntactic–semantic distributions without compromising privacy or injecting systematic biases [Qiu et al., 2023]. Each dimension interacts with environmental costs and potential hardware failure rates—the more layers added or the more mixture-of-experts partitions inserted, the steeper the training curve becomes in terms both of wall-clock time and memory footprint. Consequently, even if an organization possesses sufficient compute budget, health‑related constraints such as cooling capacity or power delivery often become bottlenecks that limit maximum attainable model size in practice.



# The Role of Downstream Task Adaptation  
The core insight behind “transfer learning” is that early transformer layers learn general-purpose representations—syntactic patterns for language or low-level texture detectors for vision—that are reusable across many end tasks once contextualized by higher layers [Brown et al., 2020]. Instead of re-training an entire gigantic mask from scratch for every new task (e.g., sentiment analysis, question answering, style transfer), practitioners typically preserve most knowledge embodied in pretrained weights while tweaking a small subset relevant to the new objective.  This localized modification offers two crucial benefits:
1. **Sample efficacy** – With fewer trainable parameters you need far fewer examples to converge meaningfully when labeled data are scarce.
2. **Computational economy** – Updating only clockwork placeholders dramatically cuts GPU usage during fine-tuning: forward-backward passes cost little more than inference time once most weights are frozen.


Downstream domain shifts—such as transitioning from purely textual inputs to multimodal scenarios—often require retraining internal attention mechanisms or feed‑forward blocks so that learned features align with new encodings or production pipelines [Zaken et al., 2022]. Even sophisticated generative repositories like stable diffusion benefit from tuned denoising steps whose parameters are sub‑inverted by small factorisation layers [Qiu et al., 2023]. In short, downstream adaptation endpoints on an intuitive economic horizon where compute time scales quadratically with network depth while memory serves as a limiting bottleneck.



# Categories of Efficient Finetuning Approaches  
Researchers have introduced multiple practical frameworks designed expressly to reduce trainable surface area without sacrificing accuracy on target tasks. These paradigms mainly diverge along how they introduce *regularity*, *inductive bias*, or *external parameter additions* onto an otherwise frozen backbone.



## Model Finetuning  
Traditional ``model finetuning'' keeps architectural hyperparameters intact but optimises only a tiny fraction—often less than one percent—of existing weights using minuscule learning rates [Huloran et al., 2022]. This low lr simply discourages changes too great relative to initialised values (£¶), effectively regularising by weight proximity rather than explicit loss geometry.



## Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)  
Within this umbrella category sits two representative families:

* **Reparameterisation-based PEFT** – Key examples include Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which expresses weight deviations as bilinear low-rank tensors \([Xiao & Widrow,\dots]\). Here extra trainable strands \(A\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) and \(B\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\) enrich each frozen matrix \(\mathbf{W}_0\) through \(\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{W}_0 + AB^\top\). The rank parameter \(r\ll d,n\) keeps swaps economically light while providing theoretically grounded expressivity scans [Huloran et al., 2022].

* **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** – Proposed by Qiu et al., OFT replaces additive updates with multiplicative right-multiplication by an orthogonal guard \(\mathbf{R}\): \(\tilde{\mathbf{W}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_0\). Optimising \(\mathbf{R}\in O(d)\) preserves pairwise angles between neuron vectors enabling spectral norm stability—an essential property often disrupted under conventional fine-tunings [Qiu et al., 2023].



Both reparameterisation paradigms rely on sparse structural decompositions such as block-diagonal matrices \( \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{R}_r)\). While block-diagonal OFT easily reduces evaluated degrees-of-freedom from \(d^2\) down to \(bd\,(\approx d r)\), it suffers inherent inductive bias because neuron spaces are forced into disjoint spatial islands defined solely by index order.



## Adapter Tuning  
When computational budgets are constrained even further or when latency must stay zero during inference, adapters insert learnable micro-networks between existing layers instead of shifting huge weights directly [Houlsby et al., 2019]. Each adapter comprises minimal gates such as projectionless bottleneck transforms combined with layer-normalised residuals—a compliant recipe yielding almost identical accuracy while keeping integration overhead negligible.



## Prompt Tuning  
Carry over into Transformer architecture prepping via *prefix embeddings* placed before any token sequence [Lester et al.; Li et al.] – essentially assigning learnable “prompt tokens” at embedding-level files rather than at weight-level fields. Prompt tuning keeps every underlying weight untouched yet lets retrieval mechanisms adapt via prompt injection during inference inside linear projection spaces.



Despite differences in methodic philosophy—block versus continuous parameter update—the overarching theme remains identical across all perspectives: **dense knowledge should be transferred efficiently without drowning GPUs**.
