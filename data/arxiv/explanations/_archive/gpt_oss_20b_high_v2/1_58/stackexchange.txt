### 'Per‑token' activation scaling versus traditional range clipping
Question:
`BitNet b‑b¹⁵⁸` rescales activations per token into [-Q_b,Q_b] instead of [0,Q_b] as before. Why remove the extra zero‑point offset coming from operand’s signed range provides negligible effect yet benefit implementation simplicity?
Answer:
In most low‑precision language models each tensor is mapped from real numbers to integers with two parameters  

\[
x_{\mathrm{int}}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)+z ,
\]
where \(s>0\) is the step size (scale) and \(z\in[0,B]\) is a *zero‑point* chosen so that a real value equal exactly to zero maps onto an integer inside the allowable code space \([0,B]\).  
With this *asymmetric* scheme negative reals are encoded by first shifting them up by \(z\); during a dot product one has to subtract this shift again:
  
\[
y=\sum_{i}w_{i}(x^{\,}_{i}+z)
   =y_{\text{symmetric}}
     + z\,\sum_{i}w_{i}.
\]

The additional subtraction per element introduces an entire register load/store cycle,
changes the instruction pipeline on SIMD/SIMD+DSP cores,
and forces every kernel that multiplies two tensors to handle this offset separately.
All these resources could as well be devoted directly to the core arithmetic  
(and later Mac libraries rely heavily on such optimisation).

The authors of BitNet b¹⁵⁸ redesigned their post‑training pipeline
to employ **symmetric** scaling instead:
denote

\[
x_{\mathrm{sym}}=\operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right)\in[-\,Q\, ,\, Q],
{\qquad}
Q=2^{b-1}-1 .
\]

Now every element has its sign explicitly encoded,
the integer domain already contains ­128…127 for 8 bits
(or ­115…115 for their effective “1 bit” ternary case),
and no explicit additive constant is needed.
During matrix multiplication only ordinary *signed* multiplies are performed:
```text
acc ← ∑_i   w_i × x_sym,i          // power‑of‑two accumulation
```

No intermediate assignment has to undo nor correct a fictitious “base address”.
Consequently every kernel becomes one fewer instruction,
does not require extra registers,
and vector units (SIMD/SIMD+) can operate on packed signed words directly,
resulting both in slightly higher throughput **and**
more natural placement into DPUs designed around binary ADI units,
exactly what BitLinear exploits.

Why does this lack of a zeropoint harm little performance?
*   **Weight symmetry** – All trainable parameters are ternary \(\{-1,\,0,+1\}\);
    their mean is essentially zero.
*   **Bias suppression** – The authors deliberately removed all additive biases  
    (RMSNorm, SWIGLU, RotaryEmbeddings,…), so any constant shift introduced  
    by a zeropoint would rapidly be cancelled out downstream.
*   **Per token dynamic range** – The scale factor \(s_t=\alpha _t^{-1}\)
    adapted online captures almost all variability of each token’s activation magnitude;
    tiny residual differences caused by dropping \(z_t=z=0\)
    fall within the stochastic noise budget added during fine-tuning.
*   Empirically, test perplexities differ by less than –   
    e.g., versus original BitNet those models matched FP16 baselines from ~3 B size upwards –
    clearly “negligible” impact compared with energy savings (~71× less macro ops energy).

Thus dropping the extra zeropoint offers clean implementation advantages—simplified kernels,
fewer constants carried through every SIMD lane,
lower register usage—and aligns perfectly with BitNet’s design philosophy:
Ternary weights + domino\<|reserved_200391|\>-style INT-only arithmetic.
The slight modelling penalty observed experimentally validates that *
the asymmetric off‑et never carries useful signal once weights are mean centred*.
