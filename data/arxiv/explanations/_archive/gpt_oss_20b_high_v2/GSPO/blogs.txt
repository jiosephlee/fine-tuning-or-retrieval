# From Token Tweaks To Sequence Strategies

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the driver behind the most advanced language models—think Qwen3 or GPT‑4‑Turbo—by teaching them how to reason about long horizons and complex puzzles.  
However, scaling RL beyond a few hundred billion parameters is still a **fragile** operation. A single misguided update can flip the model’s behavior from “helpful” to “untrainable.” 

A major source of this fragility lies in how older algorithms **correct** distribution mismatch between the current policy and the policy that produced the data. The most widespread solution has been **token‑level importance sampling**—the same trick used by PPO and its RL‑specific sibling GRPO—but it turns out that this approach blows up when you start generating really long sequences or when you add sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) layers.



## Why Token‑Level Ratios Get in the Way

When you use RL on an autoregressive language model \(\pi_\theta\), you collect a batch of responses \((x,y)\) from an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
The goal is then to estimate

\[
\E_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}[\text{(reward)}]
\]

under the *new* policy \(\pi_\theta\).  
Standard importance sampling tells us that

\[
\E_{y\sim \pi_{\theta}}[f(y)]
= \E_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)}\,f(y)
   \right].
\]

In practice, many algorithms apply this weighting **token by token**:

```text
w_{i,t} = π_θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) / π_θold(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}).
```

With GRPO you then multiply the *advantage* (the normalized reward) by each token’s \(w_{i,t}\) and clip it if it ventures too far from 1.

Why is this a problem?

| Problem | Why it matters |
|---------|----------------|
| **One sample per weight** | Every \(w_{i,t}\) comes from a single draw of next‑token distribution – there’s no averaging over many samples to make the weight reliable. |
| **High variance** | In long sequences tiny changes early on can blow up \(w_{i,t}\) exponentially, pushing it outside typical clipping bounds. |
| **Clipping feedback** | Once clipped, the gradient becomes biased or completely suppressed for that portion of the sequence—errors then accumulate across epochs. |
| **Instability at scale** | For MoE models new experts may activate after each update, making token weights *drift* dramatically from one gradient step to another.|

Empirically these issues manifest as sudden “model collapse” events that are impossible to recover from even after resetting checkpoints or adjusting hyper‑parameters.



## The Sequence‑Level Fix: Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

Instead of treating each token in isolation, **GSPO** recognises that rewards are given at the *sequence* level in RL settings—for example a verifier returns \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) after seeing an entire answer \(y\).  
The idea is therefore trivial yet powerful:

> **Match the unit of optimization (token span) with the unit of reward (entire sequence).**

### Core Ingredients

1. **Group responses per query**  
   For each query \(x\) we sample a *group* \(\{y_1,\dots ,y_G\}\) from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  

2. **Sequence‑level advantage**  
   The advantage for response \(i\) uses *group normalisation*:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_i =
   \frac{\,r(x,y_i)-\!\operatorname{mean}(r_i)\,}
        {\operatorname{std}(r_i)},
   \]
   ensuring all responses are compared fairly within their cohort.

3. **Sequence‑level importance ratio**  
   Rather than weighing tokens,

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
     \left(
     \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}
          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
     \right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
     =    \exp{\!\left(
        {1\over |y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
           \ln
           {\,\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}) 
            /\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
       \right)} .
   \]

   The fractional exponent normalises across different response lengths so that \(s_i(\theta)\) stays in a reasonable numeric window regardless of how long \(y_i\) is.


4. **Clipping whole sequences**  
   The clipping operation now operates on \(s_i(\theta)\), not on individual tokens:
   ```text
   min( s_i(θ)*Āᵢ , clip(s_i(θ),1−ε ,1+ε)*Āᵢ )
   ```
   The clipping thresholds are naturally much tighter (≈10⁻⁴ rather than ≈0.25), reflecting the smaller variation range of \(s_i(\theta)\).

These changes yield GSPO’s objective:

```math
J_GSPO(θ)=E[ avg over G & over queries(
              min( sᵢ(θ)*Āᵢ , clip(sᵢ(θ),…)*Āᵢ )
           ) ] .
```

### Gradient Picture

A glance at GSPO’s gradient shows that all tokens within a response receive an equal weighting factor \(s_i(\theta)/|y_i|\):

```math
∇ₜJ_GSPO =
 E[ 
    sᵢ(θ)/|yᵢ|
    · Āᵢ
    · Σ_t∇ₜlogπ_θ(yᵢₜ | x,yᵢ<ₜ)
 ] .
```

By contrast, GRPO multiplies each log‑probability by its own token‐wise ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), which creates heterogeneous term sizes and can explode variance.



## Experimental Takeaways

| Metric | GRPO (baseline) | GSPO |
|--------|-----------------|------|
| **Training stability** | Collapses often (> 50 % training runs)** | Stable across epochs |
| **Reward curves** | Oscillations & plateau | Smooth increase |
| **Efficiency** | Need more updates per reward increment | Faster convergence under same compute |
| **MoE suitability** | Requires additional Routing Replay strategy to prevent expert activation drift | Naturally converges without replay |
| **Infrastructure simplification** | Must recompute likelihoods under training engine due to precision mismatch | Can reuse inference engine likelihoods |

A surprising nuance was observed around *clipping fractions*: although GSPI clipped an order-of-magnitude more tokens than GRPI (because whole responses are either fully clipped or not), it still ended up using fewer gradient steps per reward unit — indicating that lower‐variance feedback at the referential unit size outweighs sheer number-of‑tokens count.



## MoE Models Made Simple

Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures introduce staggered layer activations that differ each time you call an NLG model—a problem invisible in dense models but very real for RL updates:

- With GRPI token ratios computed under *different* routing paths inciting massive fluctuations.
- With GSPI we only look at overall sequence likelihood—MoE activations average out and stay bounded—so no custom `Routing Replay` machinery is needed.

This means we no longer lose capacity or incur extra memory/sharding overhead just because RL forces us into complicated engineering traps.



## Broader Infrastructure Impact

Training engines like Megatron typically run in FP16 while inference engines such as vLLM or SGLang operate on mixed precision or even FP32 for log‐likelihood reproducibility during policy rollout collections.  

Because GSPI only cares about whole-sequence likelihoods:

- It tolerates small rounding differences between engines more gracefully than token-wise methods.
- Thus an RL pipeline can now directly feed in inference-engine outputs without recomputing likelihoods inside a heavy GPU cluster—a boon for partial rollout or multi-turn RL scenarios.


## Take Home

*Group Sequence Policy Optimization* essentially rewires RL training for large language models around these simple tenets:

1. Treat everything you’re optimizing as part of one holistic sample (the full text).
2. Compute a clean distributional correction at this very level.
3. Clip carefulies so you’re never too far off-policy yet avoid exploding gradients.
4.Reuse group normalisation so all generated responses compare fairly against one another.

By doing exactly what your reward already does—the entire answer score—it restores stability under huge batch sizes, unspooks MoE dynamics without custom hacks and lets you build smoother reinforcement pipelines.



> In short: swap fragile per‑token triage for robust sequence roping, and let your RL training stop collapsing whenever it scales up again.

# Stabilising Mixture‑of‑Experts Without Routing Replay  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become essential for pushing large language models (LLMs) toward higher reasoning capability – yet making these models stable under RL training is surprisingly hard when they use a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architecture.  
A common complaint among practitioners is that every policy update drags a different sparsity pattern into the decoding head – the set of experts that fire for a given hidden state moves around wildly – which makes per‑token importance ratios noisy and ultimately blows up training if left unchecked.\n\nIn this post I’ll explain why this happens, how a previously used remedy called **Routing Replay** works (and why it is expensive), and then show how **Group Sequence Policy Optimization** (GSPO) sidesteps all those pitfalls by looking only at a response’s *total* probability instead of individual tokens.\n\n---

## 1. Why MoEs Cause Instability During RL

MoE layers introduce sparse activation: for any given token, only a few experts are invoked while the rest stay idle. In a dense transformer each weight is updated consistently across all tokens; in an MoE model what changes between versions is essentially *where* the activation gates open.\n\nDuring RL each response \(y\) is sampled from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). When we perform an SGD step with the new parameters \(\theta\), the mapping between hidden states and experts often changes:\n\n* For some tokens the new model activates entirely different experts.\n* This produces **expert‑activation volatility** – roughly \(10\%\) of the experts on a given prompt appear under \(\pi_{\theta}\) that were absent under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) after even one gradient update.\n\nBecause most off‑policy algorithms compute *token‑level* importance weights \(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\), any drift in activation patterns immediately inflates or deflates these weights arbitrarily.\nThis corrupts gradient estimates – terms that should cancel out suddenly explode – which explains why vanilla GRPO often collapses when applied directly to MoEs.\n\n---

## 2. The Conventional Fix – Routing Replay

To arrest this collapse early work adopted **Routing Replay**:

1. After generating responses with \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), cache which experts were activated for each token’s hidden state.\n2. When re‐evaluating gradients with \(\pi_{\theta}\), *force* those same expert sets to fire so that token probabilities \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t})\) and \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t})\) are computed over identical circuits.\n\nWith identical activates sets the per‑token ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) stay within sane bounds (\([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\)) and RL training converges again.\nBut this trick is costly:\n\n* **Memory:** each rollout must retain its routing mask.\n* **Communication:** distributed training must broadcast these masks back‑and‑forth.\n* **Capacity loss:** forcing reuse of past routings limits how many experts can be explored during learning.\n\nUltimately it turns RL on an MoE into an extra engineering layer instead of pure algorithmic progress.\n\n---

## 3. Sequence–Level Importance Ratio = Stability’s Secret Sauce

GSPO replaces token‑wise ratios with a *sequence* ratio \(s_i(\theta)\). For a response \(y_i\),

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\left(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{%
      \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\right).
\]

Key things happening here:

* **Length normalization** removes exponential growth/decay of total likelihoods as length changes.\n* Because \(s_i(\theta)\) is computed *once per response*, diverging expert gates on individual tokens do not affect it – the probability mass still sums along entire sequences.\n* Even if some tokens see wildly different expert sets between policies, their contributions cancel out when averaged over all tokens in \(y_i\).\n\nWhen we clip at this level— `clip(s_i(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε)`—the same mechanism that kept PPO’s “off‑policy” samples bounded now applies reliably even when underlying activations shift dramatically.\nThis alleviates exactly the identified instability problem caused by expert volatility.\n\n---

## 4. How GSPO Eliminates Routing Replay

Because GSPI operates only at sequence level:

1. **Token ratios no longer matter** – we never evaluate \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Hence there’s no sensitivity to which experts fired last time vs now.\n2. **No need to cache or replay routing masks** – each mini‐batch gradient uses only one lookup for total probability under both old and new policies.\n3. Experiments reported show a standard RL convergence curve without invoking Routing Replay whatsoever:\n   * Training reward increases steadily until plateaus rather than crashing mid‑course;\n   * The test performance on downstream benchmarks climbs proportionally as compute grows.\n     These results come from exactly the same core LM (Qwen3‑30B-A3B‑Base), so any difference was due purely to GSPI’s new objective formulation.\n\nThe rewriting also reduces memory bandwidth drastically because we eliminate cross‑node traffic required by routing masks everywhere they would otherwise appear during training or inference interleaving chains like partial rollouts or multi‐turn dialogues.\nTherefore GSPI both simplifies infra and opens full expert capacity to be exploited freely during learning—side effects highly valuable for very deep MoE stacks where tiny gating changes can chain outside ordinary capacity limits.\n\n--- 

## 5. Summary of Benefits

| Feature | Classic GRPO + Routing Replay | GSPI |
|---------|------------------------------|------|
| Expert activation handling | Needs distinct gating replay | No gating… uses seq likelihood |
| Memory & communication overhead | High (store gates per sample) | Low |
| Training stability | Sensitive → often collapses unless replayed | Robust – even longer sequences |
| Clipping range | Large (e.g., 0–0.5 on per‑token weight) | Tiny (`3×10⁻⁴`... `4×10⁻⁴` on seq weight) |
| Extensibility | Limited by gating constraints | Unrestricted framework usage |

These gains made it straightforward for Qwen3 developers to undergo several stages of RL fine-tuning without ever resorting to complex routing tricks—an unusually clean path compared with prior literature where special case solutions wrapped around flawed importance sampling foundations would otherwise be mandatory.

--- 

## 6. Takeaway

Mixture‑of‑Experts introduces structural volatility that mars token‑wise off–policy corrections used throughout standard RL baselines like GRPO or PPO variants—including their attempts at clipping deviants via reward conditioning or minibatch partitioning alone.
By going back one level higher—from token probabilities down to *total sequence likelihood*—the Group Sequence Policy Optimization algorithm eliminates this source of noise entirely.
Consequently it no longer needs delicate constructions such as Routing Replay, freeing both kernels (training engines) and developers from having bespoke state management pipelines while still gaining full benefit from MoE scaling during reinforcement learning.

If you’re fine-tuning an LLM with heavy sparse gating layers on any domain task,
consider adopting GSPI as your next reward optimisation backbone—you’ll get rid of router cruft,
restore label stability,
and let your system harness every expert it has available!



# Clipping Matters – More Tokens Cut Off Yet Training Faster

Reinforcement‑learning (RL) has become the go‑to method for pushing large language models (LMs) beyond what supervised fine‑tuning can achieve. Yet as models grow—especially sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) variants—training stability turns into a major bottleneck. The prevailing RL approach for language modeling is *Group Relative Policy Optimization* (GRPO), which clips at every token time step using an importance ratio derived from the policy’s next‑token distribution.

A recent paper introduced **Group Sequence Policy Optimization** (GSPI—quite often simply called *GSPI*) that moves the clutter out of every token and into every whole generation instead. Surprisingly, GSPI ends up **clipping a much larger fraction of tokens** than GRPI while delivering **faster training** and higher downstream performance. Let’s dig into why this counter‑intuitive phenomenon happens.



## The Core Idea in One Equation

GRPI’s core objective looks like

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPI}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigg[\frac1G\sum_{i}\frac1{|y_i|}
    \sum_t
    \min\!\Bigl(
        w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i,t},
        \,\text{clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_{i,t}
    \Bigr)
\Bigg],
\]

with a token‑level importance ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

This means **every single generated word** goes through its own clipping test before being counted.



In contrast, GSPI replaces the token‑wise ratio with a **sequence‐wide**, length‑normalized one:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Biggl[\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t
      \log w_{i,t}(\theta)
   \Biggr].
\]

The updated objective becomes

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPI}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
   \Bigg[
       \frac1G
       \sum_i
       \min(
          s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,
          \text{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon_s,\,
                           1+\varepsilon_s)\,\widehat A_i
       )
   \Bigg],
\]

where $\widehat A_i$ is the *sequence* advantage derived from a group’s rewards.



## Why Token‑Level Clipping Is Painful

The intuition behind importance sampling allows us to reweight samples drawn from an old policy $\pi_{\text{old}}$ so that their expectation equals that under the new policy $\pi$. Crucially, this works reliably **only when many samples are averaged**—the weight should act over a *population* rather than an *individual* sample point.

With a single word $y_{t}$ sampled from $\pi_{\text{old}}$, the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ offers no meaningful correction; it simply reflects how much *that particular word* happened to change under policy update $\theta$, not how good or bad it is relative to other words or the overall sequence reward distribution. When you multiply this noisy weight with its learned advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$ or clip it separately for every time step,

* you accumulate tiny errors along a long generation,
* you amplify them further if many tensors get clipped,
* you feed back gradient noise that can push the model drastically away from its learning target.



Longer responses exactingly exacerbate this problem because each added token introduces another potentially harmful weight factor.



## Sequence–Level Clipping Learns Where It Matters

By averaging across all tokens before performing any truncation,

* **each response gets only one bonus weight**—the product of all its individual token ratios diluted by length normalization,
* **the weight behaves like a genuine probability correction** at the level where rewards actually sit,
* **the clipping criterion applies globally**, ignoring any minor fluctuations inside individual words.



This structure yields *much cleaner gradients*. Intuitively:

> “Keep your clipper looking only at whole sentences rather than each word in isolation.”



### Empirical Evidence

The paper’s experiments confirmed two counter‑intuitive facts:

| Training regime | Fraction of per‑token values clipped | Efficiency / Reward gains |
|-----------------|---------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| GRPI           | ~$10^{-2}$ | Slower convergence; training instability on MoE |
| GHII            | ~~$10^{-4}$ | Faster convergence; higher reward curves |

Even though GSPI’s clipping routines discard **twice as many tokens** as GRPI (up to two orders of magnitude difference), the later steps still converge much faster—even while allocating the same amount of computational budget.



Why? Because “cutting off” noisy signal removes weak updates that otherwise would jostle learning dynamics away from true decision boundaries.



### Gradient Comparison

Look at their theoretical gradients:
\nocite{}
For GRPI:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPI}}
= 
\mathbb E[\,w_{t}(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta \log p_t\,]
$$
The weight $w_t$ varies widely over time steps—negative/positive adv­antages get randomly scaled by values closer or farther than one.
Whereas for GSPI:
$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPI}}
= 
s(\theta)\;
      \frac1{|y|}
      \sum_t\nabla_\theta \log p_t .
$$
Here every token receives an identical multiplicative factor $s$, which itself is bounded by \(e^{[-k,k]}\). That guarantees stability regardless of how many words there are.



If you examine plots from Section 6.2 “Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions”, you’ll see precisely how many sample points get discarded by each algorithm—GSPI starts fairly high but converts most into reliable signals rather than random noise.



## Practical Upsides Beyond The Numbers

### Booming MoE Stability

Sparse MoE models further inflame this issue because expert selection can swing wildly after each policy update. Token‑level ratios become inconsistent when different experts produce wildly different probabilities under $\pi$ vs $\pi_{\text{old}}$. GSPI’s reliance only on *sequence likelihood* sidesteps expert volatility entirely—no routing replay tricks needed! This simplifies training pipelines immensely.



### Flexibility With Inferencing Engines

Because static inference engines often operate at lower precision levels than training engines, reconstructing exact log probabilities slowly becomes expensive and imprecise. Since switching back and forth requires evaluating fine‑grained logits, any misalignment hurts GT calls sharply—for example WLSL's \(10^{-8}\) loss spikes during cheaper evaluation batch execution on gating faults.\nove For sequence likelihood values they’re far less sensitive because small precision errors are averaged across many tokens anyway—a boon when you use an inference engine like vLLM directly for RL signals.



## Takeaway – Clip Harder if You Can Clip Fewer Words  

If your goal is fast RL convergence on large LMs:

1️⃣ Switch your exceedance tests from per word → per response  
2️⃣ Clip full response likelihoods using tailored epsilon bands (\(~3\times10^{-4}\)-\(4\times10^{-4}\))  
3️⃣ Accept higher overall clip-rate — your gradient noise shells diminish instead of exploding  

This simple conceptual shift lets models jump toddlers toward optimum faster than any clever value MLP trick could muster.



---

> „Removing noise entirely leaves stronger signals behind so gradients move faster toward optimal policies without compromising stability.“  
> — *Summary line distilled from Group Sequence Policy Optimization research*

Through this lens, “more clipped”‐means no longer “bad”; it simply means “better judgement about where good learning signals lie.”



# Simplifying Reinforcement Learning Infrastructure

Reinforcement learning (RL) is increasingly becoming the tool‑kit that powers state‑of‑the‑art large language models (LLMs).  However, every back‑propagation pass usually requires a handful of highly detailed operations that drain a lot of compute resources:

* Token‑level importance weights (as used by PPO/G–RPo) demand **full‑precision** log‑probabilities for every generated token.
* Re‑computing those values inside an expensive “trainer” engine such as Megatron or DeepSpeed is costly, both in memory bandwidth and idle CPU time.
* In distributed settings—partial rollouts, multi‑turn dialogues, disaggregated GPU+CPU bins—there is often a *precision mismatch* between what was produced during generation (by a lightweight server like SGLang or vLLM) and what can be fed back into the dense trainer framework.

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPI\!) tackles these pain points head‑on by shifting all model‑parameter updates onto **whole‑sequence** data rather than one token at a time.

---

## From Token‐Level Noise to Sequence‐Level Solidarity

The classic perception behind PPO is

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

where every generated **token** carries its own deviation factor when moving from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) toward a new one \(\pi_\theta\).  
When rewards apply *to an entire response* \(y=(y_1,\dots ,y_T)\), these many individual corrections accumulate into **high–variance gradients**—especially problematic on long sequences—and eventually lead RL models astray or even collapse entirely.

GSPI avoids this pitfall by treating a reply as an indivisible unit:

\[
s_i(\theta)
= \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
= \exp \Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\!\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     \Bigr],
\]

an average change factor over *all* tokens multiplied by \(1/|y|\).  
Cutting this global quantity down dramatically slashes variance while keeping clip windows far tighter (\(\sim10^{-4}\)) than token‑based methods (\(\sim10^{-1}\)), which tightens control points across batch splits **without extra bootstrapping** via value networks or auxiliary loss components.

---

## Why Whole Sequences Are Precision-Friendly

When you run an LLM forward pass on **dedicated generative hardware**, you get log‑probabilities at whatever numerical format your deployment chooses—half‑precision floats for speed on GPUs or even lower MLPCell usage on CPUs via SGLang/vLLM abstractions.  
A minor rounding error on any single token disperses little when summed in log‑space over hundreds of words—a tiny relative shift—and gets squashed again after exponentiation into \(s_i(\theta)\).  

Contrast this with token‑level importance ratios \(w_t(\theta)\): each entry depends on two separate forward passes that might differ by data center location **and** floating point format payoffs across shards—a recipe for inconsistency that instantly inflates noise because every tiny slide becomes injected directly into CRL update statistics before clipping and aggregation happenally surface themselves earlier in downstream calculations.



### Key Consequence

> **You can safely reuse probability scores computed during a lightweight “inference” pass without forcing them back through your heavyweight trainer before applying gradients**—an ability forbidden under PPO/G–RPo paradigms because they rely heavily on exact per-token logics.



---

## Practical Gains for Distributed Pipelines

| Constrained Scenario | Old Way | What GSPI Gives |
|----------------------|---------|----------------|
| **Memory & compute budget** | Recompute all logits under a massive transformer block | One *single* sequence‐likelihood number fits into an `float16` buffer |
| **f16 vs fp32 mismatch** | Token‐wise ratios amplify rounding errors → unstable gradients | Entire clamped window shielded around \(10^{-4}\), rendering f16/.32 differences negligible |
| **Disaggregated rollout / response logs** | Must copy out generation buffers then send them back to megatron GPU cluster | Send compact log probability vector once—no second copy |
| **Partial rollouts / multi-turn recommendation tasks** | Need arbitrary subset probes reconstituted inside trainer → expensive sync | On-demand lookups invoke lightweight callers like SGLang then fuse into existing gradient tables |
| **MoE expert activation volatility** | Requires special routing heuristics (`Routing Replay`) and extra route cache transfers | Pure sequence deficit ignores internal routing instability → stability recovered automatically |

> In short, simply treating “response” \( y \) as one ordinal id frees all pipeline latencies tied up in artifact round-trips across heterogeneous compute graphs.


---

## From Algorithmic Insight To Deployment Simplicity

At first glance you might think “splitting off modeling duties still leaves all reinforcement learning tricks untouched.”  
But GSPI’s design compels multiple standard RL wrappers—value network provision ‑> drop out ‑> elaborate KL terms—to collapse conveniently:

1. No approximate return baseline required—the advantage \( A_i = r(x,y_i)-\mu_r \over\sigma_r \) already normalizes reward signals groupwise.
2. No separate network is needed to capture per-token expectations—both local dynamics *and* global policy change are encoded together via normalized importance ratio.
3. Single clipping hyperparameter \( \varepsilon \) around half‑thousandth means every experience batch automatically sits inside a safe tradeoff landscape without iterative tuning cycles.



As something tangible: With Qwen3 models freshly fine-tuned offline, researcher teams discovered that simply adding one more call‐to‐SGLang world-score retrieval line reduced infrastructure footprint by 7 × while preserving benchmark gains presented earlier against GRPO baseline.

- [ ] Replace old token‑clipping objective with whole–sequence \( s_i(\theta) \)-based gradient.
- [ ] Use precomputed logits generated by your light driver framework – no CPU overlaps needed.
- [ ] Switch clipping window from \(0.\!2–0.\!27\) downwards (~\(3\times10^{-4}\)) – check *policy variance* first ballpark via early epoch trigger rollback tables.

- [ ] Release rolling batches back earlier – pipelines built on zero‑copy shared-memory show linear-scaling if scored off-fast UDP sockets link-up via GO-Port 🎯

# Real World Gains – How Qwen 3 Benefits From GSOP  

Large‑scale language models have reached the point where **reinforcement learning (RL)** becomes the single most effective means of turning raw statistical power into useful intelligence‑behaviour — solving math proofs, drafting production‑ready code or playing Go at grandmaster strength are all top‑down objectives expressed through human‑scored rewards.  
However, pushing an RL signal onto a 30‑B‑parameter Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) model isn’t cheap nor trivial: *any* tremble in the optimisation pipeline can snowball into a catastrophic collapse that wipes out massive pre‑training work and wastes an entire month of GPU time.*

The latest camp‑ground algorithm used before our work was **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** [1]. It removed the expensive value predictor that Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) typically relies on by computing an advantage inside each “group” of responses produced by an older policy for a given query \(x\). Despite its cleverness, GRPO’s core mathematics mis‑measures how much “off–policy” a sampled response really is – the **token–level importance weight** it applies never normalises over a single “rollout space”; instead it compounds noise across every generated symbol. As a result most practitioners saw persistent oscillations during training until they introduced ad‑hoc tricks such as *Routing Replay* for MoE models — essentially forcing every updated model over every old expert routing plan into one another’s computational graph just so gradients could stay bounded.

Enter **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSOP)** [2].  
The authors re‑identified the fundamental misstep in GRPO’s objective — **the unit mismatch between rewards and gradient terms** — and corrected it by redefining importance sampling entirely at the *sequence* level rather than at individual tokens.

---

## TL;DR  

Converting odd token ratios into *sequence ratios* delivers three concrete winners when applied to Qwen 3:

| Experiment | Core change | Result |
|------------|-------------|--------|
| **Stability** | Sequence–level clipping removes a major source of variance | No catastrophic collapses observed even after 10× total GPU hours |
| **MoE scalability** | No need for costly Routing Replay | Training converges routinely on full dfined MoEs without extra metadata passes |
| **Infrastructure simplification** | Likelihoods come from inference engine directly | Enables partial rollout / multi‑turn schemes without sacrificing precision |

Across mathematics puzzles (AIME’24 Pass@1), coding benchmarks (LiveCodeBench Pass@1) and competitive contests (CodeForces Elo), *every* measure climbed once we swapped from GRPO → GSOP while keeping everything else identical – same base model, same data distribution, same compute budget… just a different objective formulation.

## From Token Ratios to Response Ratios  

### The flaw in token‑wise ratios  
GRPO's core idea was fascinatingly simple:
choose \(G\) responses \((y_1,\dots,y_G)\) from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) for each query,
compute an advantage per response,
and apply PPO‐style clipping *per token*.  
Yet each token’s importance weight takes the form

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
                   {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]

Earlier groundwork teaches us that legitimate importance sampling requires **many samples drawn from the behaviour distribution before one can correct expectations** – a principle completely invalidated when you multiply dozens of single-token weights together inside one long response linearly formatted autop-runized LLM generation! No matter how many neighbours are considered, this per-token correction boils down into highly noisy estimates that reverberate along entire sequences:

∴ high‐variance gradients → wandering optimisation → sloppy update → floor hit collapse.


### The underpinning idea behind GSOP  
GSOP recognises that our reward function evaluates an entire response \(y\) once it is finished – there is no justification for penalising intermediate symbols separately once you already know whether the complete shot earned bonus points or merciless penalties afterward.
Therefore it raises its “off‑policy” detector back up one level—from every time step directly under the current policy—to *the whole likelihood*:
 
\[
s_i(\theta)=
   \Biggl(\frac{\displaystyle \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\displaystyle \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
     \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   \Biggr)^{\!{1/|y_i|}}
 = \exp\!\biggl({{1\over |y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{ |y_i|}
   \ln { \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\over
        \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}
      (y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}}\!\biggr),
\]

where the division is consequently seeded by length normalisation \(^{\,1/|y_i|}\). GM both caps variance between very short outputs (“yes”) and very long digests (“...”) while ensuring each \(s_i(\theta)\) lives inside roughly \(0.\!03 < s < 0.\!004\)’moving toward low sensitivity numbers compared with GRPO’s static \([0,.27]\).

Clipping these sequence ratios has far fewer degrees of freedom versus per‐token clipping because there are simply far fewer sequences than tokens generated per batch – hence tighter gradients and cleaner wins.

## Practical Edge For **Qwen 3**

### A cold start that never froze  
Using a fresh copy of **Qwen 3‑30B-A3B‑Base**, fine tuning was conducted with four mini-batches per rollout chunk—an everywhere‐present scenario that forces off-policy usage naturally—but nothing else changed apart from swapping Jacobian units around:
new clipping window \( [\,10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}\,) = [\,10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}\,)   vs.\,[\,0.20,\,.27]\,\. This alone made every policy update mathematically disciplined enough so that even after super-long rollouts (\(>1500\,\$.\)) there were *no occluding reset events.*

In practice:
* Prefix termination was shortened while throughput doubled;
* Early stopping criteria failed rarely because loss consistently sank;
* Archival checkpoints were reliably reused rather than reinstated constantly.



### On Mixture‑of‑Experts instability  
In dense transformer nets any probability shift stays concentrated inside one network wireframe – weights keep shifting near-on mask arrangement without straying too far off ironically known roes design budgets may spawn large amplitude changes when you insert enough sparse experts behind each layer…a classic hidden variable problem that formerly forced us to freeze entire gates during invariant epochs.*

GRPO struggled because its token ratios vary wildly whenever activation sets change after a gradient step – even a single expert swap topples one particular choice tip‐off weight off its baseline value by several decimals which accumulated until propagation disappeared forever.*  

GSOP sidestepped this altogether through downstream invariance:
since an expert swap hardly modifies *total likelihood*, \(s_i(\theta)\) stays near what it was before,
so optimisation continues without fallback measures.
In other words ***Roulate Replay became obsolete***—no longer needed framing additional buffers mapping old+new activations excise themselves permanently.
This simplification cuts both memory footprint (*MoE + Trainer*) by almost half while letting us train deeper MoEs cleanly.



### Infrastructure gains unnoticed yet critical  

Most large GPT families rely on separate engines at training time versus actual inference
(zero tolerance mismatches between Megatron/training trainum's `logprob` precision vs SGLang/vLLM),
which forces joint retraces through microbatches whenever you want pay attention
to fresh negative log penalty at least once per epoch.*  

Because every loss evaluation under GSOP uses only sequence‐level probability,
precision mismatches vanish automatically –
the small stochastic drift caused by rounding doesn’t propagate because you’re only
calculating \(p(y|\dots)\), not each \(p(y_t|\dots)\).
So you copper-sampling approaches such as *partial rollout* straightforwardly re-use inference batches’ computed log prob arrays,
cutting runtime overhead dramatically.
This opens low-latency turn-based engagement inside reinforcement loops
for complex dialogue environment simulations *without order-of-magnitude extra cost previously needed.*
