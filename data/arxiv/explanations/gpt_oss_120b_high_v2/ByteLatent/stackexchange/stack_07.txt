\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

### What is the exact FLOP calculation for BLT per byte and how does it compare to a standard token‑based transformer?
Question:
Section 4.5 provides a formula involving \(\text{Transf.FL}(h,l,m,V)\) and \(\text{Cross Attn.FL}\). Could someone break down each term (e.g., what does \(V=0\) mean for certain components)? How is the division by \(n_p\) derived for per‑byte FLOPs? And why is the embedding lookup considered zero FLOPs?
Answer:
In Section 4.5 the authors turn the raw flop count of every block in **BLT** into a single “flops‑per‑byte’’ number that can be compared against a token‑based Transformer. The expression they give is  

\[ 
\rule{\linewidth}{0pt}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathrm{FL}_{\mathrm{BLT}}
        &= 
        \frac{\mathrm{Transf.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal G},l_{\mathcal G},
                m=n_{\mathrm{ctx}}/n_{p},V=0)}{n_{p}}
        \\[1ex]
        &~~+\, 
        \mathrm{Transf.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal E},l_{\mathcal E},
                m=w_{\mathcal E},V=0)
        \\[1ex]
        &~~+\, 
        \mathrm{Transf.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal D},l_{\mathcal D},
                m=w_{\mathcal D},V=256)
        \\[1ex]
        &~~+\, 
        \mathrm{CrossAttn.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal E},l_{\mathcal E},
                m=n_{p},r=n_{p}/k)\,\frac{k}{n_{p}}
        \\[1ex]
        &~~+\, 
        \mathrm{CrossAttn.\!FL}(h_{\mathcal D},l_{\mathcal D},
                m=k,r=k/n_{p}) .
    \end{aligned}
\rule{\linewidth}{0pt}
] 

All symbols are defined in the paper:

* \(h_{*}\) – hidden dimensionality of the block,
* \(l_{*}\) – number of layers,
* \(m\)   – effective sequence length seen by that block,
* \(V\)   – size of a final vocabulary projection,
* \(n_{p}\) – average number of **bytes** contained in one patch,
* \(w_{\mathcal E},\,w_{\mathcal D}\) – sliding‑window widths used inside the local encoder / decoder,
* \(k\)   – factor by which a patch representation is split into sub‑vectors before entering the global model (the paper uses \(k=2\)),
* \(r\)   – ratio “queries $\div$ keys‑values’’ that appears in a cross‑attention matrix,
* \(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}\) – total byte context length fed to the model.

---

### What each term means  

The first line is the cost of **the latent Global Transformer** \(\mathcal G$. Its self‑attention runs over *patches*, not over individual bytes, so its effective sequence length is  
the number of patches that fit into the byte context,  
\(m = n_{\mathrm{ctx}}/n_{p}\).
The function \(\mathrm{Transf.\!FL}(\cdot)\) follows the Chinchilla flavour of transformer flop counting:
for one layer it includes QKV projections (\(3mh^{2}\)), attention scores (\(mh^{2}\)), output projection (\(mh^{2}\)), and two feed‑forward linear maps (\(8mh\,d_{\mathrm ff}}\)).  
Because \(\mathcal G\()’s output is a latent vector rather than logits over concrete byte symbols, there is **no vocabulary head**, hence \(V=0).

Even though \(\mathcal G\'s cost scales with the **number of patches**, we want a metric “flops per **byte**”. Every patch amortises its work over all its constituent bytes, so we divide this term by \(n_{p}\). This division simply spreads the cost uniformly across the average \(n_{p}\) bytes that belong to each patch—precisely what “per‑byte’’ entails.

The second line accounts for **the Local Encoder** \(\mathcal E\. Its self\-attention operates on a sliding window of width \(w_{\mathcal E}\); it therefore sees every byte inside that window and its runtime already reflects work done on a per‐byte basis, so no extra division appears. Again there is no direct vocabulary prediction here—\(V=0\\).

The third line is **the Local Decoder** \(\mathcal D\. Besides its own self‐attention on windows of size \(w_\D{}\), it finally produces logits over all possible byte values (\(|{\mathcal V}| = 256)\), which incurs an extra matrix multiply with shape \((h_\D{}\times256)\); consequently \(V=256\\).

The fourth line captures **cross‑attention from bytes $\to$ patches** inside the encoder.
Each patch creates one query vector while attending only to its own constituent bytes (the keys and values).

### Why embedding lookups count as zero FLOPs  

The authors treat embedding tables as pure lookups (“read an $h$‐dimensional vector from memory”) rather than dense matrix multiplies.
A lookup does not involve any arithmetic operations—no multiply–accumulate cycles—so its contribution
to “floating point operations’’ is nil under their bookkeeping convention
(exactly as done in most compute‐controlled scaling studies such as Chinchilla).

When BLT chooses patches whose average size is larger than a conventional token

The extra cost incurred by both local models and cross‐attention scales
with their small windows ($w_\E{}, w_\D{}$)
and modest hidden dimensions ($h_\E{}, h_\D{} \\ll h_\G{}$),
so they add only modest constant overhead—typically well under~10%
of total budget at reported scales.

Empirically this analysis matches Figure 6 (“fixed inference scaling’’):
with an average patch size ≈ 8 bytes BLT saves ≈ 50 % inference flops relative
to Llama 3 at equal model capacity,
precisely because its first term has been divided by an extra factor
≈ 8⁄4 ≈ 2 while all other terms remain comparable or cheaper.

### Takeaway  

The formula in §4.​5 simply adds up standard Transformer flop counts for each submodule,
switches off any vocabulary projection where it isn’t needed ($V=0)$,
rescales cross­attention according to how many queries versus keys exist inside
a patch, and finally normalises only those components whose cost depends on *patches*.  
Dividing the global transformer’s flop count by $\, n_p$
spreads its work over every byte covered by&nbsp;a&nbsp;patch,
turning “flops per *patch*’’ into “flops per *byte*”. Because embeddings are just table lookups they contribute zero arithmetic operations under this accounting scheme.
