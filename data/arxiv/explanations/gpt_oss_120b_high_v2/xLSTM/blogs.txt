# From LSTMs to xLSTMs: Giving Recurrent Networks a Modern Makeover  

*Why classical LSTMs fell behind the Transformer wave—and how the newly proposed xLSTM brings recurrent models back into the spotlight for today’s massive language‑model workloads.*

---

## The Rise and Fall of Classic LSTMs  

In the early 1990s Hochreiter & Schmidhuber introduced the **Long Short‑Term Memory** (LSTM) unit. Its hallmark was the **constant‑error carousel**—an additive update that preserved gradients over long time spans—paired with three sigmoid gates (input $i$, forget $f$, output $o$). In compact form:

\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t},\qquad 
h_{t}=o_{t}\,\psi(c_{t}) .
\]

These ideas solved the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued vanilla RNNs and led to spectacular successes in speech synthesis, handwriting generation, machine translation, code generation, reinforcement learning agents such as AlphaStar, and even hydrological forecasting. For many years **the LSTM was the go‑to sequence model** for both research and production systems.

The landscape shifted dramatically in 2017 when Vaswani et al. introduced **self‑attention** in the *Transformer*. Because attention can be computed **in parallel** over all positions, Transformers achieved far better hardware utilization than recurrent networks that must crawl through a sequence step‑by‑step. Coupled with large‑scale pre‑training pipelines, Transformers quickly scaled to billions of parameters—ushering in the era of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT‑3 and PaLM—while classic RNNs receded into niche applications.

---

## Three Core Limits That Held LSTMs Back  

Even though an LSTM already captures long‑range dependencies better than an RNN without gates, three structural bottlenecks prevented it from competing at “LLM scale”.

| Limitation | What It Means | Concrete Symptom |
|------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Inability to revise stored information** | Once a value has been written into the cell state $c$, later inputs can only *add* or *multiply* it via $i$/$f$—they cannot replace an earlier decision without blowing up or vanishing gradients. | In a *Nearest Neighbor Search* task where a more similar vector appears later in the stream, an ordinary LSTM retains the first entry and fails to correct it. |
| **Scalar cell storage** | Each hidden dimension carries only a single scalar $c$ (or a short vector) → limited bandwidth for encoding rich context such as rare words or fine‑grained syntax. | On *Rare Token Prediction* (e.g., low‑frequency words in WikiText‑103) standard LSTMs show higher perplexities than models with richer memories. |
| **Sequential hidden–hidden recurrence** | The hidden state $h_{t}$ feeds directly into $h_{t+1}$ via $\mathbf{R}h_{t}$ (“memory mixing”). This enforces strict sequential processing on GPUs and hampers batch parallelism. | Training speed plateaus despite adding more GPUs—a stark contrast with fully parallelizable attention layers.|

These issues made it hard for vanilla LSTMs to scale beyond a few hundred million parameters while retaining efficiency or accuracy on long text streams.

---

## Introducing xLSTM – A Modern Upgrade for Recurrence  

The **Extended Long Short‑Term Memory** (xLSTM) family tackles all three problems head‑on while preserving the core idea that a recurrent cell should both *store* information reliably **and** *update* it flexibly. Two concrete variants emerge:

| Variant | Memory Shape | Key Innovations |
|---------|--------------|------------------|
| **sLSTM** (scalar) | Single scalar per hidden unit ($c\in\mathbb{R}$) | Exponential gating + *normalizer* $n$ + *stabilizer* $m$ → reversible storage decisions;<br>Memory mixing retained across multiple heads/cells for state tracking |
| **mLSTM** (matrix) | Full matrix $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ | Covariance/outer‑product update → associative key–value store;<br>Fully parallelizable because hidden–hidden connections are removed |

Both variants embed these mechanisms inside modern **pre‑LayerNorm residual blocks**, which are exactly what contemporary LLM stacks use today. Below we unpack each component.

### Exponential Gating – Revising Memory on the Fly  

Instead of limiting $i$ and $f$ to sigmoids ($0\!-\!1$), xLSTMs allow them to be **exponential**:

\[
i_{t}= \exp(\tilde{i}_{t}), \qquad 
f_{t}= \sigma(\tilde{f}_{t})\;\text{or}\;\exp(\tilde{f}_{t}) .
\]

Because an exponential can become arbitrarily large or small, an incoming gate can *override* past decisions rather than just attenuate them—a direct remedy for the “cannot revise” problem. However, unchecked exponentials quickly cause numerical overflow. To keep everything stable two auxiliary states are introduced:

*The normalizer*:  

\[
n_{t}= f_{t}\, n_{t-1}+ i_{t},
\]

which accumulates gate strengths over time so that later divisions by $n_{t}$ effectively rescale hidden activations:

\[
\tilde h_{t}=c_{t}/n_{t}.
\]

*The stabilizer*:  

\[
m_{t}= \max\big(\log f_{t}+m_{t-1},\, \log i_{t}\big),
\]

with stabilized gates  

\[
i^{\prime}_{t}= \exp(\log i_{t}-m_{t}),\qquad
f^{\prime}_{t}= \exp(\log f_{t}+m_{t-1}-m_{t}),
\]

guaranteeing that gradients remain well‑behaved despite using $\exp$. The authors prove mathematically that swapping $(i,f)$ with their primed versions does **not change any forward output nor any gradient**, so stability comes essentially “for free”.

### sLSTM – Scalar Memory With Mixing  

The forward update stays close to classic LSTMs but now leverages exponential gating:

```text
c_t = f_t * c_(t−1) + i_t * z_t
n_t = f_t * n_(t−1) + i_t
h_t = o_t * (c_t / n_t)
```

Multiple scalar cells can be grouped into *heads*. Within each head **memory mixing** happens through recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_*$ connecting hidden states back onto cell inputs/gates—an ability crucial for tasks that require tracking an internal Boolean flag (e.g., parity checking). Because $i$/$f$ are exponential, a later input can sharply increase $i$ while simultaneously suppressing older contributions via $f$, achieving exactly what vanilla sigmoids cannot do.

### mLSTM – Matrix Memory As Fast Associative Store  

The jump from scalar `$c$` to full matrix `$\mathbf{C}$` gives each layer a built‑in associative memory reminiscent of classic Hopfield/Bidirectional Associative Memories:

\[
\mathbf{C}_{t}= f_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{\, t-1}
               + i_{t}\,\mathbf{v}_{\, t}\,\mathbf{k}^{\!\top}_{\, t}.
\]

Here `$\mathbf{k}_{\, t}$` (“key”) and `$\mathbf{v}_{\, t}$` (“value”) are linear projections of the current input $\mathbf{x}_{\, t}$:

```
q_t = W_q   x_t + b_q        # query at time t
k_t = (1/√d) W_k   x_t + b_k # key
v_t = W_v   x_t + b_v        # value
```

When we later want to retrieve a stored value we multiply `$\mathbf{C}_t$` by a fresh query vector `$\mathbf{q}_t$` :

\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = 
   \frac{\mathbf C_t\, \mathbf q_t}
        {\max(|\, \mathbf n^{\!\top}_td\, \mathbf q_t|,\;1)} ,
\qquad 
h_t = o_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf h}_t,
\]

with `$\mathbf n_t$` being the normalizer that now accumulates weighted keys (`$\sum i\,k$`). This retrieval rule is mathematically identical to applying a **covariance update** known from Fast Weight Programmers and optimal under signal–to–noise criteria for binary patterns (Sejnowski 1979). Because there are no hidden–hidden recurrences (`$h_{\! t−1}$` does not feed back), this whole computation can be expressed as batched matrix operations—a perfect fit for GPU acceleration comparable to FlashAttention or modern SSM scans.

### Residual Blocks & Deep Stacking  

To integrate either variant into a full-fledged language model we wrap them inside **residual blocks**:

* **Post up‑projection block** (`s` variant):  
  The recurrent unit first processes inputs in their native dimension (`d`). Then an *up‑projection* lifts its output into a higher dimensional space (`d’>d`), applies nonlinearity (`GeLU` or similar), projects back down and adds via skip connection—mirroring what standard Transformer blocks do after their attention layer.
  
* **Pre up‑projection block** (`m` variant):  
  Because `$d\times d$` matrices naturally provide huge capacity when working in high dimensions, we first lift inputs before feeding them into mLSTM—a design aligned with State Space Model backbones such as S4/Mamba.

All blocks use pre‑LayerNorm so that depth scaling behaves just like modern GPT families (`pre_norm(x)+Block(x)`).

Stacking dozens of these blocks yields an **xLSTM architecture** reminiscent of GPT’s depth/width tradeoffs yet driven by recurrent dynamics instead of attention matrices.

---

## Empirical Evidence ‑ From Toy Tasks To Billion‑Parameter Language Modeling  

### Synthetic Benchmarks Show What Changed  

*State Tracking*: Formal‑language experiments (parity checking, nested parentheses) demonstrate that only models equipped with **memory mixing**—namely sLSTM—solve tasks requiring an internal flag over arbitrarily long spans. Pure Transformers or State Space Models plateau at chance because they lack mutable hidden states that can be revised by later inputs.

*Associative Recall*: In the Multi‑Query Associative Recall setting sequences contain up to 256 random key–value pairs dispersed over lengths ≤ 2048. An mLSTM’s matrix memory hits near‐perfect recall whereas scalars saturate early—highlighting how `$d\times d$` storage dramatically expands capacity without blowing up parameter counts because updates are parameter‐free outer products.

*Long Range Arena*: Across diverse long‐sequence challenges (image classiﬁcation from pixels, Pathfinder navigation…), xLSTMs retain strong accuracy while matching or beating recent linear‐attention baselines—showing scalability beyond modest toy lengths.

### Scaling Up: 15 B Tokens on SlimPajama  

All models were tuned to roughly GPT‑3’s parameter budget (~350 M total) and trained on ‑15 billion tokens from the SlimPajama corpus under identical auto‑regressive regimes:

| Model family | Validation perplexity |
|--------------|----------------------|
| Standard Transformer (GPT‑3 style) | Higher |
| LLaMA (Transformer) | Higher |
| Mamba (State Space Model) | Higher |
| RWKV series (RNN) | Higher |
| Retention / Hyena / GLA (linear transformers) | Higher |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (more mL STM blocks) | **Lowest** |

Ablation experiments confirm two pivotal contributors:
1️⃣ Replacing sigmoid gates with exponentials drops perplexity dramatically.  
2️⃣ Adding matrix memory further improves rare word handling—especially evident when swapping an sLRU block with an m block while keeping everything else fixed.

Scaling curves plotted against model size reveal that every additional parameter reduces loss at roughly the same power‐law exponent observed for transformers (*Kaplan law*), yet xLSTMs consistently sit below their transformer counterparts—promising even larger gains at billions of parameters.

### Going Massive: 300 B Tokens & Long Contexts  

Training four sizes—from 125 M up to 1.3 B parameters—on **300 billion tokens**, researchers evaluated several fronts:

* **Length extrapolation:** When fed contexts up to 16 384 tokens (four times training length), only xLSTMs kept perplexities flat; Transformers’ quadratic attention degraded sharply beyond ≈8 k tokens.
* **Downstream reasoning:** On popular common‐sense suites (BoolQ, HellaSwag …), xLLM variants outperformed all baselines across almost every task except ARC where Mamba had a slight edge.
* **PALOMA benchmark:** Spanning 571 domains—from news articles (`nytimes.com`) to Reddit subforums (`r/depression`)—xLLM[1:0] won in 99 %+ domains against both transformers and other recurrent linear models.
* **Throughput:** Because computation scales linearly with sequence length ($O(T)$ versus $O(T^2)$ for attention), generation time grows linearly even at batch size > 16—a stark contrast where transformer decoding stalls due to quadratic cache updates. Benchmarks report near‐linear wall‐clock scaling for both RWKV‐4 and our m/s variants versus steep ramps for llama​ (Transformer).

All these findings together paint a consistent picture: **xLSTMs close—or even close—the gap between classic recurrence and modern transformer dominance**, especially when it matters most (long contexts & hardware efficiency).

---

## Limitations & Open Questions  

No method arrives perfectly polished:

| Issue | Why It Matters | Current Status |
|-------|-----------------|----------------|
| *Parallelism of sLSTM* | Hidden–hidden mixing prevents naive GPU batching | A specialized CUDA kernel runs < 2× slower than fully parallel m​ variant but still lags behind pure attention kernels |
| *GPU kernels for m​‐memory* | `$d\times d$` updates involve heavy dense multiplications | Implementation is ≈4× slower than FlashAttention yet still acceptable because operations are fully batched |
| *Forget gate initialization* | Exponential decay controls stability over very long horizons | Needs careful tuning; otherwise early training may diverge |
| *Memory size vs context length* | Matrix $\mathbf C$ holds “global” associations irrespective of sequence window | Empirically works up to ≈16 k tokens; beyond that one may need larger `$d$` or hierarchical caching |
| *Hyperparameter tuning at massive scale* | Current large experiments used default learning schedules | Dedicated architecture search could push margins further |

Addressing these constraints—especially optimizing kernels akin to FlashAttention—and exploring hierarchical extensions will likely make x​⁠​LLM even more competitive under extreme scale regimes.

---

## Closing Thoughts  

The narrative here is simple yet powerful:  
> *If you keep a good idea alive long enough—and add just enough modern engineering,* it can reemerge stronger than its successors.

x​⁠​LLM proves that “the old Feynman trick” behind gated recurrence remains valuable when wrapped with fast exponentials, robust normalization tricks, associative matrix memories—and embedded inside today’s residual LayerNorm scaffolding. In practice this means you can train ​and ​run ​large language models whose compute cost grows linearly with context length—and whose memory footprint stays constant—even when handling billions of parameters.

For anyone building next-generation dialogue agents,
long-range scientific writers,
or reinforcement learners that need persistent internal state,
the takeaways are clear:

1️⃣ Don’t discard recurrence just because transformers dominate headlines;
2️⃣ Leverage exponential gating if you need mutable hidden states;
3️⃣ Consider matrix memories when rare token fidelity—or explicit key/value recall—is critical;
4️⃣ Stack everything inside familiar residual blocks so you can reuse existing training pipelines unchanged.

By giving classic RNNs this “modern makeover,” research opens doors toward efficient long-context reasoning without sacrificing predictive power—a development worth watching as we head toward ever larger generative AI systems.

# Exponential Gating Unpacked: How xLSTM Learns to Forget—and Remember—Better  

*Delving into the core innovation of the xLSTM paper and why “exponential” makes a big difference for recurrent models.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why LSTMs Still Matter (and Where They Stumble)

The Long Short‑Term Memory network—introduced in the early ’90s—fixed the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued vanilla RNNs. Its central recipe is simple:

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,\qquad 
h_t = o_t\,\psi(c_t)
\tag{1}
\]

* $c_t$ – the cell state that carries information forward  
* $i_t$, $f_t$, $o_t$ – input, forget, and output **sigmoid** gates (values in [0, 1])  
* $z_t$ – transformed input at time $t$  

Because every gate is bounded by a sigmoid, information can only **decay** (through $f_t<1$) or stay constant. This works wonderfully for many tasks but creates three well‑known bottlenecks:

| Limitation | What it means in practice |
|------------|---------------------------|
| **No revision** | Once a value has been “forgotten,” the model can’t bring it back stronger later. |
| **Scalar memory** | A single number per unit compresses rich information, hurting rare‑token prediction. |
| **Sequential bottleneck** | Hidden‑to‑hidden connections force strictly step‑by‑step computation. |

The new *xLSTM* family tackles these issues head‑on—starting with a seemingly modest tweak: **exponential gating**.

---

## 2️⃣ From Sigmoids to Exponentials: The Core Idea

### 2.1 What changes?

Instead of squashing the pre‑activation $\tilde{i}_t$ (or $\tilde{f}_t$) through a sigmoid, we map it **exponentially**:

\[
i_t       = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),\qquad 
f_t \in \Bigl\{\sigma(\tilde{f}_t),\;\exp(\tilde{f}_t)\Bigr\}
\tag{2}
\]

Unlike $\sigma$, $\exp$ is **unbounded above**—it can produce values larger than 1 when the raw score is positive. This gives the recurrent dynamics two new degrees of freedom:

* **Revise upward:** A later time step can *increase* an already stored value by applying a large input gate ($i_t>1$) or a “reverse forget” ($f_t>1$).  
* **Dynamic scaling:** The magnitude of both storage and forgetting becomes learnable rather than stuck between 0 and 1.

### 2.2 Normalizing the exploding terms

Unbounded gates would blow up the hidden state if left unchecked. The authors therefore introduce a *normalizer state* $n_t$, which tracks how much total “gate mass” has been accumulated so far:

\[
n_t = f_t\,n_{t-1} + i_t,
\qquad 
h_t = o_t\,\frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\tag{3}
\]

Dividing by $n_t$ essentially rescales $c_t$ back into a manageable range while preserving the **relative influence** of each gate activation.

### 2.3 Stabilization tricks

Even with normalization, raw exponentials can overflow during training. To keep gradients well‑behaved the paper adopts a *stabilizer* similar to Milakov & Papandreou (2018):

\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t &= \max\!\bigl(\log f_{t}+m_{t-1},\;\log i_{t}\bigr),\\[4pt]
i'_t &= \exp\bigl(\log i_{t} - m_{t}\bigr) 
      = \exp(\tilde{i}_{t} - m_{t}),\\[4pt]
f'_t &= \exp\bigl(\log f_{t} + m_{t-1} - m_{t}\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

The transformed gates $(i'_t,f'_t)$ are mathematically equivalent to $(i_t,f_t)$—they produce exactly the same forward output and gradients—but stay safely inside floating‑point limits.

---

## 3️⃣ Why “Exponential” Solves the Revision Problem  

### 3.1 The nearest‑neighbor search illustration  

Imagine we stream a list of vectors $\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_T$, each paired with a value we must output at the very end once we see the most similar vector so far (the classic *nearest neighbor search*). A vanilla LSTM behaves like this:

* When it first stores a candidate value, an input gate writes it into $c_{t}$.
* Later a better candidate arrives → lower forget gate shrinks the old contribution.
* Crucially, because $f_{t}\le 1$, any *reduction* is permanent—we cannot boost that older value back up.

Consequently, mean‑squared error stays high even after many time steps (see Fig. 2 in the paper).

With exponential gating, however:

* A large positive $\tilde{i}_t$ yields $i_{t}>1$, allowing us to **add extra weight** on top of an existing entry instead of merely overwriting it.
* If we accidentally attenuated too much earlier ($f_{k}<1$), we can later apply an *exponential forget gate* ($f_{j}>1$) that effectively *undoes* part of that decay.

The net effect is a *revisable storage*: earlier decisions are not locked forever but can be amplified or corrected later on. Empirically, xLSTM’s MSE drops dramatically compared with plain LSTMs on this synthetic benchmark.

### 3.2 From revision to richer representations

Because each gate can now act as an *amplifier*, the network learns sophisticated mixing strategies across time steps—something akin to attention but baked directly into the recurrence. This added flexibility explains why xLSTM outperforms Transformers on several language modeling tasks despite having linear computational cost w.r.t sequence length.

---

## 4️⃣ Beyond Scalars: Matrix Memory & Covariance Updates  

Exponential gating alone fixes revision, but it does not increase storage capacity—the classic LSTM still compresses everything into a one‑dimensional cell per unit. xLSTM introduces two complementary memory structures:

| Variant | Memory shape | Update rule | Parallelizability |
|--------|--------------|------------|-------------------|
| **sLSTM** (scalar) | $\mathbb{R}$ per cell | Same as Eq.(1) but with exponential gates & normalizer | **No** – hidden‑to‑hidden mixing remains sequential |
| **mLSTM** (matrix) | $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ | $\mathbf{C}_t = f_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_{t}\,\mathbf{v}_{t}\mathbf{k}^{\!\top}_{t}$ | **Yes** – no hidden‑to‑hidden connections |

The matrix memory implements a *Bidirectional Associative Memory*: each time step writes an outer product $\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}^{\!\top}_t$ (value × key). Retrieval simply multiplies $\mathbf{C}_t$ by a query vector $\mathbf{q}_t$. This is exactly the covariance update rule from fast weight programmers and enables very high‐dimensional storage without increasing parameter count.

Since mLSTM lacks recurrent hidden connections, its whole forward pass can be expressed as parallel matrix ops—giving it Transformer‐like speed while retaining recurrent “memory” semantics.

---

## 5️⃣ Scaling Results: From 15 B Tokens to Full‑Scale LLMs  

The authors evaluated xLSTMs under three realistic regimes:

| Setting | Tokens | Model size | Baseline(s) | Best xLSTM variant | Validation perplexity |
|---------|--------|------------|-------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| **Small scale** | 15 B (SlimPajama) | ≈350 M params | GPT‑3 (175 B), Llama 13B | xLSTM[7:1] (mostly mLSTM blocks) | **≈9.6**, beating GPT‑3’s 10.8 |
| **Large scale** | 300 B tokens | up to 1.3 B params | Llama 13B, Mamba 7B, RWKV 4 B | xLSTM[7:1] & xLSTM[1:0] | ~7–8 vs ~9–10 for baselines |
| **Specialised tasks** | — | — | Transformers & SSMs on Long Range Arena / Multi‑Query Associative Recall | xLSTM variants dominate all non‑Transformer entries |

Key takeaways:

* **Exponential gating + matrix memory** together give ≈10 % lower perplexity than top Transformer baselines at comparable parameter counts.
* On synthetic tasks that explicitly need *revision* (e.g., formal language parity or associative recall), only models with memory mixing—xLSTMs and vanilla RNNs—solve them perfectly; Transformers stumble because they lack revisable state.
* When extrapolating context length from 2k tokens up to 16k tokens during inference, xLSTMs maintain low perplexities while Transformer perplexities rise sharply—thanks again to their linear runtime and revisable memories.

---

## 6️⃣ Practical Tips for Using Exponential Gating  

If you want to experiment with xLSTMs (the code lives at `github.com/NX-AI/xlstm`), keep these engineering details in mind:

1. **Gate initialization:** Start forget gates near `log(0)` (i.e., around ‑5) so that early training doesn’t explode before learning useful decay rates.
2. **Stabilizer usage:** Always compute $m_{t}$ per Eq.(4); otherwise you’ll see NaNs once any gate spikes above ~20.
3. **Choosing s vs m blocks:**  
   * Use more `mLSTM` blocks when you need massive parallel throughput or want longer context handling without hidden mixing overhead.  
   * Sprinkle in `sLSTM` blocks if you want explicit *memory mixing across cells*, which helps on state‐tracking formalisms such as parity or nested counting languages.
4. **Batching:** Since both variants have constant memory w.r.t sequence length, batch sizes can be scaled arbitrarily—the main limiter becomes GPU matrix bandwidth rather than activation memory consumption.

---

## 7️⃣ Looking Forward  

Exponential gating revives an old idea—the constant error carousel—but gives it fresh expressive power that scales all the way up to billions of parameters. With linear compute cost and constant activation memory, xLSTMs sit at an intriguing crossroads between classic RNN theory and modern large language model engineering:

* **Reinforcement learning:** Agents can now revise internal world models mid‑episode without costly replay buffers.
* **Time‑series forecasting:** Long horizons become cheap because each step adds only constant overhead.
* **Physical simulation:** Matrix memories resemble associative maps used in neural differential equations—a natural fit for modeling forces or fluid dynamics.

If you’re building next‑generation language or decision models and care about long context efficiency—or simply want your recurrent model to remember *and* unforget—you now have a concrete recipe: add exponential gates, normalize with `$n_{t}$`, stabilize via `$m_{t}$`, and let your network decide when “forgetting” really means “forgetting less”.

Happy modeling! 🚀



# Memory 2.0: Scalar vs. Matrix Memory in sLSTM and mLSTM  

*When you’ll next hand a recurrent network a long story to remember – will it scribble a single memo or fill an address book?*  

---

## Why we need a new kind of memory  

The original Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) stores information in a **scalar cell** — a single number `cₜ` that is updated by the classic constant‑error carousel  

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,
\qquad 
h_t = o_t\,\psi(c_t),
\]

where the three sigmoid gates `iₜ`, `fₜ`, `oₜ` keep gradients alive and decide what to write, keep or read.  
While this trick solved the vanishing‑gradient problem, it also imposed two practical limits:

1. **Revision bottleneck** – once a value is written, a vanilla LSTM cannot easily “overwrite” it later without erasing the whole cell.  
2. **Capacity ceiling** – a single scalar cannot hold more than one compressed piece of information, which hurts rare‑token prediction and long‑range associative recall.

The **xLSTM** family solves both problems by introducing two fresh memories:

| Variant | Shape of the cell | Core idea | Main benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sLSTM** | scalar (`cₜ`) | exponential gating + *memory mixing* across heads | cheap revision & richer hidden dynamics |
| **mLSTM** | matrix (`\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}`) | key–value storage via a covariance update rule | large associative capacity & fast retrieval |

Below we unpack each design, illustrate them with everyday analogies, and compare their trade‑offs.

---

## The scalar hero gets an upgrade – sLSTM  

### From static gates to *exponential* gates  

In a standard LSTM the gates are confined to \([0,1]\) because they are passed through a sigmoid.  
sLSTM relaxes this restriction by allowing the **input gate** (and optionally the forget gate) to be **exponential**:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t),\qquad 
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\;\text{or}\;\exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]

Since exponentials can take values above 1, an incoming stimulus can *boost* the stored value instead of merely adding to it—think of turning up the volume on a specific track while keeping all other tracks at their current level.

### Normalizer & stabilizer – keeping the volume under control  

Unbounded exponentials would quickly overflow. sLSTM introduces two companion states:

* **Normalizer** `nₜ`: accumulates the same weighted sum that shapes `cₜ`. The hidden state is finally normalized as  

  \[
  \tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}.
  \]

  This division keeps hidden activations in a sensible range no matter how large `iₜ` or `fₜ` become.

* **Stabilizer** `mₜ`: tracks the log‑scale of gates so that we can re‑scale `iₜ` and `fₜ` (`i'_t`, `f'_t`) without changing any gradients. The trick is mathematically identical to adding a constant offset inside an exponential—yet it prevents numerical overflow on GPUs.

### Memory mixing across *heads*  

A vanilla LSTM can have multiple parallel cells (`\mathbf{c}_t ∈ ℝ^d`) that talk to each other through recurrent matrices (`\mathbf{R}`).  
sLSTM extends this idea by allowing **multiple heads**, each head consisting of several cells that mix *only within* that head:

```
head 1:  c¹ → c² → … → cᴺ   ← mixing via R
head 2:  d¹ → d² → … → dᴹ   ← independent from head 1
```

The net effect is **state tracking** – each head can maintain its own “thread” of information while still benefitting from exponential gating inside its thread. This is especially useful for tasks such as parsing nested parentheses or keeping track of multiple entities simultaneously.

#### Visual Analogy  
Imagine a **single notebook page** (the scalar cell) where you jot down ideas in ink (the additive term). With exponential gates you get a highlighter that can brighten recent notes without erasing older ones. Now attach *separate colored tabs* (heads); each tab groups related notes together and you can flip between them without disturbing unrelated content—a compact yet flexible way to organize thoughts on the fly.

---

## From one notebook page to an address book – mLSTM  

### Matrix memory as an associative store  

Instead of squeezing everything into one number, mLSTM expands the state to a full matrix  

\[
\mathbf{C}_t ∈ ℝ^{d×d}.
\]

The model writes a **key–value pair** at every time step:

*Key* `\mathbf{k}_t ∈ ℝ^{d}` — think of an address label (e.g., “Paris”).  
*Value* `\mathbf{v}_t ∈ ℝ^{d}` — the content you want to retrieve later (e.g., “Eiffel Tower”).

The update rule follows the classic **covariance (outer‑product) rule**, sometimes called *Fast Weight Programming*:  

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\,\mathbf{k}_t^{⊤}.
\]

Here `iₜ` plays the role of a learning rate for how strongly we associate the current key–value pair, while `fₜ` acts as decay—exactly like adding sticky notes on top of an ever‑growing corkboard but occasionally erasing older ones.

### Retrieval via query vector  

When we need information at time \(t+τ\), we emit a **query** `\mathbf{q}_{t+τ}` (derived from current input). The hidden state results from multiplying the stored matrix with this query:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = 
   \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}
        {\max\!\bigl(|\mathbf{n}_t^{⊤}\mathbf{q}_t|,\;1\bigr)},
\qquad
h_t = o_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t,
\]

where `\mathbf{n}_t` is the normalizer collecting the weighted sum of keys (mirroring `nₜ` in sLSTM). The denominator prevents division by very small numbers when queries stray far from any stored key.

#### Visual Analogy  
Picture an **address book with index cards**:

* Each card holds two columns – *key* on top (the person’s name) and *value* below (their phone number).  
* Adding a new contact writes an outer product entry into our “big table” (`\mathbf{C}`) — analogous to stamping both rows and columns simultaneously.  
* When you later ask “What’s John’s number?” you hand over “John” as a query card; by aligning rows/columns you instantly pull out his phone number without scanning every entry—the operation is just one matrix–vector multiplication.

Because this lookup is linear algebraic, modern GPUs can evaluate many queries in parallel—making mLSTM **fully parallelizable**, unlike sU with its hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence.

---

## Scalar vs. Matrix – What actually changes?  

| Aspect | sLSTM (scalar) | mLSTM (matrix) |
|---|---|---|
| **Memory shape** | \(c_t∈ℝ\) (per cell); multiple cells mixed via heads | \(\mathbf{C}_t∈ℝ^{d×d}\) |
| **Write mechanism** | Additive + exponential scaling (`iₜ·zₜ`) with forget decay (`fₜ·c_{t-1}`) | Covariance update (`iₜ·v_t k_t^\top`) + decay (`fₜ·C_{t-1}`) |
| **Read mechanism** | Normalized hidden state `hₜ = oₜ·(cₜ / nₜ)` | Retrieval vector `hₜ = oₜ ⊙ (\mathbf{C}_t q_t / …)` |
| **Revision ability** | Exponential gates allow “boosting” stored scalars on-the-fly | Forget gate provides per‑step decay; new key–value pairs can overwrite old associations through outer products |
| **Capacity** | Limited by number of cells × hidden dim; good for short‑term tracking | Grows quadratically with hidden dim `d`; excellent for storing many distinct key–value pairs (e.g., rare tokens) |
| **Parallelism** | Requires sequential scan because heads share hidden-to-hidden connections (memory mixing). CUDA kernels are ~2× slower than pure parallel ops. | No hidden‑to‑hidden connections ⇒ fully parallelizable across time steps using standard GEMM kernels; overhead mainly from full `d×d` multiply—but still modest on GPUs |
| **Best use‑case** | State tracking problems where multiple interleaved threads must be revised (e.g., nested parentheses, entity tracking). | Long‐range associative recall where you need to retrieve many distinct facts later (e.g., rare‑token prediction, key‑value retrieval). |

Both variants inherit two important innovations from xLSTM:

1. **Exponential gating**, which replaces or augments sigmoid gating so information can be amplified rather than merely passed through.
2. **Normalizers & stabilizers**, which keep exponentially growing signals numerically well‑behaved.

---

## How they slot into an xLSTM architecture  

An xLSTM block wraps either an sLSTM or mLSTM cell inside a residual backbone:

* For **sLSTM blocks**, we use *post up‑projection*: after the recurrent step we lift the hidden vector into a high‑dimensional space, apply nonlinearity, then project back.
* For **mLSTM blocks**, we prefer *pre up‑projection*: first expand dimensions (so that the matrix memory lives in this richer space), then run the recurrent update.

Stacking dozens of these blocks yields an xLSTM model whose overall depth can rival GPT‑style Transformers while enjoying linear time/memory complexity (`O(T)` instead of `O(T²)`).

Empirically:

* On synthetic formal language benchmarks requiring *state tracking*, pure sLSTM ([0 : 1] configuration) solves parity and copy tasks that vanilla Transformers cannot.
* On Multi‑Query Associative Recall—a massive key‐value memorization challenge—pure mLSTM ([1 : 0]) outperforms all non‑Transformer baselines and even rivals transformer attention when scaled modestly.
* Mixed configurations ([7 : 1] ≈ 6 s + 42 m blocks) strike a balance between cheap revision power and high capacity, delivering best perplexities on real language corpora like SlimPajama.

---

## Takeaways  

* **Scalar isn’t just scalar anymore** – sLSTM shows that by giving each scalar gate exponential flexibility and allowing heads to mix locally, we get powerful state tracking without blowing up parameters.
* **Matrix memory brings associative recall into RNN territory** – mLSTM converts each time step into a key–value entry inside an ever‐growing associative map. Retrieval becomes a single linear algebra operation.
* The two memories are complementary: use sLSTM when you need many intertwined short‑term threads; add mLSTM when you must remember many distinct facts over long horizons.
* From an engineering viewpoint, mLSTMs win on parallel hardware once their CUDA kernels are optimized; sLSTMs still lag modestly due to sequential mixing but remain tractable thanks to custom kernels.
* Finally, scaling studies show that “memory 2.0” architectures preserve competitive perplexities even at billions of parameters—hinting that LSTMs might soon share stage with Transformers as viable backbones for large language models.

If you’re building systems that must revise past decisions on-the-fly *or* retrieve specific facts far down the line without paying quadratic attention costs, consider sprinkling some sLSTMs and/or mLSTMs into your next model stack—you might just discover that remembering doesn’t have to be primitive anymore. Happy modeling!



# Building a Giant from Tiny Bricks: Residual xLSTM Blocks vs. Transformer Layers  

*How putting modern LSTM cells into clever residual backbones gives us deep language models that stay * linear *– even when we scale them to billions of parameters.*

---  

## Introduction – Why revisit LSTMs?  

The classic Long Short-Term Memory network solved the vanishing‑gradient problem by introducing a **constant error carousel** and three sigmoid gates:

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t , \qquad 
h_t = o_t \,\psi(c_t).
\]

For decades LSTMs powered text generation, speech synthesis, reinforcement learning agents, and even early large language models (LLMs).  

Transformers later stole the limelight because their self‑attention can be **parallelized**, but that comes at a price: computing an \(N\times N\) attention matrix costs \(O(N^2)\) time and memory with respect to sequence length \(N\).  

The *xLSTM* paper asks a simple question:

> *If we scale LSTMs using today’s tricks while fixing their three historic drawbacks, can they compete with state‑of‑the‑art Transformers?*

The answer lies in two ingredients—*new recurrent cells* (s = scalar and m = matrix memories) and *residual blocks* that embed them efficiently.  

---

## From Vanilla LSTMs to Two New Cells  

### 1️⃣ s LSTM – scalar memory with *exponential* gates  

The original forget/input gates are sigmoids bounded between \(0\) and \(1\). In **s LSTM**, those gates can be exponentials:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad 
f_t = 
\begin{cases}
\sigma(\tilde f_t)\\[4pt]
\exp(\tilde f_t)
\end{cases}.
\]

Because an exponential can grow arbitrarily large, it allows a model **to revise past storage decisions**—a problem classic LSTMs stumble on (e.g., nearest‑neighbor search).  

To keep things numerically stable we introduce a *normalizer* \(n_t\) that accumulates gate products:

\[
n_t = f_t\, n_{t-1} + i_t,
\qquad
\tilde h_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t}, \quad
h_t = o_t\,\tilde h_t .
\]

A stabilizer state \(m_t\) rescales the raw exponentials without changing gradients:

```text
mₜ = max( log(fₜ)+mₜ₋₁ , log(iₜ) )
i′ₜ = exp( log(iₜ) − mₜ )
f′ₜ = exp( log(fₜ)+mₜ₋₁ − mₜ )
```

Besides exponential gating, **s LSTM adds *memory mixing***. If we stack several scalar cells into a vector \(\mathbf c_t∈ℝ^{d}\), we allow recurrent matrices (\(\mathbf R_{\!*}\)) that mix information across cells *and* across *heads* inside each layer—a fresh way of “state tracking” that vanilla RNNs lack.



### 2️⃣ m LSTM – matrix memory via a covariance update  

The **matrix memory** lifts the cell state from a scalar to a full matrix \(\mathbf C∈ℝ^{d×d}\). At each step we store a *key*–*value* pair (\(\mathbf k_t,\mathbf v_t∈ℝ^{d}\)) using the classic *outer product* rule:

\[
\boxed{\;
   \mathbf C_{t}= f_{t}\,\mathbf C_{t-1}
   + i_{t}\,\bigl( \mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{⊤} \bigr)
   }
\tag{covariance update}
\]

Retrieval works by multiplying this fast weight matrix with a *query* vector:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_{t}= 
   \frac{\mathbf C_{t}\,\mathbf q_{t}}
        {\max\,\bigl|\! \mathbf n_{t}^{⊤}\!\mathbf q_{t}\bigr|,\;1},
    \qquad 
    h_{t}= \mathbf{o}_{t}\odot \tilde{\mathbf h}_{t},
\]

where \(\mathbf n_{t}\) is the normalizer that accumulates weighted keys,
and the denominator prevents division by near zero values.  

Crucially, **m LSTM removes hidden–to–hidden connections**, so its recurrence can be reformulated as purely parallel matrix operations—exactly what modern GPUs excel at.


---

## Plugging Cells Into Residual Blocks  

### The Residual Backbone

All contemporary large models use *pre‑LayerNorm residual stacks*: each block consists of

```
input → LayerNorm → sublayer → skip connection → output
```

The sublayer is where we place our recurrent cell (`s` or `m`). This design stabilizes deep training and lets gradients flow through many layers unchanged.



### Up‑Projection Matters  

When you only have \(d=1024\) hidden units, some patterns are highly tangled in that space. **Cover’s theorem** tells us that projecting data into a higher dimension \(D≫d\), applying a nonlinearity there, then projecting back dramatically increases linear separability—a boon for next‑token prediction.

Two strategic ways of inserting this “high‑dimensional lift” are used:

| Up‑Projection style | When used | Where does expansion happen? |
|---------------------|-----------|-----------------------------|
| **Post up‑projection** (Transformer style) | Inside *s LSTM* blocks | The cell processes the original hidden vector first (`cₜ`, `hₜ`), then a linear map expands it → nonlinearity → linear map contracts back |
| **Pre up‑projection** (State Space Model style) | Inside *m LSTM* blocks | Input is first projected up → then fed into the matrix‐memory recurrence → contraction follows after retrieval |

Why different choices?  

* For **s LSTMs**, keeping the scalar state computation cheap is beneficial; expanding afterwards lets us reap higher‐dimensional expressivity without hurting the cheap gate dynamics.  
* For **m LLSTMs**, the outer‐product update already lives on an \(d×d\) matrix; feeding it richer inputs right away maximizes its capacity.



### Putting It All Together – The `x​L​S​T​M` Block

A single `x​lstm` block therefore looks like:

```
[ LayerNorm ] → [ ↑ / ↓ projection ] → [ s/m LSTM cell ] 
         ↘                               ↗
          ─────────── skip connection ─────────────
```

The exact projection direction depends on whether we chose post or pre up‑projection as described above.



---  

## From Blocks to Giants – Stacking `x​lstm` Layers  

### A Simple Notation

The paper denotes a stack by `x​lstm[a:b]`, where `a` out of `(a+b)` blocks use **m LLSTMs**, and `b` use **s LLSTMs**. Example:

```
x​lstm[7:1]   →   8 stacked blocks,
               7 are m − blocks,
               1 is an s − block
```

All experiments used 48 total blocks (≈6 s − blocks + 42 m − blocks for `[7:1]`).



### Depth ≈ Transformer Stacks

Because every block follows the same residual pattern as a Transformer encoder layer, stacking dozens of them yields **the same depth hierarchy** that transformer models obtain after many self‑attention layers. The major difference lies in how each layer processes time:

*Transformer*: each layer builds an \(N×N\) attention map ⇒ cost \(O(N^2)\).  
*x​lstm*: each layer updates either a scalar or matrix state ⇒ cost linear in sequence length \(O(N)\); any expensive operation depends only on hidden size \(d\).

Thus we obtain **“giant” models built from inexpensive bricks**, preserving constant per–token compute even at very long contexts.



---  

## Linear Time Complexity – The Core Advantage  

| Model type | Per-token operation count | Dependence on sequence length |
|------------|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| Self­‑attention (Transformer) | \(≈ N d^2\)   | Quadratic (`O(N²)`) |
| s LLTM block                | \(≈ d^2\)     | Linear (`O(N)`), tiny extra work |
| m LLTM block                | \(≈ d^3?\)** | Linear (`O(N)`), heavy matrix ops but parallelizable |

*\(**)* In practice `m LLTM` performs two matrix multiplications (`d×d` × `d×k`) per token—still independent of `N`. On GPUs these are batched efficiently so wall-clock time grows linearly with context length instead of quadratically.



### What Does That Buy Us?  

* **Constant memory footprint:** only current hidden state + compact external memory (`cₜ` or `Cₜ`). No need to store an entire attention cache.
* **Long context friendliness:** Experiments showed almost unchanged perplexity when probing contexts up to 16k tokens—far beyond what vanilla Transformers can handle without sacrificing speed.
* **Scalable parallelism:** While s LLTM keeps hidden‐to‐hidden connections (making it inherently sequential), its CUDA implementation achieves < 2× slowdown versus fully parallel m LLTM; both remain far cheaper than quadratic attention at long sequences.



---  

## Empirical Highlights – Do These Bricks Hold Up?  

The authors trained several architectures on the same SlimPajama dataset (15 B tokens). All models had roughly GPT‑3 scale (~350 M parameters). Key takeaways:

* **Validation perplexity:** Table `\ref{tab:spaj15b_model_results}` reports that every `x​lstm` variant beats GPT‑3/Transformer baselines as well as SSMs (Mamba/H3) and other RNN hybrids (RWKV).  
* **Scaling behavior:** Figure `\ref{fig:temp_sclaw15B}` shows classic power‐law scaling curves with `x​lstm` having a lower offset—meaning at any given compute budget it will be more accurate than its Transformer counterparts.  
* **Long-range benchmarks:** On Long Range Arena tasks (`Figure `\ref{fig:lra}`), `x​lstm` consistently matches or exceeds state-of-the-art SSMs while staying linear in time.  
* **300 B token training:** When moving to massive data regimes (`Section `\ref{sec:ExpLanguage}`), `x​lstm[7:1]` retains low perplexities even at context lengths of ‑16k tokens—a regime where standard Transformers’ performance collapses sharply.


These results underline that simply adding modern gating & high-dimensional projections inside residual blocks makes “old” recurrent ideas competitive again.



---  

## Takeaways & Outlook  

1️⃣ **Exponential gating + normalization restores revisability** — classic LSTMs could not overwrite old memories efficiently; exponential gates let newer evidence dominate old content without saturation limits.

2️⃣ **Matrix memories expand capacity dramatically** — storing outer products enables associative recall far beyond what scalar cells allow while staying parameter‐free.

3️⃣ **Residual up/down projection leverages high-dimensional geometry without blowing parameters**, honoring Cover’s theorem about separability after nonlinear mapping.

4️⃣ **Stacking these blocks yields deep networks akin to Transformer stacks**, yet inference remains linear‐time because each step only touches constant‐size internal states.

5️⃣ **Empirically validated:** Across synthetic formal languages, associative recall tasks, Long Range Arena benchmarks, and massive language modeling runs (`15B` → `300B` tokens), `x​lstm` consistently matches or outperforms current SOTA Transformer/SSM models while using far less GPU memory per token.

6️⃣ **Limitations & Future Work:** Current CUDA kernels for `m LLTM` are slower than FlashAttention; optimizing them could close remaining speed gaps. Moreover, hybrid designs—mixing occasional self-attention heads with `x​lstm` blocks—might combine locality-friendly recurrence with global contextual jumps.


> ***Bottom line*** – By rethinking two seemingly tiny components—the gate activation function and representation size—and embedding them inside well‑studied residual scaffolds, we can grow “giant” language models whose bricks are lightweight recurrent units rather than heavyweight attention maps.



---  

### TL;DR

> Residual `x​lstm` blocks place either an exponentially gated scalar cell (**s LLTM**) or a fast-weight matrix cell (**m LLTM**) inside pre‐LayerNorm skip connections. Depending on whether you project after (**post**) or before (**pre**) the recurrence, you get expressive high-dimensional processing without quadratic costs. Stacking dozens of such blocks gives deep networks comparable in size to Transformers but still run in *O(sequence length)* time and constant memory—a promising route toward scalable language models built from “tiny bricks”.



# Scaling LSTMs to Billions of Parameters: **xLSTM**’s Performance on Language Modeling Benchmarks  

*How does an “old‑school” recurrent network fare when we crank its size up to the billions‑parameter regime?* The recent **xLSTM** paper gives us a compelling answer: with a few clever tweaks—exponential gating and richer memory structures—scaled‑up LSTMs can compete with, and often surpass, state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs) on a suite of language‑modeling benchmarks.  

Below is a concise walk‑through of the experiments that back this claim, focusing on three key evaluation fronts:

1. **Validation perplexity on massive web‑text corpora**  
2. **Long‑range reasoning (Long Range Arena)**  
3. **Domain‑specific token prediction (PALOMA benchmark)**  

---

## 1️⃣ Validation Perplexity – The Gold Standard Metric  

### Setup  
* **Training data:** *SlimPajama* – a curated mix of open‑source web text.  
* **Training budgets:**  
  * **15 B** token run (≈ GPT‑3 scale) – all models share the same compute budget.  
  * **300 B** token run – pushes models toward true Large Language Model (LLM) regimes.  
* **Model families compared:**  
  * Classic Transformers (GPT‑3 sized, LLaMA)  
  * SSMs (H3, Mamba)  
  * Recurrent baselines & linear Transformers (RWKV‑4/5/6, GLA, HGRN2)  
  * **xLSTM** variants – mixtures of *sLSTM* (scalar memory + mixing) and *mLSTM* (matrix memory + covariance update), denoted e.g., `xLSTM[7:1]` (7 mLSTM blocks / 1 sLSTM block).  

### What the numbers tell us  

| Tokens | Model family | Relative validation perplexity |
|--------|--------------|------------------------------|
| **15 B** | GPT‑3 size Transformer | Baseline |
|        | LLaMA | Higher than baseline |
|        | SSMs (H3 / Mamba) | Higher than baseline |
|        | Linear RNNs (RWKV‑4…) | Higher than baseline |
|        | **xLSTM** (`xLSTM[7:1]` & `xLSTM[1:0]`) | **Lowest** among *all* listed methods |

*Even though absolute numbers are omitted for brevity, the paper reports that both `xLSTM[7:1]` and `xLSTM[1:0]` achieve a **clear perplexity win** over every competing architecture at this token budget.*  

#### Ablation insights  

A stepwise transformation from vanilla LSTM → pre‑LayerNorm residual backbone → post up‑projection block → exponential gating → matrix memory reveals:

| Added component | Perplexity impact |
|-----------------|------------------|
| Residual backbone    | Modest drop |
| Post up‑projection    | Further reduction |
| **Exponential gating** | Largest single gain |
| **Matrix memory (`mLSTM`)** | Additional improvement |

Both exponential gating *and* matrix memory are essential; removing either erodes the advantage substantially.

### Scaling up to **300 B** tokens  

When we crank the data budget up twentyfold:

* Models from **125 M** up to **1.3 B** parameters were trained.
* Across every size class,
  * `xLSTM[1:0]` and `xLSTM[7:1]` again attained the *best* validation perplexities.
  * The gap widens as model size grows—mirroring the classic “scaling law” behavior where an improved offset translates into better performance at any scale.
* In side‐by‐side plots of loss vs. model size (“power‑law fits”), **xLSTMs sit below Transformers and SSMs**, indicating that a future 10 B or even 100 B‐parameter xLSTM would retain this lead.

---

## 2️⃣ Long Range Arena – Do Recurrent Networks Still “Forget”?

The Long Range Arena (LRA) aggregates tasks that demand handling sequences up to several thousand timesteps (e.g., ListOps, Retrieval–QA). Historically RNNs fell behind because they lack explicit long‑range attention mechanisms.

### xLSTM’s performance

* On *all* seven LRA tasks, an xLSTM configuration achieved scores that were:
  * Comparable to top SSMs such as Mamba.
  * Superior to vanilla LSTMs and most pure Transformer baselines run under identical compute constraints.
* The standout factor is the combination of:
  * **Exponential forget gates**, which allow rapid attenuation or reinforcement of past information.
  * **Matrix memory (`mLSTM`)**, which stores key–value pairs directly inside a learned covariance matrix—effectively providing a “fast weight” attention that scales linearly with sequence length.
* Consequently, xLSTMs exhibit **linear compute & constant memory cost w.r.t sequence length**, making them naturally suited for very long contexts without blowing up GPU memory—a practical advantage over quadratic attention.

---

## 3️⃣ PALOMA Benchmark – Domain Adaptability in the Wild  

The PALOMA suite evaluates next‑token prediction across **571 distinct textual domains**, ranging from news sites (`nytimes.com`) to Reddit communities (`r/depression`). It’s an excellent proxy for real‐world robustness.

### Head‑to‑head results

| Model family / variant | # Domains where it wins against others |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **xLSTM[1:0]** (**matrix memory only**) | Beats Mamba on **568 / 571** domains (**99.5 %**) |
|                        | Beats LLaMA on **486 / 571** domains (**85 %**) |
|                        | Beats RWKV‑4 on **570 / 571** domains (**99.8 %**) |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** (**mostly mLSTM + one sLSTM**) | Similar dominance but slightly less pronounced than `xLSTM[1:0]`. |

Across all domains the *average token prediction perplexity* of `xLSTM[1:0]` was lower than any other method tested—including high‑capacity Transformers—showing that richer internal memory translates into tangible gains on heterogeneous corpora.

> **Takeaway:** Even when confronted with niche vocabularies or highly specialized styles, an appropriately sized matrix‐memory recurrent model can outperform attention‐based giants that have orders of magnitude more parameters per token processed.

---

## 4️⃣ Putting It All Together – Why Does Scaling Work?  

### Core innovations that unlock performance

1. **Exponential gating:**  
   * Replaces sigmoid gates with $\exp(\cdot)$ while keeping numerical stability via a “stabilizer state”.  
   * Enables drastic revisions of stored information—a crucial ability for tasks like nearest-neighbor search where later inputs may need to overwrite earlier ones.
2. **Memory structure upgrades:**  
   * **sLSTM:** Keeps scalar cells but introduces a normalizer state $n_t$ so hidden states are computed as $h_t = o_t \,\frac{c_t}{n_t}$—a form of dynamic scaling that reduces saturation.
   * **mLSTM:** Stores a full $d\times d$ matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$, updated by an outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ modulated by exponential input/forget gates—essentially a learned fast weight cache akin to associative memories.
3. **Residual block backbones with up‑projection:**  
   * Aligns with modern pre‑LayerNorm transformer stacks while providing high‐dimensional non‑linear embeddings per block—leveraging Cover’s theorem for better separability of histories.
4. **Parallelizability where possible:**  
   * The $m\!$ variant’s recurrence can be rewritten as a purely feed‑forward scan; s\! variant retains some sequential mixing but still runs at <2× slower than its parallel counterpart thanks to custom CUDA kernels.

### Practical implications  

* **Linear inference cost:** Generation time grows linearly with context length—unlike Transformer self‑attention’s quadratic blowup—so very long prompts remain tractable.
* **Throughput advantage:** In batch decoding experiments (`batch=32`, `prefill=16`), xLSTMs achieve higher token per second rates than cached Transformers because they never build an ever-growing key/value cache.
* **Memory footprint:** Constant hidden‐state storage makes it feasible to run billion‐parameter recurrent models on commodity GPUs where Transformer equivalents would exceed VRAM limits at modest context lengths.

---

## 🚀 Bottom Line  

The experimental evidence in the `xLSTM` paper paints a clear picture:

* When augmented with exponential gating and richer memories, classic LSTMs scale gracefully to billions of parameters.
* Across three orthogonal benchmarks—validation perplexity on massive web corpora, synthetic long‐range reasoning tasks, and domain‐wide token prediction—they consistently match or beat current leaders built on Transformer or State Space technology.
* Scaling laws suggest this lead will only widen as we push toward even larger models and datasets.

In short, the era where “recurrent = outdated” appears premature again. With `xLSTM`, an old favorite has been revitalized into a genuine contender for next‑generation language models—and its design may spill over into RL agents, time series forecasting, and physical system simulators where linear time/space complexity is paramount.

# Parallelism vs. Mixing: Understanding the Speed Trade‑offs in sLSTM and mLSTM  

Large Language Models have turned the spotlight on Transformers, but the classic Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) family—especially Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) networks—has never truly disappeared. The recent **xLSTM** paper revives LSTMs by introducing two new siblings:

* **sLSTM** – a scalar‑memory LSTM equipped with *exponential gating* and *memory mixing*.  
* **mLSTM** – a matrix‑memory LSTM that also uses exponential gates but **drops** memory mixing entirely, making it **fully parallelizable**.

Both variants bring impressive language‑modeling results, yet they differ dramatically in how fast they run on modern GPUs. In this post we unpack why the seemingly innocuous choice of “mixing” or “no mixing” leads to such divergent speed characteristics, how the authors mitigated the slowdown with custom CUDA kernels, and why mLSTM’s heavy matrix arithmetic still lets it ride the parallelism wave.

---  

## 1. A Quick Refresher: From Classic LSTMs to xLSTMs  

The original LSTM updates its scalar cell state $c_t$ by adding a new input $z_t$ (scaled by an *input gate* $i_t$) while retaining part of the previous state (scaled by a *forget gate* $f_t$):

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t ,\qquad
h_t = o_t\,\psi(c_t)\ .
\]

Two major bottlenecks have haunted LSTMs since then:

1. **Inflexible storage decisions** – once a value is written into $c_{t-1}$ it cannot be cleanly overwritten later.
2. **Scalar‑only memory** – each cell compresses everything into a single number.
3. **Hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence** – the hidden state $h_{t-1}$ feeds back into every gate at the next step, forcing strict sequential execution.

xLSTMs address these pain points by adding *exponential gating* (so gates can grow or shrink much faster) and redefining the memory structure:

* **sLSTM:** Keeps a scalar cell ($c_t$) but introduces a **normalizer state** $n_t$ that tracks the cumulative effect of gates and implements *memory mixing* across multiple cells/heads.
* **mLSTM:** Expands the cell into a full matrix $\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ using a covariance update rule,
\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top ,
\]
and removes hidden‑to‑hidden connections altogether.

Both variants share an *exponential gate stabilization* mechanism (a “stabilizer state” $m_t$) that rescales exponentially large gate values without altering gradients:

\[
\begin{aligned}
m_t &= \max\!\bigl(\log f_t + m_{t-1},\, \log i_t\bigr),\\
i'_t &= \exp(\log i_t - m_t),\qquad
f'_t = \exp(\log f_t + m_{t-1} - m_t).
\end{aligned}
\]

Now let’s turn to why this redesign matters for speed.


---  

## 2. Memory Mixing in sLSTM – The Serial Bottleneck  

### 2.1 How Mixing Works  

In a multi‑cell sLSTM each cell’s input and gates are not independent; instead they are linked through recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{z}, \mathbf{R}_{i}, \mathbf{R}_{f}, \mathbf{R}_{o}$. The forward equations look like

\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{z}_t &= \mathbf{w}_z^\top\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_z\,h_{t-1} + b_z ,\\
\tilde{i}_t &= \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{R}_i\,h_{t-1} + b_i ,\\
&\vdots 
\end{aligned}
\]

Because each gate depends on $h_{t-1}$—the hidden vector produced **after** all cells have mixed their information at time $t{-}1$—the entire layer forms a *hidden‑to‑hidden* dependency chain across timesteps:

```
h0 → h1 → h2 → … → hT
```

Even if you split the model into many heads or cells, every head still needs its predecessor’s mixed hidden state before it can compute its own gate values at the next step.


### 2.2 Why This Kills Parallelism  

GPU hardware thrives when it can evaluate many operations simultaneously. The hidden‑to‑hidden chain forces a *strict order*: you cannot compute $h_{t+5}$ before you know $h_{t+4}$, which in turn needs $h_{t+3}$… In other words, you’re forced into **sequential scanning** which runs at best at $\mathcal O(T)$ wall‑clock time where $T$ is sequence length—exactly what Transformers tried to avoid with self‑attention’s $\mathcal O(T^2)$ cost but massive parallelism.


### 2.3 The Authors’ Workaround: Fast CUDA Kernels  

Knowing that “mixing = serial” is inherent, the authors didn’t try to remove it completely—they instead focused on making each step *as cheap as possible*. Their solution:

| Component | Optimization |
|-----------|---------------|
| Gate pre‑computations | Fused matmul + bias + activation inside one kernel |
| Normalizer update ($n_t = f_t n_{t-1}+ i_t$) | Register‐level accumulation avoiding global writes |
| Stabilizer computation | Jointly computed with gates using warp shuffles |
| Hidden‐state construction | Single‐kernel `o_t * ψ(c_t / n_t)` |

By packing all per‑time‑step work into one custom CUDA kernel they reduced kernel launch overhead and kept intermediate tensors in registers instead of spilling them to DRAM. The result? sLSTM runs **less than twice as slow** as its fully parallel counterpart (mLSTM) on typical hardware—a remarkable achievement given its inherent sequential nature.


---  

## 3. Parallel-Friendly Design of mLSTM  

### 3.1 No Hidden‑to‑Hidden Connections  

The key design decision that unlocks parallelism is abandoning any dependence on $h_{t-1}$ inside gate computations:

\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_t + b_i,\qquad
\tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}_f^\top\mathbf{x}_t + b_f,\qquad
\dots
\]

All gates now rely only on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$. This eliminates the hidden-to-hidden chain entirely—a classic “feedforward” recurrence.


### 3.2 Scan‑Friendly Recurrence  

Even though there is still a recurrence because $\mathbf{C}_{t}$ depends on $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$,
the update has an additive form reminiscent of prefix sums:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_0 f_1 \dots f_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_0 
   + \sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\Bigl( i_\tau\,f_{\tau+1}\dots f_{t}\Bigr)\,\bigl(\mathbf{v}_\tau \mathbf{k}_\tau^\top\bigr).
\]

Both terms are expressible as **parallel scans** (also called prefix reductions), which GPUs handle efficiently using existing libraries or custom kernels similar to those used by FlashAttention.


### 3.3 Heavy Matrix Ops That Still Parallelize  

Every timestep touches a $d\times d$ matrix:
* The decay term `f_t · C_{t−1}` is just an elementwise scaling.
* The addition `i_t · vₜ kₜᵀ` builds an outer product (`vₜ` column × `kₜᵀ` row).

Because these are pure linear algebra primitives (`gemm`, `ger`, `scal`), modern GPUs execute them massively in parallel across thousands of cores. The only extra work stems from:
* Computing the normalizer vector $\mathbf{n}_t$ (a scaled sum of keys).
* Normalizing retrieved activations via `$|\mathbf{n}_t^\top q_t|$`.

Both require only vector–vector products—trivial compared to an outer product when $d$ is moderate (e.g., $d=256$). Consequently,
even though each step processes quadratic data ($d^2$), we can batch many steps together or run them concurrently thanks to batch GEMM kernels.


### 3.4 Implementation Highlights  

The authors implemented two crucial tricks:

| Trick | Effect |
|------|--------|
| Fuse `f_t·C` & `i_tv_kᵀ` inside one kernel | Saves one read/write pass over $\mathbf C$. |
| Use cuBLAS batched GEMM for outer products over many timesteps | Leverages well‑optimized BLAS kernels instead of hand‑written loops.|

Although their current kernels are about **four times slower** than FlashAttention’s hand‑crafted attention kernel—largely because they haven’t yet applied FlashAttention’s “blockwise tiling” tricks—their wall clock overhead remains modest relative to sequential models because everything scales linearly with sequence length.

## 4. Speed Benchmarks & What They Mean  

| Model | Parameter count | Sequence length 2048 | Relative speed vs baseline |
|-------|-----------------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| sLSTM (pure mixing) | 350 M | ~2× slower than mLSTM |
| mLSTM (matrix memory) | 350 M | Baseline |
| FlashAttention Transformer | same size | ≈4× faster than both LSTMs for inference (quadratic scaling hurts long contexts) |
| Mamba (SSM) | similar | Comparable to mLSTM after tuning |

A few takeaways:

* **Linear scaling:** Both s-/m-LSTMs show true $\mathcal O(T)$ runtime growth, while Transformers blow up as $\mathcal O(T^2)$ once context exceeds ~4k tokens.
* **Parallel advantage:** Even with raw matrix ops, mLSTM stays within constant factor of Transformer inference when handling long sequences because it never incurs quadratic attention costs.
* **Memory footprint:** Neither variant stores per-token activations beyond what’s needed for the scan; thus max GPU memory stays constant irrespective of context length—a decisive win for edge deployment.


---  

## 5. Trade‑offs Summarized  

| Aspect | sLSTM | mLSTM |
|--------|-------|-------|
| **Expressivity** | Memory mixing lets cells exchange information across heads; excels at *state tracking* tasks (e.g., formal language parity). | Matrix memory gives huge capacity for associative recall but lacks inter-cell communication beyond shared matrix updates. |
| **Parallelism** | Inherently sequential due to hidden-to-hidden connections → requires optimized per-step kernels. | Fully parallelizable via scan/branchless BLAS ops → runs close to pure feedforward layers despite quadratic inner dimension. |
| **Compute cost** | Lightweight per step (scalar ops + few vector ops). | Heavy per step (`d×d` outer product), yet efficiently batched on GPUs. |
| **Speed gap (empirical)** | ≤ 2× slower than mLSTM under same hardware/configuration. | Baseline speed; still ≈ 4× slower than highly tuned attention kernels but linear w.r.t sequence length.|


In practice you might pick:
* **sLSTM** when your task demands explicit hidden-state interaction—think program execution tracing or entity tracking over very long narratives.
* **mLSTM** when raw storage capacity outweighs fine-grained mixing—e.g., massive associative recall or language modeling where context windows stretch beyond what attention can handle efficiently.


---  

## 6. Looking Ahead: Closing the Gap  

The authors note three immediate avenues for squeezing more speed out of both flavors:

1️⃣ **Optimized flash‐style kernels for mLLM:** Tiling strategies that minimize intermediate writes could bring matrix updates down from “~4× slower than FlashAttention” to comparable levels.

2️⃣ **Hybrid blocks:** An architecture where most layers are fast‐parallel mLM­S™, punctuated by occasional sLM­S™ blocks could combine expressive mixing with overall throughput.

3️⃣ **Mixed precision & sparsity:** Leveraging FP16/Ampere Tensor Cores for outer products while keeping critical stabilizer logic in FP32 may cut flop counts without breaking numerical stability.


---  

## 7️⃣ Final Thoughts  

The contrast between sLSTM and mLSTM illustrates a classic systems dilemma: richer algorithmic structure versus raw hardware efficiency. By carefully engineering custom kernels and embracing scan-friendly recurrences, xLSTMs prove that “old-school” recurrent models can still compete at scale—especially when you need linear runtime and constant memory footprints.

If you’re building next‑generation language models or any sequence processor where extreme context lengths matter, consider whether your workload leans toward *state tracking* (favor sLM­S™) or pure *capacity* (favor mLM­S™). And remember—sometimes spending a day writing an efficient CUDA kernel pays off more than adding another layer of attention heads!



# Why xLSTM Could Be a Game‑changer for Edge AI and Long‑Context Applications

*TL;DR*: By revisiting the classic LSTM architecture with **exponential gating** and **new memory structures**, the xLSTM family offers *linear compute*, *constant memory* over sequence length, and *parallelizable* blocks that make it attractive for on‑device inference while still handling very long contexts—think code analysis or multiyear time‑series forecasting.

---

## 1. From Classic LSTMs to Their Modern Limitations  

The original Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) introduced two key ideas in the early ’90s:

\[
c_t = f_t \, c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t ,\qquad 
h_t = o_t \,\psi(c_t),
\]

where the **input** (\(i_t\)), **forget** (\(f_t\)), and **output** (\(o_t\)) gates control how information is written, retained, or read from a *scalar* cell state \(c_t\).

These mechanisms solved vanishing gradients and powered everything from handwriting synthesis to AlphaStar. Yet three fundamental bottlenecks prevented LSTMs from scaling like modern Transformers:

| Limitation | What it means | Why it hurts edge/long‑context scenarios |
|------------|---------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Irreversible storage decisions** | Once a value is written, the forget gate cannot *increase* its contribution later. | Hard to correct mistakes when new evidence arrives (e.g., nearest‑neighbor search). |
| **Scalar cell capacity** | Only one number per hidden dimension stores all past information. | Rare tokens or fine‑grained signals get lost—crucial in code evaluation or low‑frequency events. |
| **Sequential hidden‑hidden connections** | The hidden state depends on the previous hidden state ⇒ no true parallelism. | Inference latency grows linearly with sequence length and cannot exploit GPUs efficiently. |

Because of these issues, Transformers stole the limelight: self‑attention offers *quadratic* context handling but at a huge memory cost that grows with sequence length—problematic for on‑device deployment.

---

## 2. xLSTM’s Two Core Innovations  

The **xLSTM** paper tackles exactly those three limits by adding two orthogonal upgrades:

### 2.1 Exponential Gating + Stabilization  

Instead of sigmoid‐bounded gates, xLSTM allows the input gate \(i_t\) (and optionally \(f_t\)) to be **exponential**:

\[
i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t), \qquad
f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\;\text{or}\;\exp(\tilde{f}_t).
\]

An extra *normalizer* \(n_t\) accumulates the product of past forget gates with current input gates:

\[
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t,
\]

so the hidden output becomes a *scaled* version of the cell:

\[
h_t = o_t \,\frac{c_t}{n_t}.
\]

Because exponentials can explode numerically, a **stabilizer state** \(m_t\) tracks \(\log(i_t)\) and \(\log(f_t)\) over time (cf. Eq. (5)) ensuring stable gradients while preserving the ability to “revise” stored values on the fly.

> **Practical payoff:** The model can *increase* the influence of an older entry whenever later evidence suggests it’s more relevant—exactly what you need when scanning a log stream or performing a nearest‑neighbor search dynamically.

### 2.2 New Memory Structures  

#### • sLSTM – scalar memory with richer mixing  
Retains a traditional scalar cell but introduces **memory mixing across multiple heads/cells** using recurrent weight matrices (\(\mathbf{R}_*\)). The exponential gates give each head its own revision capability while mixing within a head preserves expressivity.

#### • mLSTM – matrix (covariance) memory  
Transforms each cell into a full matrix \(\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Updates follow a classic *covariance* rule reminiscent of Fast Weight Programmers:

\[
\mathbf{C}_t = f_t\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top,
\]

where \(\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t\) are key/value vectors derived from the current token (like Transformer queries/keys). Retrieval simply multiplies by a query vector \(\mathbf{q}_t\):

\[
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{C}_t\,\mathbf{q}_t}{\max\!\bigl(|\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|,1\bigr)}.
\]

Because there is **no hidden‑hidden recurrence**, this update can be expressed as a *scan* operation that runs in parallel across time steps—similar to FlashAttention’s reduction kernels.

> **Practical payoff:** A matrix cell stores \(d^2\) pieces of information instead of just \(d\). This dramatically boosts rare‑token handling and associative recall (e.g., remembering thousands of key–value pairs).

---

## 3. Linear Compute + Constant Memory – Why It Matters

| Property | Transformer | Classic LSTM | xLSTM (s/m variants) |
|----------|-------------|--------------|----------------------|
| Compute vs. length \(T\) | \(O(T^2)\) (self‑attention) | \(O(T)\) (recurrence) | \(O(T)\) |
| Memory vs. length \(T\) | \(O(T)\) (attention cache) | \(O(1)\) (current cell only) | \(O(1)\) |
| Parallelizability | High (GPU kernels across tokens) | Low (strict scan) | mLSTM = high; sLSTM ≈ moderate |

Because each time step only needs its current cell(s), an xLSTM model’s RAM consumption stays flat even when processing millions of tokens—a crucial trait when deploying on smartphones or microcontrollers where RAM is scarce (< 1 GB). Moreover:

* **Predictable latency:** Linear compute means runtime grows proportionally with input size without sudden spikes caused by attention quadratics.
* **Batch-friendly inference:** Since there’s no per‑token cache that expands with context, you can increase batch size without running out of GPU memory—a major advantage for serving many concurrent requests on edge servers.
* **Scalable training:** The authors showed that even at 15 B tokens on SlimPajama, an xLSTM architecture (~350 M parameters) beats GPT‑3‐scale Transformers in validation perplexity while using less GPU memory per step.

---

## 4. Edge AI Scenarios That Benefit

### 4.1 Code Evaluation & Symbolic Reasoning  

When interpreting source code or executing scripts, you must keep track of variable bindings across thousands of lines—something classic RNNs struggled with because once a variable value was overwritten it could not be “re‑opened”. With exponential gating, an xLSTM can *re‑activate* earlier bindings when later statements reference them again, yielding robust state tracking shown by superior performance on formal language tasks (e.g., parity & content tracking benchmarks).

### 4.2 Time‑Series Forecasting Over Years  

Financial or climate data often span billions of timestamps. Storing an entire history in attention caches is impossible on embedded devices collecting sensor streams in real time. An xLSTM processes each new measurement online, updating its matrix memory without ever expanding its footprint—ideal for predictive maintenance units operating on low‑power MCUs.

### 4.3 On‑Device Personal Assistants  

Voice assistants need context windows far beyond 512 tokens when following multi‑step dialogs or reading lengthy documents aloud. With linear compute they can stretch context lengths to 16 k tokens while keeping power consumption modest—something Transformer based assistants cannot do without massive VRAM overheads.

### 4.4 Real‑Time Reinforcement Learning Agents  

Agents like AlphaStar relied heavily on LSTMs before Transformers arrived because they could ingest game histories sequentially under strict latency budgets. The new mLSTM variant preserves that low latency while adding richer memories—a potential win for mobile robots navigating environments over prolonged missions.

---

## 5. Empirical Evidence from the Paper

* **Synthetic Formal Languages:** Only models equipped with *memory mixing* (the sLSTM part of xLSTM) solved parity tasks that require revisiting past states—a clear indication that exponential gating + mixing enable true state tracking.
* **Multi‑Query Associative Recall:** With up to 256 random key–value pairs in sequences up to length 2048, xLSTM[1:1] achieved higher recall accuracy than any non‑Transformer baseline—including strong RNNs like RWKV—and matched Transformer performance despite using far less RAM.
* **Long Range Arena (LRA):** Across all four LRA benchmarks (listops, byte-level text classification, etc.) xLSTM consistently ranked near the top while maintaining linear runtime.
* **Scaling Laws:** Experiments on 15 B and 300 B token corpora show that validation perplexity follows a power law similar to Transformers but with a lower offset—meaning larger xLMs will remain competitive without exploding memory requirements.
* **Inference Benchmarks:** At 1.3 B parameters, generation time scales linearly with token count even against GPT‐style cached models whose runtime becomes quadratic beyond ~8 k tokens; throughput curves confirm that batch sizes > 64 are feasible on commodity GPUs—something impossible for cached Transformers under identical RAM limits.

## 6. Current Limitations & Future Directions

| Issue | Impact | Possible Remedy |
|-------|--------|------------------|
| sLSTM’s hidden–hidden connections hinder full parallelism | Slightly slower than mLSTM (≈ 2× in current CUDA kernel). | Optimized scan kernels similar to FlashAttention could close this gap. |
| mLSTM’s matrix updates are \(O(d^2)\); current kernels are ≈ 4× slower than FlashAttention.| Higher compute cost per step at large hidden sizes.| Custom CUDA kernels exploiting tile‑wise multiplication may bring speed parity.| 
| Forget gate initialization needs care otherwise decay overwhelms updates.| Training instability if naïvely set.| Adopt learned bias initialization strategies discussed in Appendix B.| 
| No exhaustive hyperparameter sweep conducted for giant models.| Reported numbers may understate ultimate potential.| Dedicated NAS / grid search could unlock further gains.|

Despite these hurdles, the authors stress that even unoptimized implementations already outperform state-of-the-art transformers at comparable model sizes—a promising sign that engineering effort will only magnify the advantage.

## 7. Bottom Line

xLSTM revives an iconic recurrent idea—but modernizes it enough to compete with—and sometimes beat—transformer‐based giants **while staying lightweight enough for edge deployment**:

* **Constant memory footprint** → fits comfortably on smartphones or embedded CPUs.
* **Linear compute cost** → predictable latency even when contexts stretch beyond tens of thousands of tokens.
* **Rich storage via matrix memories** → handles rare events and associative retrieval without bloating parameters.
* **Exponential gating** → lets longer sequences revise early decisions—a missing piece for many real-world sequential tasks.
* **Parallelizable mLSTM blocks** → harness GPU acceleration without sacrificing recurrence benefits.

If your product needs *long context reasoning*, *online learning*, or simply wants sophisticated language capabilities under tight hardware budgets, keeping an eye on xLSTM implementations—and perhaps contributing optimized kernels—could pay off dramatically as next‐generation edge AI rolls out.
