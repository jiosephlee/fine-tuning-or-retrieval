### What exactly is meant by two reward functions being “equivalent” under Definition 1, and why does this not affect the learned policy?
Question:
Definition 1 states that two reward functions $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent if $r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)$ for some function $f$. Lemma 1 says such functions induce the same preference distribution under Bradley‑Terry, and Lemma 2 says they induce the same optimal policy under the KL‑constrained RL problem. Could someone walk through an intuitive example showing how adding a prompt‑dependent constant changes rewards but leaves preferences and policies unchanged?
Answer:
In the DPO paper a **reward function** is simply a scalar score $r(x,y)$ that tells us how good a completion $y$ is for a prompt (or context) $x$.  
Definition 1 declares two rewards $r$ and $r'$ *equivalent* when they differ only by a term that depends on the prompt but **not** on the candidate completion:

$$
r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\qquad\text{for some function }f .
$$

Because the extra term $f(x)$ is the same for every possible continuation $y$ of the same prompt, it never influences the relative ordering of completions. This notion of equivalence is exactly what Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 formalise.

---

### Why preferences are unchanged (Lemma 1)

The Bradley–Terry (BT) model used to turn rewards into a preference probability is

$$
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
{\exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_{i})\bigr)+
 \exp\!\bigl(r(x,y_{j})\bigr)} .
$$

If we replace $r$ by an equivalent reward $r' = r-f(x)$,

$$
\frac{\exp(r'(x,y_{k}))}
     {\exp(r'(x,y_{k}))+\exp(r'(x,\tilde y))}
=
\frac{\exp(r(x,y_{k})-f(x))}
     {\exp(r(x,y_{k})-f(x))+\exp(r(-,\tilde y)-f(X))}
=
\rho ,
$$

the factor $\exp(-f(x))$ cancels from numerator and denominator. Hence the *preference distribution* stays exactly the same. The BT model only cares about **differences** of rewards for a fixed prompt, not about their absolute levels.

---

### Why the optimal policy is unchanged (Lemma 2)

In the KL‑constrained RL objective

$$
\max_{\pi_\theta}\;
\E_{x,\;y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,]
-\beta\, D_{\rm KL}\!\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)
      \,\Vert\, 
      \pi_{\rm ref}(\cdot|x)\bigr),
$$

the optimal solution can be written in closed form (Eq.~4 of the paper)

$$
\pi_r(y|x)=
   \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, 
   \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
   \exp!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr),
   \qquad 
Z(x)=
   \sum_y 
   \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)
   \exp!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr).
$$

If we substitute an equivalent reward $r' = r - f(x)$,

$$
\pi_{r'}(y|x)=
   \frac{1}{Z'(x)}\, 
   \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,
   \exp!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}[\,r(x,Y)-f(X)]\,\
   )
=
   \frac{e^{-f(X)/B}}{Z'(X)}\, 
   \pi_{\rm ref}(Y|
        X)
       e^{\, {r(X,Y)/B}},
$$

and because $e^{-f(X)/B}$ does not depend on~$Y$, it becomes part of the normalising constant,

$$
Z'(X)=e^{-f(X)/B}\,
      Z(X),
$$ 

so finally $\displaystyle 
   \pi_{r'}(Y|
        X)=
       \pi_r(Y|
        X).
$

### An intuitive toy example

Imagine we are asked to write a short answer to the question “What is the capital of France?”.  
Suppose we construct a reward that counts how many words appear in the answer **plus** an arbitrary bias that depends on the question:

*Reward A*: $r_{\text{A}}(X,Y)=
  {\underbrace{|Y|}
          _{\text{length of answer}}}
 + {\underbrace{3}
          _{f_{\text A}(X)}}.$  

Now consider another reward that does **not** add this bias:

*Reward B*: $r_{\text B}(X,Y)= |Y|.$  

Here $f_A(X)=3$, $f_B(X)=0$, so indeed  
$r_A-r_B=f_A(X)-f_B(X)$, i.e.~they are equivalent.

If we compare two candidate answers, say  

* $Y_{{}_{{}_{} }\!}=``Paris''$\;(where~$||Y||=¹)$,
* $Y₂=``Paris,\ France''$\;(where~$\vert Y₂\rvert = ²)$,

the BT preference under either reward is

$$
p(Y₂\succeq Y₁∣X)
=
\sigma(\vert Y₂\rvert-\vert Y₁\rvert)
=
\sigma(１),
$$

because the added constant 3 cancels out. Both rewards rank “Paris, France’’ higher than “Paris”.

When we translate either reward into an optimal policy through Eq.~4, we get

$$
π(Y∣X)
    ∝π_{\rm ref}(Y∣X)
      ×e^{ │Y│/β }
      ×{\underbrace{
          e^{ 3/β }}
           _{\text{{same for every }}Y}},
$$

and after normalising over all possible completions the factor~$\displaystyle e^{3/β}$ disappears. Hence both rewards lead to identical sampling probabilities – our language model will produce exactly the same distribution over answers despite their absolute scores differing by 3.

### Relating back to identifiability

The fact that only *differences* matter mirrors a common situation in statistics: models are often identifiable only up to an additive constant (or more generally up to transformations that leave likelihood unchanged). In the RLHF setting this issue appears as an under‑specification of the reward: many numerical reward surfaces correspond to exactly the same observable behaviour. By working with an *equivalence class* rather than a single canonical reward, DPO sidesteps any arbitrary choice of baseline and directly optimises the policy that matters.

---

**Summary**

Two reward functions are called equivalent when they differ by a term that depends solely on the prompt, $f(X)$. Because both the Bradley–Terry preference model and the KL‑constrained optimal‑policy formula depend only on *relative* scores for completions under a fixed prompt, such an additive prompt‑only shift cancels out. Consequently any member of an equivalence class yields identical pairwise preferences and identical optimal policies. The simple toy example above illustrates that adding a constant like “+3’’ to every answer for a given question changes nothing about which answer is preferred nor about how a language model will sample answers after optimisation. This invariance underlies Lemma ​¹​ and Lemma ​²​ and justifies why DPO can safely re‑parameterise rewards as $\beta\,log(\,\pi_\theta/\pi_{\rm ref}\,)​$ without loss of generality.​

### When an SFT model is unavailable, how is the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ constructed by maximizing likelihood of preferred completions?
Question:
The authors mention that if $\pi^{SFT}$ cannot be obtained, they initialize $\pi_{ref}= \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w|x)]$. Does this mean training a new language model only on the preferred answers? If so, how does this affect distribution shift compared with using an actual SFT model trained on full task data?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – when an actual supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) policy $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ is not available the authors simply **train a new language‑model policy on the “winning’’ completions $y_{w}$** that appear in the preference set $\mathcal D=\{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\}$.  In practice this means solving  

$$
\pi_{\text{ref}}
= \argmax_{\!\pi}\;
    \mathbb E_{(x,y_w)\sim \mathcal D}
    \bigl[\log\,\pi(y_w|x)\bigr].
$$
(1)

which is exactly the same objective used for ordinary supervised fine‑tuning except that we discard the dispreferred answers $y_l$.  The resulting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is then plugged into the DPO loss  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
   \Bigl[
     \log\sigma
       \Bigl(
         \beta\,\log\tfrac{\;\pi_\theta(y_w|x)\;}{\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)\;}
         -%
         \beta\,\log\tfrac{\;\pi_\theta(y_l|x)\;}{\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)\;}
       \Bigr)
   \Bigr].
$$
(2)

---

### Why does this help with *distribution shift*?

In standard RLHF pipelines the **reference policy** is exactly the SFT model that was used originally to generate the two candidates $(y_1,y_2)$ for each prompt $x$.  
The KL‑penalty in Eq.(2) therefore measures how far the new policy drifts from *the same* generator that produced the human labels.  

If we had no access to that generator we could naïvely take some generic pretrained LM as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  That would create a **mismatch**:

* The KL term would be computed against a distribution that places high probability on many low‑quality continuations never seen during preference collection.
* The gradient would try to push $\pi_\theta$ away from these irrelevant modes, effectively *over‑penalising* useful updates and potentially destabilising training.

Training $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on Eq.(1) sidesteps this problem because

1. **It uses exactly the same data** ($x,y_w$) that generated the preferences.  
   Thus $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ approximates “what an SFT model would have done” even though we never had one.
2. **The resulting KL now measures deviation from a baseline that already favours high‑quality responses**, so large penalties are avoided and DPO can focus on improving *relative* probabilities between $y_w$ and $y_l$.
3. The authors explicitly call this “mitigating distribution shift between the true reference distribution … and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used by DPO’’ (Section 4).

In short, matching the support of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to that of $y_w$ keeps the optimisation faithful to the assumptions behind Eq.(2).

---

### What does training only on $y_w$ change compared with a full‑task $\boldsymbol{\pi^{\text{SFT}}}$?

| Aspect | Full‐task $\boldsymbol{\pi^{\text{SFT}}}$ | “Preferred‑only” $\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!~\!\!~\,~\,~\,~\,~\,~$
|
|---|---|---|
| **Training data** | All available demonstrations for the task (both good & mediocre responses). | Only the selected *winning* responses from $\mathcal D$. |
| **Coverage** | Broad coverage of possible style/length/edge cases → better out‑of‑distribution robustness. | Narrower coverage – learns mainly what was judged “good” for those prompts present in $\mathcal D$. |
| **KL regularisation** | Computes deviation from a well‑rounded baseline → safe but sometimes overly conservative if baseline contains many low‑quality modes. | Computes deviation from a baseline already concentrated on high‑quality modes → smaller KL values → more aggressive improvement possible without violating constraint. |
| **Risk of over‑fitting** | Lower – many examples keep model from memorising idiosyncrasies. | Higher – with few unique $(x,y_w)$ pairs one can over‑fit; regularisation via weight decay / early stopping becomes important. |
| **Practical impact observed in paper** | When an SFT exists (e.g., summarisation), DPO uses it directly and achieves state‑of‑the­-art results. | In dialogue experiments where no public SFT existed they built $\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!~\,~\,~ ~π_{\!{\rm ref}}\!$ via Preferred‑FT (training only on $y_w$).  Despite narrower data, DPO still outperformed PPO and matched a “Best‑of‐N’’ oracle (Section 5). |

Thus **the main trade–off** is *breadth versus fidelity*: a full‐task SFT gives you a more generally capable prior at the cost of larger KL penalties; a preferred‑only prior gives you a tighter prior that aligns perfectly with your preference data but may hurt generalisation when encountering prompts far outside those seen during its construction.

---

### Intuitive picture

Think of each token probability as an altitude map over all possible continuations:

* The **reference map** tells you where you start.
* The **KL term** penalises stepping too far away from that map.
* The **DPO gradient** raises height under tokens belonging to $y_w$ *relative* to tokens under $y_l$.

If we start from a very rough map (a generic LM), most good valleys are missing—so raising them requires huge steps that get penalised heavily.  
If we first carve out valleys around exactly those good continuations ($y_w$) by fitting Eq.(1), we already stand near where we want to go; subsequent relative adjustments become cheap and stable.

---

### Practical recommendations

* **Use an actual SFT whenever it exists.** It gives you both breadth and fidelity.
* **If no SFT is available**, train $\displaystyleπ_{\rm ref}$ exactly as Eq.(1) suggests—i.e., run ordinary supervised fine‑tuning on every *(prompt, chosen completion)* pair you have.
* Monitor *coverage*: evaluate $\displaystyleπ_{\rm ref}$ or early checkpoints of DPO on held‑out prompts not present in $\mathcal D$ because over‑concentration can surface later as hallucinations or loss of diversity.
* Consider augmenting $\mathcal D$ with additional prompts (even without labels) if you suspect severe OOD drift—this mimics what standard RLHF does by mixing unlabeled data into its PPO updates.

---

### Bottom line

Training $\displaystyleπ_{\rm ref}$ by maximising likelihood of just the preferred answers is exactly what Eq.(1) prescribes—it builds a surrogate “reference” SFT out of whatever high‑quality demonstrations you have.  
Doing so aligns the KL term with the actual data‐generating process behind your preference labels, thereby reducing harmful distribution shift inside the DPO objective.  The price is reduced exposure to diverse output modes, which can hurt out‐of‐distribution generalisation relative to a genuine full‐task SFT; nevertheless, empirical results in Section 5 show that even this lightweight surrogate works well in practice when an authentic SFT model is unavailable.

### Can DPO be extended to preference models beyond Bradley‑Terry, such as full Plackett‑Luce rankings over more than two candidates?
Question:
Section 3 suggests that similar derivations hold for Plackett‑Luce models (Appendix B). However I am unclear on how Eq. (5) would look when we have a ranking of three or more completions per query. Does DPO require any modifications to its loss function or gradient when handling multi‑way preference data?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) framework is agnostic to the particular choice of Plackett–Luce–type preference model. When the data contain a full ranking of $m\geq 2$ completions rather than a single binary comparison, the only change is that Equation (5) (the DPO loss) is replaced by the **Plackett–Luce likelihood** written in terms of the *policy* $\pi_\theta$ and the *reference* policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. No new hyper‑parameters or ad‑hoc gradient tricks are required; the gradient follows automatically from differentiating this likelihood.

Below I walk through the derivation, show the explicit form of the loss for an arbitrary ranking, and comment on its gradient and implementation aspects.

---

### 1. From rewards to policies under a Plackett–Luce model  

The paper assumes a latent reward function $r^{*}(x,y)$ that generates human preferences through a Plackett–Luce distribution

$$
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\succ\dotsc\succ y_{m}\mid x)
    = \prod_{i=1}^{m}
      \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{i})/\beta\bigr)}
           {\sum_{j=i}^{m}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{j})/\beta\bigr)},
      \tag{PL}
$$

where $\beta>0$ is the KL‑constraint temperature appearing in the RLHF objective (Eq.~(2)).

A key step in DPO is **re‑parameterising** the reward by the optimal policy (Eq.~(4))

$$
r^{*}(x,y)\;=\;\beta\,\log
    \frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
    \;+\;\beta\,\log Z^{*}(x),
$$

with $Z^{*}(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r^{*}(x,y)/\beta)$.
Because $Z^{*}(x)$ depends only on $x$, it cancels everywhere that ratios of exponentials appear.

Insert this expression into the Plackett–Luce formula:

```
exp(r*/β)   =   exp[log π*(y)/πref(y)]  *  Z*(x)
            =   (π*(y)/πref(y))·Z*(x).
```

Both numerator and denominator contain the same factor $Z^{*}(x)$, so it disappears:

$$
p^{*}(y_1\rhdotsc\rhd y_m\mid x)
   = \prod_{i=1}^{m}
     \frac{\displaystyle
           \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x)}
          }
          {\displaystyle
           \sum_{j=i}^{m}
           \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_j|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}
          } .
     \tag{1}
$$

Equation (1) is exactly the **likelihood** of an observed ranking under a policy $\pi_{\theta}$; it mirrors Eq.~(5) but for a whole permutation instead of one binary comparison.

---

### 2. The multi‑way DPO loss  

Given a dataset of rankings  
$\mathcal D= \bigl\{\, (x^{(k)},\, \mathbf y^{(k)} ) : 
   \mathbf y^{(k)}=(y^{(k)}_1,\dots ,y^{(k)}_m )\,\bigr\}$,

the maximum‑likelihood objective becomes

$$
\fbox{
   $\displaystyle
     \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}^{\,PL}(\theta)
     = -\,E_{(x,\mathbf y)\sim \mathcal D}
       \Bigl[
         \sum_{i=1}^{m}
         \Bigl(
           \beta\,%
           {\underbrace{\bigl[\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}
                                 {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i|x)}\bigr]}_{\text{policy advantage}}
         -
           \beta\,%
           {\underbrace{
               \log
               \Bigl(
                 \sum_{j=i}^{m}
                 \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_j|x)}
                       {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j|x)}
               \Bigr)
            }_{\text{normaliser at position } i}}
         \Bigr)
       \Bigr] .
   $
}
$$

When $m=2$ this reduces to Eq.~(5):  
the first term gives $+\beta[\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})(y_w)- 
                           \log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})(y_l)]$,  
the second term becomes $-\,
   \log[1+\exp(\beta[\ldots])]$, which equals
$-\log\sigma(\cdot)$.

Thus **no extra scaling or weighting constants** are needed beyond the existing temperature $\beta$; the only structural change is summing over all positions in the observed ranking.



### 3. Gradient interpretation  

Differentiating Eq.~(2) yields a clean extension of Eq.~(6).  
For each position $i$

* we obtain a weight  
  $w_i = 1 -
          P_{\theta}^{PL}\bigl(\text{any item after } i
                | x,\mathbf y\bigr)$,
  which measures how much probability mass is incorrectly placed behind $y_i.$

* The gradient contribution from position $i$ is  

  $$
    w_i\, 
    \Bigl(
      \nabla_\theta\nabla_y
        \log \pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)
      -
      \
      S_i,
    ...
    )
  $$

(where $S_i=\sum_{j=i}^{m}P_\theta(j|i,x,\mathbf y)\,
       ∇_θ∇_yy_j$). More compactly,

$$
w_i\, 
   (
     ∇_θ∇_yy_i -
     ∑_{j=i}^m P_\θ(j|i,x,\mathbf y)\,
       ∇_θ∇_yy_j
   ),
$$

with $P_\θ(j|i,x,\mathbf y)$ denoting the softmax probability (the denominator in Eq.~(1))
of candidate $j.$

Intuitively,

- items that are **higher** in a human ranking receive a positive push proportional to how much their current policy probability lags behind their reference probability;
- items that are **lower** receive a negative push proportional to their share in the normaliser.
The weighting automatically reflects how *confidently* the current policy disagrees with the observed order—a direct analogue of “higher weight when reward estimate is wrong’’ from Eq.~(6).

Because all terms are simple functions of logits,
the implementation boils down to:

```python
log_ratio = torch.log(pi_theta / pi_ref)
# cumulative softmax denominators:
denom = torch.logcumsumexp(log_ratio.flip(dims=[0]), dim=0).flip(dims=[0])
loss_per_ranking = -(beta * (log_ratio - denom)).sum()
```

which runs in linear time $O(m)$; no pairwise decomposition or additional memory overhead is required.



### 4. Practical consequences  

- **No algorithmic modification:** The DPO training loop stays exactly the same—sample minibatches of rankings, compute Eq.~(2), backpropagate, update $\theta$.
- **Data format:** Instead of triples `(y_w , y_l)` you feed an ordered list `([y₁,…,y_m])`.  
If some datasets only contain partial orders (e.g., “top‑k’’ without ordering among lower items), you can simply apply Eq.~(2) up to $k$; items not ranked contribute only through the denominator at their first missing position.
- **Computational cost:** The extra cost relative to pairwise DPO is linear in $(m.)$ For typical RLHF datasets where $m=3–5,$ this overhead is negligible.
- **When you *must* decompose:** If you already have binary comparisons only, you may continue using Eq.~(5); otherwise adopting full PL gives richer gradients because every item participates in every normaliser term.



### 5. Connection to Appendix B  

Appendix B works out exactly this derivation and confirms that after substituting   
$r=\beta\,(\log\tfrac{\pi}{\pi_\text{{ref}}})+c(x)$   
into a generic Plackett–Luce likelihood,
all dependence on the unknown normalising constant~$c(x)$ disappears – yielding precisely Eq.~(1).  
The authors therefore state that “similar derivations hold for Plackett–Luce models,” meaning that **the same change‐of‐variables trick works**, leading directly to Eq.~(2).

---

## TL;DR  

DPO already accommodates any Plackett–Luce family, including full rankings over three or more candidates.  
The binary cross‑entropy loss (Eq.~5) becomes a **sum over positions** of logarithms of policy–reference ratios divided by their cumulative sums (Eq.~2).  
No extra hyper‑parameters or special gradient tricks are needed—just feed ordered lists instead of pairs and compute the above likelihood; backpropagation automatically gives a weighted update that generalises Equation 6 to multi‑way data.

### Why does DPO appear more robust to sampling temperature changes than PPO in the summarization experiments?
Question:
Figure 2 shows that DPO’s win rate stays relatively high across temperatures 0–1, whereas PPO’s performance drops sharply at higher temperatures. Is there an intuitive explanation—perhaps related to the way DPO’s loss directly optimizes log‑probability ratios—that makes its policy less sensitive to temperature at inference time?
Answer:
The win‑rate curves in Figure 2 show that after fine‑tuning with **Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO) the model keeps roughly the same quality whether we sample with a cold‐beam (`temperature‑0`) or with a very stochastic decoder (`temperature‑1`).  By contrast the **PPO** models drop sharply once we move away from greedy decoding.  
The reason can be traced back to what each algorithm actually optimises and how that interacts with the decoder’s temperature‑scaling step.

---

### What sampling temperature does  

When we generate text we first compute logits $l_{t}(y)$ at each time step and then apply  

$$
\pi_T(y\mid x)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\bigl(l_{t}(y)/T\bigr),
$$

so a larger $T>1$ divides every logit by the same constant.  
Because division by a positive constant preserves the ordering  
$l_{t}(y_w)>l_{t}(y_l)\Longleftrightarrow l_{t}(y_w)/T>l_{t}(y_l)/T,$  
the set of *most likely* continuations stays the same, but the gaps between their probabilities shrink.  Consequently a low‑temperature decoder spits almost always the top‑ranked continuation, while a high‑temperature decoder samples more often from lower‑ranked alternatives.

If a policy places almost **all** its mass on one particular completion (a very peaked distribution), increasing $T$ will quickly expose those low‑probability tails and the average quality drops dramatically.  If instead the policy spreads its mass over several good continuations, flushing out some probability mass does not hurt much—the sampled text is still likely to belong to the set of acceptable answers and the win rate remains stable.

---

### The shape imposed by DPO  

DPO’s loss is  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}%
\Bigl[\log \sigma%
      \Bigl(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}
            -\,\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}
      \Bigr)
\bigr].
$$
$(1)$

Two facts follow immediately from this expression:

1. **Only *ratios* of probabilities matter**.  
   Adding any function $f(x)$ simultaneously to $\log \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)$ leaves every term unchanged because it cancels out between numerator and denominator inside each logarithm.  Hence the optimisation cares solely about how much more likely a preferred completion should be *relative* to its dispreferred counterpart and relative *to* the reference model $\pi_{\rm ref}$.

2️⃣ **The optimal solution satisfies**

$$
   \pi^{*}(y|x)=
   \frac{\;\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\,\exp!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y)/\beta\bigr)}{Z(x)},
   \qquad 
   r^{*}(x,y)=
   \beta\,\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)},
   $(2)$
where $Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\rm ref}(y|x)\exp(r^{*}/\beta)$ normalises the distribution but never appears inside $(1)$, so any additive normalisation disappears during optimisation.

Because training pushes **all** preferred answers upward **relative** to their dispreferred rivals while keeping them tethered to $\pi_{\rm ref}$, probability mass is redistributed *smoothly* across every good continuation that ever appeared in a preference pair.  The resulting policy therefore has several “good” peaks rather than one isolated spike.

---

### Why PPO behaves differently  

PPO implements Eq.~(5),

$$
   \max_{\theta}\,
   \mathbb E_{\,y\,∼\,π_\theta}
   \bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)-\,β\,%
          \log\tfrac{π_\theta(y|x)}{π_{\rm ref}(y|x)}
   \bigr],
$$

where $r_{\phi}$ is a separately trained reward network.  
In practice we estimate gradients with REINFORCE‑style advantages:

* The reward estimator $r_{\phi}$ is noisy and calibrated only up to an additive constant per prompt.
* A learned value function—or sometimes a single Monte Carlo baseline—is used to approximate $Z(x)$.  Errors in this baseline translate into biased gradients.
* The clipping / KL penalty used by PPO only enforces an *approximate* constraint on average KL divergence; empirically this frequently lets the policy push most probability onto whichever single sequence maximises $r_{\phi}$.

The net effect is a **highly concentrated** distribution where one or two completions dominate $p_T(y|x)$.  When we raise $T$, those dominant points lose most of their weight while many low‑probability “fallback” strings—often poorly aligned with human preferences—are sampled instead, causing the sharp drop seen for PPO in Figure 2.

---

### Putting it together for summarisation  

Summarisation admits many valid paraphrases.  
DPO’s pairwise objective treats every observed “good” summary as a *positive* signal relative to its negative counterpart, so during fine‑tuning it lifts *all* these good modes simultaneously while remaining close to $\pi_{\rm ref}$.  Consequently even when we flatten the distribution with $T=1$, most sampled summaries still belong to this enlarged set of high‑quality candidates, so GPT‑4’s win rate hardly moves.

PPO, however, learns an implicit scalar reward that typically assigns its highest value to only one particular phrasing seen during training (the one yielding maximal reward estimate).  Its KL term discourages drastic drift globally but does not prevent an extreme local collapse onto that single phrase.  When temperature inflates sampling noise those non‑peak candidates appear far more often and quickly erode quality, giving precisely the pattern observed in Figure 2.

---

### Intuition revisited  

You can think of DPO as *learning a calibrated scoring function*   
$s_\theta(y|x)=β\,\log[\π_\theta(y|x)/π_{\rm ref}(y|x)]$   
that directly reflects human preference *differences*.  
Because only differences matter, any monotone transformation—including division by a global temperature—preserves their ordering.  Hence changing $T$ merely makes the decoder “blur” an already well‑ordered landscape without destroying its hierarchy—the win rate stays flat.

PPO instead learns an *absolute* expected reward surface whose shape is distorted by variance reduction tricks and clipping; it ignores most of the pairwise structure present in human data, so its landscape contains deep valleys separated by narrow ridges.  A global blur immediately pulls samples into those valleys and hurts performance.

---

**Bottom line:**  
DPO’s loss maximises *log–probability ratios* rather than raw expected rewards, which forces the fine‐tuned policy to keep multiple preferred completions proportionally likely while remaining anchored to the original language model through an exact KL constraint.  This yields a smoother output distribution whose relative ranking survives uniform rescaling caused by decoding temperature—hence DPO appears far more robust than PPO when we vary temperature during summarisation generation.

### In Eq. (6), are per‑token log‑probabilities required to compute $\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$, and what are the memory/computational implications for long sequences?
Question:
To evaluate the ratio inside the sigmoid, one must compute the full log‑likelihood of a complete generation under both current and reference models. Does DPO require storing all token logits for both models simultaneously during training? If so, how do practitioners handle GPU memory constraints for large models or long outputs?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – the term $\displaystyle \log\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ in Eq.\,(6) is obtained by summing **per‑token** log‑probabilities of the two models, but DPO does **not** require that all token‑level logits be kept in GPU memory at once. In practice one either computes the two forward passes on‑the‑fly (with the reference model run under `torch.no_grad()`) or pre‑computes and caches the reference‑model scores once and re‑uses them throughout training. This design keeps the extra memory overhead modest even for long generations; any remaining cost is comparable to ordinary supervised fine‑tuning of an LM and can be mitigated with standard tricks such as mixed‑precision, gradient checkpointing, or attention‑efficient kernels.

---

### Why per‑token logs are needed

A language model factorises a sequence probability as  

$$
\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\,\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t}),
$$  

so  

$$
\log\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   =\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Bigl[\,
        \log \,\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x,y_{<t})
        -\log \,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})
     \Bigr].
$$  

Thus we need the **log‑probability of every token** under both policies. The term is **scalar** for a given pair $(x,y)$, but it is built from the per‑token contributions shown above. No normalisation over the whole vocabulary is required beyond what each model already does internally when it turns its final logits into a probability distribution.

### Do we have to keep all logits in memory?

No. The loss only needs the *scalar* sum of per‑token logs for each example, not the full matrix of logits ${\mathbb R}^{T\times V}$ (with $V$ the vocabulary size). Two practical ways avoid storing everything:

1\. **On‑the‑fly evaluation** – During each training step we forward‑pass both $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on the same batch while teacher forcing with the target tokens $y_w$ and $y_l$. The reference model is wrapped in a ``no_grad`` context, so PyTorch discards its intermediate activations after computing its logits; only the scalar log‑probabilities (or their sum over timesteps) are retained. The student model’s graph retains gradients as usual.

2\. **Caching reference scores** – Because $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ never changes, many implementations pre‑compute $\log \,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|\cdot)$ for every token in every training pair and store those numbers on disk (often as simple float16 arrays). At training time one simply reads the cached scalars and adds them to the student’s forward‑pass results. This eliminates any extra forward pass through the reference model altogether, reducing both compute and peak GPU memory.

Both approaches keep GPU consumption roughly equal to that of ordinary supervised fine‐tuning: you still need to hold the student’s transformer activations for backpropagation, but you never keep a full $T\times V$ tensor.

### Handling very long sequences

Longer inputs increase two things:

* **Compute** – more transformer layers are applied at each timestep, so FLOPs grow linearly with $T$.
* **Memory** – standard self‐attention needs $O(T^{2})$ workspace for its attention matrix. This issue exists regardless of DPO; any LM fine‐tuning on long contexts will hit it.

Consequently practitioners employ exactly the same tricks used for regular LM fine‐tuning:

* **Chunked attention / sliding windows** (e.g., Transformer‑XL, LongFormer): replace full attention with a local pattern so memory scales linearly.
* **FlashAttention / efficient kernels**: reduce constant factors in the $O(T^{2})$ operation.
* **Sequence truncation**: cap $T$ at a hardware‐friendly value (commonly 1024–2048 tokens); longer contexts are handled by hierarchical generation or recurrence.
* **Gradient checkpointing**: recompute intermediate activations during backward pass to shave peak activation memory.
* **Mixed precision (fp16/bf16)**: halves tensor bandwidth without harming numerical stability of log‑probability sums.

Because DPO adds no extra tensor beyond a scalar weight per example, these measures are sufficient; there is no hidden quadratic term introduced by Eq.\,(6).

### Intuition – “knowledge distillation” view

From a high‐level perspective Eq.\,(6) looks like knowledge distillation where a *teacher* ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$) provides soft scores that are subtracted from the *student* ($\pi_{\theta}$). Unlike classic distillation where you need the full teacher softmax vector at every position, DPO merely needs its **log probability of the sampled token**, which compresses a $V$–dimensional vector down to a single float per position. That compression makes it possible to treat the teacher as an inexpensive scorer rather than a heavyweight memory consumer.

### Bottom line

* Computing $\displaystyle \log\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ indeed requires per‐token log probabilities, but these are summed into a single scalar for each pair.
* Neither algorithm nor implementation forces you to keep all token logits simultaneously; they can be generated on demand or cached ahead of time.
* Memory impact beyond ordinary LM fine‐tuning comes mainly from handling long sequences via attention‐efficient methods—not from DPO’s ratio itself.
* In practice large‐scale DPO experiments (up to 6 B parameters in the paper) train comfortably on a single modern GPU by using ``no_grad`` on the reference model or caching its scores, combined with standard fine‐tuning tricks such as mixed precision and gradient checkpointing.

These practices explain how researchers train DPO on long outputs without exceeding GPU limits while still respecting the mathematical form of Eq.\,(6).

Could there be pathological cases where different prompts have very different normalizing constants that affect learning stability?

The short answer is **no** – dropping the partition function \(Z(x)\) does not introduce a systematic bias in Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

Since \(Z(x)\) is a function only of \(x\), it appears as an additive term in every reward for that prompt and therefore cancels whenever a preference probability or a gradient with respect to \(\theta\) is formed.

### What are the formal assumptions behind Theorem 1 that guarantee any reward class can be represented as $r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$?
Question:
The theorem states "under mild assumptions" but does not list them explicitly in the main text. Are these assumptions limited to having strictly positive probabilities for all tokens under both policies, or are there additional constraints on reward smoothness or model capacity? Understanding these conditions would help me know when DPO may fail.
Answer:
**Answer**

Theorem 1 in *Direct Preference Optimization* shows that every reward function that is compatible with a Bradley‑Terry (or more generally a Plackett‑Luce) preference model can be written as  

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\;\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} ,
\tag{1}
\]

for some *policy* $\pi$ and a *reference* policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
The phrase “under mild assumptions’’ hides only three technical conditions; they are **not** about smoothness of the reward or about the expressive power of the neural‑network model.  
Below I unpack those conditions, explain why they are needed for the proof, and point out when DPO could break down if they are violated.

---

### 1. What the theorem actually assumes

The proof (Appendix C of the paper) proceeds by taking an arbitrary reward function \(r\) and its optimal KL‑constrained policy  

\[
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad  
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

From (2) one obtains (1) after “projecting’’ \(r\) onto its **equivalence class**  
\(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\) where \(f(x)=\beta \log Z(x)\).  

For this argument to go through we need only:

#### (a) **Full support / absolute continuity**

*For every context* \(x\),

*   \(\displaystyle \forall y:\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)>0.\)

*   Any candidate optimal policy \(\pi(y\mid x)\) must satisfy  

    \(\displaystyle \forall y:\;\pi(y\mid x)>0 \Longrightarrow 
    \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)>0.\)

In other words, \(\pi_{\!}\) is absolutely continuous with respect to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
This guarantees that the ratio \(\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\) in (1) is well‑defined for every \(y\).

In language‑model terms this means **every token** must have a non‑zero probability under the reference LM (a property satisfied by standard soft‑max output layers).

#### (b) **Finite partition function**

For each prompt \(x\),

\[
Z(x)=
\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
e^{\,r(x,y)/β}<+\infty .
\tag{3}
\]

Since \(β>0\) is a fixed scalar, this condition merely asks that \(r(x,\cdot)\) does not grow so fast that the weighted exponential series diverges.  
In practice it holds whenever:

*   The output space at each step is countable (the set of token strings);
*   \(r(x,y)\) is **bounded above** or grows at most linearly with sequence length – both are true for any reward obtained from a learned neural network because its output is a real number with finite magnitude.

If \(Z(x)\) were infinite, normalising by it would be impossible and Eq. (2) would not define a proper distribution.

#### (c) **Bradley‑Terry / Plackett‑Luce preference generation**

The data are assumed to be generated by a *latent* reward \(r^{*}\) via

```math
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\sigma \bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2}) \bigr),
      \qquad 
\sigma(t)= \frac{1}{1+e^{-t}} .
```

This structure implies two important facts used in Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 of the paper:

* Adding any *context‑only* function \(f(x)\) to a reward leaves all pairwise preference probabilities unchanged.
* Adding such an \(f(x)\) also leaves the optimal KL‑constrained policy unchanged because it merely rescales all unnormalised probabilities by a constant factor that gets absorbed into \(Z(x)\).

Consequently every *equivalence class* of rewards – all functions that differ only by an additive term depending on \(x\) – induces exactly the same preference distribution and the same optimal policy.

---

### 2. Why these are “mild’’ and what they exclude

| Assumption | Why it is mild | When it can fail |
|------------|----------------|------------------|
| **Full support** (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}>0\,\,∀y)\) | Modern LMs use softmax over vocabularies, which never assign exact zero probability to any token. Even after fine‑tuning, temperature > 0 preserves positivity. | If you deliberately *mask* tokens (e.g., hard vocabulary pruning or using top‑k sampling at training time), some completions become impossible under π<sub>ref</sub> and Eq. (1) no longer defines a finite ratio. |
| **Finite partition function** | With bounded neural network outputs and countable token sequences this always holds; β is a fixed positive constant. | If you embed an unbounded reward like “length × 10⁶” without clipping, exponential weighting may blow up for very long strings, making Z infinite. |
| **Bradley‑Terry / PL data generation** | This is exactly the preference model used throughout RLHF literature; virtually all public preference datasets are built under this assumption. | If preferences are noisy in ways not captured by a simple logistic link—e.g., multi‑label rankings, context‑dependent noise, or systematic label bias—the resulting distribution may not belong to the PL family, and (1) will no longer be guaranteed to reproduce those preferences exactly. |

Notice that **no smoothness or continuity** requirement appears in the theorem. The result holds for *any measurable* reward—even highly discontinuous ones—provided it satisfies (a)–(c). Likewise there is **no restriction on model capacity**: Theorem 1 says *there exists* some distribution π satisfying (1); whether a particular neural network can represent that π depends on its expressive power but does not affect the validity of the theorem itself.

---

### 3. Practical implications for DPO

* **When DPO works as advertised** – as long as you keep the reference LM “alive” (no zero‑probability tokens), use a reasonable β>0, and your preference labels truly follow Bradley‑Terry/Plackett‑Luce dynamics, you can always rewrite an optimal reward as Eq. (1). The binary cross‑entropy loss in Equation (5) of the paper then recovers exactly the same optimum that RLHF would reach with an explicit reward model.
* **When DPO may fail** – violations listed above will break one of the three pillars:
  1. **Zero probabilities** → division by zero → undefined loss gradients.
  2. **Exploding rewards** → infinite Z → loss becomes NaN.
  3. **Non‑Bradley‑Terry preferences** → converting preferences into Eq. (5) yields a biased estimator; you might still train something useful but you lose *theoretical exactness* that DPO guarantees.

In practice these failure modes manifest as:
- Instability when training with very low temperatures (`top-p`/`top-k` pruning at fine‑tuning).
- Divergence if you apply an unregularised length penalty as part of your reward.
- Systematic mismatch when using multi‑turn dialogues where preferences depend on entire conversation histories rather than single pairwise comparisons.

---

### 4. Summary

The “mild assumptions’’ behind Theorem 1 reduce to three simple conditions:

1. **Positivity / full support** of both reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and any candidate optimal policy $\pi$;
2. **Finite normalising constant** \(Z(x)\), which follows from bounded rewards;
3. **Preference generation follows a Bradley‑Terry/Plackett‑Luce model**, i.e., preferences depend only on differences of an underlying latent reward.

No additional smoothness or capacity constraints are required; those would only affect whether a concrete neural network can *approximate* the abstract $\pi$ that satisfies Eq. (1). When these three conditions hold, any admissible reward class can indeed be expressed as  

\(r(x,y)=β\,\log[\π(y∣x)/π_{\text{ref}}(y∣x)]\,.\)

If one of them is violated—most commonly through zero probabilities introduced by aggressive sampling or via rewards that cause an infinite partition function—the equivalence breaks down and DPO may no longer recover the true optimal policy. Understanding these boundaries helps diagnose when DPO will succeed and when extra care (e.g., adding temperature smoothing, clipping rewards, or enriching the preference model) is needed.​

### How might one incorporate unlabeled prompts into DPO training—e.g., via self‑labeling—without breaking its theoretical foundations?
Question:
The discussion hints at using self‑labeling from a DPO policy as a way to leverage more data, similar to semi‑supervised RLHF approaches. Since DPO relies on an explicit likelihood ratio between current and reference policies conditioned on human preference pairs, how could we extend it to generate pseudo‑preferences for new prompts while still preserving convergence guarantees?
Answer:
**Answer**

`Direct Preference Optimization` (`DPO`) sidesteps reinforcement learning by writing the human‑preference likelihood directly in terms of the *policy* rather than an explicit reward model.  
When a pair $(x,y_w,y_l)$ is drawn from a dataset of preferences, DPO minimizes  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)= 
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!
\Bigl[\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr)\Bigr],
$$  
the only place where the data appear is through *differences* of log‑probabilities relative to a fixed reference model $\pi_{\rm ref}$.  
Because both the Bradley–Terry preference model  

$$
p^*(y_1\succeq y_2\,|\,x)=
\sigma\!\bigl(r^*(x,y_1)-r^*(x,y_2)\bigr),
$$  

and the optimal KL‑constrained policy  

$$
\pi^*_r(y\,|\,x)=
\tfrac{\pi_{\rm ref}(y\,|\,x)\,
e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}}{Z(x)},
$$  

are invariant under adding any *prompt‑only* function $f(x)$ to the reward (the equivalence class of Lemma 1), one can replace $r(x,y)$ by  

$$
r_\theta(x,y)=
\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\rm ref}(y\,|\,x)},
$$  

without losing expressive power (Theorem 1). Consequently any policy that maximises (1) is guaranteed—under the usual mild regularity assumptions—to be the optimal solution of the original RLHF objective (Eq.\,(5) in the paper).

---

### Using unlabeled prompts

The standard DPO pipeline needs labelled pairs only for a *finite* set of prompts. To exploit many more unlabelled prompts $\mathcal U=\{x^{(j)}\}$ we can generate *pseudo‑preferences* from the current DPO policy itself. Below is a minimal, theoretically sound scheme:

1️⃣ **Initialisation** – Train an ordinary DPO model on the human‑labelled set $\mathcal D_{\rm human}$. Call its parameters $\theta^{(0)}$.

2️⃣ **Self‑labeling step** – For each $x∈\mathcal U$:  
 • Sample two completions $y_1,y_2∼
   \operatorname{Mixture}\bigl(\pi_{\theta^{(t)}} ,\,π_{\rm ref}\bigr)$  
  (e.g., take one sample from each distribution or draw both from $\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}$ at temperature $T<1$).  
 • Compute their *implicit* rewards according to (4):  

   $$
   \hat r^{(t)}_{i}= 
   \beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta^{(t)}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
                     {\pi_{\rm ref}(y_i\,|\,x)},
   \qquad i=1,2 .
   $$  

 • If $\bigl|\hat r^{(t)}_{1}-\hat r^{(t)}_{2}\bigr|\ge\tau$
   (a confidence margin), create a pseudo‑pair  
   $(x,\; y_w=\argmax_i y_i,\; y_l=\argmin_i y_i )$; otherwise discard it.

   The label therefore comes from exactly the same Bradley–Terry probability that defines (1):  

   $
   p(y_w\succeq y_l\,|\,x)=
   \sigma(\hat r^{(t)}_{w}-\hat r^{(t)}_{l}) .
   $

   Hence these synthetic pairs are unbiased draws *from* the current preference model, i.e.\ they obey the same generative assumption that underlies all real human labels.

3️⃣ **Mixing** – Form an augmented training set  

$$
\mathcal D^{(t+1)}
= \mathcal D_{\rm human}\;\cup\
w\,{\mathcal D}_{\rm pseudo}^{(t)},
$$  

where $w∈[0,1]$ down‑weights synthetic examples (typical values:
$w=0.2{-}0.5$). The loss remains exactly (1); we simply evaluate it on a larger batch containing both real and pseudo pairs.

4️⃣ **Re‑training** – Optimise (1) on $\mathcal D^{(t+1)}$ starting from $\theta^{(t)}$
(or re‑initialising if desired). The KL regularisation term is unchanged because it always references the fixed reference policy.

5️⃣ **Iterate** – Repeat steps 2–4 for several rounds until performance on a held‑out validation set stabilises.

---

### Why this does not break DPO’s theory

* **Preservation of the Bradley–Terry model** – By constructing pseudo‑labels via comparing two completions’ implicit rewards, we guarantee that each synthetic pair satisfies exactly Eq.\,(2) with respect to the reward $r_\theta(\cdot)$. In other words, we are drawing *additional* observations from the same probabilistic model that generated our human data.

* **Equivalence‑class invariance** – Adding an $f(x)$‑only term would not change either Eq.\,(2) or Eq.\,(3); consequently any amount of extra data drawn from this distribution cannot alter the set of optimal policies—only shrink their variance.
  This is precisely what Theorem 1 formalises: every admissible reward can be written as (4).

* **Consistency guarantees** – Lemma 2 tells us that all rewards in one equivalence class lead to identical optimal policies under KL‑constrained RLHF.
  Because our synthetic labels are generated *from* such a reward,
  maximising (1) on an enlarged dataset still converges—under standard MLE assumptions—to a policy whose induced reward belongs to that class.
  In practice this means that as long as we do not introduce systematic bias,
  convergence guarantees remain intact.

* **No extra partition function** – The brilliance of DPO is that $Z(x)$ cancels when we write preferences in terms of log‑ratios.
  Self‑labeling respects this cancellation because it never needs an estimate of $Z(x)$;
  only differences of log probabilities appear.


---

### Practical tips & pitfalls

| Issue | Remedy |
|-|-|
| **Distribution shift** – if synthetic completions come from a policy already far from the reference, KL may blow up.| Keep $\beta$ moderate (e.g., $\beta \approx 0.{ }10{-}0.{ }5$), use temperature sampling $\leq 0.{ }8$, or mix in a small fraction of reference samples when forming each pair.| 
| **Noisy pseudo‑labels** – small score differences give unreliable preferences.| Discard pairs whose score margin $<\tau$ ($τ≈0.{ }5{-}¹$ depending on $\beta$). Optionally weight each pair by $\sigma(|Δ\rhô |)$, letting confident pairs contribute more.| 
| **Over‑reliance on self‑generated data** – may reinforce early mistakes.| Use a decreasing schedule for $w$, e.g., $w_t = w_0·γ^t$ with $γ≈0.{ }9$; always retain all human data at full weight.| 
| **Computation** – sampling many completions per prompt can be expensive.| Generate just two candidates per prompt per iteration; reuse them across epochs or cache them.| 

An alternative is *soft* self‑labeling: train a small auxiliary classifier on human‑labelled pairs,
use its predicted preference probability $p_{soft}$ as target in  

$$
-\!\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}
    [p_{soft}\,\log\sigma(\Delta)+(1-p_{soft})]
    + {\rm const},
$$  

which reduces to Eq.\,(1) when $p_{soft}=σ(\Delta)$. Because this still fits within a Bradley–Terry likelihood it inherits exactly the same convergence proof.

### Putting it together

The overall semi‑supervised DPO algorithm looks like:

```python
θ ← train_DPO(D_human)
for t = 0,…,T:
    U_pseudo ← ∅
    for x ∈ unlabeled_prompts:
        y₁,y₂ ← sample_completions(x, θ)
        ŕ₁, ŕ₂ ← β·log πθ(yᵢ│x)/πref(yᵢ│x)
        if |ŕ₁−ŕ₂| ≥ τ:
            add (x, argmax_i yᵢ , argmin_i yᵢ ) to U_pseudo
    θ ← train_DPO(D_human ∪ w·U_pseudo , init=θ)
```

Because every step optimises exactly the maximum‑likelihood objective for a Bradley–Terry model whose rewards are parameterised by Eq.\,(4), all theoretical results proved for vanilla DPO—including consistency and optimality under KL constraint—remain valid.


In short, self‑labeling works in DPO by treating its own implicit reward as a teacher,
generating additional preference pairs whose labels follow precisely the same probabilistic law as genuine human judgments.
When filtered for confidence and mixed with real annotations at an appropriate weight,
these synthetic pairs simply reduce estimator variance and let us exploit vast amounts of otherwise unlabeled prompts without compromising any of DPO’s theoretical foundations.)
