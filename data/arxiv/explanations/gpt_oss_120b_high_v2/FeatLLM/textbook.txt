\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

**Introduction and Motivation**

Few‑shot tabular learning sits at the intersection of two demanding constraints: real‑world problems often present data in a structured table format, yet acquiring large labeled datasets is frequently infeasible because of cost, privacy, or rarity of events.  In such settings a model must extract useful predictive signals from a handful of annotated examples while resisting the temptation to latch onto accidental patterns that happen to appear in the limited data.  Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have revealed powerful reasoning capabilities that can be harnessed as a source of external knowledge, opening a promising avenue for addressing these challenges without costly model retraining or extensive manual feature engineering.



# Real‑world importance of tabular data

Tabular datasets are ubiquitous across domains where decision making depends on heterogeneous attributes recorded in rows and columns.  Finance relies on credit histories and transaction logs; healthcare projects patient outcomes from laboratory results, demographics, and diagnostic codes; manufacturing monitors sensor readings alongside operational parameters; public policy evaluates socio‑economic indicators together with geographic information.  In each case the raw table encodes a compact representation of complex phenomena that practitioners have historically extracted manually using domain expertise.  

Because these fields often involve high stakes—such as approving loans or diagnosing disease—the reliability of predictions matters as much as raw accuracy.  Moreover, many applied settings lack large pools of annotated records: a new medical test may initially have only dozens of confirmed cases, or a nascent credit product may be rolled out to a limited pilot group before broader adoption.  Consequently, methodologies that can operate effectively on modest numbers of labeled rows while still leveraging the rich information already embedded in existing tables are crucial for translating machine‑learning advances into tangible societal impact.



# The low‑shot regime and overfitting risk

Formally, let a tabular dataset be denoted by  
\[
\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]  
where each input vector $\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ comprises $d$ heterogeneous features and $\mathbf{y}^{i}$ belongs to a finite set of classes $\{1,\dots,c\}$.  In $k$‑shot learning only $k\ll N$ labeled examples are made available for training—typically $k\in\{2,4,8\}$ per class.  

When $k$ is small, standard supervised learners tend to memorize idiosyncrasies rather than discover robust relationships.  Empirically this manifests as **spurious correlations**: patterns that hold only within the tiny training subset but vanish on new data \cite{bartlett2021deep}.  For example, an irrelevant demographic attribute might by chance coincide with positive outcomes in four labelled patients; a classifier trained on those four points would incorrectly assign predictive weight to that attribute, leading to severe performance degradation when faced with realistic test samples.  

The heightened variance inherent to low‑shot regimes also hampers reliable hyperparameter tuning or model selection because cross‑validation folds contain too few samples per class to yield stable estimates.  As a result, classical tabular learners such as logistic regression or tree ensembles either underfit (due to strong regularization needed for stability) or overfit (when regularization is insufficient), making it difficult to achieve acceptable performance without injecting additional inductive bias.



# Limitations of conventional feature engineering

Traditional approaches mitigate low‑shot overfitting by manually crafting informative features – e.g., aggregating raw measurements into ratios, encoding domain‐specific thresholds, or deriving interaction terms believed a priori to influence outcomes.  This process rests on three fragile pillars:

1. **Domain expertise requirement** – Engineers must possess deep subject matter knowledge (medical guidelines, financial regulations) to identify which transformations are meaningful.  When datasets span novel domains or when experts are scarce, this step becomes a bottleneck.
2. **Scalability constraints** – Human designers cannot feasibly generate bespoke features for each new table encountered in practice.  A pipeline that works for one dataset may need entirely different transformations for another whose columns have distinct semantics.
3. **Static nature** – Manually engineered features are fixed once defined; they cannot adapt dynamically to subtle cues present in even a few labelled examples unless re‑engineered manually.

Consequently expert feature construction does not fully solve the low‑shot problem: it reduces reliance on noisy statistical patterns but replaces it with another source of potential bias—human misinterpretation or omission—and it cannot be readily deployed at scale across heterogeneous tables.



# Opportunity offered by LLMs’ reasoning abilities

Large language models trained on massive corpora encapsulate extensive factual knowledge (“older individuals have higher disease risk”, “high credit utilization predicts default”) and possess sophisticated pattern‑matching capacities acquired through self‑supervised learning on billions of tokens \cite{brown2020language}.  When presented with concise task descriptions and a handful of exemplars—a process known as **in‑context learning**—an LLM can infer underlying causal relationships without any parameter updates \cite{wei2022emergent}.  

The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework exploits precisely this capability by turning an LLM into an *automatic feature engineer*.  Its prompting strategy decomposes the reasoning process into two stages:

1. **Problem understanding** – The model first reflects on generic knowledge about how each column type (numeric, categorical) generally relates to the target task (`Step 1`).  This isolates informative columns while discarding irrelevant ones based solely on world knowledge.
2. **Rule extraction** – Leveraging both this prior understanding and the $k$ labelled demonstrations (`Step 2`), the LLM synthesizes explicit decision rules (“if age > 65 AND cholesterol > 240 then class = high risk”).  

These rules are parsed back into executable code that yields *binary* features indicating rule satisfaction for any sample.  Because rule generation occurs only once per dataset (rather than per test instance), inference thereafter reduces to evaluating inexpensive linear combinations—a stark contrast to prior LLM-based tabular approaches that required an LLM query for every test point \cite{dinh2022lift,hegselmann2023tabllm}.  

Crucially, embedding domain knowledge within rules mitigates spurious correlations: by forcing the model’s attention toward plausible causal pathways derived from its pretraining corpus, \textsf{FeatLLM} prevents accidental reliance on unrelated columns even when those columns appear correlated within the tiny training set (as demonstrated empirically in Section 5).  Moreover, because rule extraction is phrased as natural‑language generation constrained by structured response formats, it scales automatically across diverse tables without hand‑crafting new descriptors for each column set.

In sum, LLMs furnish an *external inductive bias* that complements scant labelled data while remaining agnostic to any particular downstream model architecture.  By translating this bias into concrete engineered features—rather than treating the LLM itself as an end‑to‑end predictor—researchers obtain both computational efficiency (no per‑sample API calls) and interpretability (rules are human readable).  These advantages position LLM-powered feature engineering as a compelling solution for modern low‑shot tabular learning scenarios across finance, healthcare, manufacturing, and beyond.

# 2. Foundations: Tabular Data and Few‑Shot Learning  

In order to appreciate how **FeatLLM** transforms a large language model (LLM) into an automatic feature engineer for tabular prediction, we must first become comfortable with the basic ingredients of the problem setting.  This chapter introduces the structure of tabular datasets, distinguishes between the two canonical prediction tasks—classification and regression—examines the definition and challenges of *few‑shot* (or *k‑shot*) learning, and reviews the performance measures that are routinely employed when evaluating models on tabular data.  Each notion will be presented with an eye toward intuition, because a clear conceptual grounding is essential before delving into the more intricate mechanisms described later in this text.

---  

## Tabular Datasets: Features, Types, and Names  

A **tabular dataset** is organized as a rectangular table whose rows correspond to *instances* (also called samples or observations) and whose columns correspond to *variables* (commonly called *features*).  Formally, let  

\[
\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where \(N\) is the number of labeled instances, \(\mathbf{x}^{(i)} = (x^{(i)}_{1}, x^{(i)}_{2}, \dots , x^{(i)}_{d})\) is a \(d\)-dimensional vector of feature values for instance \(i\), and \(y^{(i)}\) denotes its associated target label.  In practice each column \(j\) bears a **feature name** \(f_{j}\) that conveys its semantic meaning (e.g., “age”, “cholesterol level”).  The paper assumes that these names are available together with succinct natural‑language definitions—for instance “age is an integer representing years since birth” or “blood type is a categorical variable taking one of {A, B, AB, O}”.  Such metadata are crucial when we later ask an LLM to reason about which columns are relevant to a given prediction task.

### Numerical versus Categorical Variables  

Features come in two broad families:

1. **Numeric (continuous or discrete) features** take values on a numeric scale.  Continuous variables such as “blood pressure” can assume any real number within a physiological range, while discrete variables such as “number of prior hospital visits” take integer values but are still treated numerically by most learning algorithms.

2. **Categorical features** encode qualitative information by assigning each instance to one of a finite set of categories.  Categorical variables may be *ordinal*—where the categories possess an inherent order (“low”, “medium”, “high”)—or *nominal*, where no order exists (“city”, “state”, “country”).  In raw form these are often represented by strings or integers that serve as symbolic identifiers rather than quantities.

The distinction matters because many modeling pipelines—including those examined in the FeatLLM experiments—must treat each type appropriately when constructing rules or analyzing relationships with the target variable.

### Feature Names as Natural‑Language Prompts  

Because LLMs process text rather than raw vectors, providing **feature names** and their definitions in plain English enables the model to draw upon its vast pre‑training corpus of domain knowledge (“older individuals have higher risk of heart disease”).  In FeatLLM’s prompting scheme each feature name appears alongside its type annotation:

```
age (numeric): age in years.
cholesterol (numeric): measured concentration in mg/dL.
smoking_status (categorical): {never, former, current}.
```

These human‑readable descriptors become part of the *basic information description* supplied to the LLM before it attempts rule extraction.

---  

## Classification versus Regression in Tabular Domains  

Once a tabular dataset is assembled, we typically aim to predict a target variable from its features.  Two broad families of supervised learning problems emerge:

### Classification  

In **classification**, the target \(y^{(i)}\) belongs to a finite set of labels \(\mathcal{C} = \{c_{1}, c_{2}, \dots , c_{K}\}\).  When \(K = 2\) we have binary classification (e.g., “diabetic / non‑diabetic”), while larger values of \(K\) correspond to multiclass problems such as predicting disease subtypes.  The learner’s objective is to produce a probability distribution over \(\mathcal{C}\), usually via a *softmax* transformation:

\[
p(y^{(i)} = c_{k} \mid \mathbf{x}^{(i)}) = 
\frac{\exp(\operatorname{logit}_{k}^{(i)})}{\sum_{\ell =1}^{K} 
      \exp(\operatorname{logit}_{\ell}^{(i)})},
\]

where each \(\operatorname{logit}_{k}^{(i)}\) is a scalar score reflecting how strongly instance \(i\)’s features support class \(c_{k}\).  In FeatLLM these logits are generated from binary rule‑based features via a simple non‑negative linear model (see Section “Inferring Class Likelihood via Rules”).

### Regression  

In **regression**, the target is continuous—in other words \(y^{(i)} \in \mathbb{R}\).  Typical examples include predicting house prices or laboratory measurements.  The model outputs a real‑valued estimate \(\hat{y}^{(i)}\), often obtained by a linear combination of features:

\[
\hat{y}^{(i)} = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{(i)} + b.
\]

Although the primary focus of FeatLLM’s empirical study is on classification tasks (binary or multiclass), the underlying pipeline could be adapted for regression by replacing the softmax layer with an identity mapping and training using mean‑squared error loss instead of cross‑entropy.

### Why Distinguish Them?  

The distinction informs several design choices:

* **Label encoding** – For classification we must preserve categorical semantics (e.g., one‑hot encodings), whereas regression simply treats targets as scalars.
* **Loss functions** – Cross‑entropy drives probabilistic calibration for classification; mean squared error encourages accurate point predictions for regression.
* **Interpretability** – Rule‐based binary features naturally lend themselves to explaining why an instance belongs to class *c* (“age > 65 AND cholesterol > 240”).  For regression similar logical rules could indicate when “risk score” exceeds a threshold, but probabilistic interpretation differs.

## Few‑Shot Learning Definition (\(k\)-Shot) and Typical Pitfalls  

### Formal Definition  

A learning algorithm operates in a **few‑shot regime** when it receives only a tiny subset of labeled examples relative to the size of its hypothesis space.  Formally, given the complete dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), we randomly select

\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = 
   \bigl\{ (\mathbf{x}^{(i)}, y^{(i)}) : i \in \mathcal{I}_{k} \bigr\},
   \qquad |\mathcal{I}_{k}| = k,
\]

with \(k \ll N\).  In an experimental context this selection is repeated across multiple random splits so that performance estimates remain reliable.

When dealing with multiple classes (\(K>2\)), it is common practice—in particular throughout this work—to enforce that every class appears at least once among the \(k\) training points so that each class has at least minimal supervision.

### Why Few Shots Are Hard  

With only \(k\) labelled examples per class, several difficulties arise:

1. **Statistical Overfitting** – Classical estimators such as maximum likelihood become highly variable because there are far fewer observations than parameters (e.g., many columns).  This leads models to fit noise rather than genuine signal.
2. **Spurious Correlations** – In low‑sample regimes random coincidences between irrelevant columns and labels can appear predictive by chance alone (“age” may correlate with outcomes only due to sampling fluctuation).  
3. **Insufficient Coverage** – Some regions of feature space may be completely absent from training data, making it impossible for pure data-driven methods to infer appropriate decision boundaries.
4. **Bias–Variance Trade‑off Amplified** – High variance due to limited data forces algorithms either toward overly simple models (high bias) or overly complex ones that memorize training points (high variance).

Consequently, successful few‑shot methods typically inject *inductive bias*: additional assumptions about plausible relationships that reduce reliance on scarce data alone.

### Leveraging Prior Knowledge via LLMs  

The central idea behind FeatLLM is precisely this injection of external knowledge through an LLM’s world model. By presenting few shot demonstrations together with natural language descriptions of each feature—information that resides within billions of text documents—the LLM can infer plausible causal links (“older age → higher heart disease risk”) even if those links are not evident from just \(k=4\) labeled rows. This prior knowledge acts as an auxiliary source that guides rule induction toward genuinely informative conditions while suppressing spurious ones—a capability demonstrated empirically by reduced performance degradation when noisy columns are attached.

## Evaluation Metrics for Tabular Classification  

When assessing how well a model solves a tabular prediction problem we need quantitative criteria that reflect both discriminative ability and practical considerations such as class imbalance.

### Accuracy  

The most straightforward measure is *accuracy*, defined as

\[
\text{Accuracy}= 
   \frac{\#\,(\text{correct predictions})}{N_{\text{test}}}
   = 
   \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}}
          \mathbf{1}\!\bigl(\hat y^{(i)} = y^{(i)}\bigr)}
        {N_{\text{test}}}.
\]

While intuitively appealing, accuracy can be misleading when one class dominates because naïve predictors that always output the majority class achieve high scores without learning any meaningful pattern.

### Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC‑ROC)

The **ROC curve** plots true positive rate (\(\operatorname{TPR}\)) against false positive rate (\(\operatorname{FPR}\)) while varying a decision threshold on predicted probabilities:

\[
\operatorname{TPR}(t)=
   \frac{\#\, (\text{positive } y^{(i)}~+~p(y^{(i)}=1|\mathbf{x}^{(i)})>t)}
        {\#\, (\text{positive } y^{(i)}}),
        \qquad
\operatorname{FPR}(t)=
   \frac{\#\, (\text{negative } y^{(i)}~+~p(y^{(i)}=1|\mathbf{x}^{(i)})>t)}
        {\#\, (\text{negative } y^{(i)}}).
}
\]

The **area under this curve**, abbreviated AUC or AUROC, summarizes overall discriminative power independent of any specific threshold:

\[
\text{AUC}= 
   \int_{0}^{1} \operatorname{TPR}(t)\,\mathrm d\, \operatorname{FPR}(t).
\]

Values range from 0 ½ (random guessing) up to 1 (a perfect ranker). Because AUC considers ranking rather than absolute probability calibration, it remains robust under severe class imbalance—exactly why all experimental tables in FeatLLM report average AUC across multiple random splits.

For multiclass problems FeatLLM computes AUC in a one‑vs‑rest fashion: each class is treated as positive while all others are negative; then macro‑averaging yields an overall score.

### Cross‑Entropy Loss During Training  

Although not strictly an evaluation metric—cross entropy serves as FeatLLM’s training objective—it also provides insight into how well calibrated predicted probabilities are relative to true labels:

\[
L_{\text{CE}} =
    -\,\frac1{|\,\mathcal D_{\text train}|}
       \sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in
            \mathcal D_{\text train}}
       \log p(y|\mathbf{x}),
\]

where \(p(y|\mathbf{x})=\mathrm {Softmax}({[\text {logit}_1,\dots , 
      \text {logit}_K]})_y.\)
A lower training loss generally correlates with higher AUC on held‐out data provided overfitting does not occur—a balance that is especially delicate when only \(k\) shots are available.

### Validation Strategies  

Given extremely limited labeled data per split, FeatLLM adopts **\(k_{\!FOLD}\)** cross validation on the tiny training set itself solely for early stopping: models trained over many epochs stop at the epoch where validation loss ceases improving across folds. This practice avoids overfitting while still exploiting all available labeled points during final inference after selection has been made.

---  

## Summary  

Understanding tabular data begins with recognizing rows as observations described by named features whose types dictate how they can be processed linguistically by an LLM.  Prediction tasks split into classification—where discrete labels encourage probability modeling—and regression—where continuous outcomes call for direct value estimation.\  
Few\-shot learning formalizes situations where only \(k << N$ labeled points exist per class; such scarcity forces us to confront overfitting and spurious correlations unless we supplement data‐driven learning with external priors such as world knowledge embodied in large language models.\  
Finally, robust assessment relies on metrics like AUC–ROC that remain reliable under imbalance and provide meaningful comparisons across methods ranging from classical tree ensembles up through modern LLM–augmented pipelines like FeatLLM.\  
Armed with these foundations you will be ready to explore how FeatLLM marshals LLM reasoning into rule‐based feature generation—an approach that achieves strong few‑shot performance while dramatically reducing inference overhead compared with naïve end‑to‑end LLM usage.\

**3. Large Language Models and In‑Context Learning**

Large language models (LLMs) have become a central tool for modern machine learning research because they combine massive amounts of textual knowledge with powerful pattern‑matching abilities.  In recent tabular learning research—most prominently in the FeatLLM framework—LLMs are leveraged not as black‑box predictors for each data point, but as “feature engineers” that automatically discover useful decision rules from only a handful of labeled examples.  To appreciate how such systems work it is essential first to understand three technical foundations: how LLMs are pretrained on massive corpora, how researchers design prompts that coax the desired behavior out of these models, what distinguishes few‑shot *in‑context* learning from traditional fine‑tuning, and finally why chain‑of‑thought prompting improves logical reasoning.  The following sections develop each idea in turn, always keeping an eye toward their role in systems such as FeatLLM.



# Pretraining on massive text corpora

The term *pretraining* refers to fitting an LLM on a very large collection of raw text before it is ever asked to solve a downstream task such as disease prediction or credit scoring.  In practice this collection consists of billions of tokens drawn from books, scientific articles, web pages, code repositories, and countless other sources \cite{brown2020language}.  Because the training objective typically asks the model either to predict missing words or next tokens given surrounding context, the resulting parameters encode statistical regularities of language together with factual associations that appear repeatedly across documents—for example “older people have higher risk of heart disease” or “a term deposit is an investment product offered by banks.”  These associations constitute *prior knowledge* that can replace expert domain expertise when solving new tasks \cite{creswell2022selection}.

From an intuitive standpoint one may view pretraining as an extended reading process: after consuming enough material a person develops an internal encyclopedia and learns how different concepts relate even without explicit instruction about any particular problem.  Likewise an LLM learns “soft” representations of entities (\emph{features}) and their typical relationships (\emph{targets}).  When later presented with a new tabular problem—say predicting whether a patient has chronic heart failure—the model can instantly draw upon its stored medical facts rather than having to discover those relationships purely from scarce training rows.  This ability underlies why researchers have been able to apply LLMs successfully across domains ranging from dialogue analysis \cite{finch2023leveraging} to program repair \cite{fan2023automated}, and more recently to tabular prediction \cite{hegselmann2023tabllm}.



# Prompt engineering basics

Even though an LLM already stores broad factual knowledge after pretraining, it does not automatically know *how* we want it to apply that knowledge unless we communicate our intentions clearly.  *Prompt engineering* is precisely the art of constructing a textual query—called a *prompt*—that encodes both the description of our target problem and any auxiliary information needed for solving it.

The FeatLLM system adopts a three‑part prompt structure:

1. **Basic information description**  
   This segment supplies essential metadata about the tabular task:
   - A natural‐language formulation of the prediction question (“Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure?”).  
   - A list of feature names together with their value types or short definitions (e.g., “`age` is an integer representing years”, “`diagnosis` takes one of {‘stable’, ‘unstable’, ‘critical’}”).  
   - A handful ($k$) of *few‑shot demonstrations* drawn from the training set.  Each demonstration is turned into plain text via a serialization function
   \[
   \operatorname{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i,F)
       = \text{``}f_1\text{ is }x^i_1.\ldots f_d\text{ is }x^i_d.\text{ Answer: }y^i\text{''}.
   \]
   Here $F=\{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$ denotes the set of natural‑language feature names while $\mathbf{x}^i$ denotes a concrete row.\footnote{Exactly this format follows prior work \cite{dinh2022lift,hegselmann2023tabllm}.}

2. **Reasoning instruction**  
   To improve logical depth the prompt explicitly asks the model to reason stepwise (“First think about which features could influence this outcome … Then formulate concrete decision rules”).  This mirrors *chain‑of‑thought* prompting (see § Chain‑of‑thought reasoning) and helps separate two distinct cognitive stages:
   - **Step 1** extracts general causal expectations solely from world knowledge encoded during pretraining;
   - **Step 2** refines those expectations using the few concrete examples supplied earlier.
   Importantly Step 1 never looks at any example rows; therefore spurious correlations present only in the small training set are less likely to bias rule generation—a useful safeguard when $k$ is very small.

3. **Response instruction**  
   Finally the prompt tells the model exactly how its output should be formatted so that downstream parsing becomes deterministic (“For each class output ten rules numbered 1–10 using syntax `if <feature> <operator> <value>`”).  By constraining format developers avoid dealing with arbitrary free‑form sentences—a practical concern highlighted by observed noisy syntactic variations such as “greater than” versus “>”.

Through this disciplined construction a researcher transforms an opaque language model into a deterministic rule generator: given $k$ labeled rows together with semantic feature descriptions it will emit structured logical conditions that roughly capture how different columns determine class membership.



# (Few‑shot) In‑context learning vs fine‑tuning

Both *in‑context learning* and *parameter fine‑tuning* aim at adapting an immutable pretrained transformer so that it solves a new problem better than out‑of‑the‑box performance would allow.  Their operational differences are stark:

*In‑context learning* places all adaptation signals directly inside the prompt itself—the few labeled rows described above are concatenated verbatim alongside instructions before asking the model for a prediction or rule set \cite{wei2022emergent}.  No gradient updates occur; therefore
the same underlying weights serve every downstream query.
In practice this means each inference call involves sending only one forward pass through an API endpoint (e.g., GPT–3.5).  

*Fine‑tuning*, by contrast, modifies internal parameters based on one or more backpropagation passes over labeled data before deployment.  Because full fine‑tuning would require millions–scale computation even for modestly sized models, recent works adopt *parameter–efficient tuning* techniques such as LoRA \cite{hu2021lora} or IA3 \cite{liu2022few}.  These approaches adjust only small low–rank additions or scaling factors while keeping most weights fixed—a compromise designed for storage‐constrained scenarios where users cannot afford speculative retraining across all possible downstream tasks \cite{hegselmann2023tabllm}.  

The trade‐offs become especially pronounced under *few–shot* regimes:
- **Data efficiency** – Both paradigms benefit from embedding just $k$ labeled samples rather than requiring thousands; however fine‐tuning often needs more than $k$ passes over data even if $k$ itself is tiny because gradient descent must converge.
- **Inference cost** – In‐context approaches must query an LLM once *per input sample*, incurring latency proportional both to number of test rows and token length (larger tables → longer prompts).  As noted in Section 1 (“Current LLM-based tabular learning methods … require at least one LLM inference per sample”), this makes real‐time deployment expensive.
- **Prompt length limitations** – Even if you could afford one request per row you may still run out of context window when you serialize hundreds of columns into a single string—a scenario where several baselines fail (“most approaches are not suitable with lengthy prompts … when …features … grow … long”).  
Fine‐tuned models sidestep these limits because after adaptation they operate like ordinary classifiers whose forward pass does not depend on textual input length beyond standard token embeddings.

The FeatLLM framework explicitly chooses *in‐context* rule extraction rather than continuous weight updates: it uses only twenty forward passes per dataset (one per ensemble member) during training time—and thereafter never calls an LLM again during test inference because all generated binary rule features are fed into a tiny linear classifier whose parameters ($\mathbf w_k$ below) are learned once via standard cross‐entropy loss:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{logit}_k^{\,i}&=\max(\mathbf w_k,\mathbf0)\cdot\mathbf z_k^{\,i},\\[0.2em]
\mathbf p^{\,i}&=\operatorname{Softmax}\bigl([\operatorname{logit}_1^{\,i},\,\dotsc ,\,\operatorname{logit}_c^{\,i}]\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]
where $\mathbf z_k^{\,i}\!\in\!\{0,1\}^{R}$ encodes whether row $i$ satisfies each of $R$\ (=10) rules belonging to class $k$, $\mathbf w_k$ denotes nonnegative trainable weights capturing rule importance,
and $c$ denotes total classes.
Thus after extracting high–quality features once via few–shot prompting,
the downstream computation reduces exactly to evaluating Eq.~(1)—a negligible cost compared
to repeatedly invoking an external API.



# Chain‑of‑thought reasoning

Standard prompting often asks an LLM simply “Answer yes or no.”  While sometimes sufficient,
the response may be surface‐level because deep logical dependencies among multiple columns get ignored.
*Chain‐of‐thought* prompting encourages models to emit intermediate reasoning steps before delivering a final decision—effectively teaching them “think aloud.”  

In FeatLLM’s design this idea appears inside the *reasoning instruction*.  
First it asks:
> “Think step by step about which attributes influence whether someone has condition X.”
Because no data rows appear at this stage,
the model must reason purely from its world knowledge generated during pretraining,
producing statements akin
to “Higher age increases risk” or “Presence of ‘chest pain’ often co-occurs.”
Only after establishing such generic relationships does it see concrete demonstrations,
and then it translates them into explicit logical conditions (“If age > 55 AND chest pain = ‘yes’ THEN class = ‘positive’”).

Two practical benefits arise:
1. **Filtering spurious correlations** – When only $k$ labelled examples are available,
random wires might suggest bogus patterns (e.g., all positive cases happen also under some unrelated column).  
Because Step 1 anchors reasoning on background facts,
the model downweights columns lacking corroborating prior evidence.
Empirically this explains why FeatLLM degrades little when noisy spurious columns are appended
(Figure 5 shows minimal performance drop compared
to baselines).

2. **Improved consistency through self‐consistency** – By running multiple generations at higher temperature,
the system gathers several plausible rule sets.
Ensembling these via bagging yields robust predictions akin
to classical bootstrap aggregating \cite{breiman2001random}.
This mirrors recent findings called *self-consistency*: answers repeated across independent sampling runs tend
to be more reliable \cite{wang2022self}.

Overall chain‑of‑thought prompting equips short contextual windows—the core limitation identified for existing tabular→LLM pipelines—with richer internal representations enabling accurate translation from raw numerical/categorical columns into symbolic decision criteria.



## Synthesis

Understanding these building blocks clarifies why modern research papers—including FeatLLM—can claim dramatic gains over traditional tabular learners while operating entirely within an API-only setting:
pretrained language models supply rich domain priors;
carefully engineered prompts coax them into revealing explicit logical descriptors;
few‑shot demonstrations replace costly gradient steps;
and chain‐of‐thought instructions enforce disciplined multi‐step reasoning that filters noise.
When combined,
the extracted binary rule features serve as compact yet expressive summaries
that enable ultra low–cost inference through Eq.~(1), sidestepping per‑sample latency incurred by earlier end−to−end methods such as TABLET or TabLLM.
For students reading contemporary literature,
recognizing these interactions demystifies how seemingly “black box’’ language models become useful assistants even for classic non‑textual problems like tabular classification.

# Conventional Baselines: Logistic Regression, XGBoost, Random Forest  

In empirical studies of tabular prediction it remains customary to begin evaluation with three well‑established machine‑learning algorithms – logistic regression, gradient boosted decision trees (as implemented in XGBoost), and random forests – because they represent distinct inductive biases while being easy to train even when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  

Logistic regression treats every input column as a numeric feature whose contribution is summed linearly before applying a sigmoid function that maps the result onto a probability space. Although it makes a strong assumption that class boundaries are approximately linear, its simplicity translates into fast training and modest memory consumption—a valuable property when only $k$ labeled rows ($k \ll N$) can be drawn from an $N$‑sample dataset $\mathcal D=\{(\mathbf x^{i},\mathbf y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$. In low‑shot regimes this model can nevertheless capture useful signal provided that informative columns exist and noise does not dominate – a circumstance often verified empirically using cross‑validation over very small folds ~\cite{chen2016xgboost}.  

Gradient boosted decision trees such as XGBoost construct an additive ensemble where each new tree seeks to correct the residual errors left by previously built trees ~\cite{chen2016xgboost}. This iterative refinement enables highly non‑linear decision boundaries while preserving robustness against overfitting through regularisation techniques baked into the objective function (e.g., shrinkage and column subsampling). When only a few labelled instances are supplied the method compensates by aggressively limiting tree depth and employing early stopping based on validation loss computed over the same tiny set. Consequently XGBoost frequently outperforms purely linear models on heterogeneous columns where interactions between categorical levels matter ~\citet{ho1995random}.  

Random forests aggregate a collection of fully grown decision trees trained on bootstrap re‑samples of the available data ~\citet{ho1995random}. Each tree makes an independent vote for a class label; the most frequent vote defines the final prediction. By virtue of bagging at training time random forests reduce variance without requiring any hyperparameter tuning specific to low‑shot conditions—a property appealing when time or compute resources constrain extensive hyperparameter searches.\footnote{In our experiments we performed grid search combined with $k$‑fold cross validation even when $k$ equals two or four ~\citet{kadra2021well}.} The three baselines together constitute a “classical” reference set against which newer approaches—including self‑supervised schemes and language‐model centric pipelines—are compared throughout the literature ~\citet{asuncion2007uci}.



---

# Self‑Supervised Pretraining  

A second direction pursued by recent research attempts to embed prior knowledge about generic tabular structure directly into model parameters before any task‐specific labels are observed. Two representative examples appear frequently in surveys of few‑shot tabular methods: **TabPFN** and **TransTab**.  

TabPFN creates synthetic training tables by sampling myriad probability distributions over feature spaces and fitting a Bayesian neural network on these generated instances ~\citet{hollmann2022tabpfn}. By exposing the network repeatedly to diverse marginal configurations—including mixtures of categorical frequency patterns, Gaussian clusters, and heavy‐tailed noise—the resulting parameters acquire an implicit understanding of how different statistical regimes manifest themselves within tabular rows. During deployment no additional optimisation is required: both training‐time examples $\{\mathbf x^{i}\}$ and test samples are concatenated into a single inference call; class probabilities are produced directly by forward propagation through the pretrained network.\footnote{This design enables “zero‑shot” operation yet also permits “few‑shot” refinement when labelled records become available.~\citet{hollmann2022tabpfn}}  

TransTab showcases an alternative route centred around **transformer architectures** that ingest column names alongside raw cell values ~\citet{wang2022transtab}. By modelling relations among columns through attention mechanisms conditioned on their textual identifiers, TransTab learns semantic embeddings capturing how certain attribute names tend co‑occur across heterogeneous tables collected during large‐scale pretraining.^[In particular the transformer learns inter‑column relationships without ever seeing actual target labels.] The pretrained encoder can then be fine‑tuned on a new task using only a couple dozen labelled rows—a scenario synonymous with low‐shot tabular learning.\footnote{The decoder component remains dormant during downstream adaptation, emphasizing transferability solely via column semantics.~\citet{wang2022transtab}}  

Both approaches exemplify **self‐supervision** because their pretraining objectives involve reconstructing masked entries or predicting distributional statistics rather than optimising classification loss on target labels – a strategy motivated by early observations that naïve semi‑supervised tricks such as pseudo‑labeling or unsupervised autoencoding often fail when feature types differ dramatically across datasets ~\citet{kadra2021well}. Nevertheless extensive benchmarking shows that while self‐supervision aids generalisation compared with naïve baselines, its benefit plateaus once very few supervised samples become available ~\citet{nam2023stunt}. In practice researchers frequently combine self‐supervision with other sources of prior information—most notably large language models—to obtain more reliable few‑shot performance.


---

# Unlabeled Data Augmentation Methods  

When labelled rows are scarce it is common practice to consult abundant unlabelled corpora through **semi‐supervised augmentation** techniques. Among these,
the method known as **SCARF** builds upon contrastive learning combined with domain‑aware augmentations ~\citet{bahri2022scarf}. The central idea is simple: generate altered versions (“views”) of every unlabelled row—for example by jittering numeric attributes or randomly swapping categories within categorical columns—then train an encoder so that representations derived from different views stay close while representations coming from distinct rows diverge.~\citet{bahri2022scarf} implements this objective using a pairwise loss reminiscent of SimCLR adapted for mixed discrete–continuous inputs.\footnote{\textbf{Why augment?} Augmentations expose invariances inherent in real data—for example age remains close despite small measurement noise—which helps mitigate overfitting when supervised signals are limited.~}  

During downstream evaluation SCARF freezes its encoder after joint training on unlabelled rows plus whatever sparse supervision ($k$ shots) exists; predictions are then made either using a shallow classifier trained atop those frozen representations or by directly comparing distances between labelled exemplars and test embeddings.^[The latter strategy mirrors nearest neighbour labelling.] Empirically SCARF pushes accuracy above traditional random forests under moderate $k$, yet it still lags behind more recent language‑model centric pipelines especially when relational domain knowledge encoded in natural language proves decisive—for example recognizing that “older age increases cardiovascular risk.”

# LLM‑Centric Methods  

Large language models (LLMs) encode extensive world knowledge acquired during pretraining on massive text corpora,and recent works harness this lexical expertise for **tabular reasoning** despite their native sequential nature.



### In–Context Learning Pipelines  

The most direct adaptation places raw table rows inside an LLM prompt together with an explicit *task description* (“Does this patient have diabetes?”) followed by several *few–shot demonstrations* where each demonstration consists of serialized feature–value pairs terminated by an answer label:
\[
\texttt{"}\!f_{1}\!\texttt{\ is}\!x^{i}_{1}\dots f_{d}\!\texttt{\ is}\!x^{i}_{d}\texttt{\ Answer:\ }y^{i}\texttt{"}.
\]
During inference every test row receives an identical prompt structure; hence each prediction triggers one forward pass through the model’s decoder—a process termed **in–context learning** ~\citet{dinh2022lift}. This approach avoids any model parameter updates yet gains substantial performance when $k$ is extremely small because the demonstrations implicitly convey class boundaries.\footnote{Chaining thought approximations (“chain ‑of ‑thought”) further improves reasoning stability ~\citet{wei2022emergent}} However its computational budget scales linearly with test set size because every instance must excite at least one API call.



### TABLET  

TABLET builds upon plain in–context prompting by attaching **external rule hints** generated beforehand through an auxiliary classifier – for example a lightweight decision tree trained on any available $k$ examples ~\citet{slack2023tablet}. The hints take form of prototype patterns (“Age > 60 ∧ BloodPressure ≈ High”) appended after the main task description so that the large model can condition its generation both on raw examples *and* coarse logical priors.\footnote{\textbf{Why add hints?} Hints steer attention toward potentially decisive columns while mitigating distraction caused by irrelevant variables—a symptom observed when feeding very wide tables directly into an unconstrained prompt.~} By exploiting both symbolic cues and natural language reasoning TABLET usually achieves higher AUC than vanilla in–context learning while retaining zero‐parameter updates.



### TabLLM and Parameter–Efficient Fine‐Tuning  

Another family treats the LLM itself as a *feature extractor* whose internal weights are adapted modestly using **parameter‐efficient fine‑tuning** strategies such as LoRA ~\citet{hu2021lora} or IA3 ~\citet{liu2022few}. Instead of re‑encoding every test row anew, practitioners first feed $k$ labelled rows through extended prompts containing *feature descriptions* (\textit{name}, \textit{type}, optional definition). After accumulating gradient updates confined mainly to low‑rank adapter matrices added onto frozen transformer layers, the tuned model can subsequently answer all remaining queries without reactivating expensive gradient computation.\footnote{Because adapters constitute far fewer parameters than full fine tuning they remain tractable even when only API level access permits hidden-state updates – provided such APIs expose gradient hooks.~} In benchmark experiments TabLLM surpasses pure prompting yet still requires initial exposure (“training”) phase consuming several hundred API calls—a cost acceptable for static deployments but undesirable for rapid prototyping.



### Summary Comparison  

All three families share a fundamental design choice: **the LLM participates directly during prediction time**, either once per sample (in–context), once per batch after auxiliary hint generation (TABLET), or repeatedly during a brief adaptation sweep (TabLLM). This design brings two practical consequences explored later:

*   **Per‑sample inference latency** grows proportionally with test set size because every row taxes an expensive forward pass through giant transformer stacks hosted behind commercial APIs.
*   **Prompt length restrictions** arise when many columns must be enumerated verbatim; exceeding token limits forces practitioners either to truncate potentially useful attributes or resort to column subsampling—both choices hurt predictive fidelity.

# Limitations of Existing LLM Approaches  

Although leveraging pretrained linguistic knowledge produces impressive boosts over classical baselines in extreme low‑shot settings, three intertwined drawbacks impede broader adoption.



### High Per‑Sample Inference Cost  

Across all published LLM‐centric pipelines—including those evaluated against FEATLLM—the inference phase obliges at least one external API request per test instance ~\citet{anil2023palm}. Even modestly sized validation sets therefore incite hundreds or thousands of costly queries whose response latency can exceed several seconds each depending upon cloud service load balancing.\footnote{When deploying real-time decision systems—for instance fraud detection—the cumulative delay becomes prohibitive unless massive parallelism is provisioned—a luxury rarely afforded under strict budgeting constraints.~} Consequently such methods suit offline analytics better than latency‐sensitive production environments.



### Dependence on Fine Tuning Access  

Parameter‐efficient techniques such as LoRA or IA3 presuppose **write access** into model weights either locally or via specialised APIs providing adapter injection capabilities ~\citet{kumar2019firstorder}. Yet many state-of-the-art conversational models released recently permit only read/forward APIs\null{}(e.g., GPT‑4), forbidding any gradient‐based updating whatsoever ~\citet{openai2023gpt4}. When facing such “black box” restrictions researchers cannot apply TabLLM’s adapters nor conduct even lightweight rehearsal steps required by TABLET’s hint trainer.\footnote{
A workaround would entail storing numerous prompt variants—but this merely relocates computational burden toward constructing ever larger prompts,
which brings us to another limitation… }  


### Prompt Length Bottleneck  

Standard token limits hover near four thousand words for most commercial GPT series models ~\citet{liu2023lost}. Encoding dozens—or hundreds—of column/value pairs quickly exhausts this capacity \emph{i.e.,} \(d \times (\text{name}+ \text{textual value}) \approx O(d)\). When this limit is breached many existing works resort either visual truncation (“keep first 50 columns”) or hierarchical chunking strategies whose efficacy remains anecdotal.\footnote{
Empirical results documented in Table 1 illustrate that vanilla in–context learning fails outright once datasets like *Communities* (over 100 attributes) exceed manageable prompt lengths.~} This phenomenon explains why several strong baselines—including plain prompting (\textbf{In\-Context}) and refined versions (\textbf{TABLET})—“failed” on high-dimensional tables reported in Section&nbsp;4’s experimental analysis.



### Interaction Between Limitations  

The three constraints reinforce one another: reducing token count typically involves discarding columns deemed nonessential according to naive heuristics,\ which amplifies reliance upon precise per-sample reasoning thus worsening latency budgets because more sophisticated prompting becomes necessary.\footnote{
FEATLLM mitigates this feedback loop partly via *feature bagging*, whereby a randomly chosen subset \(\mathcal S \subseteq \bigl[ d\bigr]\) feeds into each independent prompt; ensembles then aggregate diverse weak learners—as highlighted in Section&nbsp;5’s ablation study.~} Nonetheless these drawbacks remain intrinsic obstacles shared across all previous language-model mediated tabular frameworks,\ motivating research efforts seeking **feature engineering without perpetual reliance on costly forward calls**, precisely what FEATLLM proposes.


--- 

Through this survey we see how classical algorithms bring computational thrift yet lack domain awareness; self­supervised pretraining injects generic statistical priors but struggles when confronted solely with minuscule labels; semi­supervised augmentation enriches representations at extra implementation cost; finally large language models add rich encyclopedic cues—but suffer from expensive per-instance queries, inaccessible fine tuning pathways, and strict prompt budget caps. Understanding these tradeoffs equips researchers—and future practitioners—to select appropriate tools according both to resource constraints and desired interpretability before embracing newer hybrid designs such as FEATLLM which aim explicitly at turning “…the capability …into feature generation” rather than maintaining end-to-end black-box inference ~\citet{sungwon-han2024feattllm}.
