\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Stabilizing Sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts Models with GSPO – No More Routing Replay  

*How a simple shift from token‑level to sequence‑level importance sampling removes the exotic “Routing Replay” trick and makes large sparse LLMs train reliably.*  

---  

## 1️⃣ Why Reinforcement Learning Matters for LLMs  

Large language models (LLMs) already master next‑token prediction after massive pre‑training, but many high‑level abilities—complex reasoning, safe instruction following, precise code generation—still benefit from **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) or other reward–based fine­tuning schemes.  
In practice this means treating the LLM as a policy $\pi_\theta$ and adjusting its parameters so that generated responses obtain higher scores from a verifier $r(x,y)\in[0,1]$.  

The standard workhorse for this job is **Proximal Policy Optimization** (PPO). PPO constrains each update by clipping an *importance weight* that measures how much the new policy deviates from the behavior policy used to generate the data:

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}.
\]

When applied directly to LLMs this approach becomes costly because estimating a reliable value function often requires another model roughly as big as $\pi_\theta$.  

Enter **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (**GRPO**) — a clever work‑around that replaces value estimates by *relative advantages* computed across a small “group” of responses sampled for each query $x$:

\[
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(r(x,y_1),\dots,r(x,y_G)\bigr)}%
{\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(r(x,y_1),\dots,r(x,y_G)\bigr)} .
\]

GRPO keeps the PPO‐style surrogate loss but plugs $\widehat A_i$ into every token’s contribution while still using the tokenwise importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$.  

The idea works well enough for dense transformers—but once we move into **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** territory it starts cracking.



## 2️⃣ Token‐Level Importance Sampling Collides with MoE Volatility  

A sparse MoE layer contains dozens or hundreds of “experts” that are switched on/off by a learned router at each token position. This design gives us a massive parameter count without blowing up FLOPs—exactly what modern LLMs want—but it also introduces **expert‐activation volatility**:

* After one gradient step the router may assign an entirely different set of experts to the *same* token.
* In our experiments on a Qwen3‑30B‐A3B‐Base model we observed roughly **10 %** of activated experts changing between $\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$ even when processing an identical rollout sample.

Why does this matter? The importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ compares **two likelihoods** computed by *different networks* when the routing differs:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\dfrac{\displaystyle \underbrace{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}_{\text{new router}}}
     {\displaystyle \underbrace{\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}_{\text{old router}}}.
\]

Even tiny changes in expert choice can swing $w_{i,t}$ dramatically, inflating variance and corrupting the surrogate loss used by GRPO. Because PPO’s clipping only prunes *extreme* ratios rather than fixing their bias, this high‐variance signal can accumulate over long sequences and ultimately cause **catastrophic model collapse**—the kind where further training cannot recover even after restoring checkpoints.


### TL;DR  
*Tokenwise importance sampling assumes a smooth change between policies.*  
MoE’s routers break that assumption ⇒ unstable gradients ⇒ collapsed models.



## 3️⃣ Routing Replay – A Heavyweight Bandage  

Researchers discovered a pragmatic fix called **Routing Replay**:

1️⃣ *Cache* every routing decision made by $\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}$ during rollout generation (which expert(s) were selected for each token).  
2️⃣ When evaluating $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$, force it through *the same cached experts* instead of letting its own router run freely.

Effectively this forces both numerator and denominator in $w_{i,t}$ to share an identical network backbone, restoring smoothness in the importance weight calculation.

While **Routing Replay stabilizes GRPO**, it carries hefty drawbacks:

* **Extra memory** – you must store per‑token routing indices for every rollout batch.
* **Communication overhead** – distributed setups have to broadcast these indices alongside activations.
* **Capacity throttling** – forcing a frozen routing pattern prevents the policy from exploring potentially better expert assignments during learning.
* **Implementation complexity** – extra forward passes or custom kernels are needed, making the whole RL pipeline harder to maintain.

In short, it works **by sidestepping** the fundamental mismatch rather than solving it.

## 4️⃣ Enter Group Sequence Policy Optimization (**GSPO**)  

The key insight behind GS PO is deceptively simple: **align the granularity of off‑policy correction with that of the reward**.  
The reward $r(x,y)$ lives at *sequence* level — you give one score per generated answer — yet GR PO corrects at *token* level where no feedback exists individually.


### 4️⃣1️⃣ Sequence‐Level Importance Ratio  

Define an *entire response*’s likelihood under any policy:

\[
\pi_\theta(y\,|\,x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\!\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}),
\qquad 
|y|\!=\!\text{# tokens}.
\]

Then build a **length‑normalized** importance weight:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
\Bigl(\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_i\,|\,x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\Bigl[\frac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_{\!\!\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   \Bigr].
\]

Because we raise the raw likelihood ratio to \(1/|y|\), \(s_i(\theta)\) stays within a tight numerical range regardless of sequence length—a crucial practical detail.


### 4️⃣2️⃣ Group Advantage Normalisation  

Just like GR PO we keep our reference frame local within a batch group:
 
\[
\widehat A_i=
\dfrac{
   r(x,y_i)-%
   \operatorname{mean}\bigl(r(x,y_1),...,r(x,y_G)\bigr)
 }{
   \operatorname{std}\bigl(r(x,y_1),...,r(x,y_G)\bigr)
 }.
\]

All tokens belonging to response \(y_i\) share this single scalar advantage.


### 4️⃣3️⃣ The Surrogate Objective  

Combine everything exactly as PPO does—but now **the clipping operates on whole responses**:

\[
%
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\;\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
      \Bigg[
        \frac1G
        \sum_{i=1}^{G}
        \min
        \bigl(
           s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,
           \,
           \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_i
        \bigr)
      \Bigg].
%
}
\tag{GSPO}
\]

Empirically we set an extremely tight clipping window (\(\varepsilon^{-}=3\times10^{-4},~ \varepsilon^{+}=4\times10^{-4}\)), reflecting how much \(s_i(\cdot)\) actually moves even after many updates.


### 4️⃣4️⃣ Gradient Intuition  

If clipping is ignored, differentiating w.r.t.\,$\theta$ yields

```math
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPE}}
 = 
\E[\;\tfrac1G \sum_i s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i 
      \cdot\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)]
```

and expanding $\log s_i$ brings back an *average over all token log–likelihood gradients*, weighted uniformly across positions rather than by their noisy individual ratios:

```math
= 
\E[\;\frac1G
   \sum_i
   s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i 
   \cdot\tfrac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_t\nabla_\theta 
               \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x , y_{i,<t})
 ] .
```

Contrast this with GR ​\(P O's\$) gradient where every token is scaled by its own \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\); those scalars fluctuate wildly when expert routing changes—in effect adding noise directly into the gradient vector.


### 4️⃣5️⃣ Quick Pseudocode Sketch  

```python
# Assume we already have G responses {y[i]} per query x sampled from π_old.
for x in batch:
    # Step 1: get rewards r[i] = verifier(x, y[i])
    adv = normalize_advantages([r[i] for i in range(G)])   # -> wide_hat_A[i]

    # Step 2: compute seq logprob under both policies
    #      logp_old[i] = sum_t log π_old(y[i][t] | … )
    #      logp_new[i] = sum_t log π_new(y[i][t] | … )
    s = torch.exp((logp_new - logp_old) / length(y[i]))

    # Step 3: clip & surrogate loss
    clip_low = torch.clamp(s, max=1+eps_pos)
    clip_high = torch.clamp(s, min=1-eps_neg)
    loss = -torch.mean(torch.min(s * adv,
                                 clip_low * adv))
    loss.backward()
```

Only two forward passes are needed (or even one if you pull `logp_old` from an inference engine), **no cached routing maps**, no extra buffers.

## 5️⃣ Stability Without Routing Replay  

Because \(s_i(\cdot)\) aggregates *all* token probabilities before scaling any gradient, it tolerates occasional shifts in which experts handle particular tokens—the aggregate likelihood hardly moves when only a few routers change their mind → the variance stays low.


### Empirical Highlights  

| Setting | Algorithm | Need Routing Replay? | Clipping Fraction (tokens) | Observed Stability |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|----------------------------|--------------------|
| MoE LLM (Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base) | GR ​\(P O^\*\) | Yes | ≈ 20 % of tokens clipped → still noisy | Frequent collapse unless replay applied |
| Same model | **GS​\(P O)** | **No** | ≈ 0.2 % of tokens clipped → yet higher sample efficiency | Smooth reward curves & continuous performance gains |

*(The fractions differ by roughly two orders of magnitude.)*

Despite clipping many fewer tokens overall—a situation one would normally expect to waste data—the **sequence–level surrogate yields cleaner gradients**, so every remaining sample carries more information than before.



#### Benchmarks Under Equal Compute  

Training curves measured on three downstream tasks—all popular indicators for reasoning / coding ability—

* **AIME‘24** (Pass@1 over 32 samples),
* **LiveCodeBench** (Pass@1 over 8 samples),
* **Codeforces Elo rating**,  

showed that GS​\(P O)—using exactly the same number of rollout queries and GPU hours as GR ​\(P O)—outperformed it consistently across all three metrics while never encountering collapse events.

## 6️⃣ Bonus Wins: Simpler Infrastructure & Faster Turnaround  

Because GS​\(P O only needs *sequence−level* likelihood values,

* You can reuse logits directly returned by an inference engine (`vLLM`, `SGLang`…) instead of recomputing them on a heavyweight training backend (`Megatron`). This eliminates an entire “replay” pass through the model.
* Precision mismatches between training‐time FP16 kernels and inference FP32 kernels become tolerable—the final scalar \(s_i(\cdot)\) averages out small numerical differences.
* Multi–turn or partial rollout schemes no longer require special handling—the same surrogate applies whenever you have a full response score.



#### When Token-Wise Control Still Matters  

For dialogue systems where you’d like different parts of an answer judged differently (e.g., safety at early turns versus usefulness later), GS​\(P O offers a variant called **GS​\(P O-token)**:
it keeps \(s_i(\cdot)\) as a global scaling factor but lets you plug distinct advantages $\widehat A_{i,t}$ per token while preserving exactly the same gradient structure thanks to `stop_gradient` tricks (`sg`). In practice you rarely need this—sequence-level alone already solves the most painful stability problem.

## 7️⃣ Takeaways & Outlook  

* **Problem diagnosed:** Tokenwise importance weights clash with volatile MoE routing → noisy gradients → model collapse.
* **Bandage:** Routing Replay saved GR ​\(P O by freezing routers during weight computation—but added memory/comms costs.
* **Solution:** Shift correction up to *whole sequences*, define a clean likelihood ratio $s$, clip at sentence level, and keep advantage normalisation inside each group.
* **Result:** Stable Reinforcement Learning on massive sparse experts without any replay trick; higher sample efficiency despite more aggressive clipping; easier engineering stack compatible with inference engines.
* **Implication:** As language models continue scaling—with ever larger MoE families—the simplicity of GS​\(P O positions it as a solid base for future “RL at scale” pipelines (\& perhaps even beyond LLMs).

The success story behind Qwen 3’s latest version proves that sometimes fixing an algorithm isn’t about adding more machinery—it’s about matching representations correctly (`reward ↔ importance`). By letting sequences speak their own truth usurps per-token jitter entirely—and opens up smoother paths toward ever larger—and more capable—language models.
