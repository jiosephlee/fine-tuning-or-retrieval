### Why does Orthogonal Finetuning preserve pairwise angles between neurons?
Question:
In Section 3 the paper states that OFT applies an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\) to a pretrained weight matrix so that "pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved". I understand that an orthogonal transformation preserves inner products, but I am confused about how this translates into preserving angles between *columns* of \(\mathbf W^0\) (i.e., neurons). Does this hold for any orthogonal \(\mathbf R\) applied on the left side only? And how does this angle preservation relate to retaining semantic knowledge from pretraining?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the pretrained linear layer is written as  

$$
z=(W^{0})^{\,\top}x ,\
      W^{0}\in\rule[-1mm]{1pt}{3mm}\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
$$  

so each **neuron** (i.e. each output coordinate) is associated with a  
column vector  

$$
w_{i}^{0}=W^{0}_{:,i}\in\rule[-1mm]{1pt}{3mm}\mathbb{R}^{d}.
$$  

OFT replaces the weight matrix by  

$$
W=RW^{0},\
      R\rule[-1mm]{1pt}{3mm}\in\rule[-1mm]{1pt}{3mm}\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\
      RR^{\,\top}=I,
$$  

and therefore  

$$
z=(RW^{0})^{\,\top}x .
$$  

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why do the column‑wise angles stay unchanged?

For any two vectors $u,v\nin\rule[-1mm]{1pt}{3mm}\mathbb{R}^{d}$,

$$
(Ru)^{\,\top}(Rv)=u\nitenerrightarrowzero?? 
$$

because $R^{-1}=R^\transpose$. Thus $(RU)(RV)$ preserves inner products.
Since an orthogonal matrix also preserves Euclidean norm,
$\lVert Ru\rVert=\lVert u\rVert$, the cosine of the angle between $u$
and $v$

$$
\vphantom{\Big|}
    \cos \theta_{u,v}
      = {\displaystyle 
         {\frac{ u^\transpose v }
                {\lVert u\rVert\,\<space>\lVert v\rVert}} ,
$$

is unchanged,

%
$$
\vphantom{\Big|}
    \cos \theta_{Ru,Rv}
      =
     {\displaystyle 
       {\frac{
            (Ru)^\transpose(Rv)
          }{
            \lVert Ru\rVert\,\<space>\lVert Rv\rVert}}
       }
     =
     {\displaystyle 
        {\frac{
             u^\transpose v }
          {
             \lVert u\rVert\,\<space>\lVert v\rVert}}
     }
     = 
     \cos \theta_{u,v}.
$$

Applying this fact to the columns of $W^ {  00 }$ gives

$$
w_i'=Rw_i^ { 00 },\
    w_j'=Rw_j^ { 00 }\;
\xRightarrow{\text{orthogonality}}
    \
    \cos(\theta_{w_i',w_j'})
    =
    \
    \cos(\theta_{w_i^ {00},w_j^ {00}}).
$$

Thus **any** orthogonal matrix multiplied on the *left* of a weight 
matrix leaves all pair‑wise angles between its columns exactly 
the same. If one were to multiply on the right ($WQ,\ Q∈ O(n)$)
this property would generally fail because each column would become 
a linear combination of multiple original columns, changing their mutual 
inner products.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Connection with preserving semantic knowledge

During pretraining each column $w_i^ {00 }$ typically aligns with a 
semantic direction in the input space—for example one column may act 
as a “cat detector”, another as a “dog detector”. Many downstream 
behaviours of a deep network depend **only on the relative geometry**
of these directions:

* Cosine similarities $w_i ^ {00\,{\!\transpose}} x/
      (\lVert w_i ^ {00 }\rVert\: \<space>\lVert x\rVert)$ are used 
      directly by attention or classification heads.
* Subsequent layers treat their inputs through further linear maps;
  those maps care about *how* different directions relate rather than 
  their absolute coordinates.
* The singular‑value spectrum of $W^ {00 }$ encodes its Lipschitz constant;
  an orthogonal left‑multiplication leaves every singular value untouched,
  keeping the spectral norm—and hence training stability—identical   
  to the pretrained model.

Because OFT guarantees that every cosine similarity among the **neurons**
is exactly preserved, the relational structure learned during pretraining 
remains intact after finetuning. In other words, the “semantic map” stored 
in $W^ {00 }$ is merely rotated as a whole inside $\mathbb R ^ d$; rotating a 
constellation does not alter which points are close or far from one another.
Consequently:

* The model retains its ability to recognise previously learned concepts,
  reducing catastrophic forgetting.
* Down‑stream adapters or classification heads can specialise without having 
  to remodel an already‑distorted feature space.
* Training remains well‑conditioned because neither norms nor singular values 
  are perturbed, which matches observations that OFT often converges more 
  stably than additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

The statement in Section 3 follows directly from two elementary facts about 
orthogonal matrices:

1. **Inner‑product preservation:** $(Ru)^\transpose(Rv)=u^\transpose v.$  
2. **Norm preservation:** $\lVert Ru\rVert=\lVert u\rVert.$  

Applied uniformly on the left side of a weight matrix,
these facts imply that every column vector—the neuron’s weight—
undergoes exactly the same rotation, so all pair‑wise cosines (hence all   
angles) stay unchanged regardless of which orthogonal matrix $R∈ O(d)$   
is chosen. By keeping this geometric relationship intact, OFT safeguards   
the relational information encoded during pretraining, allowing downstream   
finetuning to adapt efficiently while retaining the original semantic   
knowledge.

Answer:
**Answer**

The Cayley transform is used in OFT/BOFT as a *retraction* from the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(d)$ onto the Stiefel manifold $\mathrm O(d)$.  
In plain terms it says “let us move freely in the space of skew‑symmetric matrices,
and whenever we need an actual orthogonal matrix we map the current point back
with a closed‑form formula”.  
Because this mapping is applied **after every gradient step**, the weight
matrix that actually multiplies a pretrained layer is guaranteed to stay exactly
orthogonal – provided two conditions hold:

* the parameter matrix $\mathbf Q$ remains perfectly skew‑symmetric;
* the matrix $\mathbf I-\mathbf Q$ stays invertible (i.e. it does not become singular).

Below we spell out why these conditions are satisfied in practice and why they
imply exact orthogonality.

### Why gradients on $\boldsymbol{Q}$ preserve this property

During training one does **not** update $\boldsymbol R$
directly.
Instead a set of independent scalar variables parametrises only the upper
triangular part of $\boldsymbol Q$.  
At every forward pass the full skew‑symmetric matrix is reconstructed,
the Cayley map is applied and the resulting $\boldsymbol R$
multiplies the frozen pretrained weight matrix.
In back‑propagation autograd first computes derivatives w.r.t.
the entries of $\boldsymbol R$, then propagates them through the closed‑form map;
the update therefore modifies only those independent scalars that appear
in $\boldsymbol Q$.  

Because these scalars are added/subtracted symmetrically,
the reconstructed $\boldsymbol Q$ stays exactly skew‑symmetric up to floating‑point
round‑off error – an error that never destroys sign symmetry again before the next
reconstruction.
Consequently **after each optimizer step** we obtain a fresh pair  
$(\,\boldsymbol,Q ,\,F )$, where $F=( {E})(… )$
and hence remains perfectly orthogonal **by construction**.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

### What would happen if $\boldsymbol {·⋯}$ becomes singular

The denominator ``$(\,E−F)=({F−G})'' could become singular only if  
$\boldsymbol F$ had an eigenvalue equal to $+\,1$.  
For a *real* skew‑symmetric matrix all non‑zero eigenvalues lie on the pure–
imaginary axis ($±iλ$, $λ∈ℝ$); therefore none satisfies $λ=+ 1$.  
Consequently **in exact arithmetic** $(E−F)$ is never singular – its spectrum lies entirely off $+ 1$, whose modulus is at least $| E |\geqslant 0.$  

In finite precision arithmetic two practical issues may arise:

* **Ill conditioning** – when entries of $\boldsymbol F$ grow very large,
the factors $(E±F)$ become poorly conditioned; solving the linear system  
instead of forming an explicit inverse mitigates overflow/NaNs.
Implementations therefore evaluate  

```python
   R = torch.linalg.solve(I - F.T , I + F)
```  

or add a tiny jitter e.g. ``$(E-F)+ϵE’’’’'' before inversion.
* **Large learning rates** – an excessively aggressive update can push some
entries of $\boldsymbol F$ far enough that round‑off makes ``det(E-F)’’
effectively zero, causing gradients explode.
The cure employed in OFT/BOFT is simply a modest learning rate together with optional norm–clipping on $\boldsymbol F$ (or penalising its Frobenius norm), exactly as one would do when optimising on any manifold.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Connection with the butterfly factorisation used in BOFT

In BOFT each sparse block–matrix factor is itself parametrised by a tiny    
skew‑symmetric sub–matrix and transformed through its own Cayley map.
Because every factor belongs to ${O(d)}$, their product also belongs to ${O(d)}$
(the set of orthogonal matrices forms a group under multiplication).
Hence even after many factors – e.g.  

$$   
   {\cal R}=∏_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\cal B}_{(d,i)}
$$   

—the overall matrix stays exactly orthogonal throughout training,
and all arguments above apply unchanged at block level.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take‑away

* The Cayley transform maps any **skew‑symmetric** matrix continuously onto an
orthogonal matrix; the proof rests only on the simple observation that 
$(I±Q)$ are transposes of one another.
* Gradient descent works on the unconstrained *tangent space*
$\mathfrak {so}(d)$; after each step we recompute $\boldsymbol R$ via the closed form,
so exact orthogonality holds automatically.
* The denominator becomes singular only for impossible eigenvalues;
numerical pathologies can be avoided by stable solves or tiny regularisers,
and practical implementations adopt modest learning rates or norm clipping.



Thus the Cayley parameterisation employed in OFT/BOFT guarantees that **the model’s learned transformation remains perfectly orthogonal at every training iteration**, while keeping updates simple and inexpensive.

### How do butterfly graphs achieve dense connectivity with only \(O(d \log d)\) edges?
Question:
The paper claims that a butterfly topology can connect every source node to every destination node using only \(O(d \log d)\) non‑zero entries (edges), compared to \(O(d^2)\) for a dense matrix. I am having trouble visualising this edge count. For instance, with \(d=8") how many edges are there in each butterfly component, how many components are needed (i.e., value of \(m")), and how do those numbers add up to roughly \(8 \log_2 8 = 24")?
Answer:
**Answer**

A “butterfly graph’’ is nothing more than the wiring diagram that appears when you write the FFT‑style factorisation  

$$
\mathbf R=\mathbf B_{m}\mathbf B_{m-1}\dots \mathbf B_{1},
\qquad 
\mathbf B_{i}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
$$

where every factor $\,\mathbf B_i$ is a very sparse orthogonal matrix.  
If we view a matrix as a bipartite graph – rows on the left side, columns on the right side – then a non‑zero entry $\,(\mathbf B_i)_{pq}\neq0$ corresponds to a **directed edge** from column‑node $q$ at level $i{-}1$ to row‑node $p$ at level $i$.  

The crucial observation used in the paper is that you can obtain **dense** overall connectivity (every source node can reach every sink node after multiplying all factors) while keeping the total number of such edges only  

$$
\underbrace{d}_{\text{edges per factor}}\times 
\underbrace{\log _{k} d}_{\text{number of factors}}
=
O(d\,\log d).
$$

Below we work out the numbers for the concrete case $d=8$.

---

### How many factors do we need?  

The construction assumes that $d$ is a power of two,
$d=2^{N}$.  
One factor is introduced for each power‑of‑two block size

$$
k=d,\; k=\tfrac{d}{2},\, k=\tfrac{d}{4},\, \dots ,\, k=2 .
$$

Hence **$m=N=\log _{2} d$** factors are required.  
For $d=8 (=2^{3})$

| factor index $i$ | block size $k_i$ | name in the paper |
|------------------|------------------|--------------------|
| 1                | $k_1=d=8$        | $\widetilde{\mathbf B}(8,8)$ |
| 2                | $k_2=d/2=4$      | $\widetilde{\mathbf B}(8,4)$ |
| 3                | $k_3=d/4=2$      | $\widetilde{\mathbf B}(8,2)$ |

So **$m=\log _{2} 8 = 3$** butterfly components.



### How many edges does one component contain?

A single **butterfly factor** $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$,

$$
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
 \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
 \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
        \qquad \mathbf d_i\,{\in}\,\mathbb R^{\,k/2},
$$

has exactly $k/2+k/2+k/2+k/2=k$ non‑zero entries – one per row and one per column.  
A **butterfly component**

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
   \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\,…,\, \mathbf B^{F}_{\,d/k}(k)\bigr)
   \in {\mathbb R}^{\,d\times d}
$$

places $d/k$ copies of this factor on its diagonal. Consequently it also contains  

$$
(d/k)\times k = d
$$

non‑zero entries.  
In graph terms there are exactly **$d** directed edges from level $i{-}1$ to level $i$.  

For our example ($d=8$) every one of the three components carries **8** edges.



### Total edge count for $d=8$

Putting everything together,

* number of levels = $m+1 = \log _{2}8 + 1 = 4,$  
* number of edges between any two successive levels = $d = 8.$  

Hence

$$
|\text{edges}| = m\times d = (\log _{2}8)\times 8
                     = 3\times 8
                     = 24 .
$$

These twenty‑four directed connections are enough to make the whole product matrix dense. You can check it by following the classic FFT “butterfly’’ pattern (the pairing rule “connect node $j$ with node $j⊕dist_i$’’ where $dist_i = 1,\; 2,\;4,\dots)$:

* **Stage 1 ($k=1$, pairings):** $0↔1,\; 2↔3,\; 4↔5,\; 6↔7.$  
* **Stage 2 ($k=2$, pairings):** $0↔2,\; 1↔3,\; 4↔6,\; 5↔7.$  
* **Stage 3 ($k=4$, pairings):** $0↔4,\; 1↔5,\; 2↔6,\; 3↔7.$  

After three hops any source index can be turned into any destination index, exactly what “dense connectivity’’ means.



### Why this counts as ***O*\,$(d\,·\,\log d)$***

In general when $d=2^{N}$,

* there are **$N=\log _{2} d$** factors,
* each factor contributes exactly $d$ trainable non‑zero entries,

so the total number of trainable parameters is  

$$
|\text{trainable}| = d·N = d·\log _{2} d ,
$$

which grows proportionally to $O(d\,·\,\log d)$.

If you use a block size larger than two ($b > 2$ in the paper), each entry inside a block becomes a $b\times b$ sub–matrix. Then every factor contributes $(b−1)/₂· d$ trainable scalars instead of $d$. The asymptotic dependence on $d$ stays $O(d· log d)$ because $b$ is fixed and independent of $d$.



### TL DR for `$d ＝ ８`

* **Butterfly components (`m`)**: $\log _{₂}8 = 3$.  
* **Edges per component**: always `$d ＝ ８`.  
* **Total edges / trainable values**: $3 × ８ = 24$, which equals `$８··  log₂⁡８`.  

Thus a butterfly topology delivers full source‑to‑destination mixing while using only *O$(d · log d)$* non‑zero elements instead of *O$(d²)$* – precisely what the paper claims.

### Why ordinary Dropout fails

Standard Dropout works by element‑wise masking  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf R}= \mathbf M\odot \mathbf R,
$$

where $\displaystyle\M$ contains independent Bernoulli variables and “$\odot$” denotes element‑wise multiplication.  
If we replace the true update by $\displaystyle~\tilde {\bf R}$ in a forward pass, the resulting matrix is almost never orthogonal:

* **Loss of unit columns:** Orthogonality requires every column vector of $\displaystyle~ {\bf R}$ to have unit norm and to be mutually perpendicular. Zeroing arbitrary entries changes column norms and introduces correlations, so generally  
  $$%
  \tilde {\bf R}^{\,!\top}\,\tilde {\bf R}\neq {\bf I}_ { d}.%
  $$

* **Rank deficiency:** Even a single zero in a row/column can make the factor singular. A singular factor destroys invertibility, which means that subsequent back‑propagation may encounter exploding or vanishing gradients because the product no longer has all singular values equal to one.

* **Breaking the parameterisation:** In BOFT each factor $\displaystyle~ {\bf \tilde B}{^{\, b}} _ {( d , i )}$ is obtained from a *skew–symmetric* generator ${\bf Q}_i$ via Cayley transform,  

$$
\tilde {\bf B}^{\, b } _ {( d , i )}=({\bf I}+ {\bf Q}_i)\bigl({\bf I}- {\bf Q}_ i\bigr)^{-1}.
$$  

Masking individual entries does not correspond to any choice of ${\hat {\bf Q}}_i$, i.e., there exists no skew–symmetric ${\hat {\bf Q}}_i$ such that ${(\hat {\bf Q}_i)}$ produces exactly ${\tilde {\bf B}} ^ { b } _ {( d , i )}$ with some entries forced to zero. Hence the optimisation would have to continually project a non‑orthogonal masked matrix back onto the manifold – an operation far more expensive than simply applying standard Dropout in additive settings like LoRA.

Because BOFT’s regulariser must stay *on* the Stiefel manifold ${\cal O}( d )$, naïve element‑wise Dropout violates this manifold constraint and therefore cannot be used directly.

### How does block size $b$ affect the trade‑off between expressivity and parameter efficiency in BOFT?
Question:
Figure 3 shows that increasing $b$ reduces parameter count but seems to worsen approximation error when trying to mimic a random dense orthogonal matrix. Conversely, smaller $b$ with larger $m$ gives better approximation with fewer parameters overall. Could someone clarify intuitively why larger blocks make the model less expressive despite having fewer components, and how one should choose $(m,b)$ for a given layer dimension $d$?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the orthogonal matrix that finetunes a layer is written as  

$$
  \mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
  \qquad 
  \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
$$

where every factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is a **block‑butterfly component** whose non‑zero pattern consists of $d/b$ blocks of size $b\times b$. Because each component is orthogonal its trainable degrees of freedom are only the $\tfrac12(b-1)d$ entries inside the $b\times b$ rotation matrices; the whole network therefore has  

$$
 P(m,b)=\frac{(b-1)}{2}\;d\,m
   \tag{1}
$$

effective trainable parameters.

---

### Why a larger block makes the model less expressive

The butterfly construction can be interpreted as an **information‑transmission graph** on a grid of $d$ source nodes and $m+1$ levels (Fig.\,3 of the paper). Each factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ contributes exactly $d$ directed edges between two adjacent levels – one edge per row – because orthogonality forces every row to contain exactly one non‑zero entry in each column pairing. The *pattern* of those edges is determined by the block size $b$.

When $b=2$, every factor connects each node only to its immediate neighbour in a **pairwise** fashion; after $\log_{2}d$ stages every source can reach every sink through many distinct routes. This yields a **dense connectivity** on the grid with only $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ edges – exactly what a full orthogonal matrix needs – while keeping each stage extremely local. Because paths can be recombined arbitrarily across stages, the reachable set of matrices is very close to the whole orthogonal group. Empirically this shows up as a low approximation error even with few parameters ($P\simeq\tfrac12 d\log d$).

If we increase the block size to $b>2$, each factor now mixes groups of $b$ dimensions at once rather than pairs. The induced graph still has one edge per node per level, but now those edges are “bundled’’ inside larger blocks, so there are **fewer possible permutations** between successive levels. In graph‑theoretic terms, the number of *edge‑disjoint* paths between any source–sink pair shrinks dramatically when blocks become large: many source nodes remain confined inside their original $b$‑sized group throughout all stages because subsequent butterfly components merely permute whole groups instead of interleaving them elementwise. Consequently the set of reachable orthogonal matrices collapses onto a structured subset that is essentially **block‑diagonal up to a limited set of permutations**. This loss of mixing ability translates directly into poorer ability to approximate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix, which explains why Fig.\,3 shows higher reconstruction error for larger~$b$.

Notice also that increasing~$b$ reduces the required depth~$m$: dense connectivity can already be obtained after roughly $\log_{2}(2d/b)$ stages because each stage halves the “granularity’’ at which information spreads. Fewer stages mean fewer opportunities for reshuffling information, which compounds the expressivity loss described above.

Hence there are two intertwined effects:

* **Fewer degrees of freedom per stage** – a rotation in an isolated $b\times b$ subspace has only $\tfrac12(b-1)b=\tfrac12 b(b-1)$ free angles instead of $(c)(c-1)/2$, so each factor contributes fewer independent parameters relative to its size;
* **Reduction in effective mixing depth**, which limits how many distinct paths connect inputs and outputs;
* Together they make large~$b$ models cheaper in parameters but poorer in expressive power.

---

### Choosing $(m,b)$ for a given hidden dimension $d$

A practical recipe follows directly from Eq.~(1) together with the “dense‑connectivity’’ requirement

$$
   m \;\geqslant\;
   \bigl\lceil\,\log_{2}\!\bigl(\tfrac{2d}{\,b}\Bigr)\,\big\rceil,
   \tag{2}
$$

which guarantees that every source node can reach every sink node once all components are multiplied.

Given a budget $P_{\max}$, one can simply search over powers‐of‐two values $b\in\{2,4,8,\dots ,d\}$:

1️⃣ Compute the minimal depth  
   $$ m_{\min}= \bigl\lceil\,\log_{2}\!\bigl(\tfrac{2d}{\,b}\Bigr)\,\big\rceil,$$
   which gives exactly what is needed for dense connectivity.

2️⃣ Evaluate the associated parameter count through Eq.~(1):
   $$ P(b)= ((\,b-1)/2)\; d\, m_{\min}. $$

3️⃣ Keep all candidates satisfying $P(b)\le P_{\max}$.  
   Among them prefer **smaller~$b$**, because it yields larger depth and consequently richer mixing.  
   If runtime or memory constraints forbid many matrix multiplications, pick the largest admissible~$b$ instead.

In most transformer or vision models ($d\simeq1024{-}2048$), experiments reported in Section 5 show that **$b∈[4,8]** strikes an excellent balance: it needs only about six–eight butterfly factors ($m≈6{-}8$), uses roughly three–four thousand trainable numbers per layer—well below LoRA’s low‐rank budgets—and still achieves approximation errors comparable to those obtained with full OFT ($m=1$, $b=d)$).

If ultra‐tight parameter budgets are required (e.g.\ <0.05 % of all model weights), setting **$b=2$, $m≈⌈\log_{2}d⌉** gives the smallest possible overhead ($≈½  d  log_{2} d.$). The extra runtime from multiple multiplications is modest because each factor is extremely sparse (only two nonzero entries per column), so training speed stays comparable to standard OFT.

Conversely, if one wishes to avoid any noticeable additional forward passes during finetuning—perhaps on very large models—choosing a moderate block such as **$b=16$, $m≈⌈log_{₂}(128)⌉≈7** reduces multiplication steps at modest cost in approximation quality while still staying well under an OFT’s quadratic parameter count.

---

### Summary

Increasing the block size~$b$ shrinks both **the number of butterfly stages** and **the granularity at which dimensions are mixed**, which in turn lowers the number of edge‑disjoint paths linking inputs to outputs in the information‑transmission graph underlying BOFT. Fewer paths mean a smaller reachable subset of orthogonal matrices and therefore higher reconstruction error when trying to imitate an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix.  

The total trainable cost grows as $\tfrac{(b-1)}{2}\,dm,$ while dense connectivity imposes  

$$
   m 
      \geqslant 
      \Bigl\lceil 
          \log_{2}\!\Bigl(\frac{2\,d}{\, b}\Bigr)
      \Bigr\rceil .
$$ 

These simple relations guide practitioners toward principled choices of $(m,b)$ that respect parameter budgets while preserving most of BOFT’s expressive power.
