Foundations of Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

# Diffusion Processes and Denoising Score Matching  

Large‑scale text‑to‑image generation relies fundamentally on *diffusion probabilistic models*.  At a high level, a diffusion model defines two stochastic processes over an image variable $\mathbf{x}$ :  

* A **forward process** that gradually corrupts a clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$ by adding Gaussian noise over $T$ discrete time steps,
  $$q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\,\beta_t\mathbf{I}\bigr), \qquad t=1,\dots,T,$$  
  where $\beta_t \in(0,1)$ denotes a variance schedule controlling how much noise is injected at step $t$.  After many steps the corrupted image $\mathbf{x}_T$ becomes essentially isotropic Gaussian noise.
* A **reverse process** parameterized by a neural network $\varepsilon_\theta$, which attempts to invert the forward corruption,
  $$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\,\mu_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t),\,\Sigma_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\bigr).$$  

Training consists of learning $\theta$ so that $$p_\theta$$ accurately predicts how to remove a small amount of noise at each step.  This learning objective can be expressed as *denoising score matching*: the network is trained to predict either the added Gaussian noise $\varepsilon$ or directly the gradient $\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}}\log q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_0)$ (the “score”) from which one recovers an estimate of $\mu_\theta$.  The seminal works on denoising score matching introduced this formulation for unconditional image synthesis \cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score,song2021denoising,dhariwal2021diffusion}.  

Intuitively, one can think of the reverse process as an iterative *denoiser*: starting from pure noise $\mathbf{x}_T$, repeatedly apply the learned denoiser conditioned on the current timestep $t$, gradually sharpening noisy patterns until an image $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ emerges.  Because every step removes only a modest amount of corruption, errors never accumulate catastrophically – this stability distinguishes diffusion sampling from single‐step generators such as GANs.\footnote{A “step” refers here to one iteration over $t$ during sampling; multiple sampling schedules exist (e.g., DDIM), all built upon this core iterative principle.}  

# Latent‑Space vs Pixel‑Space Diffusion  

Two practical design choices arise when placing a diffusion model inside an image generator:

* **Pixel‑space diffusion** operates directly on full‐resolution RGB arrays $\in[0,255]^{H\times W\times3}$.  Early large‐scale generators such as GLIDE \cite{nichol2022glide} and Imagen \cite{saharia2022photorealistic} adopt this choice because it avoids any lossy preprocessing step—every pixel receives its own gradient signal during training.\footnote{GLIDE further couples its diffusion prior with a jointly trained text encoder while Imagen keeps its textual embedder frozen.}  
* **Latent‑space diffusion** first compresses an image into a lower‐dimensional code using an autoencoder or vector quantizer, then runs diffusion over this compact representation before finally decoding back to pixels.  Stable Diffusion v1.\!5 \cite{rombach2022high} follows precisely this route: images are mapped into a discrete visual codebook learned by VQ‑GAN \cite{esser2021taming}, producing latent tensors $\mathbf{z}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times h\times w}$ whose dimensionality $d$ is far smaller than three times $HW$.  DALL·E 2 likewise learns a joint latent embedding between images and texts via CLIP \cite{radford2021learning}.  By confining denoising operations to this reduced space, training becomes dramatically cheaper both computationally and memorywise while retaining high fidelity after decoding.\footnote{The decoder part (VQ‑GAN or CLIP‑based generator) is kept fixed during most downstream adaptations so its quality remains constant across experiments.}

The trade‑off between these strategies can be phrased intuitively.  Pixel‐space offers maximal expressive power—every subtle color nuance lives explicitly in the state space—but incurs quadratic cost in image size during both forward/backward passes.  Latent‐space reduces cost quadratically while still preserving most semantic structure because the encoder has already captured texture, shape and layout information into compact feature vectors.\footnote{Empirically, large language–vision pretraining shows that semantic semantics survive strong compression when responsibility lies primarily with directions on hyperspheres—a theme revisited when discussing finetuning later.}

# Text Encoders and Cross‑Attention Mechanisms  

Textual conditioning turns an otherwise unconditional diffusion sampler into a *text‐to‐image* system.  The principal conduit between language tokens and visual features consists of **cross–attention** blocks placed inside the UNet backbone commonly used for denoising.\footnote{UNet architecture provides hierarchical spatial processing through downsampling/upsampling stages; cross–attention allows global textual context at each resolution level}.  

A *text encoder* converts an input prompt $c=\langle w_1,\dots,w_n\rangle$ into dense token embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ .  In many public systems these encoders are heavily pretrained vision–language transformers such as CLIP’s language tower \cite{radford2021learning}, although GLIDE uniquely trains its own encoder jointly with its pixel‐space prior past studies showed significant benefit from aligning language representation directly with visual generation objectives.\footnote{Joint training creates strong word–image alignment early on whereas freezing leverages large generic linguistic knowledge distilled elsewhere — both approaches appear across state-of-the-art systems}.  

Cross–attention proceeds similarly to self–attention except queries derive from visual feature maps while keys/values come from textual embeddings:
$$
\operatorname{Attn}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = 
\operatorname{softmax}\!\Bigl( \frac{\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}}{\sqrt d}\Bigr)\,\mathbf{V},
$$
where $\mathbf Q=\mathrm W_Q\,\phi_{\rm vis}$ gathers visual query vectors from intermediate feature map $\phi_{\rm vis}$,
and $(\mathbf K,\mathbf V)= (\mathrm W_K,\mathrm W_V)\,\phi_{\rm txt}$ are linear projections of token embeddings $\phi_{\rm txt}
= (\mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_n)$.  
Because attention coefficients are essentially cosine similarities between query direction $\hat{\mathbf q}$ and key direction $\hat{\mathbf k}$ after normalization,
the semantic interaction heavily depends upon *directions* rather than magnitudes—a fact emphasized later when discussing angle preservation during finetuning.\footnote{
Empirical observations suggest angular similarity encodes semantic relationships more faithfully than Euclidean distance across many vision–language tasks.
}

In practice modern generators contain several cross‑attention blocks spread across UNet layers so that textual cues affect coarse geometry early (low resolution) and fine texture late (high resolution).  This multi‑scale integration enables prompts such as “a photorealistic orange cat sitting on a marble floor” to influence both overall composition (cat pose) and local color patterns simultaneously.\footnote{
The systematic injection point matters when adapting downstream tasks —for example techniques that insert additional adapters typically target exactly these attention layers since they mediate modality mixing.
}

# Training Objectives: Reconstruction Loss and Classifier‑Free Guidance  

During pretraining each UNet receives three inputs at timestep $t$: 

* A noisy latent (or pixel) $\mathbf x_t$, 
* A conditional embedding derived from the prompt $c$, 
* The timestep scalar $t$ itself (often encoded sinusoidally).  

The network learns two intertwined objectives:

### Reconstruction Loss  

At every step it predicts either an additive noise token $\hat{\varepsilon}_\theta(\cdot)$ or directly reconstructs the clean latent $\hat{\mathbf x}_0$.  
A common choice mirrors ordinary least squares:
$$
L_{\rm rec}
= \mathbb E_{\,\mathbf x_0,\varepsilon,t}
   \Bigl[
     \bigl\| 
        \varepsilon - 
        \hat{\varepsilon}_\theta( \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf x_0+\sqrt {1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon , t,c )
     \bigr\|_2^{\,2}
   \Bigr],
$$
where $\varepsilon$ denotes ground‑truth Gaussian noise added during forward corruption.
Minimizing $L_{\rm rec}$ forces the network’s output at time $t$ to agree with known corruption direction,
hence teaching it how “to undo” exactly one infinitesimal noise step.
In downstream finetuning scenarios such as DreamBooth ~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} or our subsequent Orthogonal Finetuning framework,
the same reconstruction loss drives adaptation while preserving most pretrained capabilities through additional structural regularization.



### Classifier‑Free Guidance  

In pure conditional sampling one would simply feed both noisy latents **and** prompt embeddings into $p_\theta$.  
Classifier–free guidance augments this scheme by also training an *unconditional* version—i.e., making the network learn
$p_\theta(\cdot|\varnothing)$ alongside $p_\theta(\cdot|c)$.  
At inference time one computes a weighted combination:
$$
p^{(\gamma)}_\theta(\cdot|c)
    = p_\theta(\cdot|\varnothing)
      +\gamma\,\bigl(
            p_\theta(\cdot|c)-p_\theta(\cdot|\varnothing)
          \bigr),
$$
where coefficient $\gamma>0$ amplifies how strongly textual information steers sampling.
This procedure has become standard practice across public checkpoints—including GLIDE’s official release—to improve fidelity toward complex prompts without requiring external classifiers.
From an intuitive standpoint,
the unconditional branch supplies “generic image priors”, while scaling
the difference isolates purely semantic influence coming from words.\footnote{
When combined with finetuned orthogonal transformations,
classifier−free guidance retains its form because orthogonal updates preserve pairwise angles among weight vectors—the very quantities used when computing similarity scores inside attention.
}

Together these losses enforce accurate reconstruction under strong stochastic perturbation while allowing flexible emphasis on textual control during generation.



# Key Public Models: Stable Diffusion v 1.\!5, GLIDE, Imagen  

A concrete understanding emerges when examining three influential public checkpoints frequently used throughout recent literature:

### Stable Diffusion v 1.\!5  

Stable Diffusion adopts **latent‐space** generation : images are first encoded into VQ‑GAN codes,
after which UNet denoisers operate only on these compact representations.
Its text encoder is frozen CLIP’s language model,
so semantic grounding originates from multimodal pretraining rather than joint learning.
All attention layers use cross–attention between visual latents and CLIP token embeddings,
making it straightforwardly adaptable via parameter‐efficient methods such as LoRA ~\cite{hulora2022}
or our Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which modify only linear projections inside attention blocks while keeping base weights untouched.\footnote{
The zero initialization requirement common across adapter approaches ensures adaptation starts exactly at pretrained parameters—a property shared by OFT’s identity‐initialized orthogonal matrix $\bm R=\bm I$.}
Because latents already live on much smaller tensors ($128\times128\times4$ versus raw pixels), training epochs needed for downstream tasks shrink dramatically.


### GLIDE  

GLIDE operates entirely **in pixel space**, producing images at full resolution throughout every denoising step.
Crucially it trains its language encoder together with its dif­fusion prior,
thereby aligning linguistic token representations directly with high‐frequency visual patterns.
GLIDE also pioneered **classifier‐free guidance**, showing how changing guidance scale improves prompt adherence.
Since GLIDE lacks an intermediate compression stage,
its parameter count for unconditional generation exceeds that required for latent counterparts—
yet it demonstrates that pure pixel modeling can reach photorealistic quality when paired with massive data pretraining.


### Imagen  

Imagen follows GLIDE’s pixel‐space philosophy but makes two deliberate architectural decisions:
(1) It employs a **frozen pretrained** large language model rather than learning its own caption encoder;
(2) It stacks several cascaded super‐resolution U-Nets so coarse structures are first generated at low resolution before being refined iteratively.
These choices enable Imagen to attain exceptional photorealism even though it foregoes joint language adaptation.
As documented, employing dedicated super‑resolution stages preserves learned semantics across scales—a property reminiscent of why maintaining hyperspherical energy among neurons proves beneficial when performing downstream finetuning.


All three systems illustrate design dimensions—pixel vs latent spaces,
joint vs frozen textual encoders,
single versus cascaded UNets—that affect computational cost,
sample quality,
and flexibility for subsequent adaptation techniques.
Understanding these axes equips students with criteria necessary when selecting a backbone for custom applications such as subject-driven personalization or controllable generation.

In summary, modern text‑to‑image generators integrate three core ideas:
(1) *Diffusion* supplies an iterative probabilistic bridge between random noise and complex imagery;
(2) *Conditioning* introduces language through powerful pretrained encoders combined via cross–attention;
(3) *Training objectives* balance accurate reconstruction against amplified textual influence through classifier­free guidance.
These foundations underpin current public checkpoints—including Stable Diffusion v 1.\!5, GLIDE and Imagen—and also define what must be preserved when adapting them downstream.
Later chapters will explore how preserving pairwise angular relationships among neurons (“hyperspherical energy”) enables stable yet expressive finetuning via Orthogonal Finetuning—a strategy built expressly upon these foundational components.

Why Control Text‑to‑Image Generation?
====================================

Large diffusion models that generate images conditioned on natural‑language prompts have demonstrated an unprecedented ability to synthesize photorealistic pictures.  State‑of‑the‑art systems such as Imagen \cite{saharia2022photorealistic}, DALL·E 2 \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} and Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} are trained on billions of image–text pairs and can produce high‑quality results across a broad range of concepts.  Nevertheless, when a user supplies only a textual description the model often produces an output that only loosely matches the intended visual composition.  This section clarifies why pure text guidance is insufficient for many practical applications and introduces two concrete downstream tasks—subject‑driven generation and controllable generation—that explicitly require tighter control over the diffusion process.

## Limitations of Pure Text Guidance

Text prompts convey semantic information at the level of objects, attributes and coarse relationships (“a red cat sitting on a wooden chair”), but they generally lack detail about *how* these concepts should be arranged spatially, what exact pose an object should take, or what fine‑grained visual identity a particular instance should exhibit.  In practice this ambiguity manifests in three ways:

1. **Underspecified spatial layout** – A prompt rarely specifies exact edge contours or region boundaries.  The diffusion sampler therefore fills the scene according to its learned priors, which may deviate from the user’s intended composition.
2. **Ambiguous instance identity** – When a user wishes to generate images of a particular person or object that they have previously seen (e.g., “my pet rabbit in a park”), the generic textual description does not contain enough information to recover the unique visual appearance of that instance.
3. **Fine‑grained style or pose constraints** – Artists often need precise control over pose, lighting direction or background geometry that cannot be described faithfully with natural language alone.

Because diffusion models learn a stochastic mapping from noise to image conditioned on text embeddings, any missing cue is inferred from the model’s internal distribution learned during pretraining.  This inference can be reasonable on average but may yield results that are semantically plausible yet visually mismatched with user expectations.  Consequently, research has turned toward augmenting the conditional signal with additional modalities that directly encode the missing information.

## Definition of Subject‑Driven Generation (Personalized Generation)

Subject‑driven generation—the focus of works such as DreamBooth \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}—addresses the second limitation above.  Formally, let $\{I_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ denote a small set (often $K\leq5$) of reference images depicting a single visual entity (a person’s face, a pet’s fur texture, an object’s design).  The goal is to adapt a pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model $M^{0}$ so that given any textual prompt $p$ describing a *new* context (“$<$subject$>$ playing guitar on stage”), the finetuned model $M^{\star}$ generates images that retain the distinctive appearance of the subject while honoring the new semantic description.

Two challenges are intrinsic to this task:

* **Identity preservation** – The adapted model must retain enough of the original latent representation that encodes the subject’s unique visual features (e.g., facial geometry or fur pattern).  
* **Contextual flexibility** – At the same time it must remain capable of synthesizing arbitrary backgrounds and actions required by $p$, which demands that finetuning does not collapse the rich diversity learned during large‑scale pretraining.

In practice these demands translate into an optimization problem where only a handful of new parameters are introduced (to avoid catastrophic forgetting) while the bulk of $M^{0}$ remains unchanged.  Successful methods achieve this balance by carefully regularizing how far $M^{\star}$ can diverge from $M^{0}$, thereby preserving both semantic fidelity and generative diversity.

## Definition of Controllable Generation (Edge Maps, Segmentation, Landmarks)

Controllable generation addresses the first limitation listed above by providing auxiliary spatial cues alongside text.  In this setting one supplies an additional *control signal* $c$—for instance a Canny edge map $c_{\text{edge}}$, a semantic segmentation mask $c_{\text{seg}}$, or facial landmark coordinates $c_{\text{land}}$—and asks the diffusion model to produce an image $\hat I$ whose structure respects $c$ while still being guided by text prompt $p$.

Mathematically we extend the conditional distribution from $p(\mathbf{x}\mid p)$ to $p(\mathbf{x}\mid p,c)$ where $\mathbf{x}$ denotes an image sample.  The control signal supplies explicit geometric information:

* **Edge maps** encode contour locations without specifying color or texture.
* **Segmentation maps** assign each pixel to a class label (e.g., “sky”, “tree”, “person”) thus shaping high‑level layout.
* **Landmarks** give sparse keypoint locations on objects such as faces or bodies, enforcing accurate pose geometry.

The central difficulty is learning how to fuse these disparate modalities without sacrificing any component’s influence.  If we simply concatenate embeddings from $c$, we risk overwhelming previously learned knowledge and destabilizing training; conversely if we emphasize $c$ too weakly we lose alignment with user intent.  Therefore controllable generation calls for finetuning strategies that simultaneously respect existing pretrained representations and flexibly incorporate new control pathways.

## Application Scenarios and User Expectations

Both subject‑driven and controllable generation target real production pipelines where users demand precise visual outcomes:

* **Creative storytelling** – Scriptwriters may want their protagonist (a specific actor) placed in varied scenes described by script lines; subject ­driven finetuning ensures that each generated frame recognizably portrays that actor.
* **Product design** – Designers frequently sketch rough outlines or supply CAD rendering masks; controllable generation lets them transform those sketches into high‑resolution photorealistic renders while maintaining brand identity specified by short textual tags.
* **Virtual avatars** – In games or virtual meetings users often supply an exemplar portrait together with pose landmarks so that generated avatars can mimic real facial expressions accurately.
* **Rapid prototyping** – Engineers may wish to iterate over concept images quickly by adjusting only edge maps or segmentation masks rather than rewriting extensive textual instructions every time.
* **Personalized art** – Hobbyists want their pets or personal memorabilia rendered in imaginative settings (“my dog as an astronaut”). A handful of reference photos suffices when subject‑driven finetuning is available.

Across these scenarios users share three core expectations:

1. **High fidelity** – The output must appear photorealistic and free from artifacts introduced by finetuning.
2. **Semantic consistency** – The generated image must honor both textual description *and* auxiliary control signals without contradictions.
3. **Efficiency** – Because large diffusion models are computationally expensive, practitioners expect finetuning to require only modest GPU resources (few epochs, limited trainable weights).

When any expectation is violated—for example if finetuning collapses into memorization of training examples or fails to respect edge contours—the resulting workflow becomes impractical despite impressive underlying model capacity.

## Desiderata for Fine‑Tuning: Efficiency and Preservation of Generative Ability

Designing a fine‑tuning method for large text‑to‑image diffusion models therefore revolves around two intertwined desiderata:

### Training Efficiency

Diffusion backbones typically contain hundreds of millions of parameters; updating all weights would demand prohibitive memory consumption and training time.  Efficient approaches restrict updates either by limiting *where* updates occur (e.g., only attention layers) or by constraining *what* form those updates take (e.g., low‑rank matrices as in LoRA \cite{hulora2022}).  The goal is to introduce just enough expressive capacity to capture task‑specific knowledge while keeping computational overhead low enough for everyday users equipped with consumer‑grade GPUs.

### Generalizability Preservation

A subtler requirement concerns *how* much the finetuned model deviates from its pretrained counterpart $M^{0}$.  Early works relied on Euclidean distance $\| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^{0} \|$ between weight tensors as an implicit proxy for preserving generative quality—a smaller distance was assumed to indicate less disruption of learned semantics.  However, this scalar measure ignores relational structure among neurons within each layer.  

Recent insights reveal that *pairwise angular relationships* between weight vectors encode crucial semantic information \cite{liu2017deep,liu2018decoupled}.  These relationships are formalized by hyperspherical energy:
\[
\mathrm{HE}(\bm{W}) \;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad
\hat{\bm w}_i = \frac{\bm w_i}{\|\bm w_i\|},
\]
which measures how uniformly neurons lie on the unit hypersphere in their embedding space.  Empirically preserving $\mathrm{HE}$ across fine‐tuning correlates with retaining high‐fidelity diversity after adaptation—a hypothesis central to Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).  

Consequently an ideal fine‐tuning scheme should satisfy both practical constraints:
* **Parameter sparsity**—few trainable parameters per layer,
* **Training speed**—rapid convergence within few hundred iterations,
* **Structural preservation**—maintain hyperspherical energy so that angles among neurons—and thus their semantic organization—remain stable,
* **Explicit deviation control**—optionally bound how far orthogonal transformations drift away from identity so that the updated model stays within a calibrated neighborhood of its pretrained initialization (as implemented in Constrained OFT).

Meeting these conditions provides engineers with a reliable toolkit for customizing powerful diffusion generators without sacrificing their original creative potency—a prerequisite for deploying such models in real-world pipelines where both artistic nuance and computational pragmatism matter deeply.

remains exactly unchanged:
`HE(R·W⁰)=HE(W⁰)` . Consequently OFT guarantees that finetuning does **not**
alter pairwise neuron angles – a property observed empirically to correlate with semantic preservation (§ “Why Does Orthogonal Transformation Make Sense?”).

For subject adaptation we therefore minimise a joint objective

```
min_R  Σ_k ℒ_diff(θ_R ; x⁽ᵏ⁾ , y_subj )
       + λ_HE Σ_l ∥ HE(R_l·W⁰_l ) − HE(W⁰_l )∥²                  (4)
s.t.  R_lᵀR_l = I .
```    

Here `y_subj` denotes the prompt containing both a class description and the learned identifier token.
`λ_HE≥0` balances fidelity against deviation from pretraining structure.
When additional stability is required one adds an explicit radius constraint —the **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning** (COFT):

```
‖R_l − I‖_F ≤ ε ,                                            (5)
```  

which forces every transform to stay within an `ε`‑ball around identity while still being orthogonal.

### Mathematical formulation of controllable generation  

Controllable generation supplies an extra spatial condition `c` (e.g., canny edges,
segmentation map or facial landmark heatmap).  
A training example consists of `(x⁽ᵏ⁾ , c⁽ᵏ⁾ , y⁽ᵏ⁾)` where `y⁽ᵏ⁾` is a textual prompt.
The underlying diffusion model already accepts multiple conditioning inputs –
the usual cross‑attention pipeline processes tokens `y`, whereas an auxiliary branch
injects `c`.  During finetuning only the backbone weights are altered via OFT;
the conditioning modules remain frozen.

The optimisation mirrors Eq.(4), simply augmenting the data term with conditioning on `c`:

```
min_R  Σ_k ℒ_diff(θ_R ; x⁽ᵏ⁾ | c⁽ᵏ⁾ , y⁽ᵏ⁾ )
       + λ_HE Σ_l ∥ HE(R_l·W⁰_l ) − HE(W⁰_l )∥²                  (6)
s.t.  R_lᵀR_l = I   ,    ‖R_l−I‖_F ≤ ε   (optional COFT).
```  

The conditional diffusion loss `ℒ_diff(... | c , y )` has exactly the same form as Eq.(1); its only difference lies in feeding both signals into the denoiser at each timestep.
Thus COFT/OFT provide a unified finetuning scheme applicable both when only textual prompts change (subject-driven case) **and** when extra geometric guidance must be obeyed.

### Evaluation metrics: CLIP‑I/DINO similarity, CLIP‑T score, LPIPS diversity  

To assess whether finetuning preserves **pretrained generative quality**, three families of metrics are employed throughout the experiments.

* **Image–image semantic similarity**  
  *CLIP‑I* computes cosine similarity between CLIP visual embeddings `[radford2021learning]` extracted from a generated image `ĥx` and those from real reference images `{x_real}` depicting the target subject:
  ```
  CLIP‑I = E_x_real[ cos( f_CLIP_img(ĥx), f_CLIP_img(x_real) ) ] .
  ```  
  *DINO* follows exactly the same recipe but replaces CLIP’s encoder by ViT‐S/16 trained under self‑supervised DINO objectives `[caron2021emerging]`. Both scores reflect how well identity‐specific details survive finetuning.
* **Text–image alignment**  
  *CLIP‑T* measures cosine similarity between a CLIP text embedding of the prompting sentence `y` and a CLIP image embedding of its output:
  ```
  CLIP‑T = cos( f_CLIP_txt(y), f_CLIP_img(ĥx) ) .
  ```  
  High values indicate faithful adherence to semantic instructions.
* **Diversity across samples**  
  *LPIPS* `[zhang2018unreasonable]` evaluates perceptual distance between two images drawn from the same conditional distribution (`same y`, identical subject token). The average LPIPS value over many pairs quantifies intra‑class variety; larger values denote more diverse generations without sacrificing realism.


All four scores are reported per method after repeating finetuning with many random seeds so that stochastic effects are averaged out—exactly as described in Table 2 of the original manuscript.

### Control consistency metrics for Canny edges, segmentation maps, facial landmarks  

Controllable generation requires not only realistic output but also **faithful obedience** to its auxiliary signal. For each downstream task a dedicated quantitative consistency measure is defined by first extracting an estimated control signal from a generated image using off‑the‑shelf detectors, then comparing this estimate with its ground truth counterpart.

| Control modality | Extraction pipeline | Consistency measure |
|------------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| **Canny edge → image** (`C2I`) | Apply classic Canny detector on `ĥx`. | Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU) between detected edge map and input edge map; additionally F₁ score computed from pixelwise precision/recall. |
| **Segmentation map → image** (`S2I`) | Feed `ĥx` through a pretrained semantic segmentation network (e.g., DeepLabV3). | Mean IoU across semantic categories plus per‑pixel *mean accuracy* and *overall accuracy* averaged over all classes. |
| **Facial landmarks → image** (`L2F`) | Run a landmark detector such as FAN or MediaPipe on `ĥx`. | Average Euclidean (`ℓ₂`) distance between predicted landmarks `{ℓ̂}` and supplied landmarks `{ℓ}` after aligning coordinate systems. |

These metrics directly quantify how much information embedded in `c` survives through generations powered by OFT or COFT. Higher scores (= larger IoU/F₁ or smaller ℓ₂ error) indicate stronger controllability. They underpin Table 3 where OFT attains markedly better values than LoRA, ControlNet or T2I‑Adapter.

### α/β hyperparameters in diffusion sampling  

The forward noising process hinges on two scalar sequences:

```
β_t ∈ (0,1)             – variance added at step t,
α_t = 1 − β_t           – complementary “signal” coefficient,
α̅_t = ∏_{s=1}^{t} α_s   – cumulative product governing overall signal decay.
```  

At timestep `t` an intermediate noisy latent obeys

```
x_t = √α̅_t · x_0 + √(1−α̅_t) · ε ,      ε ∼ N(0,I).               (7)
```  

During inference one runs a reverse chain guided by predictions produced by
the finetuned denoiser (`θ_R`).  
The scalars `α_t` & `β_t` therefore dictate how aggressively noise is removed at each step,
and they also appear inside classifier–free guidance formulas where one trades off unconditional versus conditional predictions via a guidance scale γ (“strength” parameter). While those sampling‐time knobs are inherited unchanged from standard DDPM / Stable Diffusion pipelines `[ho2020denoising]`, their exact numerical schedule (`β₁,…,β_T`) influences final fidelity—particularly when new conditioning signals have been introduced through OFE/C​OFT finetuning. Consequently experimental protocols keep these schedules identical across baselines so that observed improvements stem solely from better weight adaptation rather than altered sampling dynamics.

#### Summary  

By formulating both **subject‑driven** and **controllable** finetuning as constrained optimisation problems over orthogonal transforms, OFT preserves hyperspherical energy—an angle‐preserving invariant empirically linked to semantic stability—and simultaneously adapts models to new downstream requirements. Rigorous evaluation leverages four complementary metrics—CLIP‑I/DINO for identity retention, CLIP‑T for prompt alignment, LPIPS for diversity—and three task–specific consistency scores that directly assess obedience to auxiliary signals such as edges, segmentations or facial landmarks. Together these formal problem statements and evaluation criteria provide a principled foundation for studying future fine‑tuning strategies in large text–to–image diffusion systems.
