Chapter 1: Foundations of Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

# Diffusion Processes and Denoising Score Matching  

Large‑scale text‑to‑image generation relies fundamentally on *diffusion probabilistic models*.  At a high level, a diffusion model defines two stochastic processes over an image variable $\mathbf{x}$ :  

* A **forward process** that gradually corrupts a clean image $\mathbf{x}_0$ by adding Gaussian noise over $T$ discrete time steps,
  $$q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}_t;\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\,\beta_t\mathbf{I}\bigr), \qquad t=1,\dots,T,$$  
  where $\beta_t \in(0,1)$ denotes a variance schedule controlling how much noise is injected at step $t$.  After many steps the corrupted image $\mathbf{x}_T$ becomes essentially isotropic Gaussian noise.
* A **reverse process** parameterized by a neural network $\varepsilon_\theta$, which attempts to invert the forward corruption,
  $$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t)=\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t-1};\,\mu_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t),\,\Sigma_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\bigr).$$  

Training consists of learning $\theta$ so that $$p_\theta$$ accurately predicts how to remove a small amount of noise at each step.  This learning objective can be expressed as *denoising score matching*: the network is trained to predict either the added Gaussian noise $\varepsilon$ or directly the gradient $\nabla_{\!\mathbf{x}}\log q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_0)$ (the “score”) from which one recovers an estimate of $\mu_\theta$.  The seminal works on denoising score matching introduced this formulation for unconditional image synthesis \cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score,song2021denoising,dhariwal2021diffusion}.  

Intuitively, one can think of the reverse process as an iterative *denoiser*: starting from pure noise $\mathbf{x}_T$, repeatedly apply the learned denoiser conditioned on the current timestep $t$, gradually sharpening noisy patterns until an image $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$ emerges.  Because every step removes only a modest amount of corruption, errors never accumulate catastrophically – this stability distinguishes diffusion sampling from single‐step generators such as GANs.\footnote{A “step” refers here to one iteration over $t$ during sampling; multiple sampling schedules exist (e.g., DDIM), all built upon this core iterative principle.}  

# Latent‑Space vs Pixel‑Space Diffusion  

Two practical design choices arise when placing a diffusion model inside an image generator:

* **Pixel‑space diffusion** operates directly on full‐resolution RGB arrays $\in[0,255]^{H\times W\times3}$.  Early large‐scale generators such as GLIDE \cite{nichol2022glide} and Imagen \cite{saharia2022photorealistic} adopt this choice because it avoids any lossy preprocessing step—every pixel receives its own gradient signal during training.\footnote{GLIDE further couples its diffusion prior with a jointly trained text encoder while Imagen keeps its textual embedder frozen.}  
* **Latent‑space diffusion** first compresses an image into a lower‐dimensional code using an autoencoder or vector quantizer, then runs diffusion over this compact representation before finally decoding back to pixels.  Stable Diffusion v1.\!5 \cite{rombach2022high} follows precisely this route: images are mapped into a discrete visual codebook learned by VQ‑GAN \cite{esser2021taming}, producing latent tensors $\mathbf{z}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times h\times w}$ whose dimensionality $d$ is far smaller than three times $HW$.  DALL·E 2 likewise learns a joint latent embedding between images and texts via CLIP \cite{radford2021learning}.  By confining denoising operations to this reduced space, training becomes dramatically cheaper both computationally and memorywise while retaining high fidelity after decoding.\footnote{The decoder part (VQ‑GAN or CLIP‑based generator) is kept fixed during most downstream adaptations so its quality remains constant across experiments.}

The trade‑off between these strategies can be phrased intuitively.  Pixel‐space offers maximal expressive power—every subtle color nuance lives explicitly in the state space—but incurs quadratic cost in image size during both forward/backward passes.  Latent‐space reduces cost quadratically while still preserving most semantic structure because the encoder has already captured texture, shape and layout information into compact feature vectors.\footnote{Empirically, large language–vision pretraining shows that semantic semantics survive strong compression when responsibility lies primarily with directions on hyperspheres—a theme revisited when discussing finetuning later.}

# Text Encoders and Cross‑Attention Mechanisms  

Textual conditioning turns an otherwise unconditional diffusion sampler into a *text‐to‐image* system.  The principal conduit between language tokens and visual features consists of **cross–attention** blocks placed inside the UNet backbone commonly used for denoising.\footnote{UNet architecture provides hierarchical spatial processing through downsampling/upsampling stages; cross–attention allows global textual context at each resolution level}.  

A *text encoder* converts an input prompt $c=\langle w_1,\dots,w_n\rangle$ into dense token embeddings $\{\mathbf{e}_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ .  In many public systems these encoders are heavily pretrained vision–language transformers such as CLIP’s language tower \cite{radford2021learning}, although GLIDE uniquely trains its own encoder jointly with its pixel‐space prior past studies showed significant benefit from aligning language representation directly with visual generation objectives.\footnote{Joint training creates strong word–image alignment early on whereas freezing leverages large generic linguistic knowledge distilled elsewhere — both approaches appear across state-of-the-art systems}.  

Cross–attention proceeds similarly to self–attention except queries derive from visual feature maps while keys/values come from textual embeddings:
$$
\operatorname{Attn}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V}) = 
\operatorname{softmax}\!\Bigl( \frac{\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}}{\sqrt d}\Bigr)\,\mathbf{V},
$$
where $\mathbf Q=\mathrm W_Q\,\phi_{\rm vis}$ gathers visual query vectors from intermediate feature map $\phi_{\rm vis}$,
and $(\mathbf K,\mathbf V)= (\mathrm W_K,\mathrm W_V)\,\phi_{\rm txt}$ are linear projections of token embeddings $\phi_{\rm txt}
= (\mathbf e_1,\dots,\mathbf e_n)$.  
Because attention coefficients are essentially cosine similarities between query direction $\hat{\mathbf q}$ and key direction $\hat{\mathbf k}$ after normalization,
the semantic interaction heavily depends upon *directions* rather than magnitudes—a fact emphasized later when discussing angle preservation during finetuning.\footnote{
Empirical observations suggest angular similarity encodes semantic relationships more faithfully than Euclidean distance across many vision–language tasks.
}

In practice modern generators contain several cross‑attention blocks spread across UNet layers so that textual cues affect coarse geometry early (low resolution) and fine texture late (high resolution).  This multi‑scale integration enables prompts such as “a photorealistic orange cat sitting on a marble floor” to influence both overall composition (cat pose) and local color patterns simultaneously.\footnote{
The systematic injection point matters when adapting downstream tasks —for example techniques that insert additional adapters typically target exactly these attention layers since they mediate modality mixing.
}

# Training Objectives: Reconstruction Loss and Classifier‑Free Guidance  

During pretraining each UNet receives three inputs at timestep $t$: 

* A noisy latent (or pixel) $\mathbf x_t$, 
* A conditional embedding derived from the prompt $c$, 
* The timestep scalar $t$ itself (often encoded sinusoidally).  

The network learns two intertwined objectives:

### Reconstruction Loss  

At every step it predicts either an additive noise token $\hat{\varepsilon}_\theta(\cdot)$ or directly reconstructs the clean latent $\hat{\mathbf x}_0$.  
A common choice mirrors ordinary least squares:
$$
L_{\rm rec}
= \mathbb E_{\,\mathbf x_0,\varepsilon,t}
   \Bigl[
     \bigl\| 
        \varepsilon - 
        \hat{\varepsilon}_\theta( \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\mathbf x_0+\sqrt {1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon , t,c )
     \bigr\|_2^{\,2}
   \Bigr],
$$
where $\varepsilon$ denotes ground‑truth Gaussian noise added during forward corruption.
Minimizing $L_{\rm rec}$ forces the network’s output at time $t$ to agree with known corruption direction,
hence teaching it how “to undo” exactly one infinitesimal noise step.
In downstream finetuning scenarios such as DreamBooth ~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} or our subsequent Orthogonal Finetuning framework,
the same reconstruction loss drives adaptation while preserving most pretrained capabilities through additional structural regularization.



### Classifier‑Free Guidance  

In pure conditional sampling one would simply feed both noisy latents **and** prompt embeddings into $p_\theta$.  
Classifier–free guidance augments this scheme by also training an *unconditional* version—i.e., making the network learn
$p_\theta(\cdot|\varnothing)$ alongside $p_\theta(\cdot|c)$.  
At inference time one computes a weighted combination:
$$
p^{(\gamma)}_\theta(\cdot|c)
    = p_\theta(\cdot|\varnothing)
      +\gamma\,\bigl(
            p_\theta(\cdot|c)-p_\theta(\cdot|\varnothing)
          \bigr),
$$
where coefficient $\gamma>0$ amplifies how strongly textual information steers sampling.
This procedure has become standard practice across public checkpoints—including GLIDE’s official release—to improve fidelity toward complex prompts without requiring external classifiers.
From an intuitive standpoint,
the unconditional branch supplies “generic image priors”, while scaling
the difference isolates purely semantic influence coming from words.\footnote{
When combined with finetuned orthogonal transformations,
classifier−free guidance retains its form because orthogonal updates preserve pairwise angles among weight vectors—the very quantities used when computing similarity scores inside attention.
}

Together these losses enforce accurate reconstruction under strong stochastic perturbation while allowing flexible emphasis on textual control during generation.



# Key Public Models: Stable Diffusion v 1.\!5, GLIDE, Imagen  

A concrete understanding emerges when examining three influential public checkpoints frequently used throughout recent literature:

### Stable Diffusion v 1.\!5  

Stable Diffusion adopts **latent‐space** generation : images are first encoded into VQ‑GAN codes,
after which UNet denoisers operate only on these compact representations.
Its text encoder is frozen CLIP’s language model,
so semantic grounding originates from multimodal pretraining rather than joint learning.
All attention layers use cross–attention between visual latents and CLIP token embeddings,
making it straightforwardly adaptable via parameter‐efficient methods such as LoRA ~\cite{hulora2022}
or our Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which modify only linear projections inside attention blocks while keeping base weights untouched.\footnote{
The zero initialization requirement common across adapter approaches ensures adaptation starts exactly at pretrained parameters—a property shared by OFT’s identity‐initialized orthogonal matrix $\bm R=\bm I$.}
Because latents already live on much smaller tensors ($128\times128\times4$ versus raw pixels), training epochs needed for downstream tasks shrink dramatically.


### GLIDE  

GLIDE operates entirely **in pixel space**, producing images at full resolution throughout every denoising step.
Crucially it trains its language encoder together with its dif­fusion prior,
thereby aligning linguistic token representations directly with high‐frequency visual patterns.
GLIDE also pioneered **classifier‐free guidance**, showing how changing guidance scale improves prompt adherence.
Since GLIDE lacks an intermediate compression stage,
its parameter count for unconditional generation exceeds that required for latent counterparts—
yet it demonstrates that pure pixel modeling can reach photorealistic quality when paired with massive data pretraining.


### Imagen  

Imagen follows GLIDE’s pixel‐space philosophy but makes two deliberate architectural decisions:
(1) It employs a **frozen pretrained** large language model rather than learning its own caption encoder;
(2) It stacks several cascaded super‐resolution U-Nets so coarse structures are first generated at low resolution before being refined iteratively.
These choices enable Imagen to attain exceptional photorealism even though it foregoes joint language adaptation.
As documented, employing dedicated super‑resolution stages preserves learned semantics across scales—a property reminiscent of why maintaining hyperspherical energy among neurons proves beneficial when performing downstream finetuning.


All three systems illustrate design dimensions—pixel vs latent spaces,
joint vs frozen textual encoders,
single versus cascaded UNets—that affect computational cost,
sample quality,
and flexibility for subsequent adaptation techniques.
Understanding these axes equips students with criteria necessary when selecting a backbone for custom applications such as subject-driven personalization or controllable generation.



---  

In summary, modern text‑to‑image generators integrate three core ideas:
(1) *Diffusion* supplies an iterative probabilistic bridge between random noise and complex imagery;
(2) *Conditioning* introduces language through powerful pretrained encoders combined via cross–attention;
(3) *Training objectives* balance accurate reconstruction against amplified textual influence through classifier­free guidance.
These foundations underpin current public checkpoints—including Stable Diffusion v 1.\!5, GLIDE and Imagen—and also define what must be preserved when adapting them downstream.
Later chapters will explore how preserving pairwise angular relationships among neurons (“hyperspherical energy”) enables stable yet expressive finetuning via Orthogonal Finetuning—a strategy built expressly upon these foundational components.
