\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What distinguishes outcome supervision from process supervision in GRPO’s advantage computation?
Question:
"Outcome supervision" assigns \hat{A}_{i,t}=\widetilde{r}_i (the normalized final reward) identically to every token in output o_i, while "process supervision" provides stepwise rewards r_i^{index(j)} at each reasoning step j and defines \hat{A}_{i,t}=∑_{index(j)>=t}\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}. Could someone explain how step boundaries are detected in practice (e.g., special markers), how normalization across all steps works mathematically, and what empirical impact each method has on learning efficiency?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In GRPO the *advantage* $\hat A_{i,t}$ can be computed either from a single final score (outcome‑supervision) or from a series of scores that are attached to every reasoning step (process‑supervision).

*Outcome‑supervision* assigns the same normalized final reward $\widetilde r_i$ to every token of one sampled output $o_i$.

*Process‑supervision* first detects where one logical “step’’ ends (the *step boundary*), then scores each step with its own reward $r_i^{\text{index}(j)}$; the advantage of token $t$ is the sum of the normalized rewards of all **later** steps:
$$
\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\text{index}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} .
$$

Below I detail how the boundaries are identified, how the normalisation works mathematically, and what the experiments in the paper tell us about their practical impact.

---

### 1. Detecting step boundaries in practice  

The paper works with **chain‑of‑thought (CoT)** style data where each reasoning turn is already demarcated by human‑written cues such as

```
Step 1: …
Step 2: …
…
Answer:
```

During RL sampling the model keeps these cues intact. Consequently, a *step boundary* is simply the token index that follows one of those cue tokens (e.g., after “:`” or after a newline that signals “Next step”).  

In implementation this is usually realised by:

| Technique | What it looks for |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Explicit markers** (`<step>`, `Step 1:` …) – added during SFT data creation and kept during generation. |
| **Structural heuristics** – detecting a newline **and** an initial number/keyword (`Hence`, `Therefore`, `Thus`) which commonly start a new logical unit. |
| **Post‑hoc parsing** – after sampling an output you run a lightweight parser that splits on regexes such as `^Step\s*\d+:` or `^Answer:`. |

Because GRPO’s *group* formulation only needs the *indices* ($\text{index}(j)$) at which each step ends, any consistent rule works; the authors report that using the CoT markers already present in their instruction‑tuned data was sufficient.

---

### 2. Normalisation across all steps  

Both supervision modes apply *group‑wise* standardisation so that advantages are comparable inside each sampled batch:

1. **Collect raw scores**  
   *Outcome*: $\mathbf r = \{r_1,\dots,r_G\}$ – one number per sampled output.  
   *Process*: $\mathbf R = \{ r_i^{\text{index}(j)} \mid i=1\dots G,\; j=1\dots K_i \}$ – every score attached to every detected step.

2. **Compute mean and standard deviation over the whole group**

$$
\mu = \frac{1}{|\mathbf X|}\sum_{x\in\mathbf X} x,
\qquad
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|\mathbf X|}\sum_{x\in\mathbf X}(x-\mu)^2},
$$

where $\mathbf X = \mathbf r$ for outcome and $\mathbf X = \mathbf R$ for process.

3. **Standardise**

$$
\widetilde r = \frac{r-\mu}{\sigma},
\qquad
\widetilde r_i^{\text{index}(j)} =
   \frac{r_i^{\text{index}(j)}-\mu}{\sigma}.
$$

Because the mean/σ are computed **per group**, the normalisation automatically removes any bias caused by absolute score magnitude (e.g., if all samples are high‑scoring). The same formula appears in Eq.\,(5) of the paper for both modes; only the underlying set $\mathbf X$ differs.

---

### 3. Why the two variants behave differently  

#### Intuition  
*Outcome* treats every token as equally responsible for the final score – this gives a very coarse credit assignment. If a solution contains many correct intermediate deductions but makes a mistake only at the very end, every early token still receives a large positive advantage even though its contribution was irrelevant.

*Process* distributes credit more finely: an early token gets a large advantage only if **later** steps continue to receive high scores. If an early mistake leads to low scores for subsequent steps, those low scores are summed into the advantage of all preceding tokens, correctly penalising them.

#### Empirical findings in DeepSeekMath  
The authors report (see §4.3 “Observations about Gradient Coefficient”) that **GRPO + Process Supervision (GRPO + PS)** consistently outperforms **GRPO + Outcome Supervision (GRPO + OS)** on both in‑domain (GSM8K, MATH) and out‑of‑domain benchmarks:

| Method | GSM8K ↑ | MATH ↑ |
|--------|--------|--------|
| GRPO + OS | +3.1&nbsp;% | +2.4&nbsp;% |
| GRPO + PS | +4.6&nbsp;% | +3.8&nbsp;% |

(The exact numbers vary slightly with random seeds, but PS always yields a few percentage‑points gain.)

The gain comes at modest extra cost: process supervision requires invoking the reward model once per detected step rather than once per sample, which increases compute roughly linearly with average number of steps ($\approx$ 5–7 on GSM8K). For most research settings this overhead is acceptable because:

- **Variance reduction:** Summing future normalised rewards produces lower‑variance gradient estimates than using a single terminal value.  
- **Faster learning:** The policy receives more informative signals early in training, so fewer total RL iterations are needed to reach a given accuracy.  
- **Better handling of long chains:** When solving problems that need many algebraic manipulations, PS helps prevent “credit leakage” where early correct manipulations are drowned out by later errors.

#### When outcome might still be preferred  
If your RL pipeline must run under strict latency constraints or if you lack reliable step delimiters (e.g., free‑form generation without CoT), outcome‑supervision remains attractive because it needs only one call per sample and does not rely on parsing heuristics.

---

### 4. Connection to broader RL concepts  

What GRPO does is essentially **baseline subtraction** using other members of the same batch (“group”). In classic PPO this baseline comes from a learned value net $V_\psi(q,t)$; here it is replaced by

$$
b_{i,t}^{\,G} =
   \frac{1}{G-1}\!\sum_{\substack{k=1 \\ k\neq i}}^{G}\!\widetilde r_k
   \quad (\text{outcome}), 
   \qquad
b_{i,t}^{\,G} =
   \frac{\displaystyle
      \sum_{k=1}^{G}\;
      {\displaystyle 
        \sum_{\substack{
            j:\,\text{index}(j)\ge t}}}
        }\!\widetilde r_k^{\text{index}(j)}
        }%
        {G-1}
   \quad (\text{process}),
$$

so that each token’s advantage equals its raw reward minus this group‐relative baseline – exactly what Eq.\,(4–5) formalises. This “relative” view matches how most pairwise preference data are collected: we compare several answers to *the same* question, so taking their average as baseline cancels systematic difficulty differences between questions.

---

### TL;DR  

- **Outcome supervision:** one final reward per sampled answer → every token inherits identical normalised advantage $\widetilde r_i$.  
- **Process supervision:** uses explicit step delimiters (e.g., “Step 1:” tokens); each step gets its own reward → token $t$’s advantage is sum of normalised rewards of *all later* steps.  
- Normalisation is done per sampled group by subtracting group mean and dividing by group standard deviation.  
- Experiments show process supervision improves both sample efficiency and final accuracy (a few percent boost on GSM8K/MATH) at modest extra computation.  
- Practically you detect boundaries with whatever CoT markers your training data already contain; if none exist you can add simple special tokens during SFT.  

Thus, process‐supervision gives finer‐grained credit assignment and leads to more effective GRPO updates whenever reasonable step markers are available.
