\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How do Paged Optimizers prevent out‑of‑memory spikes during gradient checkpointing?
Question:
The paper introduces "Paged Optimizers" that leverage NVIDIA unified memory.

* In practice, what tensors (e.g., optimizer states) are allocated in paged memory versus GPU memory?
* How does this interact with gradient checkpointing—does paging occur during forward recomputation or optimizer update steps?
* Are there any latency penalties when paged tensors are faulted back onto the GPU during training?

Answer:
**Answer**

`Paged Optimizers` are a thin wrapper around the CUDA Unified‑Memory (UM) runtime that treats the per‑parameter buffers of an optimiser as *paged* allocations instead of having them live permanently on the device. In QLoRA the optimiser is typically Adam (or Adam‑W), whose state consists of two full‑size tensors – the first‑moment **m** and the second‑moment **v** – that have exactly the same shape as every trainable weight matrix. For a 65 B‑parameter LLaMA model these two tensors alone would require roughly $2\times65\,\text{B}\times2\,\text{bytes}\approx260\,\text{GB}$ if kept on the GPU, which is far beyond any single‑GPU capacity.  

In practice the only tensors that stay resident in fast GPU memory are those that must be touched every forward or backward pass:  

* the quantised base weights ($\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$) and their de‑quantised BF16 view,  
* the LoRA adapters $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$ (which are tiny compared with $\mathbf{W}$), and  
* temporary activation tensors that survive because of gradient checkpointing.

All optimiser statistics (**m**, **v**, plus Adam’s scalar counters such as $\beta_1^t$, $\beta_2^t$) are allocated with `cudaMallocManaged` so that they initially reside in host RAM but appear as device pointers. When a kernel needs a page of **m** or **v**, UM triggers a page‑fault, copies that 4 KB (or whatever page size) from host RAM over PCIe into spare device memory, runs the kernel, and later may evict it again when another allocation needs space. Because these buffers are never accessed while the network is doing its forward sweep or its recomputed “checkpoint” passes, they stay idle in host RAM throughout most of the iteration and thus never consume precious device capacity.

The interaction with gradient checkpointing is therefore indirect. Checkpointing reduces
the permanent activation footprint by discarding most intermediate activations during
the forward pass and re‑computing selected blocks when back‑propagation starts.
The re‑computation itself does **not** touch optimiser state at all; it merely
re‑executes
the same linear layers that read $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$
(still on‐GPU) and write new activation results
(still on‐GPU). The only moment when optimiser state becomes necessary
is **after** all gradients have been accumulated,
i.e.\ at
the start
of
the optimiser’s `step()` call.
At that point Unified Memory pulls in all migrated pages
of **m**
and **v**, performs
the Adam update
$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon}$
on BF16 parameters ($\theta$
being either LoRA weights or any trainable parameters),
writes back
the updated moments,
and finally evicts them again
if more room is required later in
the same iteration
(for example if a subsequent micro‐batch leads to larger activation spikes).

Because paging only happens once per iteration—right before
the update—the extra data movement
is confined to
a relatively small window compared
with hours of matrix–multiply work.
Empirically (Section 4
of QLoRA)
training
a 65 B model with batch size 16 on
a 48 GB V100 shows essentially identical wall–clock speed
to

a regular optimiser,
indicating 
that under typical sequence lengths
the latency introduced by page faults
is hidden behind compute.
However,
Unified Memory does incur 
a PCIe round‐trip each time 
a page is faulted 
in 
(tens 
of microseconds 
per 
page)
and 
a copy‐out 
when 
it 
is evicted again.
If one deliberately pushes 
the system into extreme regimes—e.g.,
ultra‐long contexts 
that cause many intermediate activation pages 
to coexist 
with optimisation pages—the number 
of faults can rise sharply 
and measurable slowdown appears.
In those cases one usually observes occasional “soft”
OOMs 
that are automatically resolved by eviction rather than an outright crash.

In summary,
paged optimisers keep *only*
the heavy optimizer buffers 
in Unified Memory while leaving   
model weights   
and activations   
on‐device;
they are inactive during forward/recomputation passes 
and become resident only for   
the brief optimizer‐step phase.
The design eliminates sudden OOM spikes caused 
by trying  
to hold both large activation checkpoints *and*
Adam’s moment tensors simultaneously 
on   
a limited GPU,
and—in practice—adds little or no noticeable latency unless one operates far beyond normal sequence lengths where Page‐fault frequency grows substantially.​
