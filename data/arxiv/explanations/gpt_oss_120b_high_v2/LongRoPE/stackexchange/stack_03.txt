\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does linear positional interpolation cause ‘crowded’ positions at large extensions?
Question:
The paper says that linear PI reduces all rotation angles uniformly by $\lambda = s$, which makes token positions "crowded" and hurts performance when $s$ is large.
I am trying to visualise what "crowded" means mathematically.
* Does scaling by $s$ shrink angular distances between adjacent tokens so that their sinusoidal vectors become highly correlated?
* If so, can you show an expression for the angle difference between two consecutive positions before and after applying PI?
A concrete illustration would clarify why PI underperforms for high extension ratios such as $8\times$ or more.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “crowded‒positions” problem comes straight from the geometry of RoPE (Rotary Positional Embedding).  
In RoPE each attention head rotates a query‒key pair by a *phase* that depends on the token index **$n$** and on a frequency that is attached to the embedding dimension **$\displaystyle\frac{d}{2}$**.  
For dimension **$i$** the phase used in Eq.&nbsp;(2) of the paper is  

$$
\phi_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}},\qquad 
\beta=\theta^{2/d},
$$  

so that a single token at position **$n$** is represented by the sinusoid pair  

$$
[\,\cos \phi_{i}(n),\, \sin \phi_{i}(n)\,]_{\,i=0}^{d/2-1}.
$$  

---

### What “crowded” means mathematically

Two consequences follow from this definition:

| before interpolation | after *linear* positional interpolation (PI) |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| Phase : $\displaystyle \phi_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}$ | Phase : $\displaystyle \tilde{\phi}_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{s\,\beta^{\,i}}$\;(the paper writes it as division by $\lambda=s$) |

For any fixed dimension **$i$**, the **angular distance between two consecutive tokens** is simply  

> **Original spacing**  
> $$
> \Delta\phi_{i}^{\,{\rm orig}}=
>      \phi_{i}(n+1)-\phi_{i}(n)
>      =\frac{1}{\beta^{\,i}} .
> $$  

> **After linear PI**  
> $$
>   \Delta\phi_{i}^{\,{\rm PI}}
>   =\tilde{\phi}_{i}(n+1)-\tilde{\phi}_{i}(n)
>   =\frac{1}{s\,\beta^{\,i}}
>   =\frac{1}{s}\,\Delta\phi_{i}^{\,{\rm orig}} .
> $$  

Equation&nbsp;(2) tells us that *every* dimension’s angular step is divided by the extension ratio **$s$**.  
When **$s=8$**, each step becomes eight times smaller than it was in the pretrained model.

Because RoPE lives on a unit circle, shrinking an angular step makes adjacent vectors **more collinear**:

$$
{\rm corr}\bigl(v_{n},v_{n+1}\bigr)
   = \cos(\Delta-\!\!\phi_{i}) 
   \;\approx 1-\tfrac12(\Delta-\!\!\phi_{i})^{2}
   \quad (\Delta-\!\!\phi_{i}\ll 1).
$$

If we plug Eq.(2):

| $s=4\times?$ | $s=8\times?$ |
|---|---|
| original step $=0.25~{\rm rad}\Rightarrow {\rm corr}\approx0.97.$ | original step $=0.125~{\rm rad}\Rightarrow {\rm corr}\approx0.992.$ |
| lower‑frequency dim $(\beta^{10}\!\approx100)$: original step $10^{-2}~{\rm rad},~ {\rm corr}\approx0.99995.$ | scaled step $=1.25\times10^{-3}~{\rm rad},~ {\rm corr}\approx0.999999.$ |

Thus **high‑frequency dimensions** (small&nbsp;$i$, $\beta^{\,i}\approx 1$) still retain noticeable variation after an eight‒fold stretch, but **low‑frequency dimensions** (large&nbsp;$i$, $\beta^{\,i}\gg 1$) become almost static: all positions map onto nearly the same point on their circles.

In an attention head the inner product between a query at position **$p$** and a key at position **$q$** contains a term  

$$
q^\top k
   = \sum_{j}
     \bigl(\cos(\tilde{\phi}_{j}(p)-\tilde{\phi}_{j}(q))\bigr),
$$

so when every $\tilde{\phi}_{j}(p)-\tilde{\phi}_{j}(q)$ is tiny the cosine term is $\approx 1$ for *all* pairs $p,q$. Consequently the model loses its ability to discriminate “who is next to whom”; many distant tokens look almost identical from a positional standpoint – this is what authors refer to as *crowded* positions.

---

### Why linear PI fails for large extensions

The RoPE design deliberately spreads information across dimensions with different frequencies:

* low‑$i$\;&nbsp;→&nbsp;**high‑frequency** → rotating quickly, encoding fine‒grained relative order;
* high‑$i$\;&nbsp;→&nbsp;**low‑frequency** → rotating slowly, carrying coarse‐grained order cues that survive over very long spans.

Linear PI treats all dimensions identically by multiplying every denominator by **$s$*. The result:

* It **compresses** every frequency band by exactly the same factor;
* Low‑frequency bands—which already change only slightly per token—are compressed *further*, almost freezing their phase;
* The set of reachable phase values within any practical window collapses dramatically; mathematically one can view it as mapping millions of distinct integer indices into a tiny interval of length  

$$
L'_{\max}=%
    L'\bigl/ s
    = L .
$$  

Hence even though we now allow indices up to $L'$, their embedded phases occupy roughly the same total angular range they did before extension—so we are reusing most of it many times over. This redundancy looks like “traffic jam’’ on a narrow road – many cars (token positions) are forced into almost identical lanes (phase values).

Because attention relies on phase differences as signals for “how far apart’’ two tokens are, this crowding makes long‑range attention noisy or indistinguishable, which empirically manifests as a sharp rise in perplexity when **$s\succeq8.$**

---

### Intuition behind non‑uniform alternatives

Methods such as **NTK‑based interpolation** or **YaRN** avoid crowding by *not* shrinking all frequencies equally:

* They keep high‑frequency dimensions nearly untouched ($\lambda\simeq 1,$ extrapolation), preserving fine‒grained spacing;
* They apply larger factors ($$\lambda > s,$ extrapolation’’​…) only to low‑frequency dimensions where some compression is tolerable;
* YaRN even treats three groups separately (extrapolate → NTK → interpolate).

Mathematically this means replacing Eq.(2) with  

$$
   \Delta \tilde{\phi}_{i}= 
   \frac{c_i}{s}\;\frac{1}{\beta^{\, i}},
   ~~c_i =
     \begin{cases}
       1          & (\text{high freq})\\[3pt]
       s^{\,p(i)} & (\text{mid / low freq})
     \end{cases}
,$$

where **$c_i\fgeq 1$.**
Consequently  

* low $i$\;$⟶$\;$${c_i}\approx 1,$   
* high $j$\;$⟶$\;$${c_j}\approx s,$   

and the overall average spacing stays roughly constant across dimensions – far fewer vectors end up “crowded.’’

---

### Quick numeric illustration

Assume a toy model with just two frequencies:

* High frequency: $\beta^{0}=1 ~~⟶~~ Δφ_0^{orig}=1~{\rm rad}$;
* Low frequency : $\beta^{5}=100 ~~⟶~~ Δφ_5^{orig}=0.01~{\rm rad}$.

| method | $Δφ_0$ | $Δφ_5$ | $\cos(Δφ_0)$ | $\cos(Δφ_5)$ |
|--------|--------|--------|--------------|--------------|
| Original | 1 rad                   │ 0.01 rad │ 0.540 │ 0.99995 |
| Linear PI ($s=8$)\*\* | 0.125 rad│ 0.00125 rad│ 0.992 │ ≈ 0.999999 |
| NTK‐style (keep high freq)| ≈ 1 rad│ ≈ 0.00125 rad│ ≈ 0.540│ ≈ 0.999999 |

In the last row the high‑frequency spacing stays essentially unchanged ($≈a~rad$), while only the low‑frequency spacing suffers compression – exactly what preserves discriminability for nearby tokens while still allowing much longer overall context windows.



---

### Bottom line

Linear positional interpolation rescales every rotary angle by a common factor $\lambda=s$, which mathematically shrinks every inter‑token angular gap by $\tfrac{1}{s}$. Because sinusoidal vectors lie on circles, making their arcs tiny forces consecutive embeddings—and consequently attention scores—to become almost identical (“crowded”). When **$s\succeq8,$ especially in low‑frequency RoPE dimensions, this loss of angular resolution translates into serious information loss and therefore poor performance.  
Non‑uniform strategies recover part of this resolution by leaving fast‒rotating dimensions untouched and distributing interpolation pressure unevenly across dimensions—a principle formally captured by NTK theory and empirically exploited in LongRoPE’s evolutionary search.​
