### How large is the search space for different target extension ratios?
Question:
(a) For an extension ratio $s=4$, each $\
Answer:
The “search” mentioned in LongRoPE is not a small grid‑search over a handful of hyper‑parameters – it is a combinatorial optimisation over **one scaling factor per RoPE frequency** together with **a single token‑position threshold** that decides how many leading tokens keep their original rotary angles unchanged.  

Formally, for a model whose RoPE operates on \(d_{\text{rope}}\) dimensions we have  

* a rescaling factor \(\lambda_{i}\) for every frequency index \(i = 0,\dots ,\frac{d_{\text{rope}}}{2}-1;\)  
* an integer threshold \(\widehat n \in \{\!0,\,1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\dots ,256!\}\).

The admissible values of each \(\lambda_{i}\) are defined by the target **extension ratio** \(s=\tfrac{L'}{L}\):

\[
    \lambda_{i}\in[\,1.00,\;s\times 1.25\,],
    \qquad \text{step}=0.01 .
\]

Hence the number of distinct candidates per frequency is  

\[
    M(s)=
    \Bigl\lfloor
        \frac{s\times 1.25-1}{0.01}
    \Bigr\rfloor+1
    =
    \bigl\lfloor125\,s-99\bigr\rfloor .
    \tag{†}
\]

(The lower bound “\(1[.]00\)” corresponds to *direct extrapolation*: no interpolation at all.)

For the models used in the paper \(d_{\text{rope}}=128$, i.e. there are  
\(K=\tfrac{d_{\text{rope}}}{2}=64$\ independent frequencies.
If we ignore the monotonicity constraint that forces  
\( \lambda_{0}\leqslant \lambda_{1}\leqslant\dotsc\leqslant \lambda_{K-1} ), an **upper bound** on the raw combinatorial size is simply

\[
|{\cal S}(s)| = M(s)^K \;\times |\widehat N|
        = M(s)^{\,64}\;\times13,
        \qquad |\widehat N| =13,
        \tag{‡}
\]

where we count all values listed for `\(\widehat n`\), namely  
\(13=\{0,\,1,\dotsc ,256\}=13.\)

---

### Concrete numbers given in the paper  

For an extension ratio $s=4\times$:  

* \(M(4)=\bigl\lfloor125\times4-99\bigr\rceil
          =401\simeq400.\)

Plugging into (‡)

```math
|{\cal S}(4)| 
   ≈ 400^{64}\times14
   ≈ 4\times10^{167}.
```

This figure appears explicitly in Section 3.2 (“a $400^{128/2}\times14=4\times10^{167}$ choices”).

---

### How the space grows with larger ratios  

Because \(M(s)\) scales linearly with \(s$, while it is raised to the power \(K (=64)\), **the total space expands exponentially** when we increase the target extension ratio.

| Target ratio $s$ | $M(s)$ (≈ number per dim.) | Approximate raw space |
|-------------------|------------------------------|-----------------------|
| $8\times$       | $M(8)\approx801\simeq800$   | $800^{64}\!\times13   ≈3\times10^{191}$ |
|- $16\times$      | $M(16)\approx1601\simeq1600$ | $1600^{64}\!\times13   ≈5\times10^{227}$ |
|- $32\times$      | $M(32)\approx3201\simeq3200$ | $3200^{64}\!\times13   >10^{260}$ |

(The exact powers differ only by constant factors because we keep one decimal step.)

Even though LongRoPE imposes two practical tricks that shrink this astronomically large set—

* **Monotonically non‑decreasing constraint** (\(\lambda_{i} ≤ λ_{i+1}\)), which cuts out almost all permutations that violate NTK’s frequency ordering;  
* **Seeding** the initial population with known baselines (PI, NTK, YaRN),

the **effective** neighbourhood remains far too big for exhaustive enumeration once $s>4.$

---

### Why this matters  

The authors therefore cast the problem as an **evolutionary search** (Algorithm 1). Evolutionary methods excel when:

* The objective function (here perplexity evaluated on a few long documents) is cheap enough to compute repeatedly,
* The feasible region is discrete and high‑dimensional,
* Simple random sampling would require astronomically many evaluations (e.g., >\(10^{167}\)).

The evolution then iteratively refines promising individuals while maintaining the monotonicity rule, allowing LongRoPE to locate useful “non‑uniform’’ rescaling vectors within seconds or minutes instead of never finishing an exhaustive grid‑search.

---

### Take‑away  

In short:

* The raw search space grows roughly as  
  \[
      |{\cal S}(s)|  
      = (\lfloor125\,s-99\floorright)^{
          d_{\text{rope}}/2}
        \times |\widehat N|.
  \]
* For typical models (\(d_{\text{rope}}=128,\ |\widehat N|=13{}\)) this yields  
  * ≈ \(4\times10^{167}\) candidates at $s=4×$,  
  * ≈ \(3\times10^{191}\) candidates at $s=8×$,  
  * …and quickly reaches astronomic magnitudes beyond that.
* Because it scales exponentially with both *extension ratio* and *model dimensionality*, brute‑force optimisation becomes infeasible even for modest ratios, motivating LongRoPE’s specialised evolutionary algorithm together with monotonic constraints.

These calculations explain why the authors emphasise “the search space that identifies rescale factors expands exponentially with the target extension ratio’’ and why their evolutionary approach is essential for exploring such an immense landscape efficiently.
