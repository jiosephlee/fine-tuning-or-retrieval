# From GPU to Phone: Running Finetuned LLMs on Consumer Devices  

Large language models have become spectacularly capable, yet their **fine‑tuning** remains tied to huge compute clusters. A full‑precision (FP16) fine‑tune of LLaMA‑65 B needs **more than 780 GB** of GPU memory—far beyond what any single workstation offers. The recent **QLoRA** method flips this narrative by compressing the base model to **4 bits**, keeping only a tiny set of trainable adapter weights, and still matching FP16 performance. The result? State‑of‑the‑art chatbots like **Guanaco** that you can fine‑tune on **one consumer GPU** and run comfortably on **a phone**. In this post we unpack how QLoRA achieves those savings and what it means for privacy‑preserving personal assistants that live entirely offline.  

---

## Why Fine‑tuning Was Out of Reach  

Fine‑tuning an LLM involves two heavy hitters:

1. **Storing the whole weight matrix** during back‑propagation so gradients can flow through it.
2. **Holding activation gradients** for every layer while processing even modest batch sizes.

For a decoder‑only model such as LLaMA‑65 B this adds up to **> 780 GB** of VRAM when using standard FP16 training—something only multi‑GPU servers can provide. Existing quantization tricks drastically shrink models **for inference**, but they break when you try to back‑propagate through the quantized weights (see Wortsman et al., 2023).

---

## The QLoRA Recipe  

QLoRA solves the problem by marrying three ideas that together cut the memory budget by an order of magnitude while keeping learning dynamics intact.

### 1️⃣ 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)  

* **What it does:** Quantizes each weight block into four bits whose levels are chosen from the *quantiles* of a standard normal distribution \(N(0,1)\).  
* **Why it matters:** Pretrained LLM weights are empirically zero‑centered and roughly Gaussian (§ Appendix A). By matching the distribution’s quantiles, NF4 achieves *information theoretic optimality*—every bin receives an equal share of values, drastically reducing quantization error compared with naïve Int4 or Float4 formats.  

### 2️⃣ Double Quantization  

Quantizing a weight block also stores a **scale factor** \(c\) per block. With blocksize 64 these scales would normally add \(0.5\) bits per parameter (\(\approx\!3\) GB for a 65 B model). Double quantization runs a second round of quantization **on those scales themselves**, using FP8 with blocksize 256. The net overhead drops from \(0.5\) → \(0.127\) bits per parameter—a saving of roughly **0.37 bits/param** without measurable performance loss (§ 4·b).

### 3️⃣ Paged Optimizers  

Gradient checkpointing already slashes activation memory, but long sequences still cause occasional spikes when optimizer states try to live entirely in VRAM. QLoRA leverages **NVIDIA’s unified memory** so optimizer buffers are automatically paged out to system RAM and swapped back when needed—effectively eliminating out‑of‑memory crashes without slowing down typical workloads (§ 4·c).

### Putting It All Together  

During forward/backward passes:

1. A weight tensor \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\) is *dequantized* → BFloat16.
2. The usual linear operation \(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}\) runs in BF16 precision.
3. Only the **Low‑Rank Adapter** matrices \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\) receive gradients—the full \(\mathbf{W}\) stays frozen.
   
Formally,
\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\,\underbrace{\text{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})}_{\displaystyle \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}}
+ \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}} .
\]

The storage type stays at *four bits*, while all compute happens in *brain floating point* (BF16). No gradients ever touch the massive base matrix—memory usage collapses dramatically.

## How Much Memory Do We Actually Save?  

| Model | Full FP16 Fine‑tune | QLoRA (NF4 + Double Quant.) | Memory Reduction |
|-------|-------------------|------------------------------|------------------|
| LLaMA 7 B | ≈ 30 GB | ≈ 6–8 GB* | ~80% |
| LLaMA 33 B | ≈ 110 GB | ≈ 20–22 GB | ~80% |
| LLaMA 65 B | ≈ 780 GB | \<48 GB | > 93% |

\*The exact figure depends on batch size & sequence length; gradient checkpointing plus paged optimizers keep peak VRAM under typical consumer limits.

Crucially, *performance does not drop*: Across GLUE, SuperNaturalInstructions, MMLU and Vicuna benchmarks QLoRA matches or exceeds its FP16 LoRA counterpart, achieving **99.3 %** of ChatGPT’s Vicuna score with the 65 B “Guanaco” model trained for just **24 h** on a single professional GPU (§ 6·c).

## Putting QLoRA on Everyday Hardware  

### 🖥️ Laptop / Desktop GPUs  

Modern laptops equipped with an RTX 3060 (12 GB) or RTX 3070 (8 GB mobile variant) can now:

* **Load a quantized 7 B base** (<5 GB at inference).
* **Add LoRA adapters** (~10–30 MB total).
* **Fine‑tune** using BF16 compute through PyTorch + BitsAndBytes—training runs comfortably within the available VRAM thanks to:
  * Blockwise NF4 storage,
  * Double‑quantized scales,
  * Paged optimizer buffers evicted to system RAM when needed.

Empirically, the authors report that “the smallest Guanaco model (7 B parameters) requires just 5 GB of memory” at inference time and “can be trained in less than 12 hours on a single consumer GPU” (§ 6·c). This means developers can experiment with instruction tuning locally—no cloud credits required.

### 📱 Smartphones  

Running inference already fits inside today’s mobile RAM budgets if we keep weights at NF4:

* A **7 B NM4 model occupies ≈​5 GB**, leaving room for other app data.
* On devices supporting BF16 or INT8 matrix kernels (Apple’s Neural Engine, Qualcomm Hexagon), forward passes execute at interactive latency (~200–400 ms per token).

The paper’s broader impact notes an estimate: an **iPhone 12 Plus could fine‑tune ~3 M tokens per night while charging**, effectively allowing periodic “on‑device updates” without ever uploading user text.  
Potential workflow:

1️⃣ Download a pre‑quantized Guanaco‑7B checkpoint onto your phone (`bitsandbytes`’s `load_8bit` API works offline).  
2️⃣ Record personal notes / domain‐specific documents locally.  
3️⃣ Run a short LoRA update (`lora_train.py` wrapped in TorchScript) overnight; only adapter weights change (~30 MB).  
4️⃣ Next day you have an assistant tuned specifically to your own vocabulary and preferences—all without touching any server.

## Privacy‑First Personal Assistants  

Because all data stays inside the device:

| Benefit | Traditional Cloud Assistant | On‑Device QLoRA Assistant |
|---------|----------------------------|----------------------------|
| Data exposure risk | High – logs sent over network | Negligible – data never leaves hardware |
| Customization latency | Days → weeks (server queue) | Hours → overnight updates |
| Offline operation | Impossible | Fully functional offline |
| Model ownership | Proprietary API token | User owns both base & adapters |

Imagine a medical app that remembers your medication schedule *without* transmitting PHI, or an education chatbot that adapts instantly based on your child’s progress logs stored locally—both become feasible once fine‑tuning fits within your own hardware envelope.

## Practical Tips If You Want To Try It Yourself  

1️⃣ **Install BitsAndBytes ≥0.41** (`pip install bitsandbytes`). It provides `bnb.nn.LinearNF4` which implements NF4 dequantization under the hood.

2️⃣ **Quantize your base checkpoint:**  
```python
from bitsandbytes import QuantLinear
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-2-7b")
bnb.quantize_model(model,
                   dtype="nf4",          # NormalFloat – optimal for normals
                   double_quant=True,
                   blocksize=64)
```

3️⃣ **Add LoRA adapters everywhere:**  
```python
from peft import get_peft_model, LoraConfig
config = LoraConfig(r=64,
                    lora_alpha=32,
                    target_modules=["q_proj","k_proj","v_proj","o_proj","up_proj","down_proj"],
                    bias="none")
model = get_peft_model(model, config)
```

4️⃣ **Pick a modest dataset:** High-quality instruction sets like OASST1 (~9k samples) outperform large noisy corpora—quality trumps quantity (§ 6·b).

5️⃣ **Train with paged optimizer:**  
```python
optimizer = bnb.optim.PagedAdamW(model.parameters(), lr=2e-5)
```

6️⃣ **Monitor VRAM:** Use `torch.cuda.memory_summary()` after each epoch; you should stay well below your GPU’s limit thanks to paging.

## Caveats & Open Challenges  

| Issue | Why It Still Matters |
|-------|---------------------|
| **Compute cost** – Training still takes many GPU-hours even though it fits in RAM.| Quantization shrinks storage but does not magically speed up matrix multiplies; BF16 ops dominate runtime.<br>|
| **Battery drain** – Fine‐tuning on phones consumes noticeable power.| The “≈3M tokens/night” estimate assumes charging mode.<br>|
| **Evaluation gaps** – Current chatbot benchmarks miss safety/fairness dimensions.| The authors note moderate agreement between GPT‑4 and human judges (§ 6·d).|
| **Further compression** – Going below four bits may work once adapters recover lost information.| Future work could explore three‐bit GPTQ + LoRA.<br>|

In short, QLoRA removes the *memory barrier*, but users should still budget for compute time and energy especially when scaling beyond seven‐billion parameters.

## Looking Ahead  

QLoRA demonstrates that “large = expensive” is no longer inevitable—by aligning quantization scientifically (NF4), compressing auxiliary data (double quant), and paging optimizer state we bring research‐grade fine‐tuning onto everyday hardware. Expected next steps include:

* Combining QLoRA with RLHF or preference learning while staying within consumer constraints.
* Extending double quantization across other PEFT methods such as IA³ or prompt tuning.
* Building lightweight evaluation suites tuned for privacy‐centric use cases rather than crowd‐sourced benchmarks alone.
* Integrating directly into mobile ML runtimes (CoreML, NNAPI) so users never need desktop tools at all.

If you’ve ever imagined customizing your own ChatGPT–style assistant without handing over data—or if you’re curious how far you can push an RTX 3060 laptop—QLoRA opens that door today. Grab the open–source code (`github.com/artidoro/qlora`), give your favorite open LLM a few hundred thousand tokens of personal data, and watch it become *your* private conversational partner—all from the comfort of your own device.
