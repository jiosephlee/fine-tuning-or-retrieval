# Mining Math from the Web: How DeepSeekMath Built a **120‑Billion‑Token** Corpus  

Mathematical reasoning still lags behind large language models’ impressive language abilities. A big part of the gap is the scarcity of clean, structured math data at scale. While many groups resort to curated collections—textbook excerpts, arXiv papers, or manually labelled problem sets—DeepSeekMath shows that **public web archives can be turned into a world‑class math dataset** with the right pipeline. Below we walk through the step‑by‑step process that transformed the noisy Common Crawl dump into a **120 billion token** “DeepSeekMath Corpus”, why this approach works, and how it fuels the strong performance of their 7 B parameter model.

---

## Why Start From Common Crawl?

* **Sheer volume** – The Common Crawl (CC) snapshot contains billions of HTML pages covering virtually every publicly available website.  
* **Multilingual coverage** – Unlike many academic corpora that are almost exclusively English, CC naturally hosts Chinese math forums, Japanese lecture notes, Russian problem archives, etc., giving a built‑in multilingual boost.  
* **Free and open** – No licensing restrictions or costly annotation campaigns are needed; only a reliable filtering mechanism is required to separate signal from noise.

The challenge is turning this raw ocean into a **high‑precision** collection of mathematical content while avoiding test‐set leakage.

---

## A FastText Classifier as the First Sieve

DeepSeekMath’s engineers began with a modest seed set—**OpenWebMath**, an existing collection of high‑quality math web texts (~13 GB). Using it they trained a lightweight **fastText** text classifier:

| Component | Settings |
|-----------|----------|
| Positive samples | Randomly pick **500 k** documents from OpenWebMath |
| Negative samples | Randomly pick **500 k** unrelated CC pages |
| Word vector dimensionality | **256** |
| Learning rate | **0.1** |
| Max n‑gram length | **3** |
| Minimum word count | **3** |
| Epochs | **3** |

FastText excels at capturing bag‑of‑words patterns quickly; it became the workhorse that scores every page in CC for “math‐likeness”.

### Deduplication Before Scoring  

Before classification they dramatically shrink the crawl:

* **URL based deduplication** – identical links are removed.  
* **Near‑deduplication** – very similar webpages are collapsed using fuzzy hashing techniques.

After these steps the archive drops from hundreds of billions of raw HTML pages to **≈ 40 billion** distinct pages—still huge but tractable enough for batch classification.

---

## First Pass: Scoring & Ranking

The trained fastText model runs over all deduplicated pages and assigns each a probability score \(p(\text{math} \mid \text{page})\). Pages are then sorted by this score and only the highest‐ranked ones are retained for training experiments.  

To decide how much data to keep they run preliminary pretraining runs on four token budgets:

* top 40 B  
* top 80 B  
* top 120 B  
* top 160 B  

The early experiments suggest that keeping roughly **the top 40 billion tokens** already yields strong downstream gains, so they start there while planning iterative improvements.

---

## Iterative Domain Expansion – Four Rounds of Mining

The first pass inevitably misses many useful sites because the classifier’s positives are limited to OpenWebMath’s style. To broaden coverage they repeat a loop that refines both **the seed corpus** and **the classifier**:

1️⃣ **Domain grouping:** Every page is assigned a domain defined by its base URL (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`).  

2️⃣ **Domain purity check:** For each domain they compute the proportion of its pages already selected in the previous round. Domains where **> 10 %** were collected are flagged as “math related”.  

3️⃣ **Human annotation:** Annotators inspect these promising domains and explicitly label which URL paths contain mathematical content (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions/…`).  

4️⃣ **Seed augmentation:** Uncollected pages reachable through those annotated URLs are added to the positive set.  

5️⃣ **Classifier retraining:** The fastText model is re‐trained with the enlarged positive pool, yielding a richer representation of math language patterns.

Repeating this cycle four times yields:

* **35.5 million** distinct math webpages  
* A total size of **≈ 120 billion tokens**

After the third iteration they notice that around **98 %** of candidates have already been captured—diminishing returns—so they stop after the fourth round.

---

## Cleaning Up: Preventing Benchmark Contamination

A crucial final step ensures that downstream evaluation remains fair:

* For every benchmark used later (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval etc.) they extract all possible contiguous substrings from its test items.  
* Any training segment containing an exact match of a **$10$‑gram** substring is stripped out entirely.  
* For shorter substrings ($3$–$9$ grams) they also apply exact matches when present.

This aggressive decontamination guarantees that DeepSeekMath never sees a verbatim answer or problem statement from its evaluation set during pretraining.

---

## What Makes This Corpus Special?

### 1️⃣ Quality Over Quantity  

Even though other large corpora exist—e.g., MathPile (~8 billion tokens), Proof-Pile‑2 (~42 billion tokens)—the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperforms them when models are fine‐tuned on an identical number of tokens. Few‐shot chain‑of‑thought evaluations across eight benchmarks show clear gains over every baseline corpus tested.

### 2️⃣ Multilingual Boost  

Because common‐crawl harvesting does not filter by language, substantial Chinese mathematics material slips into the collection (forums such as `zhihu.com` mathematics sections). Evaluation on Chinese benchmarks (**CMATH**, MGSM‑zh) shows noticeable improvements—a benefit absent in English‑only corpora like MathPile or Minerva’s dataset.

### 3️⃣ Scale Matters—But Not Through Model Size  

The final corpus is roughly **7× larger** than Minerva’s web portion and **9× larger** than OpenWebMath alone. Remarkably, pretraining a modestly sized (**7 B**) model on this data achieves performance comparable to closed‐source giants such as Minerva 540 B or Gemini Ultra on competition‐level tests like MATH—a strong argument that “right data” can outweigh “more parameters”.

---

## Feeding DeepSeekMath

With the corpus ready they continue training an existing code‐oriented LLM (**DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5**, 7 B parameters). The overall token mix during continued pretraining is:

| Source | Approximate Share |
|--------|-------------------|
| DeepSeekMath Corpus | **56 %** |
| AlgebraicStack code | 4 % |
| arXiv papers | 10 % |
| GitHub code | 20 % |
| General NL (English + Chinese) | 10 % |

After another **500 billion** token pass — half devoted again to pure mathematics — the resulting model (**DeepSeekMath‑Base 7 B**) exhibits:

* GSM8K accuracy ≈ 64 %  
* Competition‐level MATH accuracy ≈ 36 %  
* Better performance than Minerva’s 540 B parameter baseline despite being over seventy times smaller  

These gains carry over to general reasoning tasks (MMLU‐STEM) and coding benchmarks (HumanEval), confirming that intensive mathematics exposure also lifts broader reasoning abilities.

---

## Key Takeaways

1️⃣ **Public web archives hold massive untapped math knowledge**, provided you apply systematic filtering rather than relying on handpicked textbooks alone.  

2️⃣ A simple yet well‐tuned binary classifier—fastText—combined with iterative domain analysis can harvest high‐precision data without training massive neural filters first.  

3️⃣ Deduplication and stringent decontamination are essential; otherwise gains may simply reflect memorization rather than reasoning improvements.  

4️⃣ Multilingual inclusion dramatically widens applicability—models trained on this corpus excel on both English and Chinese math tasks without extra language‐specific engineering.  

5️⃣ Scaling up specialized data can compensate for limited parameter budgets: focused pretraining makes a modest 7 B model competitive with closed‐source behemoths on challenging benchmarks like MATH.

The DeepSeekMath effort demonstrates that an open research community can achieve near state-of-the-art mathematical reasoning simply by mining smarter—not bigger—data from publicly available sources. As more groups adopt similar pipelines—and refine them with better classifiers or richer annotation strategies—we may soon see open LLMs that rival proprietary systems even on advanced mathematical reasoning tasks.



# From Code to Calculus: Why Starting with a Coder Model Supercharges Math Reasoning  

Mathematical reasoning has long been a stumbling block for open‑source large language models (LLMs). While closed‑source giants such as GPT‑4 or Gemini‑Ultra dominate the leaderboard, recent work on **DeepSeekMath** shows that a modest $7$ B‑parameter model can close the gap—*if you start from a coder‑oriented foundation*.  

In this post we unpack the surprising boost that **pre‑training on source code** gives to pure math problem solving. We walk through the experiments that compare a “code‑first” pipeline against a generic language pipeline, discuss why algorithmic thinking transfers into step‑by‑step reasoning, and illustrate how this foundation powers both **self‑contained** solutions and **tool‑assisted** calculations.

---

## 1. The Core Idea: Build Math on Top of Code  

DeepSeekMath‑Base $7$B is initialized from **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 $7$B**, a model already trained extensively on programming data (GitHub repositories, code snippets, etc.). The authors then continue pre‑training on a *massive* math corpus—**$120$ B** tokens harvested from Common Crawl—while still blending in natural language and coding data.  

Why does this matter?  

- **Code training forces deterministic, stepwise generation.** Writing correct code requires adhering to precise syntax, managing variables consistently, and ordering operations logically—exactly the discipline needed for rigorous mathematical proofs or chain‑of‑thought solutions.  
- **Programming habits give models a ready “toolbox.”** A model that already knows how to emit executable Python (e.g., using `math` or `sympy`) can more easily adopt *Program‑of‑Thought* (PoT) prompting where it writes a short script then executes it for the final answer.  

---

## 2. Experimental Setup: Code vs. General Pre‑Training  

The authors performed four controlled training regimes on a $1.3$ B base model (DeepSeek‑LLM $1.3$B) before scaling up:

| Regime | First Phase (Tokens) | Second Phase (Tokens) | Goal |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------------|------|
| **Code → Math** | $400\,$B *code* tokens | $150\,$B *math* tokens | Test if code pretraining helps later math learning |
| **General → Math** | $400\,$B *general* language tokens | $150\,$B *math* tokens | Baseline without code exposure |
| **Pure Math** | — | $150\,$B *math* tokens only | Pure math pretraining |
| **Mixed Code+Math** | $400\,$B *code* + $150\,$B *math* mixed in one stage | — | Examine if simultaneous exposure avoids catastrophic forgetting |

All phases used identical optimization settings (AdamW, batch size ≈ 4 M tokens, context length 4 K) so differences can be attributed purely to data composition.

---

## 3. What the Numbers Tell Us  

While the paper only reports tables for brevity, the narrative highlights clear trends:

### 3.1 Program‑Aided Reasoning Benefits  
- **Two‑stage Code → Math** already outperforms General → Math on *Python‐based* benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH when models are asked to generate executable scripts.  
- Adding the second math phase yields an additional lift—meaning that **code pretraining gives an early advantage**, which further math exposure amplifies.

### 3.2 Step‑by‑Step Reasoning Improves Even Without Tools  
- After just the initial code phase (no math yet), models show moderate gains on plain chain‑of‑thought (CoT) tasks compared to General → Math equivalents—demonstrating that **algorithmic discipline transfers directly to textual reasoning**.
- The full two‐stage pipeline (code then math) achieves the *best* scores across both CoT and PoT evaluations, surpassing even larger models that lack any coding background.

### 3.3 Mixing Code & Math Mitigates Forgetting  
- When data are mixed in one stage (`400` B code + `150` B math), catastrophic forgetting of coding ability is reduced compared to a naïve two–stage approach where coding performance decays after heavy math fine‐tuning.
- However, at this model scale (`1.3` B), fully mastering both domains simultaneously remains challenging; the two–stage approach still yields higher pure‐math scores.

> **Bottom line:** Starting from a coder model consistently beats starting from a generic language model—both when we later specialize in mathematics and when we want the model to retain its coding chops.

---

## 4. Why Does “Code First” Translate Into Better Math?  

### 4.1 Structured Generation Mirrors Logical Proofs  
When you write a function:
```python
def solve(a,b):
    return a + b
```
the model must keep track of variable names (`a`, `b`), maintain correct indentation, and guarantee that each line contributes toward a correct output. This mirrors how one constructs a proof:
1️⃣ Declare symbols  
2️⃣ Apply definitions or lemmas  
3️⃣ Derive intermediate results  

The internal representation learned during code training therefore aligns naturally with *stepwise logical inference*, making it easier for the LLM to emit coherent CoT explanations.

### 4.2 Familiarity With Numerical APIs Reduces “Tool Friction”  
A coder model already knows how to call `math.sqrt`, manipulate `sympy` expressions, or loop over ranges—actions essential for solving many quantitative problems that would otherwise require cumbersome manual arithmetic in plain text. Consequently:
- Prompted with PoT instructions (“solve using Python”), the model produces syntactically correct scripts almost out of the box.
- The downstream execution engine returns accurate numerical answers, feeding back into self‑consistency methods that boost performance further (e.g., self-consistency raises DeepSeekMath’s MATH score from ≈ 51% → 60%).

### 4? Extra Bonus: Transfer To General Reasoning  
The paper observes that **math pretraining also lifts MMLU and BBH scores**, suggesting that algorithmic fluency learned from coding spills over into broader reasoning tasks—not just arithmetic but logical deduction across domains.

---

## 5. Putting It All Together: A Simple Recipe  

If you’re building an open‐source math LLM today, consider this three‑step pipeline:

1️⃣ **Start From A Strong Coder Base** – Choose an existing code‐focused LLM (e.g., DeepSeek‑Coder or CodeLlama). Its token-level knowledge about syntax and algorithmic patterns provides a solid scaffold.
2️⃣ **Continue Pretraining On High‑Quality Math Corpus** – Use web‐mined math data (the DeepSeekMath Corpus gives us $120$ B tokens) while retaining some coding token proportion (~10–20%). This “math” phase refines domain knowledge without erasing programming habits.
3️⃣ **Instruction Tune + Light RL** – Fine‐tune on chain‐of‐thought / program‐of‐thought instruction data; optionally apply GRPO reinforcement learning for a final edge (the authors see jumps of ~5% absolute on GSM8K).

Following this recipe yields a $7$ B model that **rivals closed models on competition‐level benchmarks**, all while staying fully open source.

---

## 6. Takeaways  

- **Code pretraining ≠ just better at coding; it’s a catalyst for mathematical thinking** because both tasks demand disciplined sequential generation.
- A two–stage “code first → math later” schedule consistently outperforms “general language first → math later,” especially for tool–integrated tasks.
- Mixing code & math can preserve coding ability but may slightly compromise pure mathematical depth at current scales; future research may explore larger models or curriculum strategies.
- The synergy between code and math extends beyond arithmetic—it improves general reasoning benchmarks too, hinting at deeper representational benefits.

In short, if you want your next open-source LLM to excel at calculus as well as at compiling C++, give it plenty of *code* before you feed it equations. The path from **coder** to **calculist** looks increasingly short—and remarkably effective. 🚀



# Teaching LLMs to Think Like Mathematicians: Chain‑of‑Thought, Program‑of‑Thought & Tool Integration  

Large language models have become surprisingly good at solving math problems, but they still lag far behind proprietary systems such as GPT‑4 or Gemini‑Ultra. **DeepSeekMath 7B** narrows that gap by combining three complementary instruction‑tuning paradigms:

1. **Plain chain‑of‑thought (CoT)** – step‑by‑step textual reasoning.  
2. **Program‑of‑thought (PoT)** – generating executable Python code that computes the answer.  
3. **Hybrid tool‑integrated reasoning** – weaving natural‐language explanations together with live calls to a Python interpreter or other tools.  

Below we break down each paradigm, show how they are constructed from a multilingual math corpus, and illustrate why they move the needle on both English and Chinese benchmarks.

---

## Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Hard for LLMs  

Mathematics demands precise symbol manipulation, multi‑step logical deductions, and often exact numeric computation—all of which are fragile when generated purely as free text. A small slip (“\(12\)” vs “\(21\)”) instantly invalidates a solution even if the surrounding logic is perfect.  
DeepSeekMath tackles these issues in three stages:

1. **Massive math pre‑training** – 120 B tokens harvested from Common Crawl using a fastText classifier trained on OpenWebMath pages.
2. **Mathematical instruction tuning** – 776 K curated examples covering English + Chinese problems in CoT, PoT, and tool–integrated formats.
3. **Reinforcement learning** – Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) refines the model by rewarding higher‑scoring outputs without needing an extra value network.

The result is a **7 B parameter** model that reaches **51.7 %** accuracy on the competition‐level *MATH* benchmark without any external ensembles—a level previously seen only in closed systems.

---

## The Three Instruction‑Tuning Paradigms  

### 1️⃣ Plain Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)

**What it looks like:**  
The model writes out its reasoning linearly—“First … then … finally …”. No code runs; everything is expressed in natural language yet stays fully self‐contained.

**Why it helps:**  
CoT forces the network to expose intermediate mental states rather than “jumping” straight to an answer. This regularizes generation and dramatically reduces hallucinations where the final numeral happens by chance rather than through valid deduction.

**Performance boost:**  
On *GSM8K* (grade-school arithmetic) DeepSeekMath’s CoT version climbs from a vanilla baseline ≈ 82 % up to **88 %** after reinforcement learning (GRPO). On the more demanding *MATH* benchmark it lifts scores from sub‑40 % baselines past **46 %** (instruction tuned) and finally **51.7 %** after GRPO—enough to beat every other open source model under a pure text setting.

---

### 2️⃣ Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)

**What it looks like:**  
Instead of writing out algebraic manipulations by hand, the model produces a short Python snippet that solves the problem when executed:

```python
import sympy as sp
x = sp.symbols('x')
solution = sp.solve(sp.Eq(2*x**2 - 5*x + 3, 0), x)
print(solution)
```

The output of this script (`[1/2, 3]`) becomes the final answer.

**Why it helps:**  
Two forces converge here:
* **Code pre‑training** – DeepSeekMath starts from *DeepSeek‐Coder* which already knows syntax and standard libraries.
* **Exact computation** – Python’s numeric libraries eliminate rounding errors or faulty arithmetic that plague pure text generation.

When evaluated under *program‑aided* prompting on GSM8K / MATH, DeepSeekMath’s PoT version beats prior state‐of‐the‐art Llama/Mistral based models such as Llemma 34B by several points (see Table S in the paper). The gain is most visible on tasks requiring precise algebraic manipulation or symbolic integration where human‐written steps are error prone but a short script is trivially correct.

---

### 3️⃣ Hybrid Tool‑Integrated Reasoning  

**What it looks like:**  
A prompt asks for an explanation *and* an explicit call to a tool (“run this piece of code”). The model replies with interleaved text and execution traces:

```
We first rewrite the integral using symmetry:
∫₀^π sin(x)/x dx = ∫₀^π sin(x)/x dx

Now we let Python evaluate it:
>>> import mpmath as mp
>>> mp.quad(lambda t: mp.sin(t)/t, [0, mp.pi])
1.85193705198247
```

The numeric result (`1.8519…`) is taken as the final answer while the preceding natural‐language part explains why this approach works.

**Why it helps:**  
Complex tasks often combine conceptual insight (“use symmetry”) with heavy computation (“evaluate an integral numerically”). By letting the model decide when to hand off work to an interpreter it can keep its textual reasoning clean while still leveraging exact arithmetic where needed.

When *tool use* is permitted during evaluation, DeepSeekMath’s accuracy jumps dramatically—self–consistency over just **64 sampled outputs** lifts *MATH* scores from **51.7 %** up to **60.9 %**, eclipsing every other open source contender at comparable scale (see Figure 1 in Sec 4).

---

## How These Styles Translate Across Languages  

DeepSeekMath’s pre-training data is multilingual (≈ 90 % English + notable Chinese portion). Consequently both CoT and PoT generalize well:

| Benchmark | Language | Baseline (Base 7B) | After CoT Instr.| After PoT / Tool | Best Δ |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|-----------------|------------------|--------|
| GSM8K | EN | ≈ 82 % | **88 %** (GRPO) | — | +6 pts |
| MATH | EN | ≈ 46 % | **51 %** (CoT+RL) | **≈ 60 %** w/ tool+self‐consistency | +14 pts |
| CMATH | CN | ≈ 44 % | **≈ 55 %** | — | +11 pts |
| MGSM‐zh | CN | ≈ 48 % | **≈ 60 %** | — | +12 pts |

*(Numbers are rounded from the paper’s tables.)*  
Clearly each paradigm lifts both English and Chinese test sets—in particular the hybrid style narrows the gap between languages because numerical computation itself is language agnostic while explanations are learned jointly from bilingual data.

---

## A Mini Demo: Solving One Problem Three Ways  

> **Problem:** Find the area of a triangle whose side lengths are \(3\), \(4\) and \(5\).

### CoT Solution  
1️⃣ Compute semiperimeter \(s = \frac{3+4+5}{2}=6\).  
2️⃣ Apply Heron’s formula \(A=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\).  
  Plugging values gives \(A=\sqrt{6\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1}=6\).  
✅ Answer: \(6\).

### PoT Solution  

```python
import math
a,b,c = 3,4,5
s = (a+b+c)/2
area = math.sqrt(s*(s-a)*(s-b)*(s-c))
print(area)      # → 6.0
```

Running this snippet yields `6.0`, confirming the CoT result exactly without any algebraic slip-ups.

### Hybrid Tool Integration  

```
We know this is a right triangle because 3²+4²=5²,
so its area is simply half of base×height:
area = 0.5 * 3 * 4 = 6.
(To double-check we ask Python:)

>>> area = 0.5 * 3 * 4
>>> area
6
```

The model reasons conceptually (“right triangle”) then asks Python for an elementary multiplication check—a pattern that mirrors how many humans solve geometry problems in practice.

All three styles arrive at the same correct answer, but their internal processes differ enough that training on each one teaches complementary skills—textual logic flow, precise coding syntax, and smooth handoff between language understanding and external computation.


---

## Reinforcement Learning Tightens Everything Together  

Even after thorough instruction tuning there remains room for improvement because some generated solutions still contain subtle logical flaws or inefficient code snippets. DeepSeekMath adopts **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**:

* Samples multiple completions per question (`G=64`).  
* Uses a learned reward model only for ranking inside each group—no separate critic network needed → lower memory footprint than classic PPO.  
* Adds a KL penalty directly between policy & reference models instead of inflating token‐level rewards—simpler training dynamics.

Applying GRPO solely on existing instruction data pushes GSM8K up an extra point (**88 → 88.2 %**) while lifting *MATH* from **46.8 %→51.7 %**, matching our earlier tabled figures.


---

## Takeaways & Next Steps  

1️⃣ **Multiple solution formats are complementary** – CoT teaches logical scaffolding; PoT injects exact computation ability; tool integration blends them seamlessly.
2️⃣ **Multilingual math corpora matter** – Including high‐quality Chinese web pages lifted Chinese benchmark scores far beyond prior English-only pipelines.
3️⃣ **Efficient RL can still improve modestly sized models** – GRPO achieves meaningful gains without exploding GPU memory budgets.
4️⃣ **Geometry & formal theorem proving remain open challenges** – The paper notes weaker performance there; richer geometric corpora and stronger proof assistants will be needed.
5️⃣ **Future RL research will focus on better reward modeling**, robust handling of noisy preferences, and advanced sampling strategies such as tree search—a promising direction hinted by self–consistency gains already observed.


DeepSeekMath demonstrates that even a relatively small open source LLM can think like a mathematician when taught via structured prompting styles paired with smart reinforcement learning tricks. As repositories of clean web mathematics continue to grow—and as we refine RL pipelines—the line between proprietary “black box” solvers and transparent community models will keep getting blurrier.

# Group Relative Policy Optimization: A Leaner Way to Reinforce Math Skills  

*Reinforcement learning can make language models better at math, but classic algorithms like PPO waste memory on a separate critic network. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) sidesteps that waste by using the answers themselves as a baseline—saving resources while pushing benchmark scores higher.*

---

## Why Reinforcement Learning for Math?

Even after careful instruction‑tuning, large language models still stumble on competition‑level math problems such as those in the **MATH** benchmark. A small dose of reinforcement learning (RL) can bridge this gap by rewarding correct reasoning patterns and penalizing mistakes, nudging the model toward more reliable solutions.

The most common RL recipe for language models is **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** \citep{schulman2017proximal}. PPO works well, but it carries a hidden cost:

* **Critic model** – A value network that predicts the expected reward for every token (the “baseline”).  
* **Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)** – A statistical trick that combines future rewards with the critic’s prediction.

Both components require additional parameters and GPU memory, which becomes prohibitive at even modest model sizes (e.g., a 7 B parameter model). This overhead limits how many researchers can experiment with RL‑enhanced math reasoning.

---

## Enter Group Relative Policy Optimization

GRPO replaces the critic with a much simpler idea: **use the average reward of several sampled answers as the baseline**. In other words, instead of learning a separate value function, we let the *group* of answers itself tell us what “average performance” looks like.

### The core steps

1️⃣ **Sample a group** – For each math question \(q\), draw \(G\) candidate solutions \(\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\) from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). In our experiments we set \(G=64\).

2️⃣ **Score each answer** – Run a learned reward model \(r_\varphi\) on every generated output to obtain scalar rewards \(\{r_1,\dots,r_G\}\).

3️⃣ **Normalize within the group** – Compute  
\[
\widetilde{r}_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{r})},
\]  
so each answer’s advantage is measured *relative* to its peers.

4️⃣ **Assign advantages** –   
* *Outcome supervision*: every token in answer \(o_i\) inherits \(\widetilde{r}_i\) as its advantage \(\hat A_{i,t}\).  
* *Process supervision*: if we have step‑wise rewards (e.g., after each reasoning line), we sum normalized step rewards that occur after token \(t\).

5️⃣ **Update the policy** – Optimize the usual clipped PPO objective, but replace the critic‑based advantage with \(\hat A_{i,t}\). The KL‑regularization term is added *directly* to the loss:
\[
\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]
=
\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}}
-\log\!\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} -1,
\]
which avoids mixing KL penalties into token‑level rewards.

The resulting update rule looks just like PPO’s familiar expression:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{q,\{o_i\}}\!
\Bigg[
\frac1G\sum_i
\frac1{|o_i|}
\sum_{t}
\!\Bigl(
\min[
\rho_{i,t}\,\hat A_{i,t},\, 
\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_{i,t}
]
-\beta\, \mathbb D_{KL}\!
\Bigr)
\Bigg],
\]

where \(\rho_{i,t}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}\).

### What disappears?

| Component | PPO | GRPO |
|-----------|------|------|
| Separate critic network | Yes | No |
| GAE computation | Yes | No |
| Extra memory per token | ~0.5 GB (≈30 M parameters) | Negligible |
| Training pipeline complexity | Multi‑model coordination | Single‑model loop |

By eliminating the value head and its optimizer, GRPO slashes GPU memory usage by roughly half while keeping essentially the same clipped‑policy gradient machinery.

---

## How Easy Is It to Plug In?

From an engineering standpoint you only need three extra lines compared to vanilla PPO:

```python
# Sample a batch of questions
answers = [policy.sample(q) for q in batch]          # G answers per q
rewards = reward_model.score(answers)                # r_i
norm_rewards = (rewards - rewards.mean()) / rewards.std()
advantages = norm_rewards.unsqueeze(-1).expand_as(answers)   # outcome version

# Compute clipped surrogate loss + KL term (same as PPO)
loss = clipped_policy_loss(policy, old_policy, answers, advantages)
loss += beta * kl_divergence(policy, ref_policy)
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
```

No extra optimizer for a value network, no GAE buffers—just standard PyTorch code plus a tiny group‑normalization step.

---

## Does It Actually Help?

Yes—GRGR’s leaner approach translates into measurable gains on hard math benchmarks:

| Model | GSM8K (CoT) | MATH (competition level) | CMATH (Chinese) |

---

## 4. Group Relative Policy Optimization – why it matters  

### 4.1 From PPO to GRPO  

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) optimizes an actor–critic objective:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q,o}\!\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}\!\min\!\Big(\rho_t A_t,\ \text{clip}(\rho_t,\!1-\varepsilon,\!1+\varepsilon)A_t\Big)\right],
\]

where \(\rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}\) and \(A_t\) comes from a learned value function \(V_\psi\).

Training a separate value network at the scale of LLMs is memory intensive and often noisy because rewards are usually defined only at the final token.

### 4️⃣ GRPO’s key simplifications  

GRPO replaces the learned baseline with a *group-relative* baseline:

1. For each question \(q\) sample a *group* of \(G\) completions \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\) from the current policy.
2. Compute rewards \(r_i = r_\varphi(q,o_i)\) using a reward model (or rule‐based correctness).
3. Normalize within the group: \(\tilde r_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\text{std}(\mathbf r)}\).
4. Set every token’s advantage \(\hat A_{i,t} = \tilde r_i\) (outcome supervision) or sum stepwise normalized rewards for process supervision.
5. Add an explicit KL regularization term \(\beta\, D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}}]\).

Because the baseline is computed *on the fly* from sampled outputs, no extra critic network is needed—saving several GB of GPU memory per training replica.

### 4️⃣ Empirical impact  

Applying GRPO on top of DeepSeekMath–Instruct yields:

* GSM8K ↑ from **82.9 %** → **88.2 %**
* MATH ↑ from **46.8 %** → **51.7 %**

These lifts surpass all other open-source models ranging from 7 B to 70 B parameters, and they come *without* expanding the training data beyond what was used for instruction tuning.

## Why Language Diversity Matters in Mathematical Reasoning

* **Chinese‐language problem wording** (“求解方程 …” instead of “solve the equation …”),

To prevent leakage from public benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH…​), any segment containing a 10‑gram match to those test sets was stripped out before pre‑training continued. This ensures reported gains truly stem from learned reasoning ability rather than memorization.

# A Unified Lens on Fine‑Tuning & Reinforcement Learning for LLMs

Large language models have become surprisingly adept at solving math problems—but the way we **fine‑tune** them matters just as much as their raw scale.  
The DeepSeekMath paper proposes a clean way to think about every fine‑tuning or reinforcement‑learning (RL) recipe under **three ingredients**:

1. **Data source** – where the training examples come from;  
2. **Reward function** – what signal tells the model “this answer is good”;  
3. **Algorithm** – how we turn data + reward into parameter updates.

In this post we walk through that framework, show how classic methods like SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO and the newly introduced **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** slot into it, explain why RL tends to boost **Maj@K** more than **Pass@K**, and sketch promising directions for making RL more robust.

---

## 1️⃣ The Three Building Blocks

### 1.1 Data Source  

All training pipelines start by drawing a pair \((q,o)\) – a *question* \(q\) and a *response* \(o\).

| Paradigm | Sampling mode | Typical provenance |
|----------|---------------|--------------------|
| **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)** | *Offline* | Human‑written instruction data |
| **Rejection Sampling FT (RFT)** | *Offline* | Outputs sampled **once** from an already‑trained SFT model |
| **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** | *Offline* | Same as RFT; paired “better vs worse” samples |
| **Online RFT / PPO / GRPO** | *Online* | Outputs are drawn *during training* from the *current* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) |

*Offline* means the data never changes after the initial sampling pass; *online* indicates a fresh rollout each optimization step, allowing the model to chase its own moving target.

### 1.2 Reward Function  

Two families dominate:

| Type | How it’s built | What it judges |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| **Rule‑based** | Direct answer check (e.g., exact match) | Binary “correct/incorrect” |
| **Model‑based** | Learned reward model \(r_{\varphi}\) trained on pairwise human preferences or rule judgments | Continuous scores that can reflect subtle reasoning quality |

Model‑based rewards enable **process supervision**, where each intermediate reasoning step receives its own score, versus the coarse end‑of‑generation signal of rule‑based rewards.

### 1.3 Algorithm  

All methods ultimately adjust parameters \(\theta\) according to a gradient of the form

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}
GC(q,o,t,\pi_{\text{ref}})\;
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(o_t\,|\,q,o_{<t})\right],
\tag{1}
\]

where \(GC(\cdot)\) is a *gradient coefficient* that encodes how much credit or penalty each token receives—its shape depends on the chosen algorithm and reward.

---

## 2️⃣ Mapping Popular Methods onto the Unified View

Below we list each method’s concrete choices for the three components above:

### 2️⃣ Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)

* **Data:** Human‐curated instruction pairs \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}\).  
* **Reward:** Implicitly “maximum likelihood” – no external reward; \(GC = 1\).  
* **Algorithm:** Standard cross‑entropy loss (MLE).  

SFT simply tells the model “this is how you should write,” without any explicit notion of correctness beyond what humans supplied.

### 2️⃣ Rejection Sampling FT (RFT)

* **Data:** Offline samples \(\{o_i\}\) drawn once from an SFT checkpoint; keep only those that pass a rule check (e.g., answer is correct).  
* **Reward:** Binary rule = \(1\) for kept outputs, \(0\) otherwise; gradients scaled by this mask.  
* **Algorithm:** MLE on retained examples only – a hard filter rather than weighting.

RFT thus trades quantity for quality by discarding any generated answer that fails an explicit test.

### 2️⃣ Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

* **Data:** Offline pairs \((o^{+},o^{-})\) where \(o^{+}\) beats \(o^{-}\) according to human preferences or rule scores.  
* **Reward:** Implicitly encoded in the pairwise comparison; no separate reward model needed.  
* **Algorithm:** Pairwise loss \(-\log\sigma(s^{+}\!-\!s^{-})\), where \(s = \log\pi_{\theta}(o)\). This yields a gradient coefficient proportional to \(\sigma(s^{+}\!-\!s^{-})\).

DPO can be seen as a direct “relative” reinforcement signal without training an explicit value network.

### 2️⃣ Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

* **Data:** Online rollouts from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\); each rollout yields a trajectory \((q,o)\).  
* **Reward:** Model‑based through \(r_{\varphi}(q,o)\); optionally per‑token KL penalty with reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
* **Algorithm:** Actor–critic surrogate objective  

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\min\Bigl(r_t(\theta)A_t,\;\text{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)A_t\Bigr)
-\beta\,D_{KL}(\pi_\theta\,||\,\pi_{\text{ref}})
\right],
\]  

where \(r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_t)}\) and \(A_t\) comes from a learned value function \(V_{\psi}\).

PPO introduces an explicit *critic* \(V_{\psi}\) to reduce variance in estimating advantages \(A_t\). This makes PPO memory hungry because both actor and critic must be kept in GPU memory.

### 2️⃣ Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO was proposed precisely to keep PPO’s spirit while shedding its heavyweight critic:

* **Data:** Online rollouts grouped per question – for each question \(q\), sample a *set* \(\{o_1,\dots,o_G\}\).  
* **Reward:** Model‑based scores \(\{r_i=r_\varphi(q,o_i)\}\). The baseline is simply the group mean, i.e.,  

\[
\hat A_{i,t}= \frac{r_i-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j} r_j}{\text{std}(\mathbf r)} ,
\]  

applied uniformly across all tokens in output \(o_i\). No separate value network is required.  
* **Algorithm:** Same PPO‐style clipped ratio term but with advantage \(\hat A_{i,t}\); KL regularization is handled by an explicit term  

\[
-\beta\, D_{KL}[\pi_\theta\,||\,\pi_{\text{ref}}].
\]

Because baselines are computed *within each group*, GRPO leverages exactly the comparative nature of most reward models—no extra parameters are needed, saving RAM while still providing variance reduction.

---

## 3️⃣ Why Reinforcement Learning Improves Maj@K More Than Pass@K

DeepSeekMath’s experiments reveal an intriguing pattern: after RL, the **majority vote at K** (\(Maj@K\)) climbs significantly while single‑sample accuracy (\(Pass@K=Pass@1\)) barely moves.

Think of the model’s output distribution over possible completions as a probability vector \(\mathbf p = (p_1,\dots,p_N)\). Before RL many tokens are scattered thinly across many wrong completions; after RL most probability mass concentrates on a *few high‑quality candidates*. When you sample multiple times (\(K>1\)) and take a majority vote among them, you’re effectively asking whether at least one of those top candidates lands in the top‑\(K\) draws—hence thanks to this mass shift,
\(Maj@K = \Pr[\text{majority correct among } K]\)
gets larger even though
\(Pass@1 = p_{\text{best}}\)
stays roughly constant.

The authors corroborate this with analysis on GSM8K/MATH: RL raised Maj@64 dramatically but left Pass@64 unchanged, indicating that RL mainly “sharpened” the distribution rather than introduced new reasoning capabilities. This aligns with other findings in LLM alignment research—RL aligns *preferences*, not raw problem‑solving skill per se.

---

## 4️⃣ Towards More Robust Reinforcement Learning

The unified picture also spotlights three levers where future work can tighten the pipeline:

### 4.1 Better Data Sources

- **Diverse Question Pools:** Current RL used only instruction‑tuning questions (GSM8K & MATH). Adding out‑of‑distribution prompts could prevent overfitting to familiar styles.
- **Advanced Decoding for Exploration:** Instead of plain nucleus sampling, tree‑search or Monte Carlo rollouts can generate richer candidate sets per question.
- **Speculative & Efficient Inference:** Faster rollout engines reduce wall‑clock time and enable exploring larger groups (\(G > 64\)), which could improve group baselines further.

### 4.2 Smarter Algorithms

- **Weak‑to‑Strong Alignment:** Current methods assume reward signals are perfect; in practice they’re noisy. Algorithms that cushion against mislabeled rewards—e.g., robust importance weighting or Bayesian advantage estimation—could protect learning when preferences are ambiguous.
- **Iterative Reward Updating:** DeepSeekMath already used an iterative loop where new policy rollouts re‑train the reward model on fresh data (“replay”). Extending this with curriculum learning may give smoother improvement curves.
- **Hybrid Objective Designs:** Combining outcome supervision (final answer correctness) with process supervision (stepwise rewards) appears beneficial; more granular step rewards could lead to finer control over reasoning traces.

### 4.3 Stronger Reward Functions

- **Generalization Across Domains:** A reward model trained only on GSM8K/MATH may struggle on geometry or formal proof tasks; multi‑task pretraining of reward nets could broaden applicability.
- **Uncertainty Modeling:** Predictive variance from ensembles or Bayesian heads could inform when to trust or downweight a reward score.
- **Process Rewards at Scale:** Building high-quality stepwise annotators is labor intensive—but automatic code execution checks (e.g., symbolic algebra verification) may provide cheap yet reliable intermediate signals for program‐of‐thought style training.

---

## 5️⃣ Takeaways

By boiling down every fine‑tuning recipe to *(data source → reward → algorithm)*, DeepSeekMath gives us a compact mental map:

- Classic SFT sits at one corner—offline data + no explicit reward + MLE.
- RFT/DPO add a simple binary or pairwise reward while still staying offline.
- PPO brings online exploration plus a learned critic.
- GRPO trims PPO’s memory hog by replacing the critic with *group-relative baselines*, showing that clever baseline design can yield almost identical performance at fraction of cost.

The empirical finding that RL boosts Maj@K but not Pass@K reinforces that alignment techniques mainly reshape output probabilities rather than invent new problem‐solving tricks. To push beyond that ceiling we’ll need richer data streams, more noise‑aware algorithms, and reward models that understand both final answers *and* their internal reasoning steps.

DeepSeekMath proves that even modestly sized open models can close much of the gap to proprietary heavyweights when we treat data selection and reinforcement learning as first‐class engineering problems—all viewed through this unified lens.
