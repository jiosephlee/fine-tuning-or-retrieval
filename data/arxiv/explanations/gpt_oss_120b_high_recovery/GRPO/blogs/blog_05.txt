\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Open‑Source Math Mastery: How DeepSeekMath Rivals GPT‑4 on the MATH Benchmark  

Large language models have become surprisingly good at solving math problems—thanks to chain‑of‑thought prompting, tool‑augmented reasoning, and massive token budgets. Yet the top performers (GPT‑4, Gemini Ultra) are closed‑source and only accessible via paid APIs. The latest open‑source effort, **DeepSeekMath 7B**, narrows that gap dramatically: it reaches **51.7 % accuracy on the competition‑level MATH benchmark** without any external toolkits or voting tricks, and **60.9 %** when self‑consistency is applied over 64 sampled solutions. In this post we unpack how DeepSeekMath was built, why it performs so well, and what its success means for the broader AI community.

---

## 1. Why the MATH benchmark matters  

The **MATH** dataset consists of over 12 k problems from high‑school to undergraduate mathematics, each requiring multi‑step reasoning and symbolic manipulation. It is widely regarded as a *hard* test for language models because a single incorrect step can break the entire solution. Recent papers report best closed‑source scores hovering around 45 % while GPT‑4 tops out near 70 %. Hitting **51.7 %** with a *7 billion‑parameter* model therefore marks a noteworthy milestone: an open model is now outperforming most proprietary systems except the very largest.

---

## 2. Scaling math data without scaling the model  

### 2.1 A massive web‑sourced math corpus  

DeepSeekMath’s key ingredient is the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, a curated set of **120 billion math tokens** scraped from Common Crawl. The authors built an iterative pipeline:

1. **Seed data** – OpenWebMath (≈13 B tokens) provided a high‑quality starting point.
2. **FastText classifier** – Trained on positive examples from OpenWebMath and negative examples randomly drawn from the web.
3. **Recall & rank** – The classifier scored every deduplicated page; only the top‑ranking pages were kept.
4. **Domain expansion** – After each iteration they identified domains where ≥10 % of pages were already selected (e.g., `mathoverflow.net`) and added more URLs from those domains to the seed set.
5. **Four iterations** – Produced **35.5 M pages** (~120 B tokens).  

The pipeline also performed *benchmark decontamination*: any n‑gram ≥10 tokens that appears verbatim in MATH, GSM8K, or Chinese benchmarks was stripped away.

### 2.2 Code before math → better reasoning  

DeepSeekMath starts from **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1.5 (7 B)**—a model already tuned on source code—rather than a generic LLM. Empirical ablations show that this “code first” pretraining improves both plain chain‑of‑thought solving and program‑of‑thought (Python) solving across GSM8K and MATH. The authors argue that code training teaches structured execution patterns that transfer to symbolic math reasoning.

### 2.3 Multilingual flavor  

Unlike many prior math corpora that are English‐centric, roughly half of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is Chinese web content (e.g., `zhihu.com` math discussions). This multilingual angle yields measurable gains on Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, MGSM‑zh) while maintaining state‑of‑the‑art performance on English tasks.

## 3. From base to instruction‐tuned to RL  

| Model | Training stage | GSM8K | MATH |
|------|----------------|------|------|
| DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B | Math pre‑training only | 64.2 % | 36.2 % |
| DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B | + instruction tuning (CoT / PoT) | 82.9 % → 88.2 %* | 46.8 % → **51.7 %** |
| DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B | + reinforcement learning (GRPO) | **88.2 %** | **51.7 %** |

\*Self‐consistency over 64 samples lifts GSM8K to 88.2 %.

The progression mirrors a classic “pretrain → fine‐tune → RL” recipe:

1. **Base model** – after ingesting the math corpus it already beats most open LLMs of similar size and even outperforms closed Minerva 540B on MATH.
2. **Instruction tuning** – using ≈776 K problem–solution pairs annotated with chain‐of‐thought, program‐of‐thought, or tool–integrated reasoning pushes performance into the upper‐50’s.
3. **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – a lightweight RL variant that eliminates the separate critic network used in PPO by using *group averages* as baselines (see Section 4). With only the original instruction data (≈144 K GSM8K/MATH questions), GRPO gains another ~5 points on MATH while consuming far less GPU memory than PPO.

---

## 4. Group Relative Policy Optimization – why it matters  

### 4.1 From PPO to GRPO  

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) optimizes an actor–critic objective:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q,o}\!\left[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o|}\!\min\!\Big(\rho_t A_t,\ \text{clip}(\rho_t,\!1-\varepsilon,\!1+\varepsilon)A_t\Big)\right],
\]

where \(\rho_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_t\mid q,o_{<t})}\) and \(A_t\) comes from a learned value function \(V_\psi\).

Training a separate value network at the scale of LLMs is memory intensive and often noisy because rewards are usually defined only at the final token.

### 4️⃣ GRPO’s key simplifications  

GRPO replaces the learned baseline with a *group-relative* baseline:

1. For each question \(q\) sample a *group* of \(G\) completions \(\{o_i\}_{i=1}^G\) from the current policy.
2. Compute rewards \(r_i = r_\varphi(q,o_i)\) using a reward model (or rule‐based correctness).
3. Normalize within the group: \(\tilde r_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\text{std}(\mathbf r)}\).
4. Set every token’s advantage \(\hat A_{i,t} = \tilde r_i\) (outcome supervision) or sum stepwise normalized rewards for process supervision.
5. Add an explicit KL regularization term \(\beta\, D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}}]\).

Because the baseline is computed *on the fly* from sampled outputs, no extra critic network is needed—saving several GB of GPU memory per training replica.

### 4️⃣ Empirical impact  

Applying GRPO on top of DeepSeekMath–Instruct yields:

* GSM8K ↑ from **82.9 %** → **88.2 %**
* MATH ↑ from **46.8 %** → **51.7 %**

These lifts surpass all other open-source models ranging from 7 B to 70 B parameters, and they come *without* expanding the training data beyond what was used for instruction tuning.

## 5. Head-to-head with closed models  

| Model | Parameters | MATH accuracy |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| GPT‑4 (closed) | — | ~70 % |
| Gemini Ultra (closed) | — | ~68 % |
| Minerva 540B (closed) | 540 B | ~48 % |
| DeepSeekMath 7B (open) | 7 B | **51.7 %** |
| DeepSeekMath RL 7B (open) | 7 B | **51+ %** |

*Numbers for GPT-4/Gemini Ultra are taken from their respective papers; exact values vary with prompt style.*

Two observations stand out:

1️⃣ **Parameter efficiency** – DeepSeekMath closes the gap with Minerva despite being *77× smaller*. The dominant factor appears to be *data quality*: high‐precision math content plus multilingual coverage outweigh raw parameter count.

2️⃣ **Tool independence** – All reported numbers come from *pure language generation*. Many contemporary pipelines rely on Python execution or external solvers; DeepSeekMath achieves its scores without such crutches, demonstrating genuine reasoning ability baked into its weights.

## 6. Implications for accessibility  

### Democratizing advanced math AI  

Historically, only organizations with massive compute budgets could train models that rival GPT-4 on challenging reasoning tasks—largely because they could afford both trillions of tokens and hundreds of billions of parameters. DeepSeekMath shows an alternative path:

* **Open data pipeline:** By mining public web pages with a modest fastText classifier, anyone can reproduce a high-quality math corpus without proprietary licenses.
* **Efficient RL:** GRPO’s memory savings make reinforcement learning feasible even on single-node GPU clusters.
* **Code as a catalyst:** Starting from a code-specialized base reduces required training steps while still delivering strong algebraic skills.

Collectively these choices lower the barrier for academic labs and hobbyist groups to experiment with high-stakes mathematical reasoning models.

### Potential use cases  

* **Education assistants** – Providing step-by-step solutions in both English and Chinese without relying on external execution environments.
* **Research helpers** – Autoformalizing informal statements into proof assistant syntax (miniF2F experiments already show promising results).
* **Tool augmentation starter kits** – Since DeepSeekMath can already solve many problems purely via text generation, developers can add their own Python sandbox for edge cases rather than building tool integration from scratch.

---

## 7️⃣ Limits & future directions  

While impressive, DeepSeekMath still trails GPT-4 in geometric reasoning and theorem proving—a likely side effect of data selection bias toward algebraic problem statements found online. Moreover:

* Scaling to larger parameter counts may further boost performance but would reintroduce resource constraints.
* The current reward model relies heavily on answer correctness; richer process rewards could improve *Pass@K* rather than just *Maj@K*, as discussed in Section 4 of the paper.
* Exploring out-of-distribution question prompts and advanced tree-search decoding could push self-consistency scores even higher.

The authors also note that arXiv papers contributed little to performance; future work may revisit scientific literature with better filtering or domain adaptation techniques.

---

## Conclusion  

DeepSeekMath redefines what an open-source language model can achieve in mathematical reasoning:

* By harvesting ≈120 B high-quality math tokens from public web data,
* Leveraging code pretraining as a stepping stone,
* And introducing an efficient RL algorithm (GRPO),

the team pushes a modest 7‑billion parameter model past the once-unthinkable benchmark of **50 %+ on competition-level MATH**, edging close to proprietary giants like GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra—all without external calculators or voting ensembles.

For practitioners wanting cutting-edge math capabilities without paying API fees—or for researchers probing how data quality versus scale influences reasoning—DeepSeekMath offers an exciting blueprint—and proof—that “big” does not always have to mean “closed”.
