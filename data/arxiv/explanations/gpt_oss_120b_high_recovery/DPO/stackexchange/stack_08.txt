\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does Direct Preference Optimization relate to control‑as‑inference frameworks?
Question:
Section 4 derives an actor–critic objective (Eq.~9) that resembles a control‑as‑inference formulation where we minimize KL$(π_θ||π^*)$. In standard control‑as‑inference, a "soft value" term appears as an expectation over future rewards.
* In DPO, why does rewriting the reward eliminate the need for an explicit baseline/value function?
* Does DPO implicitly treat the KL term as part of a logarithmic normaliser, thus performing exact inference without approximation?
I’m trying to map each term in Eq.~9 onto its counterpart in typical control‑as‑inference literature.

Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) the KL‑regularisation that appears in a typical control‑as‑inference (CaI) formulation is absorbed into the *normalising* (“soft‑value”) term of the optimal posterior distribution. Because the human‑preference model (Bradley–Terry) depends only on *differences* of rewards, this normaliser cancels when we write the likelihood of a preferred versus a dispreferred completion. Consequently the loss (9) contains no separate baseline or learned value function – the “advantage’’ is obtained analytically from the ratio $\displaystyle \frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\;\pi_{\text{ref}}\!}$. In other words, DPO performs **exact** inference for the posterior defined by CaI, only at the level of whole output sequences rather than per time‑step.

Below I unpack this intuition step by step and map each piece of Eq.~(9) onto its CaI counterpart.

---

### 1. Control‑as‑inference for RLHF  

The RLHF objective used throughout the paper (Eq.~(3)) can be written  

$$
J(\pi_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}
        \bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]
        -\,\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\Bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\big|\big|\,
                \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\Bigr].
$$

In CaI one interprets this as **maximum a posteriori** inference over trajectories,
with  

* **prior** $\displaystyle \pi_{\text{ref}}$ (the SFT model),  
* **likelihood** $\displaystyle \exp(r_{\phi}/\beta)$,  
* **posterior** $\displaystyle \pi^{*}$ (the optimal policy after imposing the KL constraint).

Solving for $\displaystyle \pi^{*}$ yields exactly the exponential‑family form  

$$
\boxed{
      \;\pi^{*}(y|x)=
      \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
      \exp!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\Bigr)
      }
      \tag{4}
$$

where  

$$
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
      \exp!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\Bigr)
      =\exp!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,V_{\beta}(x)\Bigr)
$$

is the **partition function** whose logarithm $V_{\beta}(x)=\displaystyle 
   \beta\,\log Z(x)$ plays the role of a *soft value* (the CaI analogue of a critic).

If we take logs on both sides of (4) we obtain an equivalent expression for any reward that induces the same posterior:

$$
r_{\phi}(x,y)=
    \beta\,\log\tfrac{\displaystyle \pi^{*}(y|x)}
                     {\displaystyle \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
    +\,\underbrace{\displaystyle \beta\,\log Z(x)}_{V_{\beta}(x)} .
    \tag{5}
$$

Equation (5) makes precise what “reward’’ means once we already know what “optimal’’ policy we are aiming at — the reward consists of a **relative log‑probability**
(term $\displaystyle \beta\,\log (\tfrac{\pi^{*}}{\;\pi_{\text{ref}}\!})$)
plus an additive **state‑dependent** constant $V_{\beta}(x)$.

---

### 2. Why no explicit baseline/value function is needed  

Human preferences are modeled with Bradley–Terry:

$$
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
    \sigma\bigr(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
    \qquad\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .
    \tag{1}
$$

Substituting the re‑parameterised reward (5) into this expression gives

$$
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=
\sigma\bigr(
    \beta\,\log\tfrac{\displaystyle \pi^{*}(y_{w}|x)}
                     {\displaystyle \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
    - 
    \beta\,\log\tfrac{\displaystyle \ pi^{*}(y_{l}|x)}
                     {\displaystyle  pi _{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}
   \bigr),
    \tag{6}
$$

because **the soft‑value term $V_{\beta}(x)$ appears identically for both**
$y_{w}$ *and* $y_{l}$ and therefore **cancels** out.
This cancellation is precisely what Lemma 1 (“reward equivalence”) states:
adding any function $f(x)$ to all rewards leaves preferences unchanged.
Consequently we may pick any convenient representative from each equivalence class;
the authors choose the one where $f(x)= -V_{\beta}(x)$, i.e.
$r(x,y)=\: 
   $\displaystyle 
   β· log[\;\tfrac{\π(y|  x)} {\π _{{ ref }}( y | x ) }\] .
