# Demystifying Importance Sampling in RL for Language Models

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the go‑to tool for polishing large language models (LLMs) into problem‑solving powerhouses. Yet one statistical building block—**importance sampling**—is often mentioned in passing without a clear intuition. In this post we’ll break down what importance sampling really means, why the classic token‑level tricks used by PPO and GRPO can backfire, and how **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** finally gets it right by operating at the *sequence* level.

---

## 1. Importance Sampling in a Nutshell

Imagine you have an urn filled with two kinds of marbles: red ones that occur with probability $p$ and blue ones with probability $1-p$. You’d like to estimate the average value of a function $f$ over a *target* distribution $p$, but you are only allowed to draw marbles from a different *behavior* distribution $q$ (say, you can only draw blue marbles because red ones are hidden).

The trick is to **re‑weight** each sampled marble by $\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}$, where $z$ is the marble you actually drew. Formally,

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(z)] \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}\Bigl[\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}\,f(z)\Bigr].
\]

If you drew many marbles from $q$, the weights $\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}$ collectively correct the mismatch between $q$ and $p$, giving you an unbiased estimate of the target expectation.

Two key takeaways:

1. **Averages matter** – the correction works because we take an expectation over many samples; a single sample’s weight can be wildly noisy.
2. **Match units** – $f(z)$ should be defined on exactly the same object as $p$ and $q$. If $p$ is over sequences but $f$ evaluates individual tokens, you’ll be mixing apples and oranges.

---

## 2. RL for LLMs: Old Policy vs. New Policy

When we fine‑tune an LLM with RL, we treat the model as a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta$. For any query $x$, it generates a response $y$ with probability

\[
\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}).
\]

During training we usually **sample** responses using an *old* policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and then update the parameters toward a *new* policy $\pi_\theta$. The reward function $r(x,y)$ – often a scalar between 0 and 1 – evaluates the whole response $y$. 

To correct for the fact that our data comes from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ rather than $\pi_\theta$, we need an importance weight:

\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)}.
\]

This is precisely the IS ratio that matches the **unit of reward**, i.e., whole sequences.

---

## 3. Token‑Level Importance Ratios: Why They Fail

### 3.1 What PPO/GRPO Do

Standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) clips *token‑level* ratios

\[
w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

and aggregates them across time steps. GRPO inherits this idea but keeps a single advantage per response while still using $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ for every token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

### 3.2 The Core Mistake

The importance sampling principle requires **averaging** over many draws from the behavior distribution before applying $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}}{\pi_{\text{beh}}}$. In GRPO each weight is based on *one* sample from a conditional distribution (the next‑token prediction). That single draw cannot reliably correct for distributional shift; instead it injects high variance into every gradient step.

Because rewards are assigned to entire sequences, weighting gradients token‑by‑token creates a mismatch: we are trying to estimate an expectation over sequences while adjusting each token independently. As sequence length grows—common when fine‑tuning LLMs on code or math—the variance compounds, eventually leading to catastrophic model collapse even after careful hyperparameter tuning.

---

## 4. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Remedy

### 4.1 The Sequence Ratio

GSPO flips the script by using a **sequence‑level importance ratio**, denoted $s_i(\theta)$:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}\right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
    \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
    \Bigr).
\]

Two design choices deserve attention:

* **Length Normalization** – Raising to $1/|y_i|$ rescales long‐sequence ratios into roughly the same numerical range as short ones, preventing wildly different clipping thresholds for different lengths.
* **Exponentiation of Log Averages** – By averaging log ratios first, GSPO treats every token’s contribution equally when forming $s_i(\theta)$ while still preserving the correct overall likelihood correction.

### 4.2 Objective and Clipping

With group size $G$ responses per query, GSPO’s objective reads

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Biggl[
   \frac1G \sum_{i=1}^{G}
   \min    
   \bigl(
      s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\,
      \operatorname{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_i
   \bigr)
\Biggr],
\]

where $\widehat A_i$ is a *group‐based* advantage:
\[
\widehat A_i=\frac{
    r(x,y_i)-
    \operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}{
    \operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_{j=1}^G\bigr)
}.
\]

Clipping now operates on **whole responses**, dropping entirely “off‑policy” sequences rather than individual tokens. This aligns nicely with our sequence‐level IS ratio.

### 4.3 Gradient Intuition

When you expand $\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}$, every token’s log–likelihood gradient receives *the same* multiplier—the normalized sequence ratio times its advantage—so all tokens in a response move together:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\E[\,
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
   \cdot
   \tfrac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_t\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\,].
\]

Contrast this with GRPO where each token carries its own noisy weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. By equalizing token contributions within a sequence, GSPO dramatically reduces variance while still honoring importance sampling theory.

## 5. What This Means in Practice

### 5.1 Stability Gains

Empirically, training a Qwen3‑30B model with GSPO never experienced the irreversible collapse that plagued GRPO runs on long responses. The sequence‑level correction stays well behaved even as rollout length increases or when scaling compute aggressively.

### 5? Efficient Use of Data

Despite clipping *far more* tokens under GSPO (up to two orders of magnitude more than GRPO), training efficiency improves. The reason is simple: discarding highly off‑policy whole responses yields cleaner gradient estimates than keeping noisy token‐level contributions.

### 5? MoE Models No Longer Need “Routing Replay”

Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures add another source of instability—expert activation can shift dramatically after each update, breaking token‑level ratios. GRPO required “Routing Replay,” where old routing decisions were cached and reapplied during gradient computation—a heavy memory/communication cost.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely because its importance ratio cares only about overall sequence likelihood; expert activation volatility has minimal impact on $\pi_\theta(y|x)$. Consequently MoE models train stably out‑of‑the‑box without extra plumbing.

### 5? Simpler RL Infrastructure

In distributed setups it’s common to recompute old‐policy likelihoods on a training engine to avoid precision mismatches with an inference engine (e.g., Megatron vs SGLang). Since GSPO only needs **sequence** likelihoods—not per‑token scores—it tolerates those discrepancies much better. One can now reuse inference‐engine logits directly in the loss calculation, cutting down engineering overhead especially for multi‑turn or partial rollout pipelines.

---

## 6. TL;DR – The Takeaways

* **Importance sampling works when you match what you weigh with what you reward** – sequences ↔ sequence reward.
* Token‑level ratios in PPO/GRPO misuse IS because they reweight single draws from conditional distributions, leading to high variance and instability.
* GSPO introduces a *sequence‐level* IS ratio $s_i(\theta)$ with length normalization; clipping now discards whole responses that are too far off policy.
* Equal weighting of all tokens inside a response reduces gradient noise dramatically.
* The method stabilizes training of massive LLMs—including sparse MoE variants—without extra tricks like Routing Replay.
* Infrastructure becomes cleaner because only sequence likelihoods are needed; inference engines can feed directly into RL loss computations.

By aligning importance sampling properly with the granularity of rewards, GSPO unlocks both safety and scalability for next‑generation language model reinforcement learning. As research pushes toward ever larger models and longer horizons, getting this statistical foundation right will be essential—and now we have a concrete recipe that works in practice.
