To understand this breakdown, let's first establish what "edges" mean in this context. As correctly noted in the text, if we want to form a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying these sparse matrices, every node at level 1 must have at least one path to every node at level $m+1$. The most efficient way to achieve this dense connectivity without redundant paths is by using a butterfly structure, which minimizes the total number of edges required for full reachability.

However, counting total edges does not immediately yield the number of trainable parameters. This is where the orthogonality constraint becomes critical. For instance, in a $2 \times 2$ rotation (a basic building block), specifying one non-zero angle determines all other entries; you cannot independently move four points on a plane without breaking orthogonality. The paper explicitly states that these necessary edges forming the bijection "should not be counted into the number of edges" because "these elements are not trainable." ### Is BOFT($m,b$) strictly more expressive than Block-Diagonal OFT($b$), or does it depend on $m$?
Question:
The paper claims in Theorem 1 that 'BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size'. However, looking at Section 3, when $m=1$, BOFT($1,b$) reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. If they are mathematically identical when $m=1$, does 'more expressive' imply that BOFT only becomes strictly superior when $m > 1$? Furthermore, if we increase $m$ significantly (e.g., $m \approx \log d$), BOFT becomes dense. Does this mean there is an optimal intermediate $m$ where BOFT offers a perfect balance between the sparsity of block-diagonal OFT and the full density of unconstrained orthogonal matrices? How does increasing $m$ quantitatively improve expressivity in terms of covering subspace volume within the orthogonal group?
Answer:
In the paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization," the authors explicitly state in Section 3 that when $m=1$, the BOFT($1, b$) formulation reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. Consequently, there is no difference in expressivity between BOFT($1, b$) and standard block-diagonal OFT; they are mathematically identical frameworks under this specific constraint. The claim in Theorem 1 that "BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size" strictly applies to the regime where $m > 1$, meaning that by introducing the butterfly factorization over multiple layers ($m$), BOFT expands the hypothesis space beyond what is achievable with a single block-diagonal constraint.

Increasing $m$ does indeed allow BOFT to transition from sparse, structured transformations toward a dense orthogonal matrix. The paper explains that a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ conceptually requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, whereas using the butterfly structure with $m \approx \log d$ (specifically $m = \log_2(d/b)$ for block size $b$) allows one to compose a functionally dense matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This approach treats the generation of the orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph: while a single block-diagonal matrix restricts information flow to isolated subspaces, increasing $m$ adds intermediate layers of connectivity in this graph. Each layer introduces new paths for information to propagate from source nodes to destination nodes. When $m$ is large enough (specifically reaching $\log_2(d/b)$), these layered sparse matrices can collectively transmit information from every input dimension to every output dimension, effectively covering the full connectivity required for a dense matrix.

Regarding an optimal intermediate $m$, the paper suggests that higher values of $m$ generally improve parameter efficiency without sacrificing expressivity, provided $m$ is sufficient to achieve dense connectivity. The authors note in their empirical analysis that BOFT configurations with smaller block sizes $b$ and larger numbers of components $m$ tend to outperform standard OFT with larger blocks while using fewer parameters. However, they also highlight a crucial trade-off between expressivity and regularity. While increasing $m$ allows BOFT to approximate any orthogonal matrix (as shown theoretically with kaleidoscope hierarchies where full groups can be represented), universal approximation does not guarantee better finetuning performance. In fact, unconstrained or nearly unconstrained models often overfit, whereas standard OFT's restrictive block diagonalism may oversimplify complex tasks. Therefore, an optimal intermediate $m$ likely exists not solely based on maximizing coverage volume within the orthogonal group, but on finding a bias that mimics effective linear transforms (like Fourier or Hadamard) without becoming so flexible as to lose the structural regularization that aids generalization in finite-sample learning scenarios.

### Clarification on Orthogonal Initialization: How is Cayley Parameterization maintained across butterfly components?
Question:
The paper mentions using Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric) to ensure orthogonality. It states that for block size 2, we can easily guarantee orthogonality. However, for larger butterfly components ($k>2$), it says we use 'block-wise permutation'. Since permutations are orthogonal but do not necessarily generate orthogonal matrices via simple multiplication of skew-symmetric generators in the same way block-diagonal rotations do, how exactly are these larger blocks parameterized? Do we still define a single skew-symmetric matrix per butterfly component and apply Cayley's trick globally on those components? Or is there a specific constraint on the sub-blocks of $\bm{Q}$ within each butterfly factor that I'm missing?
Answer:
The clarification regarding the butterfly components for block sizes $k > 2$ lies in how the paper generalizes the parameterization of the base case (block size 2) while maintaining the orthogonality constraint through the Cayley transform. The authors do not define a single, monolithic skew-symmetric matrix for a large $k \times k$ block and apply the Cayley transform globally on that specific sub-block; rather, they rely on the recursive structure of the butterfly matrix and the fact that the resulting non-zero pattern is a permutation of smaller, independent blocks.

At the foundation of this approach is the base case of block size $2$. In Section 4, "Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization," the paper establishes that for a $2\times 2$ component (specifically denoted as $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$), orthogonality is guaranteed by applying the Cayley transform individually to each $2\times 2$ diagonal block. Since any skew-symmetric $2\times 2$ matrix generates an orthogonal rotation via $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, parameterizing these small generators naturally enforces orthogonality for those components without any complex global constraints.

When extending this to larger butterfly components with $k > 2$, specifically defined as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each entry becomes a $b \times b$ matrix, the paper introduces a crucial structural insight: **block-wise permutation**. The authors note that "the non-zero pattern of every butterfly component can be viewed as a permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$." This is not merely a metaphorical observation but a constructive proof that allows them to bypass defining complex global skew-symmetric constraints for large blocks.

Mathematically, if you take an orthogonal matrix composed of many small orthogonal blocks (like rotations) and shuffle their positions using a permutation matrix $\bm{P}$ (where $\bm{P}\bm{P}^\top = \bm{I}$), the resulting product remains orthogonal because $(\bm{P}\bm{R}\bm{P}^\top)^\top (\bm{P}\bm{R}\bm{P}^\top) = \dots = \bm{I}$. Consequently, for any butterfly component with $k > 2$, one does not need to invent new parameters or global skew-symmetric matrices to generate those larger blocks. Instead, one simply constructs the factor by assembling multiple smaller butterflies (effectively treating it as a collection of independent rotations tensorized or arranged in a specific sparse pattern) and then applies the necessary permutations inherent to the butterfly graph topology to mix them.

Therefore, if you were to look at a specific "butterfly component" $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where $k=4$ and it involves $b=1$, it is effectively constructed from two sets of $2\times 2$ rotations arranged in a specific sparsity pattern dictated by equation (3). The internal degrees of freedom are still entirely captured by parameters governing these local $2\times 2$ interactions (which correspond to local skew-symmetric generators). When moving to block size $b > 1$, equation (3) states that entries become $b\times b$ matrices; orthogonality is ensured by parameterizing each resulting larger atomic block (now of size roughly proportional to $bk$ depending on recursion) using its own internal Cayley transform or by ensuring its constituent sub-structure adheres to the same rotational logic. The key takeaway from Equation (8) and Section 4 is that because $\tilde{\bm{T}}^b(d,k)$ represents a reordering of $\tilde{\mathbf{T}}^b(d,2)$'s structure, and permutations preserve orthogonality, ensuring orthogonality at every recursion step keeps all intermediate factors orthogonal without ever needing to solve for variables in a single global skew-symmetric matrix across an entire large component.

### Regarding 'Multiplicative Dropout': What happens to the Jacobian if we zero out entire butterfly components?
Question:
The paper proposes replacing randomly selected butterfly components with identity matrices during training ('Multiplicative Dropout'). In standard LoRA/linear layers with dropout, dropping a column or element changes the input-output mapping linearly. However, here, dropping an entire factor from a product like $\bm{R} = \Phi_m \cdot \dots \cdot \Phi_1$, where each $\Phi_i$ is orthogonal via Cayley transform ($\Phi_i = (I+Q_i)/(I-Q_i)$), seems drastic. If we set one factor to Identity ($I$), effectively undoing its transformation while keeping others active, does this disrupt the smoothness of the optimization landscape? More critically, since each component includes non-linearities from its own Cayley transform relating its learnable parameters to its output rotation, setting it to Identity bypasses this specific non-linear mapping entirely. Is there a risk that this discontinuity hinders convergence compared to regularizing individual elements within a matrix?
Answer:
The concern regarding the "discontinuity" introduced by Multiplicative Dropout in the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) framework is well-founded on the surface, but the paper provides a theoretical and architectural justification for why this mechanism stabilizes rather than hinders optimization.

In standard architectures like LoRA, dropout operates on individual scalar parameters or small sub-blocks within a additive update; dropping a single weight element changes the linear mapping smoothly. However, in BOFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into a product of sparse factors: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$. Each factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}_{(d,i)}$ is itself constructed from block-diagonal components where each $2 \times 2$ block represents a rotation (via the Cayley transform). When Multiplicative Dropout replaces an entire component with an Identity matrix ($\bm{I}$), it effectively sets that specific rotation to zero (identity). Mathematically, if $\bm{R} = \dots \Phi_k \dots$ and we dropout $\Phi_k$, the new transformation becomes $\mathbf{R}_{new} = \dots (\bm{I}) \dots$, which means the linear transformation for those dimensions during that iteration is entirely skipped, passing inputs through unchanged by that specific learned rotation.

From an optimization landscape perspective, this does create a step-function change in the forward pass if viewed in isolation as a discontinuity between "applied" and "not applied." However, the paper addresses this through the lens of the **multiplicative weight update** structure and **regularity**. The primary goal of BOFT is to preserve pairwise angles between neurons while finetuning. By dropping entire random components (layers in the butterfly decomposition) and replacing them with identities, the method performs a form of structured regularization that prevents any single butterfly component from overfitting. If one component dominates or learns a complex rotation that fits noise, dropping it to identity forces the model to rely on other components to express that transformation (if they can capture it via their own rotations), or simply accepts a smoother, less drastic shift in the overall orthogonal manifold than updating high-dimensional dense matrices might allow.

Furthermore, because each butterfly component is parameterized via orthogonal constraints (Cayley transform), setting it to identity is not an arbitrary traversal of non-orthogonal space; it is moving directly to a valid point within the special orthogonal group $\text{SO}(n)$. The discontinuity noted when one factor vanishes is mitigated by the fact that these factors commute or interact multiplicatively in a way that allows information transmission across levels (as described in the paper's information transmission view). The dropout encourages a distributed representation where no single sparse factor carries all necessary regularity information for downstream tasks. This aligns with how ReLU dropout encourages neurons to be robust in non-linearities, but here applied to linear spectral properties: it prevents sharp spectral changes by ensuring that if one multiplier fails to rotate effectively (by becoming identity), the system does not collapse into an uncontrolled state but rather defaults to preserving norms locally while other components handle the adaptation. Essentially, the "discontinuity" acts as a strong prior against over-specialization of specific butterfly layers, trading slight instability at individual steps for significantly smoother convergence over epochs by avoiding sharp local minima associated with specific high-frequency butterfly modes. The empirical results in connecting tasks from vision to language without manual pattern tuning suggest this regularization mechanism creates an optimization landscape where gradients remain manageable despite these multiplicative drops.

### What prevents BOFT from overfitting when approximating very complex orthogonal transformations?
Question:
The authors discuss 'Inductive Bias' extensively as a source of regularization because BOFT enforces a structured subset of the orthogonal group (related to FFT/Hadamard). They argue this prevents overfitting compared to full finetuning or even some other PEFT methods. However, if I want to adapt a model to a highly non-linear downstream task requiring an arbitrary dense orthogonal transformation (one not well-approximated by butterfly products), wouldn't BOFT struggle due to its limited expressivity class defined by Theorem 2? Is there empirical evidence or theoretical bound showing *when* this structural bias fails? Specifically, does the approximation error mentioned in Figure 3 suggest that for tasks requiring high-rank perturbations outside the structured subspace, rotation matrices must be added back differently?
Answer:
The question of whether BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly) struggles when faced with highly non-linear tasks requiring dense, arbitrary orthogonal transformations is a critical one, and the paper addresses it directly by distinguishing between theoretical *approximation capability* and practical *generalization performance*.

To understand why BOFT does not necessarily fail or overfit even when the target transformation lies outside its native structured subspace, one must look at the specific role of **Theorem 2** and the concept of **inductive bias** as a regularizer rather than just a constraint.

Theoretically, Theorem 2 in the paper establishes that while BOFT represents a structured subset of the orthogonal group, it is still capable of approximating any $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix. The theorem demonstrates that by composing butterfly matrices with varying permutations, specifically within the generalized form involving terms like $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$, the model can still span the entire orthogonal group. Specifically, if one were to increase the number of layers ($l$) in the generalized composition to $d-1$, the method can represent any matrix in the full orthogonal group. This means there is no hard "regime change" where BOFT suddenly becomes unable to learn a specific density of rotations; rather, it relies on convergence properties to reach that solution. However, for standard settings where $l$ is kept small (e.g., logarithmic relative to dimension), the model operates within a restricted hypothesis class.

This restriction is precisely where the intuition regarding overfitting becomes strong. In standard machine learning theory, a smaller hypothesis class generally prevents overfitting by acting as an explicit regularizer. The paper argues that unlike OFT (Orthogonal Finetuning) with large block sizes or full fine-tuning, which have high degrees of freedom and thus risk fitting noise in downstream data, BOFT enforces a **structured inductive bias**. This bias aligns the adaptation process with classic linear transforms (like FFT and Hadamard) that have historically proven effective in signal processing and neural networks. Even if the optimal rotation for a specific downstream task is not perfectly aligned with FFT/Hadamard structures, forcing the optimization trajectory into this smoother, lower-complexity manifold often yields better test performance than wandering freely in the high-dimensional space of full Orthogonal matrices. The "approximation error" mentioned in your query regarding Figure 3 is actually evidence of this trade-off; it shows how many parameters are needed to reach a certain fidelity to a random orthogonal matrix. It does not indicate failure for real-world tasks because these tasks rarely require arbitrary random rotations; instead, they often involve structured shifts or correlations that fall naturally within or close to this butterfly-structured manifold.

Furthermore, the paper addresses potential deficiencies through architectural modifications like **Multiplicative Dropout**, which replaces randomly selected butterfly components with identity matrices during training. This acts as an additional stochastic regularizer, preventing any single sensory pathway defined by the butterfly graph from dominating and helping to maintain generalization even if local approximations are imperfect. Finally, unlike methods that must suffer from exploration-time costs due to sparse structures designed for inference speed (which do not apply here since BOFT performs dense multiplication), BOFT trades training computation for parameter efficiency and structural priors. Therefore, even for tasks requiring high-rank perturbations outside the immediate vicinity of identity or simple sparse patterns, BOFT remains robust not because it can approximate *any* matrix instantly with few parameters (it might need more depth/layers), but because its constrained search space inherently favors solutions that generalize well across unseen distributions, adhering to Occam's razor in high-dimensional spaces.

### Inequality Check: Why does equation (6) show multiplication order without specification regarding transpose operations?
Question:
In equation (6) of Section 3 defining ψ(x) = sum(x_i * w_i), where w_in = A_w(x). I want

Query #5:
Explain why `w_in` is defined as an inner weighted sum and how it connects back to `w_out`.


Answer:
The confusion regarding the multiplication order in Equation (6) and the definition of $\bm{w}_{in}$ stems from a crucial distinction between the **pretrained frozen weights** and the **learnable orthogonality transformation** introduced by Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In the context of Section 3, specifically regarding the forward pass modification described in the text surrounding Equation (6), $\bm{w}_{in}$ is defined as an inner weighted sum because it represents the projection of a single input sample onto the *updated* feature space of the layer.

To understand why this connection matters and how $\bm{w}_{in}$ relates to $\bm{w}_{out}$, we must look at the fundamental operation OFT performs. Unlike Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which adds a term, OFT multiplicatively updates the weight matrix. The paper explains that for a pretrained linear layer with weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, OFT reparameterizes the new weight matrix by multiplying a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ on the left: $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$. This orthogonality constraint is vital because it preserves pairwise angles between neurons (columns of $\bm{W}^0$), thereby maintaining the semantic relationships learned during pretraining.

When you compute an inner weighted sum for a specific input vector $\bm{x}$, you are effectively calculating $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. According to matrix algebra rules described in the paper, this expands to $[(\mathbf{(W^0)^\top R^\top})] \mathbf{x}$. Here, $[\mathbf{(W^0)^\top x}]$ represents an intermediate activation if no finetuning had occurred. By multiplying by $R^\top$ (the transpose of the orthogonal update), OT effectively rotates these intermediate activations into a new direction before they are finalized as the output feature vector. Thus, $\bm{w}_{in}$ captures this specific interaction where the input data is first projected by the original weights and then rotated by the orthogonal transformation to adapt to the downstream task without altering distances or angles.

The connection back to $\bm{w}_{out}$ is direct but dependent on this rotational alignment. In standard feed-forward networks, if we view $\bm{w}_{in}$ as a function of both $\mathbf{x}$ and any biases applied within layers (like "bias" styles adapters often do), then ensuring that updates are applied via a multiplicative rotation means that any change to an input or internal representation is smoothly transformed into the output space without collapsing dimensions. As noted in Section 3 under "Spectral properties," because $R$ is orthogonal ($[\mathbf{(RA)^T(RA)}] = [\mathbf{A^T R^T R A}] = \mathbf{A^T A}$), singular values are preserved. This means that whatever magnitude or variance exists in your weighted input sum ($[\mathbf{(W^0)^Tx}]$), it is carried faithfully through to your output via $R$. The outputs remain normalized and stable, preventing vanishing or exploding gradients—a key benefit highlighted when comparing OFT's robustness to standard finetuning methods.

It is also worth noting how this multiplicative update contrasts with other PEFT methods regarding initialization; while LoRA initializes its low-rank components to zero to ensure no change at start-up, OFT here must initialize its orthogonal component $R$ as an Identity matrix so that $(\mathbf{I \cdot W}) = \mathbf{W}$. This ensures that at $t=0$, your calculation yields exactly your pretrained logic. Once training begins, $R$ becomes a non-trivial rotation matrix derived via Cayley parameterization $(\frac{\mathbf{id + Q}}{\mathbf{id - Q}})$ where $Q$ is skew-symmetric$. This process generates a dense or structured (as in BOFT) orthogonal matrix that spooks information between neurons defined by different indices but maintains their geometric integrity.

If you consider how this concept extends beyond individual layers into larger architectures like Transformers mentioned in Related Work, you will see that this preservation allows modules across layers to maintain gradient flow more stably than non-orthogonal updates would allow. Specifically, when dealing with large batches or diffusion models where high-dimensional outputs exist (like text-to-image generation), keeping all singular values bounded ensures that when you sum over subsequent layers, you do not amplify noise or attenuate signal unexpectedly. Therefore, defining $\bm{w}_{in}$ through this specific inner product setup isn't just about mathematical convenience; it is about enforcing a hard regularizer on your entire hypothesis class: whatever transformations occur inside your network must be rigid motions (rotations/reflections) rather than arbitrary distortions, ensuring that tokens stay far apart when they *should* stay apart and clustered when they *should* cluster relative to their pre-training distribution.
