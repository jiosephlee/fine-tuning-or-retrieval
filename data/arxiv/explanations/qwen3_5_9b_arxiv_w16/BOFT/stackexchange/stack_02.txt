\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Is BOFT($m,b$) strictly more expressive than Block-Diagonal OFT($b$), or does it depend on $m$?
Question:
The paper claims in Theorem 1 that 'BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size'. However, looking at Section 3, when $m=1$, BOFT($1,b$) reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. If they are mathematically identical when $m=1$, does 'more expressive' imply that BOFT only becomes strictly superior when $m > 1$? Furthermore, if we increase $m$ significantly (e.g., $m \approx \log d$), BOFT becomes dense. Does this mean there is an optimal intermediate $m$ where BOFT offers a perfect balance between the sparsity of block-diagonal OFT and the full density of unconstrained orthogonal matrices? How does increasing $m$ quantitatively improve expressivity in terms of covering subspace volume within the orthogonal group?
Answer:
In the paper "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization," the authors explicitly state in Section 3 that when $m=1$, the BOFT($1, b$) formulation reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. Consequently, there is no difference in expressivity between BOFT($1, b$) and standard block-diagonal OFT; they are mathematically identical frameworks under this specific constraint. The claim in Theorem 1 that "BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size" strictly applies to the regime where $m > 1$, meaning that by introducing the butterfly factorization over multiple layers ($m$), BOFT expands the hypothesis space beyond what is achievable with a single block-diagonal constraint.

Increasing $m$ does indeed allow BOFT to transition from sparse, structured transformations toward a dense orthogonal matrix. The paper explains that a dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ conceptually requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, whereas using the butterfly structure with $m \approx \log d$ (specifically $m = \log_2(d/b)$ for block size $b$) allows one to compose a functionally dense matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This approach treats the generation of the orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph: while a single block-diagonal matrix restricts information flow to isolated subspaces, increasing $m$ adds intermediate layers of connectivity in this graph. Each layer introduces new paths for information to propagate from source nodes to destination nodes. When $m$ is large enough (specifically reaching $\log_2(d/b)$), these layered sparse matrices can collectively transmit information from every input dimension to every output dimension, effectively covering the full connectivity required for a dense matrix.

Regarding an optimal intermediate $m$, the paper suggests that higher values of $m$ generally improve parameter efficiency without sacrificing expressivity, provided $m$ is sufficient to achieve dense connectivity. The authors note in their empirical analysis that BOFT configurations with smaller block sizes $b$ and larger numbers of components $m$ tend to outperform standard OFT with larger blocks while using fewer parameters. However, they also highlight a crucial trade-off between expressivity and regularity. While increasing $m$ allows BOFT to approximate any orthogonal matrix (as shown theoretically with kaleidoscope hierarchies where full groups can be represented), universal approximation does not guarantee better finetuning performance. In fact, unconstrained or nearly unconstrained models often overfit, whereas standard OFT's restrictive block diagonalism may oversimplify complex tasks. Therefore, an optimal intermediate $m$ likely exists not solely based on maximizing coverage volume within the orthogonal group, but on finding a bias that mimics effective linear transforms (like Fourier or Hadamard) without becoming so flexible as to lose the structural regularization that aids generalization in finite-sample learning scenarios.
