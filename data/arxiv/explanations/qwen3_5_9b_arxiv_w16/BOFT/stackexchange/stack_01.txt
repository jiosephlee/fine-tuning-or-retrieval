\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the 'information transmission' analogy relate to the actual parameter counting in BOFT?
Question:
In the 'An Information Transmission View' section, the authors explain that generating a dense matrix via factorization is like transmitting information on a grid graph. They state that for a $d \times d$ dense matrix, we need enough edges to ensure information flows from every node in level 1 to every node in level $m+1$. I understand that one edge corresponds to one non-zero entry in a sparse matrix. However, I am confused about how this conceptual 'number of edges' directly translates to the formula for trainable parameters given as $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$. Specifically, why do we count edges between adjacent levels, and how does the orthogonality constraint (requiring a bijection) reduce the number of *trainable* parameters compared to the total number of edges in the graph? Can you walk through the derivation for a simple case like $d=4, b=2$ to show exactly how many edges are structural (fixed) vs. trainable?
Answer:
The connection between the "information transmission" analogy in the paper and the final parameter count of $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ relies on a specific constraint: the requirement that every intermediate matrix in the factorization must be an orthogonal matrix. While the analogy helps us visualize *which* connections (edges) are necessary to ensure information flows from the first level to the last, it is the orthogonality constraint that dictates exactly *how many* of those edges correspond to learnable parameters versus fixed structural values.

To understand this breakdown, let's first establish what "edges" mean in this context. In the grid graph where nodes represent dimensions at different levels of factorization, a non-zero entry in a matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ corresponds to a directed edge from a node at level $i$ to level $i+1$. As correctly noted in the text, if we want to form a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying these sparse matrices, every node at level 1 must have at least one path to every node at level $m+1$. The most efficient way to achieve this dense connectivity without redundant paths is by using a butterfly structure, which minimizes the total number of edges required for full reachability.

However, counting total edges does not immediately yield the number of trainable parameters. This is where the orthogonality constraint becomes critical. The paper notes that for an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{B}_i$ constructed to connect two levels of size $d$, we essentially need a bijection (a one-to-one mapping) between the nodes at level $i$ and level $i+1$. If we were building just any sparse matrix, we could theoretically choose which nodes connect freely. But orthogonality imposes strict rules: if you fix certain entries to be non-zero to satisfy the sparsity pattern or structural requirements, other entries are no longer free variables. For instance, in a $2 \times 2$ rotation (a basic building block), specifying one non-zero angle determines all other entries; you cannot independently move four points on a plane without breaking orthogonality. The paper explicitly states that these necessary edges forming the bijection "should not be counted into the number of edges" because "these elements are not trainable." They are fixed structural choices required to maintain rank and validity, often restricted to simple permutations or signs ($\pm 1$) depending on the specific sparse pattern chosen, leaving no degrees of freedom for gradient updates.

Let's walk through your specific example with $d=4$ and $b=2$ (the block size) using BOFT. In BOFT notation, we define butterfly components where each entry can itself be a $b \times b$ block. Here, $b=2$, so our graph represents connections between dimensions where information flows through blocks rather than scalar values. We aim to construct a dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ by multiplying several such components. According to Section 4, if we use $m$ factors (where $m = \log_2(d/b) = \log_2(4/2) = 1$ for this minimal case, though typically we think of building up from smaller blocks), each factor connects adjacent levels in our transmission graph.

Consider a single stage of information transmission where we move from one set of nodes to another with size related to dimension $d$. The total number of edges required in such a butterfly component to ensure connectivity scales with $\mathcal{O}(d)$. Specifically, for a component operating on dimensionality associated with block size $b$, there are effectively $d \times b$ potential connections if it were fully dense per scalar entry? No, let's look closer at the parameter formula derivation provided in the paper's text: "BOFT takes $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters."

Let's analyze one stage ($m=1$) for our case where total dimension is implicitly handled by scaling. If we consider a generic butterfly component that acts on a dimension space scaled by block size $b$, say it handles interactions within groups of size related to $d$. The total number of non-zero entries (edges) in such a sparse orthogonal component acting on dimensionality proportional to $d$ is generally proportional to $d \times b$. However, due to orthogonality constraints within each local substructure (the butterfly factors), only half degree-of-freedom per node relative change is trainable because fixing one half determines the other via rotation constraints (Cayley transform). More precisely, for each connection needed simply to swap channels or rotate basis vectors within an orthogonal basis defined by blocks of size $b$, there are $(b^2 - 1)/2$ or similar constraints depending on exact implementation?
