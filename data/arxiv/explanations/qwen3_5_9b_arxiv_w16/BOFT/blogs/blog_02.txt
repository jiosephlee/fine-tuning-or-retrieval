# Beyond LoRA: Why 'Orthogonal' Finetuning is a Stronger Contender

Large foundation models have revolutionized artificial intelligence, but their astronomical parameter counts make training them from scratch a prohibitive expense for most researchers. To adapt these models to specific downstream tasks, such as converting Stable Diffusion into an animation generator or fine-tuning a Language Model for legal consultation, we rely on Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT). While methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) have gained immense popularity, they suffer from specific limitations regarding weight preservation. Recently, the introduction of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) offers a compelling alternative by fundamentally changing how we update model weights. This post explores why preserving the geometric structure of neural weights leads to better generalization and how BOFT extends these benefits far beyond its original domain of text-to-image generation.

Traditional finetuning often updates all parameters, which can lead to catastrophic forgetting where the model loses its pre-trained knowledge to learn new tasks. Conversely, PEFT methods introduce a small set of trainable parameters while freezing the majority of the model. LoRA achieves this by decomposing the weight update into a product of two low-rank matrices added to the original weights. However, this additive approach does not strictly preserve the intrinsic geometric properties of the pretrained weight matrix. In contrast, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) operates under the principle that neurons in the same layer transform via an orthogonal matrix. Mathematically, if we optimize an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, and apply it to pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$, the pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved throughout training. This property encourages a small discrepancy between the pretrained and finetuned models, effectively preventing catastrophic forgetting while maintaining semantic knowledge from pretraining.

Despite its theoretical advantages, the original implementation of Orthogonal Finetuning faced a significant hurdle: parameter efficiency. A dense orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters to represent, which is comparable to finetuning all weights in a large dimensionality $d$. The original OFT workaround utilized a block-diagonal structure to approximate an orthogonal matrix using smaller diagonal blocks. While this reduced parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ for block size $b$, it introduced restrictive inductive biases. The fixed sparsity pattern assumed that dimensions could be arbitrarily divided into independent groups, ignoring the natural correlations between data dimensions. Furthermore, limited numbers of blocks resulted in dense connectivity issues where information could not flow effectively between certain parts of the neural network space.

This limitation has been addressed by Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which refines OFT by drawing inspiration from signal processing algorithms like the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform. In these classic algorithms, butterfly structures allow for highly efficient information exchange across nodes using sparse matrices multiplied together. BOFT applies this concept to construct a dense orthogonal matrix not as a single large matrix or rigid blocks, but as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly graph topology. By generating a dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ trainable parameters instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ or restricted block structures, BOFT achieves superior parameter efficiency without sacrificing expressivity or introducing arbitrary sparsity constraints.

The superiority of BOFT over OFT becomes particularly evident when we consider expressivity and stability through an information transmission lens. If we visualize finetuning as transmitting information across a grid of nodes where non-zero matrix entries represent possible connections, traditional block-diagonal structures force restrictive barriers that prevent full connectivity if not configured perfectly. BOFT ensures that every node can transmit information to every other node while significantly reducing the number of trainable edges required to do so compared to a naive dense approach or inefficient block structures as $d$ scales up ($\log d$ versus $d$). Additionally, spectral properties play a crucial role; both OFT and BOFT preserve the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix because they employ multiplicative updates with orthogonal matrices rather than additive ones like LoRA invariantly shifting singular values. This spectral preservation enhances training stability and convergence speed.

Empirical evidence highlights why BOFT is not just an incremental update but a significant leap forward for generic adaptation tasks. Initial applications of OFT were primarily successful in controlling text-to-image diffusion models due to their stability and ability to manipulate features without altering prompt adherence too drastically—something often difficult with additive methods that might distort image coherence. However, extending orthogonality broadly was previously limited by its computational cost in dense implementations or structural rigidity in block implementations.
