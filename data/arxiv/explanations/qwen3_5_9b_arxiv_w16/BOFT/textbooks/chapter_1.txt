\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: Chapter 1: The Challenge of Efficient Foundation Model Adaptation

The era of large-scale artificial intelligence has been defined by the rapid rise of foundation models, such as GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion, which have demonstrated remarkable generalization capabilities across diverse domains from natural language processing to image generation. These models are characterized by their immense scale, often comprising billions, if not hundreds of billions, of parameters. For instance, GPT-3 alone operates with approximately 175 billion parameters. While this massive capacity allows the models to learn complex representations and solve a wide array of tasks without explicit instruction for every specific scenario, it introduces a significant bottleneck: the computational cost required to train these models from scratch is prohibitively expensive. Training a model of such magnitude demands vast computational resources and time that are inaccessible to most researchers and organizations. Consequently, the field has shifted its focus toward the necessity of adapting these powerful, pre-trained foundation models to specific downstream tasks efficiently. The central challenge is no longer just about creating stronger models from random initialization but about effectively transferring the pre-training knowledge acquired by these massive architectures to new domains without repeating the exhaustive training process required to build them initially.

To address this challenge, researchers have primarily developed three distinct paradigms for efficient task adaptation: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each approach seeks to modify the large pre-trained model with minimal changes to preserve its general abilities while enabling performance on specific new tasks. Model finetuning remains one of the most versatile methods because it requires no inference latency; it updates a subset of the model's architecture directly during the training phase, thereby maintaining the model's original output logic without introducing additional computational overhead during inference. In contrast to adapter tuning, which involves attaching additional trainable parameters or modules into the existing layers to inject new functionality while keeping the original weights frozen, or prompt tuning, which introduces trainable prefix tokens attached to the input sequence without updating any internal weights, model finetuning operates by keeping the overall architecture intact but allowing a subset of parameters—often all parameters in certain layers—to be updated via gradient descent. While alternative methods like adapters and prompts loyally preserve most weights to save memory and parameters during training, they inherently add latency during inference or alter the input flow in ways that might not be desirable for all applications. Furthermore, current model finetuning methods often rely on ad hoc techniques such as using small learning rates to prevent catastrophic forgetting and maintain similarity between the pre-trained and fine-tuned models. However, there is a growing recognition that a more principled approach is necessary—one that goes beyond merely limiting parameter updates via learning rates and instead explicitly preserves the structural information within weight matrices during adaptation.

The principle guiding this more structured approach is rooted in understanding how information is stored within neural networks through geometric relationships between neurons. Recent research suggests that preserving pairwise angles between neurons within a layer is critical for maintaining semantic knowledge and ensuring stable generalization during adaptation. If these angles change drastically due to random noise or excessive gradient updates inherent in standard finetuning with large learning rates or unconstrained operations, valuable pre-training knowledge can be overwritten or corrupted. This insight led to the proposal of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a framework where neurons in any given layer are transformed through an orthogonal matrix rather than being updated individually or added upon arbitrarily. The core mechanism of OFT involves reparameterizing the new weight matrix $\bm{W}$ as a product of an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ applied from one side (typically $R\bm{W}^0$) and freezing the pre-trained weights $\bm{W}^0$, rather than using an additive low-rank update as seen in methods like LoRA.
