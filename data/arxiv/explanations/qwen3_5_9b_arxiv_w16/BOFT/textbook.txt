\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: The Challenge of Efficient Foundation Model Adaptation

The era of large-scale artificial intelligence has been defined by the rapid rise of foundation models, such as GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion, which have demonstrated remarkable generalization capabilities across diverse domains from natural language processing to image generation. These models are characterized by their immense scale, often comprising billions, if not hundreds of billions, of parameters. For instance, GPT-3 alone operates with approximately 175 billion parameters. While this massive capacity allows the models to learn complex representations and solve a wide array of tasks without explicit instruction for every specific scenario, it introduces a significant bottleneck: the computational cost required to train these models from scratch is prohibitively expensive. Training a model of such magnitude demands vast computational resources and time that are inaccessible to most researchers and organizations. Consequently, the field has shifted its focus toward the necessity of adapting these powerful, pre-trained foundation models to specific downstream tasks efficiently. The central challenge is no longer just about creating stronger models from random initialization but about effectively transferring the pre-training knowledge acquired by these massive architectures to new domains without repeating the exhaustive training process required to build them initially.

To address this challenge, researchers have primarily developed three distinct paradigms for efficient task adaptation: model finetuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Each approach seeks to modify the large pre-trained model with minimal changes to preserve its general abilities while enabling performance on specific new tasks. Model finetuning remains one of the most versatile methods because it requires no inference latency; it updates a subset of the model's architecture directly during the training phase, thereby maintaining the model's original output logic without introducing additional computational overhead during inference. In contrast to adapter tuning, which involves attaching additional trainable parameters or modules into the existing layers to inject new functionality while keeping the original weights frozen, or prompt tuning, which introduces trainable prefix tokens attached to the input sequence without updating any internal weights, model finetuning operates by keeping the overall architecture intact but allowing a subset of parameters—often all parameters in certain layers—to be updated via gradient descent. While alternative methods like adapters and prompts loyally preserve most weights to save memory and parameters during training, they inherently add latency during inference or alter the input flow in ways that might not be desirable for all applications. Furthermore, current model finetuning methods often rely on ad hoc techniques such as using small learning rates to prevent catastrophic forgetting and maintain similarity between the pre-trained and fine-tuned models. However, there is a growing recognition that a more principled approach is necessary—one that goes beyond merely limiting parameter updates via learning rates and instead explicitly preserves the structural information within weight matrices during adaptation.

The principle guiding this more structured approach is rooted in understanding how information is stored within neural networks through geometric relationships between neurons. Recent research suggests that preserving pairwise angles between neurons within a layer is critical for maintaining semantic knowledge and ensuring stable generalization during adaptation. If these angles change drastically due to random noise or excessive gradient updates inherent in standard finetuning with large learning rates or unconstrained operations, valuable pre-training knowledge can be overwritten or corrupted. This insight led to the proposal of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a framework where neurons in any given layer are transformed through an orthogonal matrix rather than being updated individually or added upon arbitrarily. The core mechanism of OFT involves reparameterizing the new weight matrix $\bm{W}$ as a product of an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ applied from one side (typically $R\bm{W}^0$) and freezing the pre-trained weights $\bm{W}^0$, rather than using an additive low-rank update as seen in methods like LoRA. Chapter 2: An Information Transmission Framework for Matrix Factorization

# Preliminaries on OFT Reparameterization and Cayley Transform

To understand the proposed framework, we must first ground our understanding in the foundational mechanics of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In this paradigm, when adapting a pretrained foundation model to a downstream task, the method seeks to modify the existing weight matrices rather than training them from scratch. Specifically, consider a pretrained linear layer characterized by a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $d$ represents the input feature dimension and $n$ represents the output dimension. The standard forward pass computes the output vector $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ given an input vector $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. OFT introduces a learnable transformation to this process by inserting an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ into the forward pass. The modified forward computation becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. It is important to distinguish this multiplicative approach from low-rank adaptation methods like LoRA, which add a low-rank matrix additively; OFT instead rotates the feature space via multiplication.

The motivation for enforcing orthogonality on $\bm{R}$ stems from a desire to preserve the intrinsic geometric structure of the pretrained weights during adaptation. The core insight is that neural layers function by transforming neurons such that pairwise angles between them remain informative. If we were to train a generic weight matrix, we might distort these angles, effectively erasing some of the semantic knowledge embedded in the pretraining phase. However, if $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal ($\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}_d$), it acts as an isometry, rotationally transforming vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ without altering their lengths or the angles between them. Consequently, the relative relationships between neurons defined by the columns of $(\bm{W}^0)^\top$ are preserved exactly throughout the finetuning process. This preservation ensures that the model retains its ability to handle tasks related to its pretraining while acquiring new task-specific capabilities through rotation.

To make this orthogonal matrix learnable without violating its orthogonality constraint during gradient-based optimization, direct updates like standard stochastic gradient descent (SGD) cannot be used because they typically push non-orthogonal matrices off the Stiefel manifold (the set of orthogonal matrices). Instead, we utilize an analytical parameterization strategy known as Cayley transform (or Cayley parameterization). This method maps any parameter-free skew-symmetric matrix to a unique orthogonal matrix. Specifically, if we define a learnable matrix $\bm{Q}$ such that it is skew-symmetric (\emph{i.e., } $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$, meaning $Q_{ij} = -Q_{ji}$ for all scalars $i,j$), then the matrix $\bm{R}$ can be computed as:
$$
\bm{R} = (\bm{I}_d + \bm{Q})(\bm{I}_d - \bm{Q})^{-1}.
$$
Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric and initialized to zero (representing an identity transformation), this formulation guarantees that for any value of $\bm{-Q}$ within the stable region of convergence, $(\bm{I}_d + \bm{-Q})(\bm{-I}_d - (-\boldsymbol Q)^{-1})^{-1}$ will always produce a valid orthogonal matrix $\hat{\textbf R}} {\mathcal R}}$ where 9 result uniquely determined by these same constraints$. Unlike constrained optimization approaches that require projection steps at every iteration or use complex retraction operations on manifolds beyond 9 result uniquely determined by these same constraints$. The major advantage here is computational simplicity: we can simply update $\hat{\textbf R}} {\mathcal R}}$ directly using standard optimizers while rigorously ensuring mathematical correctness. This setup forms the critical foundation upon which further structural sparsity can be overlaid.

# Formulating Matrix Factorization as Information Transmission on Grid Graphs

The central theoretical contribution of this work involves shifting perspective from raw algebraic factorization to information theory and graph connectivity. When composing a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix $\hat{\textbf R}} {\mathcal R}}$ using multiple sparse matrices multiplied together ($\hat{\textbf R}} {\mathcal R}} = (\frac{\hat{\textbf B}_m}{(\boldsymbol B'}))$, one might intuitively view it merely as multiplying smaller matrices to achieve density. However, this paper reframes such factorizations as information transmission problems defined on grid-structured graphs. In this analogy, constructing a dense connection between input nodes and output nodes is equivalent to ensuring that information originating from every source node can successfully traverse intermediate layers to reach every destination node by level $m+1$.

# Chapter 3: Orthogonal Butterfly Factorization (BOFT)

# Inspiration from Cooley-Tukey Algorithm and Butterfly Graph Topology

To understand the core innovation of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), one must first reframe the problem of adapter tuning from a purely algebraic perspective into an information transmission problem. In standard efficient fine-tuning methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which relies on low-rank matrix factorization, the intuition often stems from compressing data. In contrast, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) aims to transform the neural representations of a model without altering their relative geometric relationships. While OFT successfully preserves pairwise angles between neurons by multiplying weight matrices with an orthogonal matrix, a significant drawback of the original approach is its parameter count. A dense orthogonal matrix in a $d$-dimensional space requires $O(d^2)$ parameters, which becomes prohibitive for large foundation models. Previous attempts to mitigate this by enforcing a block-diagonal structure reduce the parameter count to $O(bd)$ but at the cost of limiting expressivity; such matrices cannot approximate general linear transforms because they assume that dimensions in different blocks are independent and never mix information across them.

The central insight that drives BOFT is that we do not necessarily need to store every entry of a dense orthogonal matrix if we can compose it as a product of several sparse matrices. This analogy draws directly from computer science, specifically the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm. The FFT achieves $O(d \log d)$ computational complexity rather than the naive $O(d^2)$ for Discrete Fourier Transforms by recursively breaking down a large transform into smaller ones through a specific pattern of connections known as "butterflies." In graph theory terms, this is a butterfly topology where nodes are connected in layers such that information can flow from any source node to any destination node through a short sequence of steps. If we interpret a dense matrix multiplication as transmitting information from input nodes to output nodes, where each non-zero entry represents a connection, generating a dense matrix via matrix factorization becomes equivalent to routing information through intermediate layers. The goal, therefore, is to design sparse matrices that connect these layers in such a way that the final product allows every input node to transmit information to every output node (dense connectivity) while minimizing the total number of trainable edges (parameter efficiency). By adopting this topological view, we move away from arbitrary sparsity patterns and toward structures engineered specifically for efficient information exchange.

The specific structural choice for BOFT is inspired by the butterfly graphs found in the radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm. In these networks, local changes in frequency domains cause global changes in spatial domains, similar to how butterfly structures allow local parameters to induce global transformations in our linear layers. The paper posits that this topology provides an ideal balance: it minimizes the number of edges required to establish dense connectivity while respecting the strict mathematical constraints imposed by orthogonality. While other sparse factorizations are theoretically possible under this information transmission framework—such as exploring diverse network topologies like hypercubes or random graphs—the butterfly structure offers a proven solution with $O(\log d)$ depth achieving full connectivity using only $O(d \log d)$ edges. This reduction in edge count translates directly into fewer trainable parameters per training iteration. Crucially, unlike LoRA which trades off precision by compressing dimensions into fixed low-rank subspaces, or block-diagonal OFT which breaks connectivity entirely, BOFT maintains full-rank density through composition but keeps each individual component sparse and structured. This allows BOFT to explore smoother interpolations between identity transformations and full orthogonal rotations, effectively creating a richer hypothesis class that benefits generalization with implicit inductive biases similar to wavelet or Fourier transforms used in signal processing and kernel approximation literature.

# Defining Butterfly Factors and Recursive Butterfly Matrices

To construct BOFT systematically, we begin with precise definitions derived from the radix-2 decomposition used in signal processing algorithms. Let us assume our model dimension $d$ is a power of 2 ($d = 2^N$). The foundation of our construction is the **butterfly factor**, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$ for a given dimension $k$. This factor encapsulates the mixing operation performed by one layer of butterfly computations. Mathematically, $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as:
$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\

$$
