\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# From Purple Goo to Perfect Poses: Solving Convergence Instability in Diffusion Models

Training large diffusion models is notoriously treacherous. For researchers trying to adapt powerful pre-trained architectures like Stable Diffusion to downstream tasks, the learning curve is often defined not by innovation, but by hyperparameter tuning. You might spend days adjusting the number of training epochs or finding the exact sweet spot for the learning rate, only to have your model collapse into "purple goo"—incoherent images that look nothing like the subject or scene you are trying to generate. This instability stems from a fundamental challenge: how to inject new control signals without destroying the rich semantic knowledge acquired during pre-training. Traditional finetuning methods often fail here, either discarding original identity details or introducing excessive artifacts. However, a new approach called Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) offers a principled solution that guarantees smooth, predictable convergence and acts as a robust safety net during the training process.

To understand why COFT stabilizes training, we must first look at how neurons represent information within these massive networks. Existing methods typically assume that staying close to the pre-trained model in Euclidean distance is enough to preserve generative quality. While intuitive, this assumption falls short because it ignores the structural relationships between neurons that define semantic knowledge. The researchers behind this framework discovered that **hyperspherical energy**—a measure of the pairwise angles between neurons on a unit hypersphere—is a far better indicator of semantic preservation. They hypothesized that maintaining these pairwise angles is crucial for keeping the model's ability to generate diverse, high-quality images intact while adapting it to new tasks.

This insight led to the development of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which applies an orthogonal transformation to all neurons in a layer simultaneously. Think of this as rotating an entire coordinate system rather than sliding individual weights back and forth in their original frame; the angles between vectors remain constant while their directions change. This allows the model to modify its output semantics for new tasks without scrambling its internal logic. However, OFT still requires careful tuning of random initialization and epoch counts to avoid instability during early training stages. This is where COFT steps in as a critical enhancement, specifically designed to tame the wild fluctuations of early optimization and ensure rapid stabilization.

The core mechanism of COFT involves placing an explicit radius constraint on the orthogonal matrix used during finetuning. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pre-trained weights and $\bm{R}$ is our learnable orthogonal transformation matrix, COFT enforces two conditions: orthogonality ($\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$) and a bounded deviation from identity ($\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq \epsilon$). The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ essentially controls how far we are allowed to stretch or rotate from our starting point during training. By initilizing $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix (which is equivalent to zero deviation) and constraining its movement via projected gradient descent on an underlying skew-symmetric parameterization $\bm{Q}$, COFT ensures that every step taken during training remains within a safe neighborhood of the pre-trained state.

Empirical evidence strongly suggests that this constraint transforms chaotic training dynamics into a smooth optimization landscape. In experiments comparing various finetuning strategies on task-dependent generation—such as creating style-consistent portraits from photos—the results were striking without exception. Models trained with standard methods like DreamBooth or LoRA frequently exhibited "model collapse" if trained past a certain number of epochs; they would lose subject identity entirely or generate hallucinated features unrelated to the prompt. In contrast, models using COFT demonstrated remarkable resilience against overfitting even with high iteration counts, while simultaneously converging much faster than baseline approaches. For instance, while other methods might take dozens of epochs before fully aligning with edge masks or facial landmarks, COFT achieved stable control by the eighth epoch alone, revealing a steady improvement rather than erratic jumps in performance quality over time parameters
