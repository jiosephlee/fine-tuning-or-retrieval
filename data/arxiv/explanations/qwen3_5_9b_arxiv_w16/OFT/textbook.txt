Chapter 1: The Challenge of Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The landscape of generative artificial intelligence has undergone a paradigm shift, moving from the generation of isolated vectors or low-resolution images to the creation of high-fidelity, photorealistic imagery guided by natural language. This revolution is largely driven by large text-to-image diffusion models. Pioneering works in this field, such as DALL-E 2, Stable Diffusion, and Imagen, have demonstrated an extraordinary capacity to translate textual prompts into coherent visual content. These models operate by learning a probabilistic mapping from a noise distribution to meaningful image data, often leveraging vast pretraining datasets to understand the complex correlations between semantics and visual features. For instance, Stable Diffusion utilizes a latent diffusion architecture combined with a learned visual codebook, while DALL-E 2 employs a hierarchical transformation via an autoregressive transformer to maintain high resolution. Despite their capabilities in generating base images that adhere broadly to textual descriptions—such as "a cat sitting on a mat" or "a cityscape at sunset"—these models face a significant bottleneck when tasked with finer-grained control over specific attributes of the generated output. While they excel at broad stylistic generation, they struggle when users require precise adherence to specific subject identities or structural constraints imposed by external signals like edge maps or segmentation masks. This limitation arises because natural language prompts are inherently ambiguous under high-dimensional conditions; the model must infer countless contextual details that are not explicitly encoded in the text. Consequently, there is an urgent need for effective intervention mechanisms that allow practitioners to exert precise control over the generation process without collapsing the rich semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining.

To address these limitations, recent research has focused on adapting preexisting large text-to-image diffusion models for downstream tasks that demand specific forms of control. The literature identifies two critical application domains where existing generative models fall short: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Subject-driven generation addresses the challenge of maintaining a consistent identity across different contexts while varying other scene elements. In this scenario, given only a few reference images of a specific subject—such as a particular dog or person—the goal is to synthesize new images where that exact subject appears in entirely different poses, clothing, or environments as dictated by a text prompt. This task requires the model to learn highly specific attributes associated with the subject's identity while suppressing overfitting that would degrade its ability to generate generic variations of that class. The Emergent Context (DreamBooth) framework was introduced as a pioneering approach to this problem, utilizing class-specific prior preservation techniques alongside reconstruction losses on the reference images. However, standard implementations often struggle with stability and accuracy when trying to balance identity preservation with contextual diversity.

Complementing subject-driven generation is the task of controllable generation, which enables users to impose additional structural constraints beyond simple text descriptions through explicit control signals such as Canny edges, pose landmarks, or semantic segmentation maps. In this context, provided with an image that contains these auxiliary signals alongside a text prompt, the objective is to generate an image that strictly follows both conditions simultaneously. Contemporary methods like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter have emerged as leading solutions in this realm by designing modules specifically trained to ingest these control signals and guide the diffusion process accordingly. Despite their success in achieving high controllability, these methods typically require substantial adaptation through finetuning processes that can be computationally expensive during inference or prone to degrading image quality if not carefully regularized. Furthermore, adapting these complex architectures often introduces significant parameter overhead during testing stages unless extreme architectural modifications are employed rawling for efficiency gains without compromising performance. Thus, there remains a fundamental gap in providing unified approaches capable of handling both identity-based adaptation and signal-based control effectively within robust frameworks designed Chapter 2: The Hyperspherical Energy Principle in Neural Networks

# Introduction to Neuron Geometry: Weight vectors as points in high-dimensional space

To understand the foundational mechanism proposed for controlling text-to-image diffusion models, one must first visualize neural network weights not merely as static coefficients for matrix multiplication, but as geometric entities occupying a high-dimensional space. In a fully connected layer, the weight matrix $\bm{W}$ is composed of $n$ distinct weight vectors, or neurons, $\bm{w}_1, \cdots, \bm{w}_n$, where each vector $\bm{w}_i$ resides in a $d$-dimensional real space ($\mathbb{R}^d$). Traditionally, optimization algorithms like gradient descent treat these vectors within a coordinate system defined by their Euclidean magnitude and direction. However, the geometry of these neurons reveals that their relative configurations hold more significance for semantic representation than their absolute positions. When considering a neuron's function in processing input data, its behavior is determined by the normal vector it defines to the decision hyperplane. Consequently, two weight vectors that point in exactly the same direction but have vastly different lengths (magnitudes) perform the same classification or feature extraction task with respect to the normalized input, differing only by a scalar scaling factor. This geometric insight motivates the normalization of neurons onto a unit hypersphere, where every vector $\hat{\bm{w}}_i$ has a unit Euclidean norm ($\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i\| = 1$). By projecting all neurons onto this unit hypersphere, we isolate their directional information—their angles—from their magnitudes. This transformation establishes a canonical coordinate system where the position of each neuron is defined solely by its orientation relative to an origin and other neurons. The collective arrangement of these points on the hypersphere forms a manifold that encodes the structural knowledge of the network. Understanding this geometric encoding is crucial because it shifts the focus from simple parameter value deviation to the preservation of relational structures among neurons. In modern diffusion models and broader deep learning architectures, it is hypothesized that this relational structure on the hypersphere is what preserves semantic integrity during generation tasks. Therefore, any strategy aimed at adapting such models—such as finetuning for specific subjects or adding control signals—must account for how operations alter this high-dimensional geometric arrangement.

# Defining Hyperspherical Energy: Pairwise cosine similarities and uniformity on the unit sphere

Building upon the geometric visualization of neurons on a unit hypersphere, we introduce The Hyperspherical Energy (HE) as a rigorous mathematical metric to quantify the internal structure of a neural layer. Hyperspherical energy is derived from pairwise relations between all distinct pairs of centered neurons in a layer $l$. Formally, for a set of normalized neurons $\{\hat{\bm{w}}_1, \dots, \hat{\bm{w}}_n\}$ in dimension $d$, hyperspherical energy is defined based on two complementary properties: pairwise similarity and hypervolume coverage. The pairwise similarity between two normalized vectors corresponds to their cosine similarity projection angle $\alpha_{ij}$. Since these vectors lie on a unit sphere defined by radius one ($r=1$), there exists an intrinsic relationship between Euclidean distance and angular separation; specifically, Euclidean distance vanishes if and only if zero-mean random variables are uncorrelated in specific contexts. More importantly for HE calculation on the unit sphere as defined in this context, it serves as an aggregate measure of how uniformly distributed these points are across the surface. The formal definition states that HE($\bm{W}$) is proportional to $1 - \exp(-2C_d \sum_{i<j} d(\hat{\bm{w}}_i,\hat{\bm{w}}_j)^{\gamma})$, which intuitively correlates with covering large areas on spheres with small numbers of nodes and compact regions with larger numbers of nodes. In practical terms within this framework, high Hyperspherical Energy indicates that neurons are packed closely together or clustered significantly towards certain regions of the hypersphere (low uniformity), whereas low HSQ (Hyperspherical Quality or minimized Energy) indicates that neurons are spread out evenly over large surfaces like polyhedral faces or tessellated regions accurately capturing information diversity without redundancy.

The core intuition behind Hyperspherical Energy relates directly to information locality and generalization capacity observed in prior research on classification tasks. When training neural networks with weights constrained to lie on spheres (maintaining constant norm), empirical studies have demonstrated that minimizing HSQ—or maximizing uniformity by minimizing overlap—is superior for model initialization and stability. # Chapter 3: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Framework and Theorems

## The Core Hypothesis: Modifying semantics requires rotating neuron directions

To understand the fundamental rationale behind Orthogonal Finetuning, one must first re-examine how information is stored within the weights of a neural network, particularly in the context of text-to-image diffusion models. Traditional intuition often equates the magnitude of a neuron's weight vector with its importance, assuming that increasing the scale of a weight strengthens its influence. However, empirical observations and theoretical insights suggest a more nuanced reality where angular information holds far more semantic significance than magnitude. The core hypothesis driving this chapter is that modifying the semantic content of generated images effectively requires rotating the directions of neurons, rather than simply scaling their magnitudes.

This distinction is illustrated through the concept of hyperspherical similarity. When neurons are viewed as vectors, their pairwise relationships are defined by the angles between them on a unit hypersphere. If we consider the set of neurons in a specific layer as points on this sphere, the "shape" formed by their relative angles constitutes the semantic representation learned during pretraining. Research has demonstrated that angular feature differences well characterize semantic gaps. For instance, in experiments involving convolutional autoencoders trained on image data, it was observed that normalizing neurons onto a unit hypersphere allows them to fully capture essential information from the input. Specifically, when reconstructing input features, utilizing only the angular information (direction) of neurons was found to almost perfectly recover input images, whereas relying solely on magnitude information yielded negligible useful content. This implies that during training, a standard inner product operation $\bm{z} = \bm{w}^\top \bm{x}$ implicitly learns to assign magnitudes that are relatively arbitrary or compressible, while assigning specific directions $\hat{\bm{w}} = \bm{w} / \|\bm{w}\|$ to encode the critical patterns necessary for generating realistic textures and structures.

Therefore, any method aiming to adapt a pretrained diffusion model to new tasks—such as generating a specific subject from few shots or adhering to an external control signal like an edge map—must alter these directions. If one were to simply add new weights or scale existing ones by large factors without preserving these directional relationships, the delicate balance that allows the model to generate high-fidelity images could be shattered. The hypothesis posits that changing neuron directions is indeed the key mechanism for semantically modifying generated images; however, doing so with a large degree of freedom risks destroying pretraining knowledge. A naive approach might rotate individual neurons by arbitrary amounts independently. While this offers maximum flexibility, it lacks stability. If neuron $i$ is rotated by 30 degrees and neuron $j$ by 100 degrees independently, they may end up pointing in similar directions despite being originally orthogonal. This collapse of angular diversity leads to redundant representations and model instability during finetuning.

Consequently, we require a transformation strategy that permits direction changes while maintaining structural integrity. The proposed solution relies on the observation that altering directions uniformly via an orthogonal transformation preserves all pairwise angles between neurons globally within a layer.
