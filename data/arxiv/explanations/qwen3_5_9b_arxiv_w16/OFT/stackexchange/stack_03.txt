### Why does Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) require a projection step on $\\bm{Q}$ but OFT does not?
Question:
I have a detailed question regarding the implementation of COFT vs standard OFT. The paper introduces COFT with the constraint $\\|\\bm{R}-\\bm{I}\\|\\leq \\epsilon$. It mentions using Cayley parameterization ($\\bm{R}=(\\bm{I}+\\bm{Q})(\\mathbf{I}-\\bm{Q})^{-1}$) and then projecting a constraint onto $\\bm{Q}$ using Neumann series approximation ($\\|\\mathbf{{{Q}}}-\\mathbf{{{0}}}||||leq \\epsilon'$). However, standard OFT only enforces orthogonality ($\\constexpr R^	op R = I$). Why isn't there an explicit upper bound on the distance from identity in standard OFT? If OFT allows $R$ to be any orthogonal matrix (including one far from identity), doesn't that violate the goal of preserving pretrained semantics better than implicitly assuming small changes?
Answer:
The distinction between why Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) does not require a projection step on the constraint matrix $\bm{Q}$, whereas Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) does, stems from the specific mathematical roles these matrices play in relation to their respective optimization objectives: preserving pairwise angles versus limiting the magnitude of change relative to the pretrained model.

In standard OFT, the primary goal is to adapt the diffusion model to a downstream task while provably preserving the hyperspherical energy of the neuron weights. The authors demonstrate that hyperspherical energy—the sum of inverse distances between all pairs of normalized neurons—remains invariant under any orthogonal transformation. Mathematically, this means that for any orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{O}(d)$ such that $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, applying it to the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ as $\bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$ yields $\text{HE}(\bm{W}) = \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$. Because the objective function depends only on these pairwise angles and is completely indifferent to how far $\bm{R}$ deviates from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, there is no need to restrict the range or magnitude of $\bm{R}$. The "freedom" allowed by OFT is purely in rotating or reflecting the neuronal basis as much as necessary to minimize the task loss, provided orthogonality holds. Consequently, once we parameterize $\bm{R}$ using a skew-symmetric matrix (via Cayley transform as discussed in Eq. 107-109 of your paper), we simply optimize that parameter directly without needing an additional bound; enforcing such a bound would arbitrarily restrict the model's ability to learn complex adversarial updates required for controllability.

Conversely, COFT introduces an explicit regularizer designed specifically for finetuning stability and faster convergence. As noted in Section 3.4 ("Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning"), while full orthogonality preserves semantic structure well, allowing arbitrary rotations can sometimes lead to instability or slower convergence if the learned transformation swings neurons too far from their original directions in hyperspace. To address this, COFT imposes an additional Lipschitz-like constraint: $\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. This constraint explicitly limits the Euclidean distance between the transformed weight matrix and the pretrained one, ensuring that only small perturbations are applied during finetuning. This is crucial because standard Euclidean finetuning implicitly assumes small changes work well via a small learning rate, but COFT makes this heuristic an explicit structural guarantee.

The reason this constraint necessitates a projection step onto $\bm{Q}$ (as opposed to simply clipping gradients) involves interaction with the Cayley parameterization used to enforce orthogonality efficiently.
