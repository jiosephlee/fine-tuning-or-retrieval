\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the iterative fastText selection pipeline specifically avoid benchmark contamination while maximizing data volume?
Question:
I am reading the 'Data Collection and Decontamination' section and I'm confused about the mechanics of the contamination filter. The paper states they remove any text segment containing a '10-gram string' that exactly matches benchmark substrings. However, mathematical problems often vary slightly (e.g., changing numbers), or benchmarks might be shortened versions of common problems found on the web. If a webpage contains a detailed worked example that is *similar* to a benchmark problem but not identical, will it be filtered out? Conversely, if I just find a 10-gram match on every single math problem on the web, isn't it impossible to collect any 120B tokens without this filter? How does the pipeline balance recall and precision in this specific filtering stage?
Answer:
The iterative fastText selection pipeline in DeepSeekMath avoids benchmark contamination while maximizing data volume through a rigorous, multi-stage filtering mechanism that targets exact textual overlaps rather than relying solely on semantic similarity. As described in the "Data Collection and Decontamination" section, the authors specifically implement a subtractive filter where any text segment containing a **10-gram string** that exactly matches a substring from evaluation benchmarks (such as GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval) is discarded from the training corpus. This approach directly addresses your concern about mathematical problems varying slightly—by requiring an exact match of consecutive words, the pipeline allows for data to be retained even if the numbers change, the specific phrasing is similar but not identical, or if the problem is only loosely related to a benchmark example. If a webpage contains a detailed worked example that differs sufficiently at any point within those ten consecutive tokens (e.g., different variable names, altered constants, or rephrased logic), it will pass the 10-gram check and remain in the dataset; it is only if the exact sequence of ten words appears verbatim in both the web text and the benchmark that removal occurs.

This method effectively balances recall and precision because it acknowledges that perfect 10-gram hits are statistically rare compared to shorter strings, yet sufficient to catch verbatim evidence when they exist. The authors note that benchmarks often contain "shorter versions" of problems found online; for those specific cases shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens long, they employ an additional layer of safety by using **exact matching on substrings** (implied by the standard definition of n-grams where smaller windows are checked if the larger window fails or as part of a complementary strategy). In practice, exhaustive testing suggests that math problems vary enough linguistically in natural language web data—due to formatting variations like LaTeX delimiters ($x^2$ versus x$^2$), spacing differences, or synonyms—that finding true positives for both contamination sources simultaneously without removing valid training data would require an impractically high number of training tokens per problem to guarantee any content retention. The 10-gram threshold was chosen specifically because empirical studies have shown it serves as an effective trade-off where contamination removals far outnumber potential false positives where identical phrases appear in legitimate educational contexts merely due to commonality (uniqueness filtering).

You correctly identify that finding a public web page with an infinitely long string matching every single benchmark problem would be practically impossible with *this specific* filter threshold; however, this is precisely why such pipelines balance precision and recall. If they lowered the threshold to say 3 grams or used fuzzy similarity matching based on semantic vectors instead of character-level n-grams, they would indeed face catastrophic over-filtering where common mathematical explanations are mistaken for leaks. The pipeline's ability to collect nearly 120 billion tokens demonstrates that for high-volume datasets like those derived from Common Crawl or OpenWebMath, exact contiguous matches are sparse enough not to hinder scale but dense enough to meaningfully reduce data leakage. While there remains a theoretical risk of "long-middle-contamination" where unique problems share no short prefixes/suffixes but have identical contiguous blocks buried deep within long texts without overlapping boundaries with query points—a known limitation addressed via URL-based deduplication—the primary mechanism here relies on the high specificity of character-level exact matching over semantic approximation. This ensures that while maximally preserving diverse training content by allowing paraphrased or numerically distinct instances, it strictly prohibits the model from learning directly from leaked test-set answers that might induce rote memorization rather than genuine reasoning capabilities.
