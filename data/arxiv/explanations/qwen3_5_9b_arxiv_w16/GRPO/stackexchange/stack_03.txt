\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does training on ArXiv papers seem ineffective for mathematical reasoning according to DeepSeekMath, isn't arXiv full of math proofs?
Question:
In the 'Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training' section, the authors claim that training on ArXiv corpora (like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama) shows 'no notable improvements' on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, whereas web-scraped data works well. My intuition is that ArXiv contains dense, rigorous mathematical content which should be superior to uncurated web pages. What specific characteristics of industrial-grade math solvers are missing in standard ArXiv preprocessing (LaTeX removals mentioned)? Is it because ArXiv papers focus more on formal proofs and less on step-by-step problem solving demonstrations which benchmarks like MATH require? Or is it purely a matter of data quantity vs quality as implied by the comparison with Common Crawl?
Answer:
The authors' observation that training on arXiv papers like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama yields no notable improvements on benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH contrasts sharply with the intuition that these sources contain dense mathematical reasoning. The primary reason for this ineffectiveness, as detailed in the DeepSeekMath paper, is the mismatch between the nature of arXiv content and the specific skills required by competition-level benchmarks. The Core web data from Common Crawl, which performed well, contains informal math problems with step-by-step solution demonstrations (e.g., from sites like Wolfram Alpha or educational blogs), whereas arXiv papers predominantly feature formal proofs and theoretical derivations lacking the explicit "step-by-step problem-solving" structure that drives performance gains on datasets like MATH.

The authors validate this hypothesis through a dedicated ablation study comparing their Common Crawl-based corpus against standard arXiv-centric datasets. They tested a model trained exclusively on **MathPile** (over 85\% of which consists of scientific arXiv papers) and found that despite including roughly 8.9 billion tokens of math-related content, the model showed no significant improvement—and in some cases, deterioration—on quantitative reasoning tasks. For instance, a model trained solely on an arXiv corpus failed to achieve notable gains on GSM8K or MATH compared to models trained on internet-scraped math data. This suggests that the rigorous, proof-oriented writing style of arXiv does not translate effectively to the algorithmic and heuristic reasoning patterns needed to solve contest math problems without external guidance or tool use.

While the authors note that combining code training with math training yields superior results, they explicitly identify the quality and format of pre-training data as a critical factor independent of model scale. The DeepSeekMath corpus succeeds not just because it is large (120B tokens), but because it filters Common Crawl for "informal" math explanations that demonstrate *how* to solve problems, rather than just stating *that* a theorem is true. The researchers also acknowledge limitations in their conclusion: they have not yet determined if arXiv data becomes beneficial at significantly larger model scales or when combined with other data modalities, leaving open the possibility for future investigation into whether formal proofs eventually contribute to reasoning capabilities once the basic informal surface knowledge has been mastered.
