### How does the iterative fastText selection pipeline specifically avoid benchmark contamination while maximizing data volume?
Question:
I am reading the 'Data Collection and Decontamination' section and I'm confused about the mechanics of the contamination filter. The paper states they remove any text segment containing a '10-gram string' that exactly matches benchmark substrings. However, mathematical problems often vary slightly (e.g., changing numbers), or benchmarks might be shortened versions of common problems found on the web. If a webpage contains a detailed worked example that is *similar* to a benchmark problem but not identical, will it be filtered out? Conversely, if I just find a 10-gram match on every single math problem on the web, isn't it impossible to collect any 120B tokens without this filter? How does the pipeline balance recall and precision in this specific filtering stage?
Answer:
The iterative fastText selection pipeline in DeepSeekMath avoids benchmark contamination while maximizing data volume through a rigorous, multi-stage filtering mechanism that targets exact textual overlaps rather than relying solely on semantic similarity. As described in the "Data Collection and Decontamination" section, the authors specifically implement a subtractive filter where any text segment containing a **10-gram string** that exactly matches a substring from evaluation benchmarks (such as GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, and AGIEval) is discarded from the training corpus. This approach directly addresses your concern about mathematical problems varying slightly—by requiring an exact match of consecutive words, the pipeline allows for data to be retained even if the numbers change, the specific phrasing is similar but not identical, or if the problem is only loosely related to a benchmark example. If a webpage contains a detailed worked example that differs sufficiently at any point within those ten consecutive tokens (e.g., different variable names, altered constants, or rephrased logic), it will pass the 10-gram check and remain in the dataset; it is only if the exact sequence of ten words appears verbatim in both the web text and the benchmark that removal occurs.

This method effectively balances recall and precision because it acknowledges that perfect 10-gram hits are statistically rare compared to shorter strings, yet sufficient to catch verbatim evidence when they exist. The authors note that benchmarks often contain "shorter versions" of problems found online; for those specific cases shorter than 10 tokens but at least 3 tokens long, they employ an additional layer of safety by using **exact matching on substrings** (implied by the standard definition of n-grams where smaller windows are checked if the larger window fails or as part of a complementary strategy). In practice, exhaustive testing suggests that math problems vary enough linguistically in natural language web data—due to formatting variations like LaTeX delimiters ($x^2$ versus x$^2$), spacing differences, or synonyms—that finding true positives for both contamination sources simultaneously without removing valid training data would require an impractically high number of training tokens per problem to guarantee any content retention. The 10-gram threshold was chosen specifically because empirical studies have shown it serves as an effective trade-off where contamination removals far outnumber potential false positives where identical phrases appear in legitimate educational contexts merely due to commonality (uniqueness filtering).

You correctly identify that finding a public web page with an infinitely long string matching every single benchmark problem would be practically impossible with *this specific* filter threshold; however, this is precisely why such pipelines balance precision and recall. If they lowered the threshold to say 3 grams or used fuzzy similarity matching based on semantic vectors instead of character-level n-grams, they would indeed face catastrophic over-filtering where common mathematical explanations are mistaken for leaks. The pipeline's ability to collect nearly 120 billion tokens demonstrates that for high-volume datasets like those derived from Common Crawl or OpenWebMath, exact contiguous matches are sparse enough not to hinder scale but dense enough to meaningfully reduce data leakage. While there remains a theoretical risk of "long-middle-contamination" where unique problems share no short prefixes/suffixes but have identical contiguous blocks buried deep within long texts without overlapping boundaries with query points—a known limitation addressed via URL-based deduplication—the primary mechanism here relies on the high specificity of character-level exact matching over semantic approximation. This ensures that while maximally preserving diverse training content by allowing paraphrased or numerically distinct instances, it strictly prohibits the model from learning directly from leaked test-set answers that might induce rote memorization rather than genuine reasoning capabilities.

### In the GRPO algorithm, how is the baseline advantage $ar{r}$ calculated when using Group Relative Advantage Estimation?
Question:
I am studying Equation (2) for the GRPO objective. The text states that advantages $ar{A}_{i,t}$ are calculated based on relative rewards within each group. It defines outcome supervision as $ar{A}_{i, t} = 	ilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i- {m mean}(\mathbf{r})}{{m std}(\mathbf{r})}$. However, in Equation (2), there is a summation inside the min/clip function over tokens $t=1$ to $|o_i|$. Since the normalized reward $	ilde{r}_i$ is a single scalar value for the entire output sequence $o_i$, does this mean every token $t$ in that sequence receives the exact same advantage value? How does this differ from PPO where $A_t$ varies per token based on future rewards or value function estimates?
Answer:
In the DeepSeekMath paper, when using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with outcome supervision, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i, t}$ for every token $t$ within a specific output sequence $o_i$ is indeed identical to the normalized reward of that group. Specifically, Equation (2) in the paper's main text (referenced as \ref{eq:GRPO-obj} in the document) shows a summation over tokens $t=1$ to $|o_i|$, but for outcome supervision, the advantage term being summed is a constant scalar $\widetilde{r}_i$ defined as:
$$ \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})} $$
Here, $r_i$ is the raw reward given by the reward model for the entire output sequence $o_i$, and $\mathbf{r}$ represents the vector of rewards $\{r_1, r_2, \dots, r_G\}$ collected from sampling $G$ outputs for that same question. Because GRPO assigns this single normalized value to every position in the sequence ($\hat{A}_{i, t} = \widetilde{r}_i$), each token effectively receives the same gradient signal proportional to whether its group won or lost relative to others.

This design choice fundamentally differs from standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in how it handles credit assignment and baseline estimation. In traditional PPO described in Section III-A of the paper, computing the advantage requires a learned value function $V_\psi$, which attempts to estimate the expected future return at each timestep $t$. Consequently, in PPO, $A_t$ varies significantly across tokens because it incorporates future rewards and position-specific state evaluations; solving "5 x 5 + 2" might yield a very high advantage for generating "25" and a lower one for generating "x" if those are at different positions relative to predicted future returns. Furthermore, PPO relies on an external critic model that incurs substantial memory overhead and is difficult to train accurately when rewards are sparse (e.g., only at the end).

GRPO eliminates this need by shifting from absolute advantage estimation to *relative* advantage estimation within a group. The intuition is that if a model generates 10 diverse solutions for one math problem, comparing them to each other provides a stronger signal than comparing them against a generic pre-tuned value function. If one solution scores 900 while others score between 100 and 400, assigning all tokens of that top-scoring solution a large positive standard-scores-based advantage encourages the policy to adopt that pattern globally for all steps in that sequence. This approach aligns with comparative reward models and drastically reduces memory usage by removing the critic network entirely. Since polynomial RKHS mapping states are not mentioned in this paper or relevant context here we focus on why outcome only relies on final reward.

The authors also note in Subsection III-D ("Process Supervision RL with GRPO") that while outcome supervision treats all tokens equally regardless of their temporal position or reasonability during derivation, one could imagine variants where advantages are computed differently (like summing future normalized step-rewards as shown: $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j)\ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$). However, for the standard outcome supervision setup asked about regarding Equation (2), every token shares the exact same advantage value derived solely from the performance of its instance relative to peers solving the same prompt at once.

### Why does training on ArXiv papers seem ineffective for mathematical reasoning according to DeepSeekMath, isn't arXiv full of math proofs?
Question:
In the 'Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training' section, the authors claim that training on ArXiv corpora (like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama) shows 'no notable improvements' on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, whereas web-scraped data works well. My intuition is that ArXiv contains dense, rigorous mathematical content which should be superior to uncurated web pages. What specific characteristics of industrial-grade math solvers are missing in standard ArXiv preprocessing (LaTeX removals mentioned)? Is it because ArXiv papers focus more on formal proofs and less on step-by-step problem solving demonstrations which benchmarks like MATH require? Or is it purely a matter of data quantity vs quality as implied by the comparison with Common Crawl?
Answer:
The authors' observation that training on arXiv papers like MathPile or ArXiv-RedPajama yields no notable improvements on benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH contrasts sharply with the intuition that these sources contain dense mathematical reasoning. The primary reason for this ineffectiveness, as detailed in the DeepSeekMath paper, is the mismatch between the nature of arXiv content and the specific skills required by competition-level benchmarks. The Core web data from Common Crawl, which performed well, contains informal math problems with step-by-step solution demonstrations (e.g., from sites like Wolfram Alpha or educational blogs), whereas arXiv papers predominantly feature formal proofs and theoretical derivations lacking the explicit "step-by-step problem-solving" structure that drives performance gains on datasets like MATH.

The authors validate this hypothesis through a dedicated ablation study comparing their Common Crawl-based corpus against standard arXiv-centric datasets. They tested a model trained exclusively on **MathPile** (over 85\% of which consists of scientific arXiv papers) and found that despite including roughly 8.9 billion tokens of math-related content, the model showed no significant improvement—and in some cases, deterioration—on quantitative reasoning tasks. For instance, a model trained solely on an arXiv corpus failed to achieve notable gains on GSM8K or MATH compared to models trained on internet-scraped math data. This suggests that the rigorous, proof-oriented writing style of arXiv does not translate effectively to the algorithmic and heuristic reasoning patterns needed to solve contest math problems without external guidance or tool use.

While the authors note that combining code training with math training yields superior results, they explicitly identify the quality and format of pre-training data as a critical factor independent of model scale. The DeepSeekMath corpus succeeds not just because it is large (120B tokens), but because it filters Common Crawl for "informal" math explanations that demonstrate *how* to solve problems, rather than just stating *that* a theorem is true. The researchers also acknowledge limitations in their conclusion: they have not yet determined if arXiv data becomes beneficial at significantly larger model scales or when combined with other data modalities, leaving open the possibility for future investigation into whether formal proofs eventually contribute to reasoning capabilities once the basic informal surface knowledge has been mastered.

### How does GRPO handle varying output lengths within a group for advantage estimation?
Question:
When sampling a group $\\{o_1, o_2, \\cdots, o_G\\}$ from Equation (3), these outputs may have different lengths ($|o_i|$ varies). The advantage calculation involves summing over tokens $t$. In PPO with a critic, we usually pre-compute $V(o)$ at the end of each sequence to normalize advantages at each step. GRPO uses relative rewards within the group. If one output in the group has length 100 and another has length 500, how exactly are their token-wise advantages aligned? Does GRPO pad shorter sequences with zero-reward tokens? If so, how does that affect the gradient calculation compared to PPO where we have a specific baseline value function $V_\\psi(o)$?
Answer:
The mechanism of handling varying output lengths in GRPO relies on normalizing advantages **per group per prompt**, rather than attempting to align token sequences across different group members. The paper presents this by sampling a fixed number of outputs, $G$, for a single question $q$, forming the set $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$, where each output $o_i$ can naturally possess a different length $|o_i|$. Crucially, the algorithm does not pad these sequences or truncate shorter ones to match a common length; instead, it treats each sampled output as an independent trajectory within the context of that specific question.

The advantage calculation $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is derived entirely from the statistical properties of rewards within this specific group. For outcome supervision, GRPO computes the total reward $r_i$ for each completed sequence $o_i$ using the reward model. It then calculates the mean $\text{mean}(\mathbf{r})$ and standard deviation $\text{std}(\mathbf{r})$ specifically over these $G$ scalars. The normalized reward for any sequence $i$ is determined solely by $\frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$. Consequently, if one sequence has length 100 and another has length 500, their respective normalized rewards are two distinct values used to define their internal advantage curves. For process supervision, this logic extends step-wise: the algorithm identifies specific token indices defining reasoning steps for each output and normalizes the rewards at those indices relative to all other step-rewards in the group before summing them up to establish $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for every token present in that specific sequence.

This approach fundamentally differs from PPO's reliance on a global critic model $V_{\psi}(q)$. In standard PPO, $V_{\psi}(q)$ is a scalar predicted by a separate value network that represents the baseline expected return for question $q$, independent of how many samples were generated or what their lengths were. The advantage in PPO is calculated as $A_t = r_t - V_{\psi}(q)$ (simplified), requiring the value estimate to be consistent across all sampling attempts. In contrast, GRPO obviates the need for this external model entirely by using the "group score" as its own baseline. By subtracting the group mean from individual rewards before dividing by standard deviation, GRPO effectively centers each sequence's reward distribution relative to its peers in that batch. This ensures that the gradient update for a token in a short prominent answer accounts for how much better or worse it performed compared to answers (of varying lengths) generated by the same policy at that moment. Because there is no attempt to align tokens across different output lengths via padding or truncation in GRPO itself—those sequences are processed as distinct entities within a single gradient step—the computational graph is straightforward: we optimize over the sum of per-token contributions where each contribution comes from its own unique length-based trajectory relative to its group's average performance. This design choice eliminates the memory overhead associated with maintaining a learned value function and simplifies implementation since one need not worry about complex masking strategies required to handle variable-length padding when comparing sequences against a static critic baseline.

### What is the precise definition of 'Process Supervision' versus 'Outcome Supervision' in terms of how $\\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is computed?
Question:
The paper distinguishes between Outcome Supervision RL (OS) and Process Supervision RL (PS) using GRPO. For OS, it says $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is set to the normalized final reward for all tokens. For PS, it provides rewards at specific indices ($index(j)$). The formula given is $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$. This looks like an n-step return or cumulative sum. In standard Reinforcement Learning theory, wouldn't we need a discount factor $\\gamma$ here? The paper doesn't explicitly mention a discount factor in these summations. Is it assumed to be 1 (no discounting)? If so, does this cause instability if reasoning steps are very long chains, effectively giving equal weight to errors near start vs end of computation? Also, how does this differ fundamentally from computing advantages using a learned Value Function in standard RL?
Answer:
In the context of the DeepSeekMath paper, the distinction between Process Supervision and Outcome Supervision lies fundamentally in **when** the learning signal is provided during generation and **how** that signal is propagated backward to optimize every token in the chain of thought.

For **Outcome Supervision (OS)**, the reward model evaluates only the final correctness of an output. Consequently, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ assigned to every single token $t$ within that output is simply set to the normalized final reward $\widetilde{r}_i$. Mathematically, if an output receives a normalized score of $0.8$, every token in that sequence gets an advantage update based on $0.8$. This treats all reasoning steps as equally critical solely because they contribute to a successful final answer; intermediate errors that were later "fixed" by subsequent steps receive the same positive gradient as correct steps.

**Process Supervision (PS)** aims to provide finer-grained control by leveraging a Process Reward Model (PRM) that assigns a score at specific reasoning steps. If we denote the end token indices of these steps as $index(1), index(2), \dots$, the PRM produces rewards $\mathbf{R} = \{r_1^{index(1)}, r_2^{index(2)}, \dots\}$. When these are normalized to get $\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$, they represent how well each specific step performed. To calculate the advantage for any token $t$, the paper employs a formulation equivalent to an $n$-step return in standard RL:
$$ \hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{j: index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} $$
This formula sums up all future process rewards from token $t$ onward, implying that a token's quality is judged by its contribution to all subsequent reasoning milestones, not just the very end.

You correctly identified that standard RL typically includes a discount factor $\gamma < 1$ to prioritize immediate rewards over distant ones. In this specific formulation, it is indeed implicitly assumed that $\gamma = 1$. While this might seem risky for long chains—potentially giving equal weight to an early setup step and a late-stage calculation—it serves a specific purpose here: mathematical correctness often requires strict adherence at *every* intermediate step. By setting $\gamma=1$, the method ensures robust learning signals propagate forward without attenuation, reinforcing that skipping or erring at any point in a rigorous proof invalidates future successes just as severely as an early error does.

Furthermore, this approach differs fundamentally from using a learned Value Function ($V_\psi$) found in traditional PPO. In standard PPO, advantages are calculated relative to such a value function (often using GAE), which attempts to estimate how good a state is *before* seeing the full return, serving as a baseline for variance reduction. The paper replaces this second model entirely with group-relative statistics (subtracting mean and dividing by standard deviation across a group of outputs). By combining Group Relative estimation with PS ($\gamma=1$), DeepSeekMath eliminates the memory cost of training a critical value network while using step-level rewards to guide policy gradients more precisely than outcome-only signals could ever achieve on complex reasoning tasks.
