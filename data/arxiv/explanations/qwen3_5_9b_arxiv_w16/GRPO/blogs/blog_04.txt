# Closing the Gap: How a Tiny 7B Model is Beating Massive Competitors

For years, the gold standard for mathematical reasoning in artificial intelligence has been shrouded in walls of code. Models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra consistently dominate benchmarks with scores that seem impossible to replicate. These systems typically rely on massive parameter counts—often exceeding hundreds of billions—and proprietary training data that researchers cannot inspect. The prevailing assumption in the AI community was simple: to solve the hardest math problems, you need a giant model and secret ingredients. However, the release of DeepSeekMath by DI DeepSeek-AI challenges this dogma. Through careful data engineering and algorithmic innovation, they have demonstrated that an open-source model with only 7B parameters can approach the mathematical performance of these giant closed models without relying on external toolkits or voting mechanisms.

The journey to build DeepSeekMath began not with collecting random internet text, but with a meticulous campaign to curate high-quality mathematical data from the vast Common Crawl repository. Standard web scraping often yields noisy content, so the DeepSeekMath team developed a sophisticated data selection pipeline to extract relevant information. They started with a high-quality seed corpus known as OpenWebMath and used a fastText-based classifier to identify similar pages within Common Crawl. Crucially, this process was iterative; after collecting the initial batch, they added new sources (like specific math-focused domains) to retrain their classifier, making it increasingly precise. This allowed them to amass a "DeepSeekMath Corpus" totaling 120 billion tokens—the largest math-focused dataset derived from public web data at the time. The results speak for themselves: their base model, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, achieved a 51.7% score on the competition-level MATH benchmark, outperforming significantly larger models like Minerva (540B parameters) and rivaling GPT-4's zero-shot performance when using chain-of-thought reasoning.

One of the most intriguing aspects of this research is answering a long-standing debate about whether code training improves reasoning abilities. By initializing their model with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 before infusing it with mathematical data, the team observed substantial gains in mathematical problem solving that carried over even when the model did not use external tools to execute code internally. Furthermore, they tested various data sources for pre-training temper found that including arXiv papers did not significantly boost performance compared to general web pages or textbooks. This suggests that for mathematical reasoning, structured content like textbooks and problem-solving sites is more valuable than raw academic papers, which often lack the "show your work" structure required for training robust reasoning models. The multilingual nature of their corpus also led to surprising improvements in Chinese mathematical benchmarks compared to English-centric competitors.

The second pillar of success in building DeepSeekMath involves a novel approach to Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically an algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Traditional RL methods for LLMs often rely on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which requires a separate "critic" model to estimate value functions alongside the policy model itself.
