\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Why Code Training Makes a Better Mathematician Than You Thought

For years, the community has debated the best way to teach mathematical reasoning to Large Language Models (LLMs). A dominant hypothesis suggests that training models on academic papers, particularly those hosted on arXiv, is essential for mastering complex mathematics. After all, one might assume that mathematical knowledge resides primarily within formal documents and research articles. However, the DeepSeekMath paper presents a compelling counter-narrative. By conducting extensive ablation studies, the authors demonstrate that starting with a code-focused foundation provides a more robust pathway to mathematical excellence than simply dumping arXiv papers into the training mix.

The core of this finding rests on the specific architecture of **DeepSeekMath-Base 7B**, which is initialized with **DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B**. This initialization highlights a strategic shift: rather than beginning with general language data or academic text, the model starts with a strong codifying foundation. The authors found that training on code tokens before transitioning to math tokens yields significantly better results for both tool-aided and non-tool-aided reasoning tasks. This approach not only enhances mathematical capability but also mitigates "catastrophic forgetting"—a common issue where models lose previously learned skills when exposed to new data types.

To understand why code training outperforms paper-heavy training in this context, we must look closely at the experiments comparing different training regimes. The researchers designed three distinct scenarios to isolate the impact of data order and composition. First, they tested a **two-stage training** approach where the model was trained for 400B tokens on code alone before receiving 150B tokens of math data. In a control experiment, they replaced the initial code phase with general language tokens. The results were striking: while general tokens offered no advantage in subsequent math performance, code training provided an immediate boost to program-aided reasoning on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.

The second experiment explored **one-stage training**, where 400B code tokens and 150B math tokens were mixed from the outset. Interestingly, this hybrid approach proved even more effective at solving math problems without external tools compared to strict two-stage training. Furthermore, it successfully alleviated catastrophic forgetting, allowing the model to retain coding proficiency while achieving state-of-the-art math scores. This suggests that for smaller models like the 7B variant being studied, simultaneous exposure to code and math helps assimilate concepts without degrading foundational skills. Conversely, attempting to teach math on top of pure general language foundations resulted in inferior performance compared to leveraging a coder's innate logical structures carried over from code training.

Surprisingly perhaps for some readers was the finding regarding arXiv papers. Despite their ubiquity in math-focused pre-training datasets like MathPile and Proof-Pile-2, arXiv papers did not yield notable improvements across various mathematical benchmarks when used as standalone pre-training corpora. In one experiment involving DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B trained exclusively on an arXiv corpus containing around 28B tokens, neither quantitative reasoning nor formal theorem proving saw any significant gains compared to baselines trained on web-scraped data or other mixtures. The authors note that if anything, models trained solely on arXiv showed deterioration in performance on complex tasks like miniF2F formal mathematics tests compared to those trained on curated web data derived from Common Crawl via a specific mining pipeline.

It is important to clarify that this does not mean arXiv content is useless for all contexts; its ineffectiveness here may stem from issues related to decomposition quality or model scale limitations rather than an intrinsic flaw in using academic papers entirely. However, within the specific scope of these experiments aiming for competition-level performance in open-source settings, web-sourced mathematical content enriched by rigorous filtering processes proved far more potent than raw LaTeX articles scraped from repositories like arXiv or StackExchange alone when combined with heavy features of programming logic instilled early in life through coding data ingestion protocols outlined earlier during base model iteration phases prior final instruction tuning stages involving group relative policy optimization (GRPO).
