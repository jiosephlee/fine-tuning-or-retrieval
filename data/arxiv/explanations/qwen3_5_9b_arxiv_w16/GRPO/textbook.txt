Chapter 1: The State of Mathematical Reasoning in Open-Source LLMs

Large language models (LLMs) have fundamentally transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence, offering unprecedented capabilities in natural language understanding and generation. A particularly exciting frontier within this domain is quantitative reasoning, specifically the ability of models to solve complex mathematical problems. This capability has spurred significant advancements in both general quantitative reasoning benchmarks and specialized fields like geometry reasoning. Furthermore, these models have proven instrumental in assisting human experts, such as mathematicians known as "math bards," in deriving solutions to intricate problems. However, despite these strides, a significant disparity exists between the most capable commercially available models and those accessible to the open-source research community. Cutting-edge proprietary systems like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra represent the state-of-the-art in mathematical performance but are not publicly available. Consequently, currently accessible open-source models notoriously trail behind in performance, leaving a substantial gap that hinders reproducibility and limits the ability of researchers to push the boundaries of automated mathematical discovery without relying on black-box technologies. The primary contribution of this research addresses this critical imbalance by introducing a domain-specific language model designed to significantly outperform existing open-source alternatives while approaching the sophisticated reasoning levels of closed-source benchmarks.

The evolution of LLMs in quantitative reasoning reveals a clear trajectory from simple pattern matching to sophisticated, step-by-step logical deduction. Initially, models struggled with multi-step arithmetic, often failing due to a lack of persistent context or an inability to maintain coherent logical chains over long derivations. This challenge has been partially mitigated by techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting, which encourages models to generate intermediate reasoning steps before arriving at a final answer. Recent advances have further incorporated program-of-thought and tool-integrated reasoning, allowing models to write and execute Python code for verification or to utilize external calculators for complex computations. These methods have yielded impressive results on standard benchmarks like GSM8K, which consists primarily of grade-school word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic operations aligned with U.S. state assessments. However, true mathematical reasoning is characterized not just by arithmetic fluency but by deep structural understanding applicable across diverse domains, from algebraic manipulation to competition-level problem solving requiring advanced concepts like combinatorics and differential equations.

Despite the rapid progress of public models, a widening gap separates them from proprietary counterparts like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra. These closed-source models leverage vast amounts of private data and extensive compute resources that remain inaccessible to the public sphere. As a result, open-source researchers face a difficult reality where their best-performing models considerably lag behind industry leaders. # Chapter 2: Large-Scale Mathematical Corpus Construction

Large-scale data curation stands as one of the most critical yet often overlooked components in training high-performance language models. For mathematical reasoning specifically, the challenge is compounded by a unique convergence of problems: extreme data scarcity relative to the model's hunger for tokens, and severe dataset bias toward specific sources like arXiv preprints. While autoregressive language models thrive on the sheer volume of internet text, pure web scraping yields an unacceptably high noise-to-signal ratio for mathematics. Most publicly available mathematical data is concentrated in curated repositories or academic preprint servers, which are insufficient in quantity to train models at the scale required to compete with proprietary systems. This section provides a comprehensive technical deep-dive into the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, detailing how a meticulously engineered pipeline extracts billions of high-quality mathematical tokens from the noisy expanse of Common Crawl, thereby overcoming these fundamental limitations through iterative refinement and rigorous validation.

### The Problem of Dataset Bias and Data Scarcity in Math Training

To understand the necessity of a customized corpus for mathematical reasoning, one must first examine the landscape of existing public data and its inherent deficiencies. The prevailing hypothesis in the field regarding mathematical pre-training has been that access to current arXiv papers constitutes a near-panacea. However, empirical analysis reveals that training exclusively on arXiv papers yields diminishing returns and fails to provide significant improvements across diverse mathematical benchmarks compared to training on general internet text or more diverse web corpora. This suggests that while arXiv contains formal and rigorous content, it lacks the breadth and quantity necessary to robustly teach a model to reason about mathematics; furthermore, relying solely on such a homogenous source introduces a bias where models may overfit to specific academic writing styles that do not translate well to solving standardized problems found in datasets like GSM8K or MATH.

Simultaneously, there is a profound scarcity when it comes to raw, high-quality mathematical web content on the broader internet. Datasets like OpenWebMath represent a significant effort but remain limited in scale—totaling approximately 13.6 billion tokens—and generalize primarily English-centric content to around 68 billion tokens depending on versioning. These figures, while substantial compared to previous efforts, fall short of the hundreds of billions of tokens typically required to push open-source models toward the performance ceiling of GPT-4 or Gemini-Ultra without scaling law assistance via massive parameter counts (e.g., Minerva's 540B parameters). There exists a critical gap between these existing datasets and the vast potential hidden within publicly available web sources indexed by Common Crawl (CC). The CC scrapes almost all publicly accessible pages on the internet, meaning there are undoubtedly vast treasuries of math emagazines examples from university math questions - math relevant mark embedded within textbooks code repositories forum discussions that remain largely untapped by standard pre-training strategies which typically discard them as low-quality noise during initial filtering stages. Addressing this scarcity requires moving beyond simple inclusion lists and adopting an active learning approach where data quality is treated as a variable to be optimized rather than static ground truth.

Chapter 3: Pre-training Strategy and Base Model Optimization

# Initializing Mathematical Reasoning with Code-Trained Models

The architectural foundation of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B represents a deliberate departure from the standard practice of initializing language models with general-purpose pre-trained weights. Instead, the authors establish DeepSeekMath-Base by continuing the pre-training of DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This decision is grounded in the observation that initializing a domain-specific model for mathematical reasoning with a code-trained model yields superior outcomes compared to starting from a general LLM. The intuition behind this choice stems from the structural and syntactic similarities between programming languages and mathematical notation. Both domains require rigorous logical progression, precise syntax adherence, and a capacity for step-by-step decomposition of complex problems into executable routines. By building upon a model that has already mastered the constructing chains of logic and conditional execution inherent in code, the mathematical pre-training phase serves to refine these latent capabilities rather than learning them from scratch. This synergy explains why the initialization with a code model provides a more robust starting point for handling the abstract symbols and algorithmic structures frequently encountered in advanced mathematics, effectively narrowing the distributional gap between foundational training and target task performance.

Furthermore, the paper provides compelling evidence through ablation studies that addressing this specific architectural choice positively impacts both tool-augmented and non-tool-augmented reasoning capabilities. The experimental design involved comparing models trained on pure code tokens versus those trained on general tokens before transitioning to mathematical data. The results demonstrated that when utilizing code training as an initial stage prior to math training—specifically training for 400 billion code tokens followed by 150 billion math tokens—the model exhibited significantly enhanced proficiency in solving mathematical problems using Python scripts. This highlights that the internal representations learned during code training are highly transferable to program-aided mathematical reasoning, where the ability to generate correct Python code is crucial for obtaining accurate results. In contrast, models initialized with general token sequences did not exhibit this same level of proficiency in formulating executable solutions, suggesting that the specific skill sets developed in coding contexts are better suited to bridge the gap between abstract mathematical formulation and concrete algorithmic implementation.

Beyond the immediate benefits for program-aided tasks, initializing with a code-trained model also confers advantages even when external tools are not utilized during inference. The pre-exposure to code allows the model to develop a heightened sensitivity to logical consistency and procedural reasoning during its subsequent mathematical pre-training phase. When these models were evaluated on benchmarks requiring natural language step-by-step reasoning without tool usage, those trained on an initial stage of code data achieved higher performance levels compared to their counterparts initialized with general data or those where math data was mixed indiscriminately with general data without a dedicated coding stage. This counter-intuitive finding suggests that coding acts as a powerful catalyst for logical thinking; by forcing the model to adhere to strict syntactic and logical rules during the initial phase, it builds a stronger internal framework for deduction that translates effectively into pure mathematical contexts later on. Thus, the choice of DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 as the backbone is not merely a resource optimization but a strategic move to leverage cross-domain transfer learning specific to logical structures.

# Token Distribution Strategy in Pre-training

The composition of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B's training corpus reflects a carefully calibrated strategy designed to maximize exposure to diverse data modalities while maintaining high relevance for mathematical tasks. Unlike many domain-specific models that rely exclusively on raw text from shared vision-language corpora or specialized repositories like arXiv, DeepSeekMath employs a multi-modal token distribution derived from several distinct high-quality sources. Specifically, out of roughly 500 billion total tokens used for continuation pre-training beyond the initial check-point, approximately 56% originates from custom-curated math-related webpages collected via common crawl (the DeepSeekMath Corpus), while 4% comes from AlgebraicStack, which contains specialized algebraic code examples. This heavy weighting towards curated mathematical content ensures that the fine-grained syntactic patterns unique to mathematical typesetting (such as LaTeX) receive sufficient learning signal density to overcome potential noise present in raw web scraping or other sources.
