# (a) Experimental Configuration: Datasets, Models, and Baselines

To rigorously validate the efficacy of QLoRA, it is essential to establish a robust experimental framework that compares the quantized approach directly against established baselines. The primary objective is not merely to show that QLoRA runs fast, but to prove that it achieves parity with full 16-bit fine-tuning across diverse model architectures and scales. The authors structure their validation experiment around three distinct architectural classes: encoder-only models, encoder-decoder models, and decoder-only models. This diversity ensures that the findings are not architecture-specific but rather fundamental properties of the QLoRA methodology when applied to transformers generally. To cover the necessary ground truth for these comparisons, two specific datasets are utilized for the first phase of validation: GLUE for evaluating encoder models and Super-Natural Instructions for assessing encoder-decoder models. These tasks provide well-established benchmarks for measuring performance on standard linguistic understanding and instruction following, respectively.

For the second phase of validation, which focuses on instruction following and comprehension at scale, the authors turn to the limited-context MMLU benchmark (Massively Multitask Language Understanding) evaluated in a 5-shot setting. To prevent bias introduced by data leakage or similarity between training and evaluation sets, specific care was taken with some experiments where identical scales of data from training and validation sets would overlap in real-world scenarios; however, to ensure statistical significance without this bias at this scale, they focus on distinct evaluation tasks. Most notably, the empirical study extends to significantly larger language model scales than previously feasible for such comprehensive comparisons. The authors tune models ranging from small 7B parameter variants up through massive 65B parameter LLaMA models. This range is critical because while small models might absorb quantization errors more easily due to simpler internal structures, large languagemodels often rely heavily on precise parameter interactions; therefore, validating quantization performance up to 65B parameters is necessary to confirm that accuracy does not degrade as model size increases under the constraints of low-precision storage.

The baseline comparison strategy is twofold: standard full finetuning in 16-bit precision and standard Low-rank Adapters (LoRA) finetuning also performed in high precision. Full finetuning serves as the gold standard against which any new training procedure must be measured to claim performance recovery. If QLoRA were less accurate than full finetuning without matching LoRA performance, it would suggest an inherent loss of information irrecoverable by adapters alone. Conversely, if QLoRA matches full finetuning but outperforms or matches standard high-precision LoRA on tasks requiring broad knowledge updates (like MMLU), it validates the necessity of propagating gradients through quantized base weights rather than just updating adapter layers within frozen blocks. The experimental setup involves tuning these models on instruction-following datasets such as Alpaca and FLAN v2 across a wide spectrum of parameters—specifically examining sizes including 7B, 13B, 33B, and 65B—to provide a comprehensive view of how memory-efficient training impacts final predictive capability relative to resource-intensive training methods.

# (b) Hyperparameter Sensitivity Analysis: The Criticality of Adapter Count

A pivotal finding emerging from the hyperparameter sensitivity analysis is that the number of adapters applied is far more critical than other typical LoRA hyperparameters like the projection rank $r$ or dropout rates when aiming for full performance recovery on large base models. In many previous implementations of LoRA or attempts at parameter-efficient fine-tuning, researchers often experimented with higher values for $r$, hoping that increased adaptability would mitigate issues arising from frozen weights or quantization errors. However, our investigation into LLaMA 7B fine-tuned on Alpaca reveals that varying these parameters within reasonable ranges yields little impact on the final performance gap compared to using default settings versus multi-layer adapters. This suggests that simply increasing the dimensionality ($r$) of a single adapter does not compensate for architectural bottlenecks or data representation constraints introduced during quantization or by limiting gradient flow to specific sub-networks. Consequently, other optimization strategies such as tuning learning rates across $1e-6$ to $5e-5$ ranges become significantly more influential than adjusting $r$ or experimental dropout schedules during this initial matching phase against full finetuning baselines.

The crucial distinction lies in how gradients propagate through network layers during training with quantized versus unquantized weights.
