Chapter 1: Introduction to LLMs in Tabular Learning

The landscape of machine learning has recently undergone a seismic shift with the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), systems capable of tackling complex reasoning tasks across diverse domains without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. While these models have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in natural language processing, their potential extends far beyond text generation into structured data analysis. Specifically, tabular learning—the task of inferring patterns and making predictions from datasets organized in rows and columns, such as medical records, financial logs, or sensor readings—has emerged as a critical application area given its prevalence in real-world decision-making processes. However, standard approaches to tabular learning often face significant hurdles when labeled data is scarce, a scenario known as few-shot learning. In this environment, traditional algorithms frequently fail to generalize adequately or suffer from severe overfitting due to the inability to identify robust statistical correlations from insufficient samples. This chapter explores the convergence of these fields, examining how the inherent reasoning capabilities of LLMs can overcome these data efficiency challenges. It will detail the specific bottlenecks plaguing existing methods that attempt to plug LLMs directly into tabular workflows and introduce FeatLLM, a novel framework that reimagines the role of the LLM not as a final predictor, but as an autonomous feature engineer capable of distilling high-value insights from minimal training signals.

The challenge of few-shot learning in tabular data is particularly acute because tabular information is heterogeneous; it comprises a mix of continuous numerical values and discrete categorical labels that often lack uniform semantic meaning. Standard machine learning models rely heavily on large volumes of labeled data to learn these distributions effectively. When only a handful of labeled examples are available, conventional methods struggle significantly. For instance, a simple linear model might capture noise instead of signal if the feature space is high-dimensional relative to the number of samples $N$. More sophisticated tree-based ensembles like Random Forests or XGBoost, while powerful with large datasets, can become unstable or overfit when trained on very small $k$-shot subsets ($k \ll N$). Recent efforts have attempted to mitigate this by leveraging unlabeled data through semi-supervised learning techniques such as co-training or contrastive learning. However, these strategies are not universally applicable; they depend on assumption conditions regarding cluster assumptions or require finding augmentations that make sense for unfamiliar data structures. Furthermore, other state-of-the-art approaches for few-shot tabular learning involve pre-training specialized models on massive collections of unrelated tables before adapting them to new tasks via transfer learning. While effective in some contexts, these methods are computationally expensive during the pre-training phase and may still struggle with domains entirely outside their training distribution. Ultimately, without access to expert domain knowledge or vast amounts of labeled examples, traditional models lack the inductive bias required to perform accurate inference in low-data regimes found in critical fields like healthcare diagnostics or rare event detection.

In response to these limitations, Large Language Models offer a compelling solution by providing a rich repository of prior world knowledge and advanced reasoning capabilities that can substitute for missing labeled data or domain expertise. LLMs are trained on extensive text corpora encompassing encyclopedic knowledge, scientific literature, and procedural instructions across a broad spectrum of human activities. This means that an LLM already possesses an implicit understanding that older individuals are at higher risk for certain diseases or that specific financial metrics correlate with loan default rates long before it ever encounters specific patient records or loan applications for training purposes. By conditioning an LLM on a task description and a few illustrative examples—a technique known as few-shot prompting—the model can attend to critical features and reason about relationships between variables based on this pre-existing knowledge base rather than solely relying on statistical patterns within the limited dataset provided at inference time. Unlike rigid mathematical functions that require gradients to adjust weights from scratch given $k$ examples, an LLM can synthesize rules like "if blood pressure is above 140 AND age is over 60 then increase risk probability" by drawing upon its general knowledge and applying it to the specific few-shot context provided by the user input serialized into natural language descriptions. This ability allows LLM-based approaches to outperform conventional baselines in low-shot scenarios by effectively leveraging external priors that traditional models cannot access without explicit human engineering or massive pre-training efforts spanning unrelated domains.

Despite their promise, current methodologies for integrating LLMs into tabular learning suffer from three fundamental constraints that hinder their scalability and practical deployment: high inference costs per sample due to end-to-end architectural dependencies, reliance on inaccessible model training loops due to commercial API restrictions, and strict limitations imposed by prompt window sizes. The first limitation arises because many existing frameworks treat the LLM as an end-to-end classifier where every single inference requires sending all prediction inputs directly through the language model's processing pipeline. Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations and Problem Formulation

# Problem Definition: Formalizing the Classification Task with Labeled Samples

The foundation of any machine learning task begins with a precise mathematical definition of the problem at hand. In the context of tabular learning and classification, this involves a dataset composed of $N$ distinct samples, denoted as $\mathcal{D} = \{(\mathbf{x}^i, \mathbf{y}^i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$. Each sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ represents a single instance of the data and is mathematically formulated as a $d$-dimensional vector containing $d$ input features. These features are not merely numerical values; they are semantically defined by a set of natural language descriptions, represented as $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, which provide crucial metadata such as the data type (e.g., categorical vs. numerical) and domain-specific definitions. The relationship between these features and the target outcome is captured by the label $\mathbf{y}^i$, which belongs to a predefined finite set of classes. This formulation allows us to treat the task as a standard supervised learning problem where the goal is to map an input vector $\mathbf{x}$ to a specific class label $\mathbf{y}$.

In this framework, a critical concept for "few-shot" learning scenarios is the distinction between the full dataset available for analysis and the limited subset used for training. In low-data regimes, one cannot rely on large volumes of labeled examples; instead, only a small number $k$, where $k < N$, are randomly sampled to form the training set. This constrained environment necessitates model architectures that can generalize from minimal information while leveraging external prior knowledge. The formalization establishes that every sample possesses explicit feature definitions ($F$) alongside its observed values ($\mathbf{x}^i$), setting the stage for converting these heterogeneous data types into a structured format suitable for computational processing. Furthermore, recognizing that real-world datasets vary significantly in dimensionality—with some containing over 100 attributes—the problem definition must account for scenarios where $d$ is large, which historically poses challenges for methods dependent on fixed context windows in language models.

# From Natural Language to Boolean Logic: Translating Heuristic Rules into Binary Indicators

A pivotal innovation in bridging the gap between human reasoning and machine inference is the transformation of natural language rules into computable binary indicators. Traditional machine learning models operate directly on raw feature vectors, whereas this approach introduces an intermediate step where an interpretable model (such as an LLM) generates "rules" based on prior knowledge and data observations. These rules are human-readable logical conditions that describe specific criteria under which a particular class should be identified—for instance, "If age is greater than 50 AND blood pressure exceeds 140, then High Risk." To make these linguistic insights machine-processable, they must be translated from semantic text into Boolean logic operators (AND, OR, NOT) and mathematical comparisons (greater than, less than).

The theoretical mechanism behind this translation relies on parsing strategies that convert textual descriptions into executable code or direct logical evaluations. Since large language models often generate syntactic variations—such as writing "greater than" instead of "$>$"—the system employs an automated parsing procedure using the LLM itself to generate Python code based on these inferred rules. This code represents the semantic intent of the rules without requiring manual engineering or complex custom parsers for every dataset variant. Once parsed and executed against each sample in the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, these logic gates yield binary outcomes for each rule relative to each class. Specifically, for any given sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ and class $k$, if sample $i$ satisfies rule $r$, it produces a binary value; otherwise, it does not. This process effectively projects the high-dimensional semantic space of natural language descriptions onto a discrete Boolean space, creating new intermediate variables derived solely from logical operations rather than raw data patterns alone."

Chapter 3: Architecture of FeatLLM – Rule Extraction via Prompt Engineering

The core innovation of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework lies in its strategic shift from using Large Language Models (LLMs) as end-to-end predictors to repositioning them as expert feature engineers. By structuring the interaction between the model and the data through a rigorous macro-logical process, \textsf{FeatLLM} harnesses the in-context learning capabilities of LLMs to extract high-quality decision rules. These rules are not merely suggestions but are converted into actionable binary features that can be processed by downstream, computationally efficient machine learning models. The architecture of \textsf{FeatLLM} relies heavily on a meticulously designed prompt that guides the LLM through a specific chain-of-thought reasoning path, ensuring that generated rules are causally grounded and logically sound rather than spurious correlations derived from noise. This chapter details the architectural components of this prompt design, analyzing how each section—from basic information description to deductive rule derivation—contributes to the overall efficacy of the system.

Prompt Design Philosophy: Guiding Chain-of-Thought reasoning step-by-step

The fundamental design philosophy underpinning the \textsf{FeatLLM} architecture is the implementation of a structured, two-step reasoning chain designed to mimic human expert decision-making processes in tabular data analysis. Standard few-shot approaches often feed raw data and task descriptions directly into an LLM, relying on the model's implicit ability to associate features with outcomes. However, this approach can lead to inconsistencies or spurious correlations, particularly in low-shot regimes where statistical patterns are scarce. To mitigate this, \textsf{FeatLLM} decomposes the complex task of rule generation into distinct cognitive steps that must be completed sequentially within a single generation pass. This decomposition forces the LLM to engage in deep reasoning rather than superficial pattern matching.

The first step in this architectural flow involves activating prior knowledge without immediate reference to specific training examples. The model is prompted to infer causal relationships or tendencies between features and the target task based solely on general world knowledge or common sense present in its pre-training corpus. This step is critical because it leverages the vast domain-specific knowledge encoded in the LLM's weights before it even touches potentially noisy or limited training data. For instance, if predicting a chronic disease, the model is encouraged to recall general medical knowledge linking age or specific biological markers to disease outcomes without being constrained by a single outlier sample in the training set. By isolating this reasoning stage, \textsf{FeatLLM} ensures that foundational logic is established independently of small-sample biases. The second step then builds upon these inferred relationships, introducing specific few-shot demonstrations and previously deduced tendencies to derive precise classification rules for each class. This separation prevents the model from blindly fitting noise into examples without understanding underlying mechanisms and creates a robust regularization effect inherent to the prompting strategy itself.

Sub-task 1: Basic Information Description – Structuring feature definitions, types, and few-shot examples effectively

The foundation of any effective prompt for tabular learning is a comprehensive and structured ingestion of basic information about the problem domain and dataset attributes. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, this sub-task serves as the context setting phase where essential metadata is communicated to transform abstract natural language instructions into grounded problem-solving actions. The prompt explicitly includes a detailed description of the task at hand, often formulated as a direct question such as "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?". This framing aligns with common language patterns used by users and sets clear expectations for the output format immediately upon input receipt. Alongside task definitions, feature descriptions are provided for every column in $\mathbf{x}$ consisting of $d$ dimensions. These descriptions are not limited to generic names; they include value types (e.g., integer, float) and optional definitions explaining what each variable represents physically or conceptually. For categorical variables where discrete states are relevant for rule formation (e.g., specific tumor stages), explicit lists of possible categories are included in the basic information block.
