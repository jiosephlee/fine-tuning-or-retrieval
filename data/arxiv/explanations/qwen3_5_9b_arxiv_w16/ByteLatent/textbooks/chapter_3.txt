# System Overview

The Byte Latent Transformer (\textsc BLT) reimagines the fundamental unit of computation for large language models by abandoning the static, fixed-vocabulary tokens that have historically defined architectures like Llama in favor of dynamically sized patches derived from raw bytes. At a high level, the system operates as a sophisticated pipeline where raw byte sequences are first fragmented into variable-length groups, or patches, based on their information density. These patches then serve as the input for a massive global transformer that performs the bulk of the reasoning and context processing. Finally, these processed latent representations are decoded back into byte sequences. This workflow is designed to solve the core inefficiency of traditional models: the indiscriminate allocation of compute. In tokenization-based systems, every token incurs the same computational cost regardless of whether the next token is trivial to predict or requires immense reasoning. \textsc BLT addresses this by segmenting data such that high-entropy, difficult-to-predict regions result in smaller patches (requiring more transformer steps), while predictable, low-entropy regions coalesce into larger patches (requiring fewer transformer steps). By controlling the average patch size, the architecture effectively tunes the frequency with which the expensive global model is invoked relative to the local encoding and decoding costs. This results in an architecture where a single inference or training step of the global model processes a variable amount of raw data, allowing for a dynamic trade-off between model capacity and data coverage that fixed-token architectures cannot achieve.

# The Latent Global Transformer

The heart of the \textsc BLT architecture is the Latent Global Transformer, denoted as $\mathcal{G}$. This is a standard autoregressive transformer model equipped with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers, serving as the primary engine for understanding long-range dependencies and performing complex reasoning over the input data. Unlike models that operate directly on every character or byte, $\mathcal{G}$ operates exclusively on a sequence of latent patch representations $\pmb{p}=\{p_j\}$. The model maps an input sequence of these latent representations into an output sequence through attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks. A critical architectural choice for $\mathcal{G}$ is its attention mask; it utilizes a block-causal mask which restricts attention to be strictly up to and including the current patch within the current document during pre-training. This ensures that the generation process remains autoregressive while respecting dynamic boundaries. Because this model consumes by far the majority of Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) during both training and inference phases due to its depth and dimensionality, its invocation frequency dictates the overall efficiency of \textsc BLT. The brilliance of this design lies in its intermittent activation: while two smaller local models operate on every individual byte for encoding and decoding tasks respectively, $\mathcal{G}$ only needs to be executed once per patch generated by those local models. Consequently, by increasing the average patch size, one reduces how often $\mathcal{G}$ runs without needing to decrease model capacity or lose representational power at that scale, thereby freeing up computational budget elsewhere or allowing for larger models within fixed constraints.

# The Local Encoder

Flanking the global transformer on either side are lightweight local models designed specifically to bridge the gap between raw data and latent space. The Local Encoder Model, denoted by $\mathcal{E}$, is responsible for ingesting raw bytes $b_i$ and mapping them into expressive latent patch representations $p_j$. Structurally, $\mathcal{E}$ mirrors modern transformer giants in its core composition but differs significantly in scale; it typically employs far fewer layers than its global counterpart ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$). Its architecture follows an alternating stack of standard transformer layers and cross-attention blocks, specifically designed to aggregate information across all bytes belonging to a specific patch before passing it upward. The encoder processes bytes using a "local block causal" attention mask within each layer, meaning each byte can attend to a fixed forward-looking window $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ without crossing document boundaries or arbitrary dynamic patch boundaries prematurely. This ensures that local context modeling remains focused and efficient within small neighborhoods before being projected into high-dimensional latent space via cross-attention mechanisms detailed later in this chapter.
