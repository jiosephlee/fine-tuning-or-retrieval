Chapter 1: The Limits of Static Tokenization

Modern large language models have been dominated by a specific architectural paradigm where raw data is first converted into a discrete set of symbols before being processed by the neural network. This process, known as tokenization, has been an essential heuristic, yet it introduces fundamental constraints that prevent models from scaling efficiently or robustly. The prevailing approach employs subword tokenizers such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) or WordPiece. These methods operate offline to construct a static vocabulary prior to training, grouping bytes into tokens based on their frequency in the training corpus. The core hypothesis driving this approach is that compressing text into high-frequency tokens reduces the sequence length of the input, thereby speeding up computation. However, as we explore the architecture of these models, it becomes evident that this compression strategy trades immediate efficiency for long-term rigidity. Because the vocabulary is fixed and derived from statistical heuristics rather than semantic necessity, static tokenization creates a mismatch between where computation is actually needed and where it is allocated. This chapter will delve into these limitations, establishing the motivation for a new paradigm where compute is dynamically allocated based on the complexity of the data itself.

The fundamental role of tokenization in Large Language Models (LLMs) is to bridge the gap between the continuous stream of bytes that make up digital text and the discrete input requirements of transformer architectures. Transformer models are trained to predict the next element in a sequence; however, they cannot operate directly on raw bytes due to massive vocabulary sizes and high redundancy in compressed data representations like whitespace or common characters like 'a' and 'e'. Instead, tokenizers perform an offline compression step. For instance, BPE begins with individual bytes (or characters) and iteratively merges pairs of tokens that appear most frequently together during training until it reaches a target vocabulary size, often capped around 32k or 128k tokens for modern models like Llama 3 or GPT-4. This process incorporates an end-of-at-context (EOT) thought process implicitly through merging statistics. While effective at reducing sequence length for common content—turning the word "the" into a single token rather than three—this method relies entirely on arbitrary boundaries defined by frequencies in the training set rather than linguistic meaning or information entropy. Consequently, every position within a padded batch requires exactly one model pass per token. Even if predicting the next byte after "the" in the sentence "the cat sat" is statistically trivial because it is almost certainly "cat", a tokenizer-based model must commit full computational resources to generate that prediction because "t", "h", "e" are compressed into one abstract unit, and "cats" might be split or merged differently depending on context in other batches, breaking consistency across similar inputs.

Despite its ubiquity, static tokenization suffers from several critical limitations that hinder robustness and generalization in large-scale applications. Perhaps most notably is domain and modality sensitivity; because tokens are learned heuristically from specific corpora like CommonCrawl or Wikipedia, features specific to domains not well-represented in those sources may result in poor predictions or odd token splits. A model fine-tuned on code might struggle with scientific notation if subword boundaries drawn during pre-training were biased toward natural language patterns. Furthermore, static tokenizers lack inherent orthographic knowledge; they treat foreign loanwords or novel neologisms as opaque sequences of bytes until those specific combinations appear frequently enough to be merged into tokens during training phases deemed sufficient for optimization purposes but insufficient for capturing subtle spelling variations required by tasks requiring deep character manipulation benchmarks such as CUTE or phonology tasks like grapheme-to-phoneme conversion without auxiliary training objectives specifically designed for these nuances. # Chapter 2: Dynamic Patching Strategies

The fundamental challenge in training language models on raw byte data, as opposed to compressed tokens, lies in the explosion of sequence length. When a model processes every single byte as an individual token, the computational cost scales linearly with the amount of input data, making it prohibitive to train large-scale models within practical budgets. To address this, the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) introduces the concept of "patching," a mechanism that groups consecutive bytes into larger computational units called patches. These patches serve as the primary inputs to the model's global latent transformer, effectively reducing the number of steps required to process a sequence of text. While early approaches to byte modeling relied on static rules or fixed strides, leading to inefficient resource allocation, BLT moves toward a dynamic paradigm where the grouping of bytes is guided by the underlying complexity of the data itself. This section will explore the evolution of patching strategies within BLT, starting from simple static methods and culminating in the sophisticated entropy-based approach that forms the core of its efficiency gains.

### Static Strided Patching

The most straightforward method for converting a raw byte sequence into patches is static strided patching. In this approach, bytes are grouped non-overlapping windows of a fixed size $k$, such that every $k$-th byte initiates a new patch boundary regardless of the content or context. While this strategy is computationally inexpensive to implement and allows for precise control over the number of floating-point operations (flops) required by simply adjusting $k$, it suffers from significant shortcomings regarding compute allocation. By enforcing a rigid structure, static patching treats all regions of text identically: it allocates an equal amount of model capacity whether it is predicting whitespace-filled HTML tags or complex mathematical notation. This uniformity leads to wasted resources on low-complexity regions where few parameters are needed for accurate prediction and insufficient resources on high-complexity regions that demand deeper reasoning. Consequently, while static patching establishes a baseline for reducing sequence length compared to per-byte processing, it fails to adaptively allocate compute based on data complexity, limiting its potential for achieving compute-optimal performance at scale. To overcome this inefficiency, simpler heuristics were explored before moving toward data-driven methods.

### Whitespace Patching (SpaceByte)

Recognizing that not all bytes carry equal information density naturally occurring boundaries provide opportunities for efficient segmentation without sacrificing context integrity. The SpaceByte approach introduces whitespace patching, leveraging space-like bytes—such as periods, commas, or newlines—as natural linguistic delimiters between tokens or phrases. Instead of arbitrarily splitting words in half like static strided patching might cause with a stride of four or five bytes, redefining two groups always encompass full words or meaningful linguistic units whenever possible ensures that coarse-grained predictive tasks aligned with semantic boundaries are handled uniformly across different sequences. Chapter 3: The Byte Latent Transformer Architecture

# System Overview

The Byte Latent Transformer (\textsc BLT) reimagines the fundamental unit of computation for large language models by abandoning the static, fixed-vocabulary tokens that have historically defined architectures like Llama in favor of dynamically sized patches derived from raw bytes. At a high level, the system operates as a sophisticated pipeline where raw byte sequences are first fragmented into variable-length groups, or patches, based on their information density. These patches then serve as the input for a massive global transformer that performs the bulk of the reasoning and context processing. Finally, these processed latent representations are decoded back into byte sequences. This workflow is designed to solve the core inefficiency of traditional models: the indiscriminate allocation of compute. In tokenization-based systems, every token incurs the same computational cost regardless of whether the next token is trivial to predict or requires immense reasoning. \textsc BLT addresses this by segmenting data such that high-entropy, difficult-to-predict regions result in smaller patches (requiring more transformer steps), while predictable, low-entropy regions coalesce into larger patches (requiring fewer transformer steps). By controlling the average patch size, the architecture effectively tunes the frequency with which the expensive global model is invoked relative to the local encoding and decoding costs. This results in an architecture where a single inference or training step of the global model processes a variable amount of raw data, allowing for a dynamic trade-off between model capacity and data coverage that fixed-token architectures cannot achieve.

# The Latent Global Transformer

The heart of the \textsc BLT architecture is the Latent Global Transformer, denoted as $\mathcal{G}$. This is a standard autoregressive transformer model equipped with $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers, serving as the primary engine for understanding long-range dependencies and performing complex reasoning over the input data. Unlike models that operate directly on every character or byte, $\mathcal{G}$ operates exclusively on a sequence of latent patch representations $\pmb{p}=\{p_j\}$. The model maps an input sequence of these latent representations into an output sequence through attention mechanisms and feed-forward networks. A critical architectural choice for $\mathcal{G}$ is its attention mask; it utilizes a block-causal mask which restricts attention to be strictly up to and including the current patch within the current document during pre-training. This ensures that the generation process remains autoregressive while respecting dynamic boundaries. Because this model consumes by far the majority of Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) during both training and inference phases due to its depth and dimensionality, its invocation frequency dictates the overall efficiency of \textsc BLT. The brilliance of this design lies in its intermittent activation: while two smaller local models operate on every individual byte for encoding and decoding tasks respectively, $\mathcal{G}$ only needs to be executed once per patch generated by those local models. Consequently, by increasing the average patch size, one reduces how often $\mathcal{G}$ runs without needing to decrease model capacity or lose representational power at that scale, thereby freeing up computational budget elsewhere or allowing for larger models within fixed constraints.

# The Local Encoder

Flanking the global transformer on either side are lightweight local models designed specifically to bridge the gap between raw data and latent space. The Local Encoder Model, denoted by $\mathcal{E}$, is responsible for ingesting raw bytes $b_i$ and mapping them into expressive latent patch representations $p_j$. Structurally, $\mathcal{E}$ mirrors modern transformer giants in its core composition but differs significantly in scale; it typically employs far fewer layers than its global counterpart ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$). Its architecture follows an alternating stack of standard transformer layers and cross-attention blocks, specifically designed to aggregate information across all bytes belonging to a specific patch before passing it upward. The encoder processes bytes using a "local block causal" attention mask within each layer, meaning each byte can attend to a fixed forward-looking window $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ without crossing document boundaries or arbitrary dynamic patch boundaries prematurely. This ensures that local context modeling remains focused and efficient within small neighborhoods before being projected into high-dimensional latent space via cross-attention mechanisms detailed later in this chapter.

Chapter 4: Methodology, Scaling Laws, and Experimental Setup

"How do we know it works?" To answer this fundamental question, we must move beyond anecdotal success on small tasks and rigorously evaluate the Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}) against state-of-the-art tokenization-based models. In this chapter, we dissect the experimental machinery built to validate { \textsc BLT }'s claims of superior efficiency and robustness. We begin by examining the massive datasets used to train models up to 8B parameters, ensuring that our training data is treated fairly. We then establish a precise vocabulary of metrics, defining how we measure computational cost in Floating Point Operations (FLOPs) and how we quantify model performance independently of tokenizer artifacts using Bits-Per-Byte. With these foundations laid, we dive into the specific hyperparameters governing the architecture's function, from learning rate schedules to attention mechanisms. Finally, we detail the two distinct scaling regimes studied: a compute-optimal regime to prove parity with baselines under fair conditions, and a fixed-inference regime that reveals { \textsc BLT }'s unique ability to scale both model size and patch length without increasing cost.

Dataset Construction

The validity of any large language model hinges on the quality and quantity of its training data. For our experiments, we utilized two primary datasets designed to serve different purposes within our scaling study. The first is the Llama 2 dataset, a mixture comprising 2 trillion tokens gathered from various publicly available sources. This dataset was cleaned and filtered to improve data quality and was extensively used for our compute-optimal scaling law experiments. By using this industry-standard mixture, we ensured a direct comparison with leading tokenization-based models like Llama 3 during our initial scaling runs. It allowed us to test whether { \textsc BLT } could achieve efficient scaling trends under the same conditions where those dominant models succeeded.

The second dataset represents a significant leap in scale: {\textsc BLT}-1T. This new dataset contains approximately 1 trillion tokens (equivalent to roughly 4 trillion bytes) collected from public sources and includes a substantial subset of pre-training data released by Datacomp-LM. Unlike the Llama 2 mix used for initial hyperparameter tuning on smaller scales, this larger dataset was employed for a complete end-to-end pre-training run of an 8B parameter { \textsc BLT } model to benchmark against real-world models trained on even larger scales. Notably, neither of these datasets includes data gathered from Meta products or services, ensuring that all incoming data remains synthetic in its origin relative to our training environment but comprehensive in its coverage of human knowledge found on the public web.

To maintain a level playing field when comparing byte-level models against tokenized ones, it is crucial to understand how sequence length interacts with patch size during batching. In standard tokenization-based models like Llama 3 with a vocabulary size of 128K tokens, the average token size is roughly 3.7 to 4 bytes depending on the specific tokenizer version (Llama 2 vs. Llama 3). For { \textsc BLT }, however, because patches are dynamically sized based on entropy rather than fixed by a heuristic vocabulary boundary before training begins, there is inherent variability in patch lengths within a single batch. To avoid giving { \textsc BLT } an unfair advantage due to artificially longer context windows generated by fewer steps, we strictly controlled the average batch size in terms of bytes rather than sequences. Specifically, for experiments on the Llama 2 data mix, we maintained an average context length of 8k bytes per sample while training processes that packed batches consistently at an average size of 16M bytes. When moving to the {\textsc BLT}-1T dataset for larger scale evaluations, we increased this average context length to roughly 16k bytes while keeping the byte-wise batch size constant at 16M bytes. Additionally, during loading phases for both datasets, sequences were padded or truncated—specifically capped at sequences upending memory limits—to manage memory spikes caused by unusually long patches generated by dynamic entropy calculations. This rigorous control ensures that any performance differences observed are due to architectural superiority rather than implicit access to larger contexts or padded inefficiencies inherent in dynamic segmentations.

Metrics for Comparison

When working with tokenization-free architectures like { \textsc BLT }, standard perplexity becomes an ambiguous metric because perplexity is traditionally defined as $V^{1/n}$ or related distributions over tokens from a finite vocabulary $V$. Since { \textsc BLT } does not use a fixed vocabulary for its patches but operates directly over raw bytes or latent representations derived from them, comparing its likelihood scores directly to BPE-based models is apples-to-oranges comparison unless normalized correctly. To resolve this ambiguity and enable fair comparisons across any architecture operating on byte streams regardless of discretization strategies—whether fixed BPE tokens or our dynamic patches—we adopted Bits-Per-Byte (BPB). BPB serves as our tokenizer-independent version of perplexity and allows us to compare compression efficiency directly at the byte level without intermediate rounding artifacts introduced by sub-word boundaries.

$$
\text{BPB}(\pmb{x}) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}
$$
