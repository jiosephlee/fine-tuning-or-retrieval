# Chapter 1: Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a transformative shift in recent years, moving beyond simple pattern recognition to the development of systems capable of deep reasoning, complex code generation, and rigorous mathematical problem-solving. At the heart of this evolution stands Reinforcement Learning (RL), which has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling language models. Unlike earlier approaches that relied primarily on static datasets or human feedback labels collected once, RL allows language models to actively engage with environments—be they competitive math problems, coding benchmarks, or logical puzzles—and learn through trial and error. By undertaking deeper and longer reasoning processes, models trained via large-scale RL develop the capability to tackle sophisticated tasks that were previously inaccessible. This progression marks a move from merely predicting the next word to mastering complex cognitive skills, positioning RL as the primary driver for pushing the boundaries of what large language models can achieve.

However, unlocking this potential requires navigating a treacherous terrain known as training instability. As we invest greater computational resources into these systems, employing massive model sizes and extended response lengths, the foremost prerequisite for success becomes maintaining stable and robust training dynamics. Without stability, the immense compute invested yields diminishing returns or worse, complete failure. The journey toward advanced AI capabilities is currently hindered by severe stability issues exhibited by current state-of-the-art algorithms when applied to gigantic language models. These instabilities often manifest as catastrophic and irreversible model collapses, where the learning process derails completely, rendering subsequent attempts futile regardless of hyperparameter tuning or checkpoint recovery. Understanding why this happens and how to overcome it is central to grasping the future direction of AI research.

To appreciate the urgency of addressing these stability issues, one must trace the evolution of LLM alignment techniques. Initially, researchers focused on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where models learned from curated examples of desired outputs. While effective for basic instruction following, SFT alone was insufficient for cultivating true reasoning abilities or handling open-ended domains like competitive programming or Olympiad-level mathematics. Consequently, the field pivoted toward Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and its variants like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). These methods introduced reward signals derived from human preferences or automated verifiers to guide the model away from generic text generation toward helpfulness and correctness. Yet, as models grew in scale—from billions to trillions of parameters—the complexity of their internal representations increased exponentially. The simple reward structures used in early iterations became inadequate for guiding such vast architectures safely through the loss landscape. The transition from SFT to RLHF represented a qualitative leap in capability, but it also exposed a critical fragility: as model size and task difficulty scaled up alongside compute budgets, existing algorithms began to fail spectacularly under their own weight.

This brings us to the specific crisis facing modern training pipelines: the instability inherent in current state-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO was designed specifically to address some inefficiencies in prior methods by eliminating the need for a separate value model—a component that traditionally required significant memory and was difficult to train reliably at scale. Instead, GRPO computes advantages relatively within groups of responses generated by an old policy before updating to a new one. Despite its innovations in architecture efficiency and memory footprint reduction, GRPO suffers from fundamental flaws when deployed on gigantic models engaged in long-context reasoning tasks. The core issue lies in how it handles off-policy corrections during training. Because RL involves sampling actions (tokens) based on an older version of the policy while trying to optimize towards a newer version—a classic off-policy scenario—it relies heavily on importance sampling weights to correct for distributional shifts between generations taken by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ versus those preferred by $\pi_\theta$.

The limitation of GRPO arises because it applies these importance sampling weights at every single token level within a sequence rather than treating the entire response as a coherent unit of optimization. When generating long responses typical of complex reasoning tasks involving hundreds or thousands of tokens, small errors compound dramatically along the trajectory created by individual token probabilities being multiplied together over time $t$. Applying high-variance correction factors at each step amplifies this noise exponentially across long sequences rather than dampening it as theory might suggest under ideal conditions with sufficient sample averaging per sample point. # Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations of Off-Policy Correction

In the realm of large-scale Reinforcement Learning (RL) for language models, the ability to train efficiently and stably is paramount. As models grow in complexity—particularly with architectures like Mixture-of-Experts—and as the expected response lengths increase, the computational cost of generating data grows exponentially. Consequently, practitioners must rely heavily on off-policy learning techniques, where policies are updated using data generated by older versions of themselves. While this approach maximizes hardware utilization, it introduces a fundamental statistical challenge: correcting for the distributional shift between the behavior policy that collected the data and the target policy currently being optimized. To understand why modern algorithms struggle with stability and how newer approaches like Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) resolve these issues, one must first establish a rigorous understanding of the mathematical tools used to bridge this gap. This chapter dissects the core concepts of RL in this domain, explores the theoretical framework of importance sampling, analyzes the mechanics of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), and critically examines why applying importance corrections at the token level can lead to catastrophic failures.

## Core Concepts: Policies, Queries, Responses, Verifiers, and Rewards

Before delving into optimization objectives, we must precisely define the environment and variables governing Large Language Model (LLM) training via RL. An autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$ acts as a stochastic policy denoted as $\pi_\theta$. This policy takes a prompt or query $x$ from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and generates a sequence of tokens $y$ one step at a time. The probability of generating a complete response $y$ given a query $x$ under policy $\pi_\theta$ is calculated as the product of conditional probabilities for each token in the sequence:
$$ \pi_\theta(y|x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t}) $$
Here, $|y|$ represents the total number of tokens in the response $y$, and $y_{<t}$ denotes all tokens preceding index $t$. This formulation highlights that every token prediction depends on its immediate history within that specific trajectory.

To determine whether a generated response is good or bad, we employ a scoring mechanism known as a verifier. For any given query-response pair $(x, y)$, this verifier assigns a scalar score called a reward, denoted as $r(x, y)$, which typically falls within the interval $[0, 1]$. In practical implementations involving groups of responses—as seen in GRPO and GSPO—the raw rewards are often normalized relative to other responses generated for the same query to create advantages that guide learning. The ultimate goal of reinforcement learning in this context is to adjust parameters $\theta$ such that responses yielding higher rewards become increasingly probable under $\pi_\theta$, while low-reward responses become less likely. However, because we generate all candidate responses using an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, we cannot simply maximize expected reward directly; we must account for how likely these specific trajectories were under our current generation process versus our desired target process. This necessity gives rise to off-policy correction methods grounded in probability theory.

## Importance Sampling Theory: Estimating Expectations Under Different Distributions

The central mathematical tool enabling off-policy learning is Importance Sampling (IS). Consider a scenario where we wish to estimate the expectation of some function $f(z)$ with respect to a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}(z)$:
$$ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [f(z)] $$
Directly sampling from $\pi_\text{tar}$ might be computationally prohibitive or impossible if we are trying to learn what happens when we reach that distribution. Instead, suppose we have access to samples drawn from a different "behavior" distribution $\pi_\text{beh}(z)$. Importance Sampling allows us to rewrite this expectation by weighting each sample according to how much more likely it was under our target distribution compared to our behavior distribution:
$$ \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)} f(z) \right] $$
The term $w(z) = \frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$ is known as the importance weight or importance ratio. Crucially, for IS to work correctly and reduce variance effectively, one must average these weights over many independent samples drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$. The law of large numbers ensures that as the number of samples increases ($N \gg 1$), the weighted average converges reliably to true expectations under $\pi_\text{tar}$. Chapter 3: Diagnosing the Failure Mode of Token-Level Optimization

# The Unit Mismatch Problem: Rewarding Sequences but Optimizing Tokens

At the heart of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for language models lies a fundamental tension between how success is measured and how parameters are updated. In modern architectures like Large Language Models (LLMs), the reward signal is almost exclusively granted at the sequence level. A verifier evaluates an entire response $y$ generated for a query $x$, producing a scalar score $r(x, y)$ that reflects the overall quality, correctness, or utility of that complete output. However, the optimization algorithms used to train these models, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its variants like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), traditionally operate by decomposing this global reward into local signals assigned to individual tokens. This decomposition creates what we call the "Unit Mismatch Problem." When an algorithm attempts to optimize a sequence-level objective using token-level importance ratios, it violates the basic principles of probability theory required for unbiased gradient estimation in off-policy settings.

To understand why this mismatch is critical, one must recall the mathematical foundation of Importance Sampling (IS). IS allows us to estimate expectations under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ using samples drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. The validity of this technique rests on a crucial assumption: the importance weight must represent the ratio of probabilities for the *entire* event being evaluated. Mathematically, if we wish to estimate $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}} [f(z)]$, we rewrite it as $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}} [\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)} f(z)]$. Here, $z$ represents the full object of interest—in our case, the complete response sequence $y$. The weight $\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y|x)}$ correctly accounts for how much more likely the entire sequence is under the new policy compared to the old one.

In contrast, algorithms like GRPO apply this logic incorrectly by defining importance weights at every single timestep $t$. They calculate $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$ and then sum these weighted contributions over the length of the sequence to form an advantage estimate. This approach treats each token prediction as an independent off-policy correction step. However, because each token conditional probability depends on all preceding tokens in that specific trajectory ($y_{i,<t}$), these local ratios do not aggregate cleanly into a valid estimator for the total sequence likelihood ratio without additional assumptions about independence that simply do not hold in autoregressive generation. By decoupling the correction factor from the actual unit receiving the reward—the whole sequence—GRPO introduces a systematic bias and high-variance noise into its gradient estimates that grows monotonically with response length.

Furthermore, this unit mismatch exacerbates issues related to consistency between policy updates and routing decisions in sparse models. In Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, which utilize dynamic routing mechanisms to activate subsets of experts based on input content, small perturbations in early tokens can cascade into completely different sets of activated experts later in the sequence. If importance weighting is applied token-by-token based on conditional probabilities derived from potentially divergent expert paths at intermediate steps, the resulting weights become unstable indicators of true policy shift. The algorithm ends up penalizing or rewarding tokens based on artifacts of transient routing choices rather than genuine improvements in final outcome quality. Consequently, optimizing at this granular granularity forces conflicting pressure on different parts of a long chain: improving one part might destabilize another due to altered routing contexts downstream or upstream, whereas optimizing at the sequence level ensures that every parameter update moves toward maximizing $r(x,y)$ coherently across all timesteps simultaneously.

# Variance Accumulation Over Long Horizons: How Noise Destroys Convergence

The instability inherent in token-level optimization becomes particularly devastating when dealing with long-horizon tasks common in advanced reasoning applications such as competitive mathematics or complex coding challenges. As language models generate longer responses involving hundreds or thousands of tokens, errors introduced by incorrect importance weighting do not remain isolated; they accumulate additively across every timestep until they overwhelm useful learning signals entirely. Consider a response composed of many tokens where each carries an independently estimated importance ratio $w_t$. Even if these ratios are nominally bounded by clipping mechanisms designed to keep them close to unity—for instance within $(1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)$—their product over a long horizon determines their collective influence on gradients via terms resembling $(1+\delta)^L$, where $L$ is sequence length and $\delta$ represents deviation from 1 due to noise or approximation error.

Chapter 4: Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

# The Core Innovation: Redefining Importance Ratios via Sequence Likelihood

To understand the transformative nature of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), one must first confront a fundamental flaw in the prevailing Reinforcement Learning (RL) paradigms for Large Language Models, specifically the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm. The central thesis of this chapter is that GRPO suffers from an ill-posed objective because it misapplies the principles of importance sampling. In standard statistical theory, importance sampling is designed to estimate the expected value of a function under a target distribution by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution. Crucially, this method relies on aggregating corrections over many independent samples to smooth out variance and accurately reflect the distributional shift. However, GRPO calculates importance weights at every single token position within a response. Because these weights are computed based on conditional probabilities for individual tokens—essentially treating each step as an isolated event rather than part of a coherent whole—the algorithm fails to perform genuine distribution correction. Instead, it injects high-variance noise into the gradient estimates. As these noisy signals accumulate over increasingly long sequences, they destabilize the training process, often leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse.

GSPO addresses this foundational issue by fundamentally shifting the unit of analysis from the token level to the sequence level. Rather than calculating an importance ratio for every discrete prediction made by the model along the generation path, GSPO defines a single importance ratio for the entire response $y$. Mathematically, this ratio represents how much more likely (or less likely) the complete sequence $y$ is under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ compared to the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. By anchoring the optimization metric to the total sequence likelihood, GSPO ensures that the correction factor directly corresponds to how well-aligned a specific full response is with the desired outcome. This approach aligns perfectly with how rewards are assigned in RLHF contexts: rewards are scalar values awarded to entire generations, not summed or averaged across individual tokens arbitrarily. Consequently, applying off-policy correction at the sequence level respects the integrity of both the reward signal and the generative process, providing a theoretically sound basis for stable learning dynamics that token-level methods simply cannot achieve.

# Deriving GSPO’s Objective Function: Equation Analysis and Interpretation

The formal definition of GSPO emerges directly from this sequence-level perspective. The algorithm optimizes an objective function that mirrors structural similarities to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) but operates entirely on aggregated response data rather than token-by-token updates. The core objective function for GSPO is expressed as an expectation over queries $x$ sampled from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and groups of responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ generated by an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Within each group corresponding to a query $x$, there are $G$ distinct responses generated independently. For each response $y_i$, GSPO computes two critical components: a normalized advantage score $\widehat{A}_i$ and a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. The final update rule seeks to maximize:
$$
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta)  \widehat{A}_{i},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon}\right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right]
$$
Here, $\widehat{A}_i$ is calculated using group-relative normalization, subtracting the mean reward of all responses in the group and dividing by their standard deviation:
$$
\widehat{A}_{i} = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean} \left( \{ r(x, y_i) \}_{i=1}^G \right) } { { std }   { }\left( { }\left( { }\right)\right)}
$$
This formulation ensures that if one response in a group receives significantly higher feedback than others generated for that same prompt, it will receive positive credit regardless of whether its absolute reward score was low or high globally. The term inside the minimum function combines this advantage with the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ before applying PPO-style clipping. The clipping mechanism restricts $s_i(\theta)$ to lie within $[1-\varepsilon_-, 1+\varepsilon_+]$, preventing excessively large updates driven by rare events or drastic shifts in probability mass between policies. Importantly, unlike GRPO which clips thousands of times per batch (once per token), GSPO clips exactly once per response in each group ($G$ times per batch). Despite reducing these operations significantly—a fact we will revisit regarding efficiency—this coarse-grained clipping proves far more effective at filtering out unstable gradients caused by poor off-policy alignment.

# Length Normalization Strategies for Variance Control

A subtle yet critical component of GSPO's design is its treatment of sequence length within the calculation of importance ratios. If one were naively to define importance simply as $\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)}$, extreme variations could arise solely due to differences in generation length between two plausible responses generated by slightly different policies or due to stochasticity in early decoding steps affecting subsequent paths differently over time. Specifically, small fluctuations in probability assignments at early tokens can compound exponentially as they propagate through long chains of autoregressive predictions ($|y|$ can be hundreds or thousands). Without mitigation, these compounded errors would cause massive swings in raw likelihood ratios unrelated to semantic quality or adherence to instructions merely reflecting path divergence accumulation over distance traveled down decision trees formed during generation phases themselves!
