# No More Routing Replay: How GSPO Simplifies MoE Training

Training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models using Reinforcement Learning (RL) has long been plagued by instability. The sparse activation nature of these architectures means that the set of experts processed for a single response can fluctuate significantly between gradient updates. This "expert-activation volatility" often leads to catastrophic model collapse or prevents convergence unless heavily mitigated. In our recent work on Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), we identify the root cause of this instability and demonstrate how shifting the optimization unit from tokens to sequences fundamentally resolves the issue, rendering complex workarounds like Routing Replay obsolete.

To understand why standard methods struggle, consider the objective function of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO optimizes an importance ratio calculated at every individual token position. In a dense model, this works reasonably well, but in an MoE model, the dynamics change drastically. Because RL training frequently updates routing parameters, the activated experts for a specific prefix can change between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$. When calculating the token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, we are essentially comparing probabilities computed by different sub-networks for the same input token. This mismatch causes these weights to fluctuate wildly and become statistically invalid, introducing high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and derails training.

To combat this, previous implementations relied on a strategy called **Routing Replay**. This approach caches which experts were activated in $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and forces $\pi_{\theta}$ to use exactly those same experts during the current update step when computing ratios. While effective at stabilizing gradients momentarily, this technique introduces significant overhead. It requires additional memory to store routing states and complex communication logic to enforce matching activations across distributed training clusters. More critically, it acts as an artificial constraint; by reusing past routing modes, you are effectively limiting the moe model's capacity to explore better routing configurations during optimization. We cannot simply bind our large models into a specific expert structure forever.

GSPO addresses this by realigning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Since RL rewards are assigned to entire responses rather than individual tokens, our importance sampling should logically operate at the sequence level as well. GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the total likelihood of response $y_i$, normalized by its length:

$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right). $$

Because this metric aggregates probabilities over the entire response sequence rather than comparing token-by-token distributions independently, it becomes inherently robust to fluctuations in individual expert activations. Even if one or two experts switch during generation, the overall sequence likelihood remains a stable indicator of how much better or worse the sequence is compared to a sample from older weights. Consequently, GSPO allows us to compute these ratios using standard inference logic without forcing routes to match those of previous steps. We demonstrated empirically that GSPO converges normally on massive MoE architectures without needing any form of Routing Replay or other complex stabilization strattegies that impose memory and communication costs. This not only simplifies our RL infrastructure but also enables models to fully utilize their dynamic routing capabilities during fine-tuning.
