# The Counter-Intuitive Paradox: Why Clipping More Tokens Leads to Better Results

In the realm of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for large language models, we have long operated under a specific assumption about optimization stability: the clipping mechanism should be applied granularly to prevent drastic updates. Algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have traditionally clipped the importance ratio at every single token position. The logic is sound in theory—if a specific token generation deviates too much from the previous policy, we dampen its influence to avoid divergence. However, our latest research into Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) reveals a startling counter-intuitive truth: clipping more tokens actually leads to superior training efficiency and higher performance. By shifting our focus from individual tokens to entire sequences, GSPO demonstrates that being "conservative" with what we update can unlock significant gains in model capability.

### The Problem with Token-Level Noise

Current state-of-the-art algorithms like GRPO rely on a token-level view of importance sampling. In this framework, the algorithm computes an importance ratio for every single token in a response and clips this ratio individually. While this prevents immediate instability from one bad prediction, it introduces a hidden source of noise. Because the advantage signal is distributed across many tokens based on these individually calculated ratios, the gradients suffered by these high-variance noise terms accumulate over long response lengths. This accumulation eventually precipitates what we call "model collapse," where training dynamics become so unstable that recovery is impossible, even with hyperparameter tuning or checkpoint resumption.

Essentially, by trying to manage every tiny fluctuation at the token level, GRPO allows noisy gradient estimates to dominate the learning signal. This approach treats all positions in the sequence equally regarding their potential for divergence, failing to align with the fundamental principle that reinforcement signals are bestowed upon the entire sequence as a unified whole. The unit of optimization (the token) does not match the unit of reward (the sequence), creating a misalignment that degrades performance over time.

### The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Clipping

GSPO solves this by redefining where clipping happens. Instead of clipping individual tokens within a response, GSPO calculates a single sequence-level importance ratio and clips the entire response based on that value. The importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as the geometric mean of log-probabilities across all tokens in a sequence, raised to a normalization factor:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
By applying clipping at this level, we ensure that only sequences that meaningfully deviate from the previous policy contribute to the gradient update. If an entire response is explored via unlikely paths relative to its overall probability mass under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, it gets clipped out entirely rather than allowing individual noisy components from that path to pollute the average gradient of successful sequences.

This shift creates an observable and curious phenomenon regarding data usage efficiency. In our experiments comparing GSPO against GRPO on identical hardware and compute budgets, we noticed something unexpected: GSPO physically clips a significantly higher fraction of tokens compared to GRPO. Under standard metrics where "clipping more" implies "throwing away more useful data," one would expect GSPO to be less efficient due to fewer samples contributing to gradient estimation per batch.

### Why Clipping More Tokens Improves Performance

Empirical results tell us that despite discarding more tokens—clipping entire responses rather than saving most individual ones—GSPO achieves faster convergence and better final performance on benchmarks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench. This suggests that CRITICAL insight: GRPO's approach of preserving many noisy gradients was actually inefficient because those gradients correlated poorly with true value improvements due to high variance.

GSPO effectively acts as a high-pass filter for signal quality over sample quantity.
