\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ use the geometric mean (length normalization) instead of the raw likelihood ratio?
Question:
In Equation (10), the paper defines the importance ratio as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The text explains that without this length normalization, large fluctuations could occur from a few tokens, and ratios for different lengths would require varying clipping ranges. I want to understand the mathematical intuition behind why simply taking the $1/|y_i|$ power solves the variance accumulation problem better than just applying a different clipping bound for long sequences. If I have a sequence of length 100 where one token has a huge probability drop ($w_t \to 0$), how exactly does raising the total product to $1/100$ prevent this single token from destabilizing the entire optimization compared to just bounding $w_t$ directly?
Answer:
In the context of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the use of the geometric mean—mathematically realized as raising the likelihood ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$—is not merely a scaling heuristic but a fundamental necessity for stabilizing reinforcement learning on long sequences, addressing issues that simple token-level clipping cannot resolve.

To understand why this approach is superior to applying different clipping bounds based on sequence length, consider the behavior of products versus sums in probabilistic settings. Likelihoods are computed by multiplying individual token probabilities: $\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. When comparing a new policy $\pi_\theta$ against an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the raw importance ratio is this product of per-token ratios $w_t$. The problem arises because the variance of a product scales multiplicatively. If a single token $t$ has an extremely low probability under the old policy relative to the new one (or vice versa), that single ratio $w_t$ can be enormous or tiny. In a long sequence, even if only one token causes a massive fluctuation, multiplying it by hundreds or thousands of other tokens amplifies this instability to astronomical levels. Without normalization, the scale of the importance ratio becomes dominated by this single worst-case deviation, rendering the gradient signal useless and prone to exploding gradients.

Raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$ transforms this product into an exponential of an average: $\left( \prod w_t \right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum \ln w_t \right)$. Mathematically, this converts multiplicative accumulation into additive averaging. By taking the logarithm implicitly via this exponentiation, we normalize the contribution of each token. Instead of one hypothetical "bad" token dictating the fate of a 100-token sequence through multiplication (where its impact grows with length), its impact is now averaged over all 100 positions. If one log-ratio is huge and negative while others are moderate, dividing by 100 significantly dampens its influence on the final gradient term compared to letting it multiply through 100 times. This ensures that length normalization provides stability regardless of whether sequence lengths vary; it enforces a unified statistical property where every additional token contributes roughly equally to the importance score rather than compounding errors exponentially.

Critically, attempting to solve this variability by simply setting different clipping ranges ($\varepsilon$) for different sequence lengths fails due to fundamental optimization stability constraints. While you could theoretically set a very wide clip bound for long sequences and a narrow one for short ones to encompass these fluctuations, this breaks Robust Gradient Ascent logic in PPO-based algorithms like GSPO and GRPO. PPO relies on maintaining weights within $(1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ relative to their current value whenever advantages are positive or negative in opposite directions. If you allow long sequences to have vastly larger importance ratios simply because they are long, you regain all the pathological behaviors GSPO seeks to eliminate: high-variance noise that corrupts batch mean estimates and forces spurious updates that destabilize training dynamics across different response lengths mixed in mini-batches. More importantly from a training efficiency standpoint, allowing such wide variance prevents effective averaging across samples; if some ratios are $0$ and others are $10^{12}$ due to length scaling artifacts rather than genuine preference changes, the optimizer cannot learn a coherent direction from mini-batches containing both short safe responses and long fragmented responses with extreme spikes.

Intuitively, GSPO's geometric mean aligns with how humans assess "overall quality" of long stories versus detection errors—errors accumulate additively in severity rather than multiplying unbounded per-word probabilities—and it directly targets unit variance regardless of token count. This ensures that when GSPO clips entire responses at uniform thresholds (such as those observed around $3\times 10^{-4}$), it is operating on a statistically comparable metric whether responding with 5 tokens or 50 tokens, thereby ensuring consistent optimization dynamics independent of rollout length variations which accumulation into large-scale Mixture-of-Experts models would otherwise compromise stability more severely than in dense architectures do? Certainly not dense ones but also particularly critical there too! 

Ultimately, taking roots functions like these powers down variance by design and ensures uniformity in batch variance so that model policies do not collapse under training stress beyond control limits imposed by clipping errata.

### How exactly do gradient discrepancies arise from 'expert-activation volatility' in MoE models during GRPO training?
Question:
Section 3.3 discusses 'expert-activation volatility' in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. It states that after a gradient update, the routers might activate different experts for the same input compared to the old policy. The text implies this makes token-level importance ratios invalid. However, I am confused about the specific mechanism: if Policy A activates Expert Mix {E1, E2} and Policy B activates Expert Mix {E1, E3}, why does this make comparing $P(A|t)$ and $P(B|t)$ impossible or high-variance? Specifically, in GRPO, we compute $w_{i,t} = P_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t}) / P_{old}(y_t|x,y_{<t})$. If the active experts define which parameters are used to calculate that probability mass at step $t$, does switching experts mean we are effectively comparing apples to oranges (different parameter subnetworks)? How does GSPO avoid this by looking at whole-sequence probability instead of per-token probabilities from potentially different expert subsets?
Answer:
In the context of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the issue of "expert-activation volatility" arises from the fundamental mismatch between how reinforcement learning updates guide a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model versus how importance sampling weights rely on consistent parameter usages. To understand why this makes token-level comparisons invalid while sequence-level comparisons remain robust, we must look at how the routing mechanism affects probability distributions and how GSPO aligns its objectives with the unique architecture of MoE models.

At the core of GSPO's design is the recognition that in an autoregressive language model, the probability of generating a full response $y$ is the product of conditional probabilities of each token given its history, formally expressed as $\pi_{\theta}(y|x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_{\theta}(y_t | x, y_{<t})$. In standard algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the importance ratio is computed token-by-token as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. This approach assumes that when we compare the new policy to the old policy for a specific token, we are comparing two related distributions over a consistent computational pathway. However, in MoE models, each expert represents a distinct subnetwork with different weights and parameter niches. The router dynamically selects a subset of these experts based on the input context. Consequently, even if a model generates the exact same next token $y_t$, if Expert A is active under $\pi_\theta$ and Expert B was active under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ for that same position, the numerical values of $\pi_\theta(y_t|\dots)$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t|\dots)$ are derived from completely different sets of weights.

This phenomenon creates a situation where comparing token probabilities becomes akin to comparing "apples to oranges." When experts switch due to activation volatility—such as seeing roughly 10% of experts change after a single gradient update—the conditional probabilities are no longer variations along a smooth trajectory but rather samples from disjoint functions. This introduces massive variance into the importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ because they reflect not only differences in learned preferences but also artifacts of which subset of experts happened to be triggered. Since GRPO clips these individual weights directly ($1-\epsilon$ to $1+\epsilon$), this high-variance noise gets amplified or blocked unpredictably by the clipping mechanism as it propagates through long sequences, eventually leading to training instability and model collapse.

GSPO circumvents this issue by fundamentally shifting the unit of evaluation from individual tokens to entire sequences. Instead of relying on per-token likelihoods that depend heavily on specific expert activations at $t$, GSPO defines its importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the total sequence likelihood: $s_i(\theta) = (\pi_{\theta}(y_i | x) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x))^{1/|y_i|}$. The key insight here is that while individual token predictions care deeply about *which* expert fired at that precise step, the overall sequence likelihood tends to be much more stable across policy updates. Even if experts swap roles at various positions during training, as long as both versions of the model retain similar aggregate capabilities—which is guaranteed as long as all experts are functional parts of a valid language model—the overall probability mass assigned to generating entire correct responses remains relatively consistent. By aggregating evidence over all tokens in $y_i$, GSPO effectively averages out the noise introduced by switching specific subsets of experts at individual time steps. Once this stable sequence likelihood ratio is computed and potentially clipped for rejection sampling against overly divergent samples, GSPO uses it merely as a scaling factor for gradients rather than letting it dictate which tokens receive updates unevenly. Specifically in GSPO-token variants seen in later sections of their work, they even detach and reuse this stable sequence-level score to weight per-token advantages without re-evaluating volatility-prone router decisions at every step. Thus, GSPO avoids expert-activation volatility not by forcing routers to stay fixed during training via replay strategies—which adds memory overhead—but by ignoring fragile per-token comparisons in favor of sequence-level robustness that naturally tolerates dynamic routing changes without invalidating importance ratios.

### In GSPO-token, what is the specific purpose of `sg[.]` in defining $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ if numerically it equals 1?
Question:
Equation (15) defines GSPO-token ($s_{i,t}(\theta)$) as: `sg[ s_i(theta) ] * ( pi_theta(...) / sg[ pi_theta(...) ] )`. The paper notes this term evaluates to 1 numerically so GSPO-token reduces to GSPO when advantages are uniform. In standard PPO implementations, `detach` or `sg` is often used within scaling factors to prevent exploding gradients during warmup or specific stability regimes. However, here it seems strictly numerical identity is claimed. Is there a theoretical scenario where token-level advantages ($\widehat{A}_{i,t} \neq \widehat{A}_i$) utilize this token-level importance ratio differently than just re-weighting by the sequence ratio? Or is this variant purely an implementation detail allowing flexibility in adventure assignment without changing the gradient flow direction?
Answer:
The purpose of the `sg` (stop gradient) operator in the definition of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ for GSPO-token is to mathematically decouple the sequence-level normalization scaling from the token-specific probability ratios during backpropagation, thereby enabling token-wise advantage assignment without altering the fundamental gradient direction established by GSPO.

When you define $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[ s_{i}(\theta) ] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }$, you are structurally separating the "importance scale" from the "token likelihood ratio" in the computational graph. The term $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }$ evaluates to 1 numerically because both the numerator and denominator reference the exact same forward pass probability value, but the application of `sg` to the denominator effectively removes any gradient dependency on that specific probability value for that term's derivative contribution. Consequently, when you apply the chain rule to compute gradients as shown in Equation 18 of the paper, $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)$ becomes zero for the second factor because its derivative is blocked by `sg`. This forces the gradient to flow solely through $\nabla_\theta s_i(\theta)$, which retains its role as a stabilizing sequence-level importance weight derived from methods analogous to Importance Weighted Regression or standard importance sampling corrections.

The theoretical utility of this construction arises specifically because GSPO-token introduces token-level advantages ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}$), which differs from standard GSP where all tokens in a response share a single group advantage ($\widehat{A}_i$). In a standard importance sampling formulation without this `sg` trick, re-weighting individual log-probabilities by an updated sequence importance ratio would create a mathematical inconsistency: you would be treating the sequence importance ratio as if it were a dynamic function of every individual token being updated simultaneously within that same batch update step. By fixing $s_i(\theta)$ using `sg`, you ensure that the optimization objective remains consistent with GSPO's core premise—that off-policy correction should be driven by the aggregate sequence likelihood—while simply allowing you to change *how* that correction is applied across different tokens via $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. Without `sg`, if $s_i(\theta)$ were computed dynamically including gradients from every $y_{i,t}$, including it in every step with naive multiplication inside a stochastic sum could amplify variance and disrupt the stable gradient flow that GSPO claims offers over GRPO; effectively, you are treating $s_i(\theta)$ as a constant coefficient derived from previous states or independent samples rather than a live variable dependent on every specific token update in parallel.

### Does using sequence-level objectives invalidate backpropagating credit attribution for individual tokens during training?
Question:
The core argument of GSPO is moving from token-level weighting ($w_t$) to sequence-level weighting ($s$). In standard language modeling with soft maximum pooling or cross-entropy loss at every step, backpropagation attributes error gradients to every token proportionally. If GSPO computes a single scalar gradient update based on the derivative of $s_i(\theta) = \exp(\frac{1}{L}\sum \log p_t)$, how does SGD distribute this score among all tokens? Equation (28) shows $\nabla_\theta J = E[...] [ s_i ... * \frac{1}{L}\sum \nabla_\theta \log p_t ]$. This looks like every token still contributes equally to the gradient ($1/L$). But if GRPO clipped certain toxic tokens by reducing their weights significantly ($w_t < 0.8$), while GSPO essentially applies a global scalar clip/scale $s$, isn't riskier information suppression lost? Does switching to sequence-level weighting mean we assume all tokens in a generated response contribute equally to 'badness', losing fine-grained control over specific erroneous parts of a long chain-of-thought?
Answer:
Based on the paper "Group Sequence Policy Optimization," using a sequence-level objective does not invalidate backpropagation for individual tokens; rather, it fundamentally changes the *weighting scheme* applied to those tokens during the gradient update, effectively replacing variable token-level importance ratios with a uniform weight per sequence.

In standard token-level approaches like GRPO or PPO, the gradient update for a specific token $y_{i,t}$ within a response $y_i$ is scaled by its specific importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, as seen in Equation (28) of the GRPO derivation. This means that tokens contributing to a high likelihood ratio receive a significantly larger credit (or blame) than those with lower ratios. This mechanism relies on the core principle of importance sampling: since samples are drawn from an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, we re-weight them by $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}$ to estimate the expectation under the new policy $\pi_\theta$. However, as the authors argue in Section "Motivation," applying this correction at the individual token level for long sequences introduces high-variance noise. Because importance sampling theory requires averaging over many samples to correctly estimate expectations, applying it token-wise on single trajectories ($N=1$ per token position) creates unstable gradients that accumulate over long lengths, leading to model collapse.

GSPO resolves this by shifting the unit of importance sampling from individual tokens to the entire sequence. The algorithm defines a single sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ in Equation (4), which is essentially the geometric mean of all token-level importance ratios within that response. Mathematically, this corresponds to taking the logarithm of the product of likelihoods divided by the length of the sequence:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right) $$
When you compute the gradient of this objective, as shown in Equation (28) of your question and derived in Section "Gradient Analysis," you end up multiplying every token's gradient contribution $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$ by the same scalar value $s_i(\theta)$ distributed evenly across all tokens due to the length normalization factor $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$.

This shift changes how credit is attributed without stopping it from propagating. Instead of some tokens having gradients magnified by an importance ratio near $1+\varepsilon$ and others attenuated, every token in a given sequence contributes equally to the update direction within that sequence's context window. The riskier information suppression you feared—where clipping might arbitrarily discard gradients for specific bad tokens—is avoided because GSPO verifies "badness" globally based on whether the entire sequence falls outside a clip range defined by $s_i(\theta)$ versus its advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. If a sequence is deemed "off-policy" based on its overall likelihood drop, it is clipped entirely; if not, all tokens within it contribute equally to improving that whole path.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this trade-off and offers a solution called **GSPO-token**. If fine-grained control over specific erroneous parts (like toxic words generated mid-chain-of-thought) is required while maintaining GSPO's stability benefits, Section "GSPO-token: A Token-level Objective Variant" introduces an algorithm where you can use different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ for individual tokens. In this variant, introduced in Equation (9), you multiply each token's log-probability gradient by $s_i(\theta)$—the same stable sequence-level ratio—but allow $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to vary per token based on immediate rewards or other signals. This ensures you regain fine-grained credit attribution where needed (by modifying $\widehat{A}$ rather than relying on noisy $w_t$), while still benefiting from GSPO's global stability mechanism provided by $s_i(\theta)$. Thus, switching to sequence-level weighting prevents volatility by unifying importance ratios across a response, but it does not prevent backpropagation; it simply ensures that no single token disproportionately dictates the update due to statistical noise inherent in short-sequence sampling.

### How does sampling responses from the 'old policy' remain stationary enough when traitors effectively render $(x,y)$ pairs stale during large-scale RL?
Question:
"Inference" in RL requires responding samples drawn from an old behavior policy while updating towards a current optimized policy. The paper mentions partitioning batches for memory efficiency but notes "off-policy" risks are managed by importance ratios. However, with sequences of length |y|=2000+, consider that intervening updates change $P(y|x)$ drastically even slightly? If I sample a response under Model V5.0 but optimize towards V5.5+, why don't we hit severe off-policy catastrophes immediately? Does GSPO's claim that 'clipping entire responses' allow larger off-policy deviations fundamentally change how we think about effective batch size versus rlLOM time-step? Specifically, if I generate one example and optimize over it once per epoch due to small batch size constraints, doesn't that require massive KL penalties rather than just clipping?
Answer:
The stability of sampling from an "old" policy in large-scale Reinforcement Learning (RL) while optimizing towards a "current" policy is the central challenge of off-policy learning. Your concern regarding the staleness of $(x, y)$ pairs, especially with response lengths exceeding 2000 tokens, is valid: if the probability distribution $P(y|x)$ shifts drastically between the time a response is sampled and when it is optimized, off-policy algorithms risk introducing high-variance noise or even catastrophic model collapse.

The fundamental reason GSPO avoids these severe off-policy catastrophes, even with long sequences and frequent updates, lies in its strict adherence to the **unit of optimization matching the unit of reward**, a concept explicitly highlighted as the core failure mode in previous token-level approaches like GRPO. In standard off-policy estimation, you require a large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{old}}$ to accurately estimate the expectation under the target distribution $\pi_{\text{curr}}$ using importance weights. The paper argues that applying importance sampling weights at the *token level* ($w_{i,t}$) violates this principle because each token likelihood $\pi(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ is estimated from a single sample trajectory. When responses are long (e.g., $|y| > 2000$), small shifts in model parameters can cause individual token probabilities to diverge significantly. In GRPO, these divergent per-token weights accumulate across hundreds of steps, creating a noisy gradient signal that effectively treats every token's probability update as if it were an independent decision made by a different policy. This accumulation of high-variance noise over long sequences destabilizes training, leading to model collapse.

GSPO resolves this by shifting the importance ratio calculation from individual tokens to the entire **sequence likelihood**. Instead of weighting each token based on its local divergence, GSPO computes a single sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ defined as:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
where $\pi(y_i | x)$ is the probability of generating the entire response sequence $y_i$. By averaging the log-likelihood ratios over all tokens inside this ratio (as seen in Equation 3 of the paper), GSPO smooths out local fluctuations at specific time steps $t$. A drastic change in one token's probability does not dominate the gradient unless it significantly alters the total sequence probability. Consequently, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ represents a holistic measure of how well-matched the *entire* sampled response is to the current policy's preferences for that complete output.

This sequence-level perspective fundamentally changes how we view "staleness." In token-level methods, staleness is fatal because any divergence amplifies across many summands in the objective function. In GSPO, however, if an old response becomes "stale" relative to new parameters—meaning $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ differs greatly from $\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)$)—the algorithm handles it gracefully through **sequence-level clipping**. The paper demonstrates that GSPO clips these entire responses based on their aggregate ratio $s_i(\theta)$. If a response has drifted too far from the current policy's distribution (whether due to parameter updates or natural variance), its $s_i(\theta)$ will exceed clipping bounds $(1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ and be dropped from gradient estimation entirely. This prevents stale data from corrupting updates without requiring massive KL penalties or reliance on historical caches like "Routing Replay."

Regarding your specific point about small batch sizes and optimizing one example once per epoch: this scenario actually reinforces GSPO's robustness rather than causing instability. Because GSPO weights all tokens equally within a sequence during optimization (as derived in Equation 9), there are no unpredictable per-token scaling factors accumulating error. The gradient update for a single long sequence remains consistent regardless of length variations. The "effective batch size" versus "time-step" concern you raised applies primarily to scenarios where noisy estimates propagate; since GSPO eliminates per-token weight noise by design via averaging and clipping at the sequence level, even infrequent optimization of long sequences yields stable gradients that reflect true sequence rewards rather than erratic token-level corrections.

Furthermore, this approach naturally addresses precision discrepancies between training and inference engines mentioned in Section 6 because sequence likelihoods are more tolerant of minor computational variations than iterative token-by-token probabilities—an issue critical when dealing with hundreds or thousands of tokens where small floating-point errors compound wildly in autoregressive generation. By focusing on $P(y|x)$ rather than $P(y_t|x,y_{<t})$, GSPO ensures that sampling remains stationary enough for effective policy updates: it filters out responses where the global behavior has drifted too far, while retaining those where local probabilistic shifts have balanced out over long contexts to produce valid improvement signals.
