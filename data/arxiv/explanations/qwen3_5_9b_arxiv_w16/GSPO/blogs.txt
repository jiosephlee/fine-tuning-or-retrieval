# Beyond Tokens: Why Training LLMs is Too Hard at the Token Level

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the cornerstone for pushing large language models (LLMs) to unprecedented capabilities in math, coding, and reasoning. However, as we scale these models to massive sizes, a critical challenge has emerged: current state-of-the-art algorithms are becoming unstable. We previously observed that algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) often lead to catastrophic model collapse when training gigantic models or when generating long responses. This instability halts progress and makes recovering from training failures nearly impossible. In a recent study, we identified that the root cause lies not in the hardware or the data, but in a fundamental flaw in how we measure improvement at the token level.

To understand why this happens, we must look at how RL algorithms update models. Standard methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) used importance sampling to adjust for the difference between samples generated by an old policy and one we are currently training. These methods typically calculate an "importance ratio" for every single token in a response. This approach assumes that correcting the probability distribution at each individual step is sufficient. However, as response lengths increase and models become more complex, specifically with Mixture-of-Experts architectures where different parts of the network activate dynamically for each token, these per-token corrections introduce high-variance noise. This noise accumulates with every new token generated, eventually overwhelming the learning signal and causing the model to collapse.

Our solution introduces Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), an algorithm designed from the ground up to solve this stability issue by shifting our perspective from individual tokens to entire sequences. The core insight behind GSPO is that importance sampling should be applied at the same unit level as the reward itself. Since a model is rewarded or penalized based on its ability to generate a complete coherent response rather than every single word independently, our optimization should reflect that granularity. We define our importance ratio based on the sequence-level likelihood of the entire response rather than the product of individual token probabilities. By doing so, we align our optimization objective directly with how success is measured in generation tasks.

Mathematically, this shift changes how gradients are computed and applied during training. In GRPO, tokens within a sequence are weighted unequally based on their specific importance ratios derived from next-token predictions. In contrast, GSPO treats all tokens within a generated sequence $y_i$ equally when updating gradients for that sequence's advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. We achieve this by calculating an importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ using the geometric mean of log-likelihood ratios across all tokens:

$$
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right).
$$

This formulation ensures that clipping mechanisms—which prevent overly risky updates—act on sequences as whole units rather than constraining individual token transitions arbitrarily. We found that simply moving from token-level clipping to sequence-level clipping resolves variance issues caused by long contexts and dynamic network routing in MoE models without requiring complex workarounds like "routing replay." Furthermore, because GSPO relies on sequence-level likelihoods rather than fragile per-token estimates immediately after generation, it becomes more robust to precision discrepancies between training engines and inference servers like vLLM or SGLang, potentially simplifying infrastructure design for decentralized RL setups.

The empirical results demonstrate that GSPO significantly outperforms GRPO in terms of training stability and final capabilities. Unlike GRPO, which requires hand-tuned hyperparameters and complex routing strategies to avoid crashing during long-context training or with MoE models, GSPO maintains stable trajectories even under aggressive scaling conditions such as increasing generation length or refreshing query sets mid-training. Notably, while GSPO clips a much higher fraction of tokens due to its sequence-level thresholding approach—which effectively discards unreliable samples—the remaining valid samples provide a far stronger learning signal because they are not corrupted by token-level noise paradoxes found in GRPOs gradient estimates. This efficiency allows GSPO to achieve superior benchmark performance on tasks ranging from competitive mathematics measured by AIME '24 pass@1 metrics to coding challenges evaluated via Elo ratings on CodeForces using comparable computational resources.

By refocusing reinforcement learning away from noisy micro-adjustments toward holistic sequence evaluation, GSPO removes critical bottlenecks that have limited previous scaling efforts with LLMs beyond Token 500M thresholds held constant per update batch size constraints imposed elsewhere necessitate therefore enabling continued advancement through larger batch sizes utilized during rollouts across distributed systems architectures coupled with improved sample efficiency metrics leading directly into subsequent improvements made possible through consistent activation patterns observed throughout experimentation phases spanning months duration amounts calculated across multiple runs totaling unique configurations tested against established baselines demonstrating clear progressive gains without requiring external stabilization techniques previously employed exclusively within prior generative adversarial network frameworks dependent upon carefully balanced generator discriminator interactions maintaining equilibrium throughout iterative refinement processes thereby achieving novel levels of domain adaptation capabilities previously thought unattainable given current hardware limitations



# No More Routing Replay: How GSPO Simplifies MoE Training

Training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models using Reinforcement Learning (RL) has long been plagued by instability. The sparse activation nature of these architectures means that the set of experts processed for a single response can fluctuate significantly between gradient updates. This "expert-activation volatility" often leads to catastrophic model collapse or prevents convergence unless heavily mitigated. In our recent work on Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), we identify the root cause of this instability and demonstrate how shifting the optimization unit from tokens to sequences fundamentally resolves the issue, rendering complex workarounds like Routing Replay obsolete.

To understand why standard methods struggle, consider the objective function of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO optimizes an importance ratio calculated at every individual token position. In a dense model, this works reasonably well, but in an MoE model, the dynamics change drastically. Because RL training frequently updates routing parameters, the activated experts for a specific prefix can change between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$. When calculating the token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, we are essentially comparing probabilities computed by different sub-networks for the same input token. This mismatch causes these weights to fluctuate wildly and become statistically invalid, introducing high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and derails training.

To combat this, previous implementations relied on a strategy called **Routing Replay**. This approach caches which experts were activated in $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and forces $\pi_{\theta}$ to use exactly those same experts during the current update step when computing ratios. While effective at stabilizing gradients momentarily, this technique introduces significant overhead. It requires additional memory to store routing states and complex communication logic to enforce matching activations across distributed training clusters. More critically, it acts as an artificial constraint; by reusing past routing modes, you are effectively limiting the moe model's capacity to explore better routing configurations during optimization. We cannot simply bind our large models into a specific expert structure forever.

GSPO addresses this by realigning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Since RL rewards are assigned to entire responses rather than individual tokens, our importance sampling should logically operate at the sequence level as well. GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the total likelihood of response $y_i$, normalized by its length:

$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right). $$

Because this metric aggregates probabilities over the entire response sequence rather than comparing token-by-token distributions independently, it becomes inherently robust to fluctuations in individual expert activations. Even if one or two experts switch during generation, the overall sequence likelihood remains a stable indicator of how much better or worse the sequence is compared to a sample from older weights. Consequently, GSPO allows us to compute these ratios using standard inference logic without forcing routes to match those of previous steps. We demonstrated empirically that GSPO converges normally on massive MoE architectures without needing any form of Routing Replay or other complex stabilization strattegies that impose memory and communication costs. This not only simplifies our RL infrastructure but also enables models to fully utilize their dynamic routing capabilities during fine-tuning.



# The Counter-Intuitive Paradox: Why Clipping More Tokens Leads to Better Results

In the realm of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for large language models, we have long operated under a specific assumption about optimization stability: the clipping mechanism should be applied granularly to prevent drastic updates. Algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have traditionally clipped the importance ratio at every single token position. The logic is sound in theory—if a specific token generation deviates too much from the previous policy, we dampen its influence to avoid divergence. However, our latest research into Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) reveals a startling counter-intuitive truth: clipping more tokens actually leads to superior training efficiency and higher performance. By shifting our focus from individual tokens to entire sequences, GSPO demonstrates that being "conservative" with what we update can unlock significant gains in model capability.

### The Problem with Token-Level Noise

Current state-of-the-art algorithms like GRPO rely on a token-level view of importance sampling. In this framework, the algorithm computes an importance ratio for every single token in a response and clips this ratio individually. While this prevents immediate instability from one bad prediction, it introduces a hidden source of noise. Because the advantage signal is distributed across many tokens based on these individually calculated ratios, the gradients suffered by these high-variance noise terms accumulate over long response lengths. This accumulation eventually precipitates what we call "model collapse," where training dynamics become so unstable that recovery is impossible, even with hyperparameter tuning or checkpoint resumption.

Essentially, by trying to manage every tiny fluctuation at the token level, GRPO allows noisy gradient estimates to dominate the learning signal. This approach treats all positions in the sequence equally regarding their potential for divergence, failing to align with the fundamental principle that reinforcement signals are bestowed upon the entire sequence as a unified whole. The unit of optimization (the token) does not match the unit of reward (the sequence), creating a misalignment that degrades performance over time.

### The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Clipping

GSPO solves this by redefining where clipping happens. Instead of clipping individual tokens within a response, GSPO calculates a single sequence-level importance ratio and clips the entire response based on that value. The importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as the geometric mean of log-probabilities across all tokens in a sequence, raised to a normalization factor:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
By applying clipping at this level, we ensure that only sequences that meaningfully deviate from the previous policy contribute to the gradient update. If an entire response is explored via unlikely paths relative to its overall probability mass under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, it gets clipped out entirely rather than allowing individual noisy components from that path to pollute the average gradient of successful sequences.

This shift creates an observable and curious phenomenon regarding data usage efficiency. In our experiments comparing GSPO against GRPO on identical hardware and compute budgets, we noticed something unexpected: GSPO physically clips a significantly higher fraction of tokens compared to GRPO. Under standard metrics where "clipping more" implies "throwing away more useful data," one would expect GSPO to be less efficient due to fewer samples contributing to gradient estimation per batch.

### Why Clipping More Tokens Improves Performance

Empirical results tell us that despite discarding more tokens—clipping entire responses rather than saving most individual ones—GSPO achieves faster convergence and better final performance on benchmarks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench. This suggests that CRITICAL insight: GRPO's approach of preserving many noisy gradients was actually inefficient because those gradients correlated poorly with true value improvements due to high variance.

GSPO effectively acts as a high-pass filter for signal quality over sample quantity. # Breaking the Hardware Barrier: Using Inference Engines for Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) training for large language models has historically relied on a rigid infrastructure architecture. For algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the standard workflow requires re-computing the probability of every single token in a response using the model's dedicated training engine. This is necessary because previous methods calculate importance ratios on a per-token basis, demanding high-precision, differentiable access to the network weights for every step of the generation. However, this requirement creates significant bottlenecks in compute utilization and complicates system design, especially given the precision discrepancies that often exist between training kernels and inference kernels. The introduction of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) fundamentally changes this dynamic by shifting the optimization unit from individual tokens to entire sequences. This shift not only resolves severe stability issues but also unlocks a new paradigm for RL infrastructure, allowing training systems to directly leverage high-throughput inference engines without expensive recomputation loops.

The core inefficiency in traditional RL workflows stems from the need to calculate token-level importance weights. In standard setups, a sampling engine generates responses which are then evaluated by a compute-intensive training engine just to determine their likelihoods under an old policy. This separation is mandatory when algorithms treat every token independently, as they cannot tolerate the precision mismatches that naturally arise between different compute backends. The result is a disjointed pipeline where data generated for inference must be re-processed almost entirely by hardware optimized for training parameters passing, leading to redundant computation and resource contention. GSPO challenges this assumption by applying importance sampling at the sequence level rather than the token level. By defining the importance ratio based on the total likelihood of a response $\pi_\theta(y_i | x)$ relative to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)$, GSPO aggregates the probability information across all tokens in a single value.

This architectural change is significant for three main reasons regarding entropy and noise reduction. First, while GSPO computes quotients of exponential terms involving sums of log-probabilities across all tokens $|y_i|$, these operations act as powerful averaging mechanisms that smooth out minor numerical fluctuations inherent in floating-point arithmetic between engines. Second, because GSPO optimizes based on $s_i(\theta)$ calculated over whole responses, it filters out granular noise that plagues token-level approaches when precision varies slightly across layers or expert assignments. Consequently, GSPO proves robust enough to rely directly on likelihood outputs from serving engines like SGLang or vLLM without requiring the high-precision mem2mem re-runs traditionally necessary for stability checks or gradient calculations matched against training weights exactly.
