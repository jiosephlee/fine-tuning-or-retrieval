\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ use the geometric mean (length normalization) instead of the raw likelihood ratio?
Question:
In Equation (10), the paper defines the importance ratio as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The text explains that without this length normalization, large fluctuations could occur from a few tokens, and ratios for different lengths would require varying clipping ranges. I want to understand the mathematical intuition behind why simply taking the $1/|y_i|$ power solves the variance accumulation problem better than just applying a different clipping bound for long sequences. If I have a sequence of length 100 where one token has a huge probability drop ($w_t \to 0$), how exactly does raising the total product to $1/100$ prevent this single token from destabilizing the entire optimization compared to just bounding $w_t$ directly?
Answer:
In the context of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the use of the geometric mean—mathematically realized as raising the likelihood ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$—is not merely a scaling heuristic but a fundamental necessity for stabilizing reinforcement learning on long sequences, addressing issues that simple token-level clipping cannot resolve.

To understand why this approach is superior to applying different clipping bounds based on sequence length, consider the behavior of products versus sums in probabilistic settings. Likelihoods are computed by multiplying individual token probabilities: $\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$. When comparing a new policy $\pi_\theta$ against an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the raw importance ratio is this product of per-token ratios $w_t$. The problem arises because the variance of a product scales multiplicatively. If a single token $t$ has an extremely low probability under the old policy relative to the new one (or vice versa), that single ratio $w_t$ can be enormous or tiny. In a long sequence, even if only one token causes a massive fluctuation, multiplying it by hundreds or thousands of other tokens amplifies this instability to astronomical levels. Without normalization, the scale of the importance ratio becomes dominated by this single worst-case deviation, rendering the gradient signal useless and prone to exploding gradients.

Raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$ transforms this product into an exponential of an average: $\left( \prod w_t \right)^{1/|y_i|} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum \ln w_t \right)$. Mathematically, this converts multiplicative accumulation into additive averaging. By taking the logarithm implicitly via this exponentiation, we normalize the contribution of each token. Instead of one hypothetical "bad" token dictating the fate of a 100-token sequence through multiplication (where its impact grows with length), its impact is now averaged over all 100 positions. If one log-ratio is huge and negative while others are moderate, dividing by 100 significantly dampens its influence on the final gradient term compared to letting it multiply through 100 times. This ensures that length normalization provides stability regardless of whether sequence lengths vary; it enforces a unified statistical property where every additional token contributes roughly equally to the importance score rather than compounding errors exponentially.

Critically, attempting to solve this variability by simply setting different clipping ranges ($\varepsilon$) for different sequence lengths fails due to fundamental optimization stability constraints. While you could theoretically set a very wide clip bound for long sequences and a narrow one for short ones to encompass these fluctuations, this breaks Robust Gradient Ascent logic in PPO-based algorithms like GSPO and GRPO. PPO relies on maintaining weights within $(1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ relative to their current value whenever advantages are positive or negative in opposite directions. If you allow long sequences to have vastly larger importance ratios simply because they are long, you regain all the pathological behaviors GSPO seeks to eliminate: high-variance noise that corrupts batch mean estimates and forces spurious updates that destabilize training dynamics across different response lengths mixed in mini-batches. More importantly from a training efficiency standpoint, allowing such wide variance prevents effective averaging across samples; if some ratios are $0$ and others are $10^{12}$ due to length scaling artifacts rather than genuine preference changes, the optimizer cannot learn a coherent direction from mini-batches containing both short safe responses and long fragmented responses with extreme spikes.

Intuitively, GSPO's geometric mean aligns with how humans assess "overall quality" of long stories versus detection errors—errors accumulate additively in severity rather than multiplying unbounded per-word probabilities—and it directly targets unit variance regardless of token count. This ensures that when GSPO clips entire responses at uniform thresholds (such as those observed around $3\times 10^{-4}$), it is operating on a statistically comparable metric whether responding with 5 tokens or 50 tokens, thereby ensuring consistent optimization dynamics independent of rollout length variations which accumulation into large-scale Mixture-of-Experts models would otherwise compromise stability more severely than in dense architectures do? Certainly not dense ones but also particularly critical there too! 

Ultimately, taking roots functions like these powers down variance by design and ensures uniformity in batch variance so that model policies do not collapse under training stress beyond control limits imposed by clipping errata.
