\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does sampling responses from the 'old policy' remain stationary enough when traitors effectively render $(x,y)$ pairs stale during large-scale RL?
Question:
"Inference" in RL requires responding samples drawn from an old behavior policy while updating towards a current optimized policy. The paper mentions partitioning batches for memory efficiency but notes "off-policy" risks are managed by importance ratios. However, with sequences of length |y|=2000+, consider that intervening updates change $P(y|x)$ drastically even slightly? If I sample a response under Model V5.0 but optimize towards V5.5+, why don't we hit severe off-policy catastrophes immediately? Does GSPO's claim that 'clipping entire responses' allow larger off-policy deviations fundamentally change how we think about effective batch size versus rlLOM time-step? Specifically, if I generate one example and optimize over it once per epoch due to small batch size constraints, doesn't that require massive KL penalties rather than just clipping?
Answer:
The stability of sampling from an "old" policy in large-scale Reinforcement Learning (RL) while optimizing towards a "current" policy is the central challenge of off-policy learning. Your concern regarding the staleness of $(x, y)$ pairs, especially with response lengths exceeding 2000 tokens, is valid: if the probability distribution $P(y|x)$ shifts drastically between the time a response is sampled and when it is optimized, off-policy algorithms risk introducing high-variance noise or even catastrophic model collapse.

The fundamental reason GSPO avoids these severe off-policy catastrophes, even with long sequences and frequent updates, lies in its strict adherence to the **unit of optimization matching the unit of reward**, a concept explicitly highlighted as the core failure mode in previous token-level approaches like GRPO. In standard off-policy estimation, you require a large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{old}}$ to accurately estimate the expectation under the target distribution $\pi_{\text{curr}}$ using importance weights. The paper argues that applying importance sampling weights at the *token level* ($w_{i,t}$) violates this principle because each token likelihood $\pi(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ is estimated from a single sample trajectory. When responses are long (e.g., $|y| > 2000$), small shifts in model parameters can cause individual token probabilities to diverge significantly. In GRPO, these divergent per-token weights accumulate across hundreds of steps, creating a noisy gradient signal that effectively treats every token's probability update as if it were an independent decision made by a different policy. This accumulation of high-variance noise over long sequences destabilizes training, leading to model collapse.

GSPO resolves this by shifting the importance ratio calculation from individual tokens to the entire **sequence likelihood**. Instead of weighting each token based on its local divergence, GSPO computes a single sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ defined as:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
where $\pi(y_i | x)$ is the probability of generating the entire response sequence $y_i$. By averaging the log-likelihood ratios over all tokens inside this ratio (as seen in Equation 3 of the paper), GSPO smooths out local fluctuations at specific time steps $t$. A drastic change in one token's probability does not dominate the gradient unless it significantly alters the total sequence probability. Consequently, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ represents a holistic measure of how well-matched the *entire* sampled response is to the current policy's preferences for that complete output.

This sequence-level perspective fundamentally changes how we view "staleness." In token-level methods, staleness is fatal because any divergence amplifies across many summands in the objective function. In GSPO, however, if an old response becomes "stale" relative to new parameters—meaning $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ differs greatly from $\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i|x)$)—the algorithm handles it gracefully through **sequence-level clipping**. The paper demonstrates that GSPO clips these entire responses based on their aggregate ratio $s_i(\theta)$. If a response has drifted too far from the current policy's distribution (whether due to parameter updates or natural variance), its $s_i(\theta)$ will exceed clipping bounds $(1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)$ and be dropped from gradient estimation entirely. This prevents stale data from corrupting updates without requiring massive KL penalties or reliance on historical caches like "Routing Replay."

Regarding your specific point about small batch sizes and optimizing one example once per epoch: this scenario actually reinforces GSPO's robustness rather than causing instability. Because GSPO weights all tokens equally within a sequence during optimization (as derived in Equation 9), there are no unpredictable per-token scaling factors accumulating error. The gradient update for a single long sequence remains consistent regardless of length variations. The "effective batch size" versus "time-step" concern you raised applies primarily to scenarios where noisy estimates propagate; since GSPO eliminates per-token weight noise by design via averaging and clipping at the sequence level, even infrequent optimization of long sequences yields stable gradients that reflect true sequence rewards rather than erratic token-level corrections.

Furthermore, this approach naturally addresses precision discrepancies between training and inference engines mentioned in Section 6 because sequence likelihoods are more tolerant of minor computational variations than iterative token-by-token probabilities—an issue critical when dealing with hundreds or thousands of tokens where small floating-point errors compound wildly in autoregressive generation. By focusing on $P(y|x)$ rather than $P(y_t|x,y_{<t})$, GSPO ensures that sampling remains stationary enough for effective policy updates: it filters out responses where the global behavior has drifted too far, while retaining those where local probabilistic shifts have balanced out over long contexts to produce valid improvement signals.
