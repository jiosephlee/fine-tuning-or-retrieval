Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

The landscape of artificial intelligence has undergone a transformative shift in recent years, moving beyond simple pattern recognition to the development of systems capable of deep reasoning, complex code generation, and rigorous mathematical problem-solving. At the heart of this evolution stands Reinforcement Learning (RL), which has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling language models. Unlike earlier approaches that relied primarily on static datasets or human feedback labels collected once, RL allows language models to actively engage with environments—be they competitive math problems, coding benchmarks, or logical puzzles—and learn through trial and error. By undertaking deeper and longer reasoning processes, models trained via large-scale RL develop the capability to tackle sophisticated tasks that were previously inaccessible. This progression marks a move from merely predicting the next word to mastering complex cognitive skills, positioning RL as the primary driver for pushing the boundaries of what large language models can achieve.

However, unlocking this potential requires navigating a treacherous terrain known as training instability. As we invest greater computational resources into these systems, employing massive model sizes and extended response lengths, the foremost prerequisite for success becomes maintaining stable and robust training dynamics. Without stability, the immense compute invested yields diminishing returns or worse, complete failure. The journey toward advanced AI capabilities is currently hindered by severe stability issues exhibited by current state-of-the-art algorithms when applied to gigantic language models. These instabilities often manifest as catastrophic and irreversible model collapses, where the learning process derails completely, rendering subsequent attempts futile regardless of hyperparameter tuning or checkpoint recovery. Understanding why this happens and how to overcome it is central to grasping the future direction of AI research.

To appreciate the urgency of addressing these stability issues, one must trace the evolution of LLM alignment techniques. Initially, researchers focused on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where models learned from curated examples of desired outputs. While effective for basic instruction following, SFT alone was insufficient for cultivating true reasoning abilities or handling open-ended domains like competitive programming or Olympiad-level mathematics. Consequently, the field pivoted toward Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and its variants like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). These methods introduced reward signals derived from human preferences or automated verifiers to guide the model away from generic text generation toward helpfulness and correctness. Yet, as models grew in scale—from billions to trillions of parameters—the complexity of their internal representations increased exponentially. The simple reward structures used in early iterations became inadequate for guiding such vast architectures safely through the loss landscape. The transition from SFT to RLHF represented a qualitative leap in capability, but it also exposed a critical fragility: as model size and task difficulty scaled up alongside compute budgets, existing algorithms began to fail spectacularly under their own weight.

This brings us to the specific crisis facing modern training pipelines: the instability inherent in current state-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO was designed specifically to address some inefficiencies in prior methods by eliminating the need for a separate value model—a component that traditionally required significant memory and was difficult to train reliably at scale. Instead, GRPO computes advantages relatively within groups of responses generated by an old policy before updating to a new one. Despite its innovations in architecture efficiency and memory footprint reduction, GRPO suffers from fundamental flaws when deployed on gigantic models engaged in long-context reasoning tasks. The core issue lies in how it handles off-policy corrections during training. Because RL involves sampling actions (tokens) based on an older version of the policy while trying to optimize towards a newer version—a classic off-policy scenario—it relies heavily on importance sampling weights to correct for distributional shifts between generations taken by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ versus those preferred by $\pi_\theta$.

The limitation of GRPO arises because it applies these importance sampling weights at every single token level within a sequence rather than treating the entire response as a coherent unit of optimization. When generating long responses typical of complex reasoning tasks involving hundreds or thousands of tokens, small errors compound dramatically along the trajectory created by individual token probabilities being multiplied together over time $t$. Applying high-variance correction factors at each step amplifies this noise exponentially across long sequences rather than dampening it as theory might suggest under ideal conditions with sufficient sample averaging per sample point.
