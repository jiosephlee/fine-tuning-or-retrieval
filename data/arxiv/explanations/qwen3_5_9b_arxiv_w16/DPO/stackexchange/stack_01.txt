\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out in the DPO derivation, and what does it mean that the optimal policy is a reparameterization of the reward?
Question:
In the paper, the authors derive Eq. 2 by expressing the reward function $r(x,y)$ in terms of the optimal policy $ar{\pi}(y|x)$ and then substituting this into the Bradley-Terry preference model. They state that this substitution makes the partition function $Z(x)$ 'cancel out', allowing us to write preferences directly in terms of policies (Eq. 3). 

I am struggling to follow the algebra strictly and understand the conceptual implication here. Does 'cancelling out' mean $Z(x)$ becomes exactly 1? If I rearrange Eq. 2, I still see a term involving $\sum_y \pi(y|x) \exp(r(y)/\beta)$. Since $\pi$ itself depends on $r$ and this sum must equal 1 for any valid probability distribution, isn't this just using the definition of normalization rather than a unique cancellation of $Z(x)$ from a fixed reward model to a variable policy? Furthermore, if every reward function has multiple equivalent forms differing by a term that depends only on $x$, how do we know DPO isn't selecting a biased subset of reward functions compared to explicit reward modeling?
Answer:
The cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ in the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) derivation is a direct consequence of the Bradley-Terry model's reliance on reward differences rather than absolute reward values, combined with the specific mathematical structure of the optimal policy under KL regularization.

To understand why $Z(x)$ effectively cancels out, we must look at how preferences are modeled versus how the optimal policy is derived. In standard RLHF, the human preference probability is modeled using the Bradley-Terry equation: $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2))$. When substituting the reparameterized reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ into this equation, you are indeed left with a term $\beta \log Z(x) y$ for each response. However, because this term appears identically for both $y_1$ and $y_2$, it represents a constant offset for any given prompt $x$. When calculating the difference in rewards to determine which response is preferred ($r(y_1) - r(y_2)$), these identical constant terms subtract from one another exactly, yielding zero. Thus, the cancellation does not mean $Z(x)$ equals 1; rather, it means any function that depends solely on $x$ acts as a constant shift in reward that has no bearing on relative ordering. Since human preferences are only defined up to an arbitrary additive function of $x$, discarding this term via cancellation is physically meaningful and mathematically necessary to isolate the policy contributions.

This insight leads to a profound conceptual shift: optimizing with DPO implies that your language model is secretly acting as its own reward model. The term often denoted as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ in your question is not a new function you are guessing at; it is a specific reparameterization of the reward that guarantees $\pi_\theta$ is the optimal policy for that reward subject to the KL constraint relative to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This relationship creates an equivalence class where any two rewards differing only by a term dependent solely on $x$ (like $\beta \log Z(x)$) induce the exact same optimal policy and generate identical preference distributions under the Bradley-Terry model. Therefore, DPO does not get "stuck" or select a biased subset of rewards because it exploits this redundancy; it simply chooses the most convenient member of this equivalence class—one where the partition function constraint is baked directly into the log-ratio definition of the logit difference, eliminating the need to explicitly compute or estimate $Z(x)$.

Finally, regarding your concern about bias or infinite rewards summing to one: this confusion arises from mixing up two different normalizations. In Equation 2 (the optimal policy), $\sum_y \pi_r(y|x) = 1$ by definition because it is a probability distribution. The term involving $\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y))$ acting as $Z(x)$ ensures that if you start with a valid probability distribution and apply an exponential transformation scaled by $\beta$, you can normalize it back to 1 using $Z(x)$. In DPO (Equation 4), we never use absolute rewards; we only use relative differences for pairs $(y_w, y_l)$. Because we are never asked to normalize an unnormalized exponential distribution explicitly during training—only to maximize likelihood based on which of two outcomes "wins"—the scaling factor required to make probabilities sum to one disappears from the objective function entirely. This allows us to train directly on preferences without ever sampling responses or estimating values, solving what was traditionally an intractable intersection between reinforcement learning and statistical modeling.
