### ; Is there an inherent bias toward high-probability completions in DPO due to its dependence on ${\pi}_{ref}$?
Question:

Answer:
Yes, DPO inherently biases toward high-probability completions, but this is a necessary and well-constrained feature rather than an artifact of bias. This behavior arises directly from the mathematical structure of the DPO objective in Eq. (5), which is designed to solve a constrained optimization problem: maximizing reward while keeping the policy close to a reference model via a KL-divergence penalty.

To understand why this happens, we must look at the reparameterization used in DPO. The method posits that the implicit reward function $r(x,y)$ is proportional to the log-ratio of the policy's probability over the reference policy's probability, $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$. When optimizing Eq. (5), the model updates parameters to increase this ratio for preferred outputs ($y_w$) relative to dispreferred ones ($y_l$). Specifically, the term $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$ is pushed up, while $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$ is pushed down. Because these probabilities are normalized within each generation (they must sum to one), drastically increasing the probability of a single specific token or sequence necessarily requires decreasing the probabilities of all other sequences. Additionally, the hyperparameter $\beta$ acts as a strength multiplier for this update; larger values of $\beta$ encourage more drastic shifts toward high-probability modes to satisfy preferences, effectively enforcing a stronger preference for confident predictions over uncertain ones in contexts where data is sparse or contradictory.

This mechanism distinguishes DPO from naive likelihood minimization because it relies on ratios rather than absolute probabilities. However, it does mean that DPO will not hallucinate low-probability completions even if they are labeled as "preferred" by humans unless sufficient training data exists to overcome the prior confidence encoded in $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In practice, this acts similarly to the KL constraint in RLHF methods like PPO described in Eq. (4), preventing the model from "degenerating" toward mode collapse or assigning excessive probability mass to unlikely gibberish just because a human labeled it as preferred in isolation. Therefore, while high-probability completions are naturally favored due to optimization dynamics and normalization constraints, this is precisely how DPO maintains stability and prevents catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained knowledge during alignment.
