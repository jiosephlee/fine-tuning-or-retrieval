\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What are the theoretical bounds on generalization for DPO policies versus PPO policies when moving out-of-distribution?
Question:
In Section 5.4 ('Generalization to a new input distribution'), the authors show DPO outperforms PPO on OOD data (CNN/DailyMail after training on Reddit TL;DR). They argue this might be because PPO uses additional unlabeled prompts during fine-tuning while DPO does not.

However, Eq. 9 implies that any reward model consistent with Plackett-Luce can be represented by a policy parameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log (\pi / \pi_{ref})$. If both methods optimize towards satisfying human preferences implicitly defined by an unknown 'true' reward function, shouldn't their generalization properties be theoretically similar if they converge to approximately optimal policies? Does DPO actually converge differently because it optimizes likelihood directly without an iterative counterfactual step (PPO's importance weights)? Specifically, how does the absence of variance in estimating importance weights in PPO (due to discrete generation) affect its robustness versus DPO's implicit reward estimation?
Answer:
The Short Answer provides a crucial distinction between how DPO and PPO optimize the policy that explains the generalization gap, yet it does not detail the theoretical underpinnings.

## Theoretical Bounds on Generalization for DPO vs. PPO

The theoretical bounds on generalization for DPO versus PPO policies when moving out-of-distribution (OOD) stem from their fundamentally different optimization dynamics. While both methods optimize the same underlying constrained objective—maximizing reward while minimizing KL divergence from a reference policy—their convergence properties lead to different robustness characteristics in OOD settings.

At the core of this difference is how each method estimates and optimizes its reward signal. Both methods solve for the optimal policy $\pi^*$ under a Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce preference model, but they do so through different parameterizations and optimization procedures that lead to different implicit reward estimation biases and variances.

PPO relies on an actor-critic framework where it learns a separate reward model $r_\phi$ first, then uses reinforcement learning to optimize a policy $\pi_\theta$ based on estimated importance weights. In each update step, PPO requires estimating $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}[r_{\phi}(x, y)]$ through sampling from the current policy. This introduces variance in two critical ways: first, through the stochastic nature of Monte Carlo estimation of rewards and values in discrete generation spaces; second, through the importance sampling weights that can become highly variable when policies diverge significantly from their reference distribution. This variance becomes particularly problematic in OOD settings where the rewarding function may have different characteristics than what was observed during fine-tuning, leading to unstable gradient estimates and potential policy collapse.

Moreover, PPO's need to use "additional unlabeled prompts during fine-tuning" as mentioned in Section 5.4 of the original work creates a specific distributional mismatch issue. These unlabeled prompts are used implicitly during training (through sampling) but represent a data distribution that differs from both the preference-labeled training data and potentially from new test distributions. When faced with OOD data like CNN/DailyMail after training primarily on Reddit TL;DR summaries, PPO's policy may have over-optimized for the specific distributional characteristics of those unlabeled prompts rather than learning robust features of human preferences themselves. This leads to poorer generalization because the model has essentially learned artifacts of its fine-tuning procedure rather than directly optimizing for preferences across diverse domains.

In contrast, DPO bypasses this issue entirely by directly optimizing a policy parameterization equivalent to an implicit reward function without requiring separate reward modeling or RL loops. The key insight from Equation 9 in the paper shows that any reward model consistent with Plackett-Luce can be represented as $r(x,y) = \beta \log (\pi / \pi_{ref})$, meaning DPO optimizes over this entire class of functions directly through maximum likelihood estimation on preference data alone. By eliminating intermediate steps like separate reward model training and RL-based policy optimization, DPO reduces variance in its learning process significantly—there are no importance weighting estimates with potentially infinite variance, no separate value function estimation, and no need for exploring additional unlabeled data distributions during training.

The critical difference lies in how each method handles counterfactual reasoning about rewards without ground truth labels. PPO must estimate gradients using sampled trajectories and bootstrap value estimates, which introduces approximation errors that can compound when test distributions differ substantially from training distributions. These estimation errors make PPO more sensitive to OOD perturbations because small changes in input distribution can lead to large changes in estimated reward signals due to the bootstrap nature of RL's value function estimation combined with importance sampling variance.

DPO's absence of variance in estimating importance weights (and indeed absence of importance weighting altogether) fundamentally improves robustness because it removes one entire source of estimation error that plagues RL-based methods in high-dimensional discrete action spaces like language generation tasks discussed throughout Section 3 ("Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms") where such variance is particularly severe due to computational constraints forcing proximal modifications rather than true gradient ascent on expected rewards over sampled trajectories.
