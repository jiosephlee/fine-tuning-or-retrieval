Chapter 1: Chapter 1: Introduction to Language Model Alignment and Preference Learning

Large-scale unsupervised language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in acquiring world knowledge and reasoning skills. These models are trained on vast datasets of text generated by humans with diverse goals, priorities, and skillsets. While this exposure allows the models to understand a wide variety of topics, it also means they inherit not only desirable traits but also undesirable behaviors present in the training data. For instance, a coding assistant might understand common programming mistakes well enough to generate them if prompted with malicious intent, or a conversational model might perpetuate misconceptions held by a significant portion of its training population. The central challenge in building useful AI systems is not merely teaching the model *what* is true or possible, but selecting the desired responses from its broad spectrum of capabilities to ensure safety and helpfulness. This process is known as alignment—steering the model's behavior to match human intent. While unsupervised pre-training provides raw intelligence, it often lacks the nuanced judgment required for safe interaction. Consequently, researchers have developed sophisticated methods to fine-tune these powerful models based on human feedback, moving from simple instruction following to optimizing for relative human preferences using complex reinforcement learning procedures.

Before diving into the machinery of alignment, it is essential to understand why standard pre-training is insufficient for these tasks. Unsupervised pre-training excels at learning statistics and patterns in large text corpora, allowing models to predict probable continuations based on low-level linguistic rules and high-level factual associations. However, this competency alone does not guarantee that a model will produce outputs that are safe, truthful, or aligned with specific values when making choices between equally plausible options. The problem lies in the nature of probabilistic generation; an unsupervised model assigns non-zero probability to many different endings for a given prompt, including those that are harmful or unhelpful. Without an explicit mechanism to filter these choices based on human judgment, there is no guarantee that the highest-probability completion is the one humans actually prefer. For example, when given a prompt asking for advice on a controversial topic, an unsupervised model might generate multiple valid-seeming responses ranging from balanced analysis to biased propaganda. Aligning the model requires shifting its probability distribution so that preferred responses become significantly more likely than dispreferred ones while maintaining coherence and avoiding degeneration into repetitive or nonsensical text. This necessity drove the development of reinforcement learning frameworks designed specifically for language generation, culminating in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

The transition from raw intelligence to helpful alignment often begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). While SFT improves a model's ability to follow instructions compared to pure next-token prediction alone by exposing it to high-quality examples of user prompts paired with ideal responses, it has distinct limitations regarding preference learning. SFT requires curated datasets containing demonstrations where both questions and perfect answers are provided by experts. Collecting such data is labor-intensive and expensive because it demands not just a preference between two options but a full demonstration of how an ideal agent should respond from start to finish. In many practical scenarios where we want our AI assistant to produce results similar to those created by experienced humans but with slight variations in style or focus, only relative judgments can be easily obtained—for instance, telling an annotator which of two generated summaries is better without necessarily producing both summaries manually in advance. Furthermore, even when instruction-following models are trained on thousands of expert demonstrations, they may still fail at specific downstream tasks like sentiment modulation or safety constraints unless explicitly optimized for them again through methods that leverage preference data rather than just supervised demonstrations alone.
