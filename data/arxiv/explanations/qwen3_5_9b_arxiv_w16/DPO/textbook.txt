Chapter 1: Introduction to Language Model Alignment and Preference Learning

Large-scale unsupervised language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in acquiring world knowledge and reasoning skills. These models are trained on vast datasets of text generated by humans with diverse goals, priorities, and skillsets. While this exposure allows the models to understand a wide variety of topics, it also means they inherit not only desirable traits but also undesirable behaviors present in the training data. For instance, a coding assistant might understand common programming mistakes well enough to generate them if prompted with malicious intent, or a conversational model might perpetuate misconceptions held by a significant portion of its training population. The central challenge in building useful AI systems is not merely teaching the model *what* is true or possible, but selecting the desired responses from its broad spectrum of capabilities to ensure safety and helpfulness. This process is known as alignment—steering the model's behavior to match human intent. While unsupervised pre-training provides raw intelligence, it often lacks the nuanced judgment required for safe interaction. Consequently, researchers have developed sophisticated methods to fine-tune these powerful models based on human feedback, moving from simple instruction following to optimizing for relative human preferences using complex reinforcement learning procedures.

Before diving into the machinery of alignment, it is essential to understand why standard pre-training is insufficient for these tasks. Unsupervised pre-training excels at learning statistics and patterns in large text corpora, allowing models to predict probable continuations based on low-level linguistic rules and high-level factual associations. However, this competency alone does not guarantee that a model will produce outputs that are safe, truthful, or aligned with specific values when making choices between equally plausible options. The problem lies in the nature of probabilistic generation; an unsupervised model assigns non-zero probability to many different endings for a given prompt, including those that are harmful or unhelpful. Without an explicit mechanism to filter these choices based on human judgment, there is no guarantee that the highest-probability completion is the one humans actually prefer. For example, when given a prompt asking for advice on a controversial topic, an unsupervised model might generate multiple valid-seeming responses ranging from balanced analysis to biased propaganda. Aligning the model requires shifting its probability distribution so that preferred responses become significantly more likely than dispreferred ones while maintaining coherence and avoiding degeneration into repetitive or nonsensical text. This necessity drove the development of reinforcement learning frameworks designed specifically for language generation, culminating in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

The transition from raw intelligence to helpful alignment often begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). While SFT improves a model's ability to follow instructions compared to pure next-token prediction alone by exposing it to high-quality examples of user prompts paired with ideal responses, it has distinct limitations regarding preference learning. SFT requires curated datasets containing demonstrations where both questions and perfect answers are provided by experts. Collecting such data is labor-intensive and expensive because it demands not just a preference between two options but a full demonstration of how an ideal agent should respond from start to finish. In many practical scenarios where we want our AI assistant to produce results similar to those created by experienced humans but with slight variations in style or focus, only relative judgments can be easily obtained—for instance, telling an annotator which of two generated summaries is better without necessarily producing both summaries manually in advance. Furthermore, even when instruction-following models are trained on thousands of expert demonstrations, they may still fail at specific downstream tasks like sentiment modulation or safety constraints unless explicitly optimized for them again through methods that leverage preference data rather than just supervised demonstrations alone. Thus while SFT serves as a crucial foundation by setting up basic task competency—it acts as our reference policy $ \pi^{SFT} $ established from traditional supervised fine-tuning phases—it cannot Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations of Policy Optimization

# Formulating Alignment as Constrained KL-Regularized Reward Maximization

To understand how Direct Preference Optimization achieves its results, we must first rigorously define the objective that existing Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) methods attempt to solve. At the core of the alignment problem in language modeling lies a tension between two competing goals: we desire the model to produce high-quality responses that maximize human preference, yet we simultaneously want the model to remain faithful to its original training distribution to avoid degenerate outputs or catastrophic forgetting. This trade-off is mathematically captured by a constrained optimization problem that seeks to maximize an expected reward while penalizing deviations from a reference policy.

Consider a language model defined by a policy $\pi_{\theta}$, where $\theta$ represents the model parameters being optimized, and let $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ denote a reference policy, often initialized from a supervised fine-tuned model. We assume there exists a latent reward function $r(x, y)$ that captures human preferences for a given prompt $x$ and response $y$. The goal of RLHF is to find a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that maximizes the expectation of this reward function over the data distribution, minus a penalty term that measures how much the new policy diverges from the reference. This divergence is quantified using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, denoted as $\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)]$. The KL divergence serves as a regularizer; it acts as an anchor, preventing the model $\pi_{\theta}$ from drifting arbitrarily far into regions of the output space where the learned reward function might be unreliable or where generated text might be incoherent. The parameter $\beta$, a hyperparameter controlling this constraint strength, scales this penalty. In many practical implementations, including those optimized via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), this constraint is moved inside the objective function, effectively creating a single reward signal that combines the external score and an internal negative log-likelihood relative to the reference model.

The formal mathematical statement of this goal is expressed as:
$$ \max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr] $$
where $\mathcal{D}$ represents the dataset of prompts. It is crucial to note that because natural language modeling generates discrete tokens autoregressively, computing gradients with respect to this objective directly with standard stochastic gradient descent is impossible. The action space is discrete and non-differentiable at each token prediction step. Consequently, traditional approaches rely on reinforcement learning algorithms like PPO to approximate this optimization by estimating gradients through Monte Carlo sampling. These algorithms are notoriously unstable and require complex tune-ups because they involve optimizing both an actor (the policy) and a critic (an estimate of value), often leading to mode collapse or divergence if hyperparameters are not tuned with extreme care. Despite being grounded in optimal theory, solving this equation in practice is fraught with computational difficulty due to these discrete constraints and sampling requirements.

# The Constrained MDP Objective: Maximizing Reward Subject to KL-Divergence Constraints

The optimization problem described above can be understood within the framework of Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs). In this formulation, the agent (our language model) interacts with an environment (the task generation process) over time steps $t$. However, unlike standard MDPs where rewards are scalars collected at each step and summed into an episode return $G_t = \sum_t r_t$, in language generation using Reinforced Attention or standard next-token prediction models, we often treat the entire sequence $y$ as generated jointly from an initial state $x$ via autoregressive probability products: $\prod_t \pi_\theta(a_t | s_{t-1})$. In this setting for DPO, we are not strictly deriving trajectories step-by-step during training but rather optimizing the full set of probabilities assigned to sequences based on preference data pairs $(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})$.

The constraint on the KL-divergence is fundamentally about safety and stability rather than just efficiency. If we were to maximize $\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]$ without any constraint on $\beta$, even if we knew exactly what humans preferred based on our small dataset of labels—say, "polite but wrong" answers over "abrupt but correct" answers—the optimizer would likely force every single query $x$ into providing only those specific types of responses regardless of whether it led to coherent or helpful output for that particular context $x$.
