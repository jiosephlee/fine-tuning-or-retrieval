# Beyond the Transformer: Why LSTMs Are Making a Massive Comeback

For over a decade, the landscape of natural language processing was defined by a single paradigm shift: the Transformer. Introduced in 2017, this architecture leveraged parallelizable self-attention mechanisms to outperform its predecessors, fundamentally changing how we train Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT. The focus shifted entirely to scaling up. However, a recent study titled xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory challenges this narrative. It asks a simple yet profound question: How far can we push Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) if we scale them to billions of parameters and apply modern techniques? The answer suggests that LSTMs are not dead; they are evolving into a serious competitor for Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs).

To understand this resurgence, we must first recall why LSTMs were eventually overshadowed. The original Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) unit revolutionized sequence modeling in the 1990s by solving the vanishing gradient problem through gating mechanisms and a constant error carousel. Equation~\ref{eq:lstm_idea} captures the core update rule where the cell state $c_t$ is updated based on previous information and new inputs via gates $f_t$ and $i_t$. Despite their success in tasks ranging from text generation to game-playing agents like AlphaStar, traditional LSTMs hit three major ceilings that prevented them from scaling to trillion-parameter models.

The first limitation is the inability to easily revise storage decisions. In a standard LSTM, once information is written to memory or keyed with a position, it is difficult to overwrite or update it based on new context that appears later in the sequence. For example, in a nearest neighbor search problem within a long sequence determined by context, an LSTM often struggles to correct an early stored vector when a more similar one appears later. This rigidity stems from fixed gating rules that do not dynamically adjust the retention value with sufficient magnitude.

The second issue concerns limited storage capacity. Traditional LSTMs store information in scalar cell states ($c_{t-1} \in \mathbb{R}$). Consequently, distinct pieces of information must be compressed into these scalar values, leading to interference where forgetting one piece of data might inadvertently erase another vital signal. In experiments regarding rare token prediction, LSTMs performed poorly because they could not robustly store low-frequency tokens without losing high-frequency ones due to this compression bottleneck.

Finally, there is the critical issue of parallelization. Because standard LSTMs mix hidden states across memory cells using recurrent connections ($h_{t-1}$ influencing multiple outputs), they serve sequential computation requirements at every step of the sequence generation process. This makes them computationally inefficient compared to the massive parallelism offered by Transformers and recent fast state-space models like Mamba or Hyena.

The xLSTM paper proposes two specific architectural modifications to address these flaws without discarding the intuitive strength of RNNs: exponential gating and novel memory structures. By replacing simple sigmoid gates with exponential activation functions for input and forget paths, xLSTM gains the ability to perform large-magnitude updates later in the sequence based on earlier inputs effectively solving the dispute revision problem. To handle this numerically stable approach while preventing overflow during training, the authors introduce an additional stabilizer state derived from previous gate magnitudes, ensuring that parameter updates remain consistent with traditional gradients despite the non-linear scaling factors [see Eq.\ref{eq:slstmforward}].

Perhaps more transformative is the introduction of new memory structures tailored for specific tasks while maintaining efficiency across others. The researchers developed two variants integrated into residual blocks. The scalar sLSTM retains memory mixing capabilities by using multiple heads but restricts mixing within heads rather than across them entirely; exceptionally, it can support multi-cell mixing similar to traditional LSTMs but enhanced with exponential weights for updating stored states [see Eq.\ref{eq:slstmstabil}]. On the other hand, mLSTM introduces a matrix memory ($\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$) that functions similarly to matrix factorials used in Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAM). Instead of pooling information into a single scalar number, mLSTM stores key-value pairs as outer products in its covariance matrix [see Eq.\ref{eq:mlstm_recurrent_begin}]. This structure allows for direct retrieval operations that are significantly more robust than scalar compression as demonstrated by results on multi-query associative recall tasks where xLSTM[1:0] matched or surpassed non-Transformer baselines like Mamba and RWKV [Figure\ref{fig:mqar-main}].
