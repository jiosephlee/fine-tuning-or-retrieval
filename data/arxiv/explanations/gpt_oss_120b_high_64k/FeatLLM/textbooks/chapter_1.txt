\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: **Fundamentals of Tabular Few‑Shot Learning and Large Language Models**

In modern data‑driven applications a great many problems are naturally expressed as tables: a collection of records (rows) each described by a set of attributes (columns).  While the tabular format is simple to understand, building accurate predictive models from it can be surprisingly hard—especially when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  This chapter introduces the key properties of tabular data that set it apart from vision or text modalities, explains why few‑shot learning is challenging for such data, reviews the inductive biases traditionally encoded in classical tabular learners, formalizes the notion of *k‑shot* learning used throughout this book, and finally presents the capabilities of modern large language models (LLMs) that enable them to serve as powerful knowledge sources through *in‑context* learning.  The discussion is anchored in the approach proposed by **FeatLLM**, a framework that treats LLMs as automatic feature engineers for few‑shot tabular tasks.

---

### Structure of Tabular Datasets – Numeric versus Categorical Columns and Natural‑Language Feature Names

A tabular dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) consists of \(N\) samples \(\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\).  Each sample \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\) is a vector of \(d\) attributes,
\[
\mathbf{x}^{i} = \bigl[x^{i}_{1},x^{i}_{2},\dots ,x^{i}_{d}\bigr]^{\!\top},
\]
and each attribute is identified by a human‑readable name \(f_{j}\in F=\{f_{1},\dots ,f_{d}\}\).  In practice these names are often short phrases such as *“age”*, *“blood‑pressure”*, or *“marital status”*.  The accompanying documentation may also contain a textual definition—for example, “*blood‑pressure* is measured in mm Hg after five minutes of rest”.  Such natural‑language descriptors are crucial when an external system—particularly an LLM—must reason about what each column means.

From a statistical point of view, columns fall broadly into two families:

* **Numeric columns** contain real‑valued measurements (e.g., age, salary, laboratory test results).  They admit an ordering and arithmetic operations.
* **Categorical columns** enumerate discrete categories (e.g., gender ∈ {Male,Female}, education level ∈ {High School,Bachelor,…}).  Categorical values may be nominal (no intrinsic order) or ordinal (an ordering exists).

The mixed nature of these column types creates challenges for generic transformation pipelines: many algorithms must either treat them differently or convert all features into a common numerical representation via one‑hot encoding or embedding techniques.  Moreover—unlike raw image pixels or word tokens—tabular features lack an inherent spatial or sequential structure.  Consequently any model must discover which subsets of columns actually carry predictive information for a given task.

---

### Why Few‑Shot Learning Is Difficult for Tabular Problems Compared with Vision or Text Domains

Few‑shot learning refers to the situation where a learner receives only a small collection of labeled examples—often fewer than ten per class—and must still generalize reliably to new data.  In visual recognition tasks this setting has proven tractable because deep convolutional networks trained on massive image corpora acquire *generic visual priors*: edge detectors, texture patterns, and shape concepts that transfer across tasks with minimal fine‑tuning.  Analogously, large language models learn linguistic structures that allow them to perform new text tasks by simply being shown a few demonstrations.

Tabular data lack such universal structural regularities:

1. **Heterogeneous Feature Types** – A single table can combine continuous measurements, categorical identifiers, missing values, and even free-form textual notes.  No single “visual” or “syntactic” prior covers all these modalities simultaneously.
2. **Sparse Semantic Signals** – The relationship between any column and the target label may be highly domain specific (e.g., older age increases cardiovascular disease risk).  Without sufficient labelled examples it is easy for a learner to latch onto spurious correlations arising from random coincidences in the small sample.
3. **Absence of Self‑Supervision** – Unlike images where one can generate pseudo‑labels via contrastive methods or predict masked pixels, constructing meaningful self‑supervised objectives for arbitrary columns is nontrivial because there is no obvious invariance across samples.
4. **Limited Data Augmentation** – Standard augmentation strategies such as cropping or synonym replacement have no obvious analogues for generic tables; naive perturbations often destroy real-world semantics.

Because expertise about which columns matter typically resides with domain specialists rather than in large publicly available corpora, conventional few‑shot learners suffer from high variance and overfit easily when applied directly to raw tables.

### Typical Inductive Biases in Decision Trees, Linear Models and Gradient Boosting

To compensate for scarcity of labelled data classical tabular learners embed **inductive biases**—assumptions about the form of the target function—that guide training toward plausible solutions even when data are limited.

* **Linear Models (e.g., Logistic Regression)** – Impose a *global linear* relationship between features and log‑odds:
  \[
  \log \frac{p(y=1|\mathbf{x})}{p(y=0|\mathbf{x})}= \mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}+b .
  \]
  This strong bias favors additive effects and discourages complex interactions among columns.  When the true decision boundary can be approximated by weighted sums (as often happens after careful feature engineering), linear models perform well even with few examples; otherwise they underfit severely.
  
* **Decision Trees** – Recursively split the input space along single feature thresholds (“\(x_{j}>c\)”).  The bias here encourages *piecewise constant* approximations where each leaf captures a homogeneous subset defined by logical conditions on individual columns.  Trees naturally handle mixed numeric/categorical data without preprocessing and can represent interaction effects through successive splits.
  
* **Gradient Boosting (e.g., XGBoost)** – Constructs an ensemble of shallow decision trees trained sequentially on residuals.  The bias inherits tree‐like piecewise constancy but augments it with *additive modeling*: each weak learner corrects errors made by its predecessors, enabling highly expressive functions while retaining regularization mechanisms such as tree depth limits and shrinkage rates.

All three families have demonstrated strong empirical performance on benchmark tables when enough labeled data are available (e.g., tens or hundreds per class).  However, their training procedures—especially gradient boosting—rely heavily on gradient estimates computed over many samples; consequently their predictions become unstable under extreme low‑shot regimes.

### Definition of *k*-Shot Learning Setups Used Throughout This Book

The formalism adopted by FeatLLM follows standard few‑shot notation.   Suppose we have an entire labeled pool \(\mathcal{D}\) consisting of \(N\) examples. In a *k*-shot experiment we randomly select
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \bigl\{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\mid i=1,\dots ,k\bigr\},
\quad k<N,
\]
as the training set while reserving the remaining samples for evaluation.   For classification problems with \(c\) classes we denote each label vector as one-hot encoded \(\mathbf{y}^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{c}\).  

Throughout this text we consider two representative regimes:

| Shots per class | Typical total shots (\(k=c\times {\rm shots}\)) |
|-----------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| 4               | \(k=4c\)                                       |
| 8               | \(k=8c\)                                       |
| 16              | \(k=16c\)                                      |

These values are deliberately small compared with conventional supervised training where dozens to hundreds per class are common.   All methods—including baseline tree ensembles and LLM‐based approaches—are evaluated under identical random splits so that performance differences reflect how well each algorithm leverages priors versus raw data.

### Core Capabilities of Modern Large Language Models That Make Them Useful as Knowledge Sources

Large language models such as GPT‑3.5 have been pretrained on massive text corpora spanning scientific articles, news stories, biomedical literature, and code repositories.   Their size endows them with several properties particularly relevant to tabular few‑shot learning:

1. **Extensive Prior Knowledge** – Because they have observed billions of sentences describing real‐world facts ("older patients have higher risk of heart disease"), they can retrieve domain‐specific associations just from a textual description of features.
2. **Reasoning Over Structured Prompts** – When supplied with a prompt containing a task description plus illustrative examples—a technique called *in‑context learning*—the model can condition its output on those demonstrations without any weight updates.
3. **Chain‑of‑Thought Generation** – By encouraging stepwise reasoning (“First infer causal relationships… Then derive rules”), LLMs produce intermediate logical statements that resemble human expert analysis.
4. **Code Generation Ability** – Trained on large code bases (e.g., GitHub), modern LLMs reliably convert natural language conditions into executable snippets (Python `if` statements), which facilitates automatic parsing into binary features.
5. **Parameter‐Efficient Adaptation** – While some works fine-tune LLMs via low‐rank adapters (e.g., LoRA), FeatLLM avoids any fine-tuning entirely; it merely queries the model at inference time using an API key—a practical advantage when only black‐box access is available.

These capabilities collectively enable an LLM to act like an *automatic feature engineer*: given only several labeled rows and feature definitions it can emit human‐readable decision rules that encode useful transformations for downstream classifiers.

### Principle of In‑Context Learning Without Updating Model Parameters

In contextual prompting we embed all information needed for solving a new task directly into a textual window presented to the LLM:

```
Task: Predict whether a patient will develop chronic heart failure.
Features:
 - Age (numeric): patient's age in years.
 - Cholesterol (numeric): serum cholesterol level.
 - Smoker (categorical): {yes,no}
...
Examples:
 Patient A: Age = 68 … Answer: Yes
 Patient B: Age = 45 … Answer: No
```

The model processes this prompt token by token using its precomputed attention weights; during generation it conditions on *both* the textual description of columns (**priors**) *and* the concrete example rows (**data evidence**) while leaving its internal parameters untouched.  

Feasibly three steps occur internally:

1. **Task Understanding** – The model extracts from the natural language description what outcome is required (“predict chronic heart failure”) and how each column should be interpreted (numeric vs categorical).
2. **Pattern Extraction From Demonstrations** – By attending to example rows together with their answers it forms tentative associations between column values and labels.
3. **Rule Synthesis** – Guided by system prompts instructing a “two‑step reasoning process”, it first hypothesizes causal tendencies (“higher age increases risk”) then enumerates concrete logical conditions (“If Age > 60 AND Smoker = yes then Likely Yes”).

Crucially there is no gradient descent performed on model weights; all adaptation happens through *prompt engineering*.   By varying demonstration order or sampling different subsets (“bagging”), FeatLLM induces diverse rule sets across multiple runs—an effect analogous to stochasticity in ensemble methods but realized entirely at inference time.

Once rules are produced they are parsed into executable Python functions using another LLM call designed solely for code generation—the output resembles:

```python
def rule_1(sample):
    return int(sample["Age"] > 60) & int(sample["Smoker"] == "yes")
```

Applying these functions to every record yields binary indicator vectors \(\mathbf{z}_k^i \in \{0,1\}^R\), where \(R\) denotes the number of extracted rules per class \(k\).  
A downstream linear model,
\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k,\mathbf{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{z}_k^i,
\qquad
p_k^i = \frac{\exp(\text{logit}_k^i)}{\sum_{h=1}^{c} \exp(\text{logit}_h^i)},
\]
is then trained on the limited \(k\)-shot set to learn rule importances (\(\mathbf{w}_k \geq 0\)).  

Because inference now consists solely of evaluating binary conditions followed by a lightweight linear map—not invoking the massive LLM per test sample—the overall latency matches that of traditional classifiers while still benefiting from rich semantic priors extracted once during rule generation.

### Summary

Tabular datasets present unique hurdles for few‑shot learning due primarily to mixed feature types, lack of universal priors, and susceptibility to spurious correlations when only sparse labels are available. Classical learners mitigate these issues by imposing strong inductive biases—linearity for logistic regression or hierarchical piecewise partitions for trees/gradient boosting—but often require more labeled data than feasible in practice.

Large language models provide an orthogonal source of knowledge: pretraining endows them with world facts about how attributes relate to outcomes across domains ranging from medicine to finance. By exploiting *in‑context learning*, we can elicit these models to generate high‐level decision rules directly from natural language descriptions and few annotated rows—all without updating any parameters. FeatLLM illustrates how such rules become new binary features amenable to simple linear training; ensembles built through bagging further enhance robustness while keeping inference efficient.

Understanding these foundations equips readers to appreciate why subsequent chapters delve deeper into algorithmic design choices—prompt templating, rule parsing strategies, ensemble constructions—and experimental evaluations that demonstrate FeatLLM’s superiority over both conventional tabular baselines and prior LLM-based approaches under strict low‑shot constraints.
