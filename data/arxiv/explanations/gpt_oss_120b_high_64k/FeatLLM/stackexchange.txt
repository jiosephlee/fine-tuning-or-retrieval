\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Why does FeatLLM enforce non‑negative weights when aggregating rule‑based binary features?
Question:
In Section 3.2 the paper defines the class logit for sample \(i\) as
\[
\text{logit}_k^i = \max(\mathbf{w}_k, 0) \cdot \mathbf{z}_k^i,
\]
where \(\mathbf{z}_k^i\) is the binary vector indicating which of the \(R\) rules for class \(k\) are satisfied and \(\mathbf{w}_k\) are trainable weights. The authors say that projecting the weights into a positive subspace ensures that "features generated by the LLM do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning." I am not convinced I understand why negative weights would be problematic here – could a rule legitimately be indicative of **not** belonging to a class? How would allowing negative weights affect interpretability or performance? What alternatives might exist (e.g., using a bias term or regularization)?
Answer:
In **FeatLLM** every rule that the language model writes is turned into a *binary* feature  

$$
\mathbf{z}^{i}_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{R},
$$  

where $R$ is the number of rules extracted for class $k$ and $((z^{i}_{k})_{r}=1)$ iff sample $i$ satisfies the $r$‑th rule of that class.  The downstream predictor is a linear model without a bias term  

$$
\text{logit}^{i}_{k}= \max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)\cdot \mathbf{z}^{i}_{k},
\qquad 
\mathbf{p}^{i}= \operatorname{softmax}\big(\text{logit}^{i}_{1},\,dots ,\,\text{logit}^{i}_{c}\big),
$$  

where $\max(\mathbf{w}_{k},0)$ denotes element‑wise ReLU applied to the trainable weight vector $\mathbf{w}_{k}$.  
The authors explicitly state that “projecting these weights into a positive subspace ensures that the features generated by the LLM do **not** reduce the likelihood of a class during learning.’  

### Why are only non‑negative contributions allowed?

A rule produced for class $k$ is meant to describe **positive evidence** that a sample belongs to that class – e.g., “age $> 60$", “blood pressure $\ge 140 mmHg$”. The corresponding binary feature is therefore an indicator *that such evidence holds*. If we permitted $\mathbf{w}_{k,r}<0$, then whenever the condition were true it would *decrease* $\text{logit}^{i}_{k}$. In other words a feature intended as “support” would become a “penalty”, which contradicts the semantic role given by the prompt (“derive decisive rules … distinguish classes”).  

Because each rule lives inside its own “class‑specific” bucket, any lack of support is already encoded implicitly: when a rule is **not** satisfied its feature value is 0, contributing nothing to $\text{logit}^{i}_{k}$. Thus negativity is unnecessary – zero already represents “no positive evidence”. If one really wants an “anti‑evidence” clause such as “if chest pain = No then NOT heart disease”, it should be expressed explicitly as another rule (“chest pain = No”) whose binary indicator belongs either to the opposite‐class set or to its own dedicated bucket. In this way every learned weight can stay non‑negative while still capturing both sides of a relationship.  

### What would happen if negative weights were allowed?

1. **Interpretability loss** – With unrestricted signs the magnitude of $w_{k,r}$ no longer has an immediate meaning. A large positive weight still signals an important supportive rule, but a large *negative* weight signals an important *contra*-evidence rule hidden inside a feature labelled as “support”. To understand what drives predictions one would have to examine both sign and value, making it harder for users (especially domain experts) to read off which conditions favor a class.  

2. **Higher variance in low‑shot regimes** – In the few‑shot setting only a handful of labeled examples guide learning. Gradient descent can easily assign strong negative values to rules that happen, by chance, to co‑occur with the opposite label in those few examples (spurious correlations). Since such values actively suppress logits they can destabilise training and lead to overly confident yet wrong predictions. Constraining all coefficients to be non‑negative halves the hypothesis space and acts as a strong inductive bias aligned with how the features were constructed, thereby reducing overfitting.  

3\. **Monotonicity guarantees** – The ReLU projection guarantees **monotonicity**: adding any satisfied rule cannot lower the score for its own class. This property matches intuition from many interpretable models such as weighted voting schemes or additive rule ensembles (RuleFit, Bayesian Rule Lists), where each active clause contributes positively toward its target label.  

The paper’s ablation study (“‑Tuning”) shows degradation when this component is removed, confirming that letting arbitrary signs creep in harms performance on average across datasets.  

### Could other design choices achieve similar goals?

Yes. One could keep unrestricted weights but add mechanisms that mitigate their drawbacks:

* **Bias terms** – Adding a per‑class intercept $b_{k}$ would allow systematic upward or downward shifts without needing any particular feature to act negatively. However this does not solve sign ambiguity inside $\mathbf{w}_{k}$; it merely supplies a global offset.  
* **Explicit complementary features** – For every generated clause one could also create its logical negation (“not …”) as an extra binary variable. Then every coefficient may safely stay non‑negative because both directions are represented explicitly.  
* **Regularisation enforcing positivity** – Instead of hard clipping via ReLU one might penalise negative entries with an extra term $\lambda\,\sum_{r}\max(-w_{k,r},0)$. This needs an extra hyper‑parameter $\lambda$ and complicates optimisation—something the authors deliberately avoid given their emphasis on minimal tuning.  
* **Constrained optimisation** – Solving $\min_{\mathbf w\,\ge\,0}\mathcal L(\mathbf w)$ directly (e.g., using projected gradient descent) yields exactly the same effect as applying ReLU after each update; however writing it explicitly adds implementation overhead while offering no practical benefit beyond what their simple $\max(\cdot ,0)$ already provides.  

In practice these alternatives trade simplicity against additional hyper‑parameters or larger prompt lengths (the extra complementary features double dimensionality). The authors chose hard non‑negativity because it preserves **interpretability** (weights become “importance scores”), keeps **training stable** in low‑shot contexts, and incurs negligible computational cost—exactly what FeatLLM aims at: an inexpensive downstream linear model whose parameters can be learned from very few examples without sacrificing clarity about what each LLM‑derived rule contributes.  

### Summary

FeatLLM treats every LLM‑generated clause as *positive evidence* for its associated class; consequently it constrains the downstream linear coefficients to be non‑negative by applying an elementwise ReLU projection $\max(\mathbf w_{k},0)$. This design guarantees that satisfying any extracted rule can only increase—or leave unchanged—the logit for its own class, preserving a clear interpretation (“how much does this condition help?”), limiting variance when data are scarce, and preventing accidental penalisation caused by spurious correlations in few‑shot training data. Allowing negatives would break this semantic alignment, make explanations harder to read and increase susceptibility to overfitting; alternatives exist (bias terms, complementary rules, regularisation), but all introduce extra complexity while offering little practical advantage over the simple positivity constraint adopted in the paper.[NO SHADOW POPUP SELF]

### How are natural‑language rules parsed into executable Python code in FeatLLM?
Question:
The paper mentions that after extracting rules from the LLM (Section 3.2), they feed a prompt containing function signatures and rule descriptions back to the same LLM to obtain Python code that implements those rules, which is then executed via ``exec()``. I am unclear about several details:

1. What does the prompt look like exactly (e.g., examples provided)?
2. How does the system guarantee syntactically correct Python code despite possible variations like using "greater than" instead of ">"?
3. What fallback mechanisms are used if ``exec()`` raises an exception?
4. Could this approach be replaced by a deterministic parser instead of re‑using an LLM?

Any concrete examples would help me understand this step.
Answer:
**Answer**

In *FeatLLM* the transition from a natural‑language description of a rule (“age greater than 60 …”) to an executable Boolean test is performed by a second‑stage call to the same large language model that was used for rule extraction.  
The authors deliberately keep this step very small‑scale – only a handful of functions have to be generated per trial (10 rules per class × c classes), so it is feasible to ask the LLM itself to produce clean Python source code rather than hand‑crafting a dedicated parser.

---

### The prompt that drives code generation  

After the first stage has produced a list of textual rules (the *output instruction* part of Figure 2 guarantees that every rule follows a simple template such as “*feature* ≥ *value* AND *feature* < *value*”), those strings are fed back into the model together with an explicit coding template.  
A typical prompt looks like this (the exact wording can be found in Appendix A.4, Fig.~4):

```
You are given several natural‑language conditions describing when an instance belongs
to class “Yes”. Write a separate Python function for each condition.
• Each function must be named `rule_yes_i(sample)` where i starts at 1.
• `sample` is a dict that maps feature names (as strings) to their values.
• Return True iff the condition holds, otherwise return False.
• Use only plain Python operators (> , >= , < , <= , == , != ) and logical
  keywords `and`, `or`. Do NOT import any library.
• Do NOT write any extra text outside the function definition.

Here are two examples you must follow:

Condition: "age greater than 60 AND cholesterol $\le$ 200"
Function:
def rule_yes_1(sample):
    return sample["age"] > 60 and sample["cholesterol"] <= 200

Condition: "blood_pressure $\ge$ 140 OR ejection_fraction < 30"
Function:
def rule_yes_2(sample):
    return sample["blood_pressure"] >= 140 or sample["ejection_fraction"] < 30

--- End of examples ---

Now write functions for the following conditions:
1) {rule_1}
2) {rule_2}
…
```

The placeholders `{rule_j}` are filled with the raw strings that came out of the first query (“age greater than 60 …”, “blood pressure $\ge$ 140 …”). Because the request contains concrete *few‑shot examples* showing exactly how a textual description should be turned into Python, the model reliably reproduces that pattern even when it sees synonyms such as “greater than”, “over”, “more than”, etc.; it simply maps them onto `>` during generation.

---

### Why does this yield syntactically correct Python?

Two design choices make syntax errors extremely unlikely:

1. **Strict response format** – The prompt tells the model *exactly* what it may output (a single `def …` block surrounded by no extra prose). The few‑shot examples reinforce this constraint, so stray explanations or malformed indentation are rarely emitted.

2. **Post‑generation sanity check** – After receiving the generated source string the implementation calls `compile(code_str,'<string>','exec')`. This step catches any `SyntaxError` before execution. If compilation fails, the system automatically re‑issues the request adding a short note such as “Your previous answer contained a syntax error; please fix it”. In practice this one‑round re‑prompt solves almost all problems caused by unusual tokenisation (“greaterthan” → “>”) because the model has already seen many examples where it must replace English relational words with their symbolic counterparts.

Because every rule ends up inside its own tiny function (`def …:`), even an isolated mistake does not corrupt other rules; faulty functions are simply dropped from the feature set while preserving all valid ones.

---

### What happens when `exec()` still raises an exception?

The authors anticipate three kinds of runtime failures when applying a freshly generated function to a data row:

* **NameError / KeyError** – The generated code refers to a column name that does not exist in the supplied dataset (e.g., because a feature was omitted during bagging). The wrapper catches this exception and substitutes `False` for that rule on that instance.
* **TypeError** – A comparison between incompatible types occurs (e.g., comparing a string field with an integer constant). Again the wrapper treats this as “rule not satisfied”.
* **General RuntimeError** – Any other unexpected exception results in discarding that particular function after logging a warning.

All these safeguards are lightweight enough that they do not affect overall latency; only functional rules survive into the binary feature matrix $\,\mathbf{z}_k^i$. Consequently an occasional malformed piece does not cripple training or inference – it merely contributes a zero column which later receives weight 0 in Eq.~(2).

---

### Could we replace this second LLM call by a deterministic parser?

In principle yes. One could define an explicit grammar such as  

$$
\text{cond} \;\;\rightarrow\;\;\text{feature}\ \text{op}\ \text{value}\ \mid\ (\text{cond})\ \text{logic}\ (\text{cond}),
$$  

where $\displaystyle \text{op}\in\{\!>,\,\ge,\,<,\le,\neq,\==\,\}$ and $\displaystyle \text{logic}\in\{\text{and},\,\text{or}\}$. A hand‑crafted lexer would map lexical variants (“greater than”, “over”, “≥”) onto their symbolic counterparts, substitute categorical equality expressed as “is one of” → membership tests, and finally emit simple lambda functions or `eval`‑ready strings.

However there are two practical obstacles that motivated the authors’ design choice:

1. **Lexical variety across datasets** – Rule extraction happens on dozens of domains (medical records, financial tables, synthetic sequences). Each domain brings its own phrasing (“has‑a value higher than”, “exceeds”, “≤ the threshold”). Exhaustively enumerating every synonym quickly becomes unmanageable; any missed pattern leads either to discarded rules or—in worst case—to incorrectly parsed conditions.
2. **Low engineering cost vs. robustness trade‑off** – Writing a reliable parser demands substantial effort (grammar design, tokenisation tuned for numeric vs categorical values, handling missing values). By delegating translation to an LLM already fine‑tuned on massive amounts of source code, *FeatLLM* obtains comparable robustness while keeping implementation succinct (the entire parsing stage consists essentially of two templated prompts).

That said nothing in principle prevents swapping out the second LLM query for a deterministic pipeline once you have amassed enough linguistic coverage for your target domain(s). Such a parser would guarantee zero syntax errors *by construction*, possibly improve reproducibility across API versions, and reduce dependence on external API calls during training time. For research prototypes where flexibility across many heterogeneous tabular tasks matters more than absolute determinism, however, leveraging an LLM remains an elegant shortcut—exactly what the authors exploit in Sections 3.2–3.3 and Appendix A.5–A.7.

---

### Summary  

*FeatLLM* turns textual rules into binary features by feeding them back into GPT‑3.5 together with a tiny coding template and two illustrative examples. The template forces an unambiguous output format; after generation each snippet is compiled (`compile`) before being handed off to `exec`. If compilation or runtime execution fails, the offending rule is quietly ignored while other correctly compiled functions continue to contribute features. Although one could replace this step with a custom lexical parser built around an explicitly defined grammar, doing so would require extensive hand‑crafting for every linguistic variant encountered across domains—a cost that outweighs its benefits given how reliably GPT‑3.5 produces short pieces of well‑formed Python when guided by precise prompts. This pragmatic combination of constrained prompting plus lightweight sanity checks explains why *FeatLLM* can safely execute automatically generated code without sacrificing stability or performance.

### "Bagging" in FeatLLM – is it applied to features, instances, or both?
Question:
In Section 3.2 under "Ensembling with bagging" the authors state that "bagging helps reduce it [prompt size]" and that they "select a subset of features or instances for each trial." I am uncertain whether:

* They randomly sample a subset of *columns* (features) from the original table before constructing the prompt;
* They randomly sample a subset of *training rows* (instances) used as demonstrations;
* Or both are performed simultaneously.

Could someone clarify exactly what is being bagged at each trial and how this choice interacts with limited prompt windows?
Answer:
**Short answer:** In every ensemble‐member (“trial”) Feat LLM randomly samples *either* a subset of the table’s columns **or** a subset of the few‑shot rows that are placed in the prompt – it can do both, but which one is active is a configurable hyper‑parameter. The purpose is simply to shrink the prompt so that it stays inside the LLM’s context window; repeating this process many times lets the final model see all columns and all examples across trials even though each individual query only sees a part of them.

---

### What “bagging” means in Feat LLM

Section 3‑2 (“Ensembling with bagging”) says:

> “To utilize the diversity… we adopt **bagging to select a subset of **features** or **instances** for each trial… By appropriately adjusting *the number of features* and *samples* chosen in bagging, the challenge of excessive prompt size is mitigated.”

The sentence explicitly mentions **features** *or* **instances**. Earlier in the same subsection the authors already introduced two ways that tokens accumulate in a query:

1. **Feature descriptions** – every column name together with its natural‑language definition is listed under “Basic information description”.
2. **Few‑shot demonstrations** – each training row is serialized as ``f₁​ is … f_d​ is … Answer:`` …

If neither set is trimmed, a dataset with dozens or hundreds of attributes easily exceeds the model’s maximum token limit (e.g., GPT‑3.5‑turbo’s $\,≈\,4K$ tokens). Therefore before each call they perform **random sub‑sampling**:

* **Feature‑bagging** – draw a random subset $F_{t}\subseteq F$ (e.g., $\sqrt d$ columns as in classic random forests). Only those column names and definitions appear in the prompt, so all generated rules will involve only this reduced vocabulary.
* **Instance‑bagging** – draw a random subset $\mathcal D_{t}\subseteq\mathcal D_{\text{few-shot}}$ of the demonstration rows and serialize only these rows. The order of these rows is also shuffled because order affects LLM responses.

Both operations are independent knobs; an experiment may enable just one (the most restrictive one for its data) or both simultaneously if both feature‑side and instance‑side token consumption threaten the context window.

### How this solves the prompt‑size problem

The total token budget $L$ can be approximated as  

$$
L \approx 
   \underbrace{|F_{t}|\cdot\tok(f)}_{\text{feature block}} +
   \underbrace{|\,\mathcal D_{t}\,|\cdot\tok(\text{row})}_{\text{demo block}} +
   \text{fixed tokens from task description and instructions}.
$$

Reducing either $|F_{t}|$ or $|\mathcal D_{t}|$ shrinks $L$ linearly. By controlling these two numbers per trial, Feat LLM guarantees that every individual query fits comfortably inside the model’s context window even on datasets like *Communities* or *Myocardial* where $d>100$.

### Why an ensemble is still effective

Bagged trials produce diverse rule sets because different subsets expose different parts of the data to the LLM:

* If some important column was omitted from trial 1’s feature set, it will almost certainly appear in another trial where it can contribute rules.
* Likewise, when demonstrations are sub‑sampled, variation in ordering combined with temperature‑driven randomness yields alternative rule formulations even for identical columns.

After rules have been parsed into binary “does this sample satisfy rule $r$?” features, every trial learns a non‑negative weight vector $\mathbf w[t]$. The final prediction averages class probabilities over all trials *(Eq.~(2))*​.​ This aggregation mirrors classical bagged ensembles such as Random Forests—individual weak learners may miss parts of the signal, but their mean recovers most information while also reducing variance.

### Practical configuration

The authors do not lock a single recipe in the main text; instead they expose two hyper‑parameters—how many columns and how many demonstrations to draw per trial—and show (§4 “Hyper‑parameter analysis”) that performance improves as more ensembles are added until saturation ($≈\,20$ trials). In their experiments on datasets with up to 120+ attributes they primarily rely on **feature‑bagging**, because even 4–8 demonstration rows fit easily within the window whereas listing all column names does not. When running on very small tables where token pressure comes mainly from demonstrations (e.g., when deliberately using many shots), they switch on **instance‑bagging** instead.

---

#### Bottom line

- **Feature bagging** = random selection of a column subset → reduces tokens spent describing features.
- **Instance bagging** = random selection (and shuffling) of few‑shot rows → reduces tokens spent serializing demonstrations.
- Either operation can be turned on alone or together; their sole purpose is to keep each LLM call under the context‐window limit while an ensemble over many such calls restores full coverage of all columns and all training examples.
- This design lets Feat LLM reap classical bagging benefits—robustness through diversity—while directly addressing practical constraint that LLM prompts cannot be arbitrarily long.​

### What is the intuition behind splitting LLM reasoning into two steps (causal inference then rule deduction)?
Question:
"Reasoning instruction" in Section 3.1 describes a two‑step process:

1️⃣ Infer causal relationships between features and task description **without** looking at example demonstrations;
2️⃣ Use those inferred relationships plus few‑shot examples to deduce concrete rules.

Why would separating these steps improve rule quality compared with asking the LLM to directly output rules given all information at once? Are there empirical studies supporting this claim, or could this separation sometimes lead to contradictory reasoning?

Answer:
The two‑step “reasoning instruction’’ used by FeatLLM is motivated by the way a human expert solves a new tabular problem.  
First we ask the LLM to **declare what it already knows** about the relationship between each column and the target (“causal tendency’’). This does not involve any concrete training examples; it only draws on the world‑knowledge stored in the model (e.g. “older patients have higher risk of heart disease’’). In the second step we let the model **refine those hypotheses** by looking at the few labelled rows that are supplied as demonstrations, and from this combination we ask it to express explicit decision rules (e.g. “$\text{if } age > 65 \text{ and } cholesterol > 200 \text{ then class}= \text{positive}$”).  

Why does this help compared with giving the LLM all information at once?  

* **Separating prior knowledge from noisy data** – With only a handful of labeled rows it is easy for a model to infer spurious correlations (e.g. a column that happens to be high only because of random sampling). By asking the model first to filter out columns that are *a priori* irrelevant, we constrain its attention to those features that have a plausible causal link to the task. When the demonstrations are later consulted, they can only adjust or instantiate rules for this reduced set of candidates. The paper’s ablation study (Table 4 “‑Reasoning’’) shows that removing Step‑1 degrades average AUC by several points, especially in the 2‑shot and 4‑shot regimes, confirming that causal pre‑filtering contributes most when data are scarce.  

* **Reducing combinatorial explosion of rule search** – A naïve prompt that asks “list ten rules for each class given all columns and examples’’ forces the LLM simultaneously to select relevant features **and** craft logical conditions for each feature type. This dual burden increases the chance of malformed or overly generic rules, as evidenced by the higher incidence of parsing errors reported in Section 4.2 when Step‑1 is omitted.  

* **Mitigating contradictions** – If an LLM’s internal priors conflict with patterns present in the few‑shot data (for instance, a dataset where a “non‑causal” feature happens to be highly predictive), Step‑1 may bias rule generation away from that feature. The authors acknowledge this possibility: they note that when columns highly correlated with the target but unrelated causally are introduced (the “spurious correlation’’ experiment on Heart + Adult‐columns), FeatLLM still learns modestly from them because Step‑2 can still incorporate them if they appear consistently in the demonstrations. Moreover, their ensemble with bagging (Section 3.3) dilutes any single contradictory reasoning trace—different runs may keep or discard a disputed feature, and averaging over many models yields a robust final predictor.  

* **Alignment with chain‑of‑thought literature** – The split mirrors the chain‑of‑thought prompting strategy (Wei et al., 2022) where intermediate reasoning steps improve downstream generation quality. In FeatLLM these steps are explicitly “causal inference’’ followed by “rule deduction”, which is exactly what the authors refer to when they say “the LLM’s inference process is divided into two steps”.  

Empirically, beyond the ablation mentioned above, Figure 5 (“Effect of spurious correlations’’) demonstrates that FeatLLM’s performance declines far less than baseline methods as irrelevant columns are added, indicating that its early causal filter indeed shields rule extraction from misleading data cues. The authors also report that across 13 datasets FeatLLM consistently ranks first or second, while variants without Step‑1 fall behind by an average of $\approx 10\%$ relative AUC.

In summary, splitting reasoning into a causal‐knowledge stage followed by a data‑driven rule synthesis stage gives the LLM a clean “prior + evidence” template: first prune away irrelevant features using world knowledge, then specialize rules using scarce examples. The design yields higher‑quality rules as shown by ablations and robustness experiments, while any occasional contradictions between prior knowledge and data are softened through ensemble averaging and weight tuning rather than being catastrophically propagated.
