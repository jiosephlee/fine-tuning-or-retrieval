\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Beyond Butterflies: The Information Transmission View of Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning  

*How visualizing sparse orthogonal factorizations as flows on a grid graph reshapes our thinking about efficient model adaptation – and why the next frontier may lie in network topologies beyond butterflies.*

---

## 1. Why finetune at all?

Foundation models such as GPT‑3, Vision Transformers or Stable Diffusion have billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is no longer a routine research task—​the compute budget far exceeds what most labs can afford. Yet we still need these massive models to solve new downstream problems (sentiment analysis, image classification, style‑controlled generation, …).  

The prevailing solution is **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)**: keep the pretrained weights frozen and train only a tiny set of extra parameters. Popular PEFT schemes include adapters, prompt tuning, LoRA’s low‑rank updates, and **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—the latter modifies the whole weight matrix by left‑multiplying an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). OFT enjoys strong generalization because orthogonal transforms preserve angles between neurons and keep the spectral norm unchanged.

The catch? A full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix naively requires \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) trainable entries—a huge overhead for each layer. The original OFT sidestepped this by forcing \(\mathbf{R}\) to be block‑diagonal. While that cuts parameters dramatically, it also **cripples expressivity**: different blocks never interact, which limits the kinds of linear transforms that can be represented.

Is there a way to keep \(\mathbf{R}\) dense (so we retain full expressive power) while still using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) parameters? The answer lies in looking at orthogonal matrices through a **graph‑theoretic lens**.

---

## 2. From matrices to information flows

Consider a dense square matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Each non‑zero entry \(R_{ij}\) indicates that information can travel from input node \(j\) to output node \(i\). If we factor \(\mathbf{R}\) as a product of \(m\) sparse matrices,
\[
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{m}\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\cdots \mathbf{B}_{1},
\]
then each factor \(\mathbf{B}_{k}\) can be interpreted as a **layer** of a directed graph that connects one set of \(d\) nodes (level \(k-1\)) to another set of \(d\) nodes (level \(k\)). Stacking the factors yields a **grid graph** of size \(d\times(m+1)\): source nodes on level 0 flow through successive edge sets until they reach level \(m\).

Two design goals emerge naturally:

1. **Dense connectivity** – every source must have at least one path to every sink so that the final product \(\mathbf{R}\) is fully dense.
2. **Minimal free edges** – we want as few *trainable* edges as possible because each edge corresponds to a learnable parameter (orthogonality will further restrict the degrees of freedom).

In this picture, block‑diagonal OFT looks like a set of completely disconnected sub‑graphs: each block only talks to itself across all levels. No path ever crosses block boundaries, so dense connectivity is lost unless you make each block almost as large as the full matrix—exactly what defeats the purpose of parameter reduction.

---

## 3. Butterfly graphs – an elegant solution

The **Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT)** introduced a recursive “butterfly” communication pattern that spreads information across all entries in just \(\log_2 d\) stages. Translating this into our grid graph:

- Each stage corresponds to one **butterfly factor** \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\), where \(k\) denotes the block size inside the factor.
- The non‑zero pattern couples pairs of nodes that are half‑a‑range apart, then quarter‑a‑range apart in the next stage, and so on—exactly mirroring how FFT blends frequencies.

Mathematically we can construct a **butterfly matrix** \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) as
\[
\mathbf{B}(d)= \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d/2)\,\dots\,\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2),
\]
where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is block‑diagonal with blocks that are themselves sparse butterfly factors
\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf{d}_{i}\in \mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

If every factor is constrained to be orthogonal—e.g., by Cayley parametrizing each \(2\times 2\) block—the product \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) remains orthogonal while being specified by only \(\mathcal O(d\log d)\) learnable scalars (the elements inside the vectors $\mathbf d_i$). This is precisely what **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** does:

- Replace OFT’s single orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ with $\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}$, where $m$ is the number of butterfly stages and $b$ controls block size.
- When $m=1$ we recover the original block‑diagonal OFT; when $b=d$ we recover full OFT.
- For $b=2$ and $m=\log_2 d$ we obtain an extremely compact representation with just $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ parameters while still yielding a *dense* orthogonal transform.

Thus the butterfly graph satisfies our two desiderata perfectly: every source reaches every sink after $\log d$ hops (dense connectivity), yet each hop introduces only $d$ fixed edges plus a handful of trainable weights (minimal free edges).

---

## 4. Why does this matter for finetuning?

### 4.1 Better expressivity with fewer weights  
The butterfly structure can reproduce classic linear transforms—FFT, Hadamard, cosine/sine transforms—by simply picking appropriate values for its trainable entries. Empirically, BOFT matches or exceeds the performance of LoRA and standard OFT across vision transformers, large language models and diffusion models while using orders of magnitude fewer parameters per layer.

### 4.2 Preserved spectral properties  
Because BOFT multiplies by an orthogonal matrix from the left,
\[
\widetilde{\mathbf W}= (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top},
\]
the singular values of $\widetilde{\mathbf W}$ are exactly those of $\mathbf W^{0}$. The spectral norm never drifts—a property that stabilizes training and improves generalization compared with additive low‑rank updates.

### 4.3 Simple initialization & regularization  
All butterfly components are initialized as identities (zero skew‑symmetric matrices under Cayley transform), guaranteeing zero change at step 0—a common trick for safe finetuning. BOFT also introduces a *multiplicative dropout*: randomly replace whole butterfly stages or individual diagonal blocks by identities during training – an analogue of standard dropout that respects multiplicative updates.

---

## 5. Beyond butterflies: other network topologies?

The information transmission view opens up an entire design space: any sparse factorization whose induced grid graph satisfies dense connectivity can serve as an orthogonal parameterization. Butterflies are just one point on this spectrum.

### 5.1 Hypercube routing  
A $k$‑dimensional hypercube connects each node to $k$ neighbours differing in exactly one bit position. Traversing $\log_2 d$ dimensions ensures any node can reach any other via a unique shortest path. Factorizing an orthogonal matrix into hypercube layers would again require only $\mathcal O(d\,k)$ parameters ($k=\log_2 d$), but the pattern of connections differs from butterflies and may be more suitable for tasks where binary partitioning aligns with data structure.

### 5.2 Benes or Clos networks  
Benes networks are rearrangeable non‐blocking switching fabrics popular in packet routing hardware. They consist of $\log_2 d$ stages with $2\times 2$ switches much like butterflies but interleaved with permutations that allow any permutation to be realized exactly. Embedding orthogonal $2\times 2$ gates within such stages could yield *exact* universality with $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ params while preserving strong regularity.

### 5​.​ Expander graphs  
Sparse expander graphs have excellent mixing properties: random walks quickly become uniform over nodes despite having only constant degree per node. If we could devise an *expander‐based factorization* where each stage corresponds to applying an adjacency‐like operator followed by orthogonalisation within small blocks, we might achieve even better trade–offs between depth (number of stages) and parameter count.

### 5​.​ Multi‐scale wavelet trees  
Wavelet transforms recursively split signals into coarse and detail components using filter banks—conceptually similar to butterflies but with different scaling matrices per level. A wavelet-inspired factorization could capture locality priors valuable for vision tasks while still delivering $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ scalability.

All these topologies share two common requirements:
1. Each stage must be *orthogonalizable* (e.g., via Cayley transforms or direct rotation parametrizations).
2. The composition must guarantee dense overall connectivity so that any pairwise neuron interaction can be expressed after sufficient stages.

Designing such factorizations amounts to choosing an underlying **communication graph** before training begins—a decision that injects an inductive bias tailored to specific domains (e.g., hypercubic connections for binary data structures or wavelet trees for images).

## 6. Empirical takeaways from BOFT

| Model family | Task | Compared method | Params per layer | Relative performance |
|--------------|------|-----------------|------------------|---------------------|
| Vision Transformer | ImageNet classification | LoRA | $\sim0.02\,d^2$ | +1–2 % top‑1 |
| LLaMA 7B | Zero‑shot QA | Full finetune | full | +0.x % |
| Stable Diffusion | Text→image style control | OFT block‐diag | $bd$ | +3 % FID improvement |

*(Numbers are illustrative; see the paper for exact figures.)*  

Key observations:

- **Parameter efficiency** scales linearly with depth ($m$) and block size ($b$): BOFT($m$, $b$) uses roughly $\frac12(b-1)d\,m$ trainable scalars.
- Even with very small blocks ($b=2$, many stages), BOFT attains comparable or better accuracy than LoRA despite using far fewer parameters.
- Because BOFT’s orthogonal transform can be *merged* into the frozen weight after training, there is no inference overhead – unlike adapter or prefix approaches that add extra layers or tokens at test time.

## 7. Looking forward

The butterfly factorization demonstrated that **sparse orthogonal factorizations can be both expressive and cheap** when viewed as information highways over a grid graph. By reframing finetuning architecture design as a network topology problem we gain:

- A principled way to count required trainable edges.
- A clear path toward *designer* inductive biases via choice of communication pattern.
- The possibility of borrowing decades of research from parallel computing and networking to improve machine learning workflows.

Future research directions include:

1️⃣ **Systematic search over topology families** – automated methods could rank hypercubes, Benes nets, expanders etc., according to criteria such as parameter count, depth, ease of orthogonalisation.

2️⃣ **Hybrid factorizations** – mixing butterfly layers with low‑rank updates could combine angular preservation with rank flexibility seen in LoRA-like methods.

3️⃣ **Dynamic routing** – learning not only continuous rotation angles but also which edges become active per task may lead to adaptive sparsity patterns without sacrificing theoretical guarantees on connectivity.

4️⃣ **Hardware acceleration** – because many candidate topologies consist solely of small block operations (e.g., $2\times 2$, $4\times 4$ rotations), they map well onto GPUs/TPUs or even specialized ASICs designed for fast linear transforms.

In short, viewing parameter‐efficient finetuning through an *information transmission* lens turns what used to be an obscure matrix algebra problem into something more intuitive—and opens up fresh avenues inspired by centuries-old networking theory.

---

## 8️⃣ Conclusion

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) showed us how clever factorisations—drawn from FFT’s butterfly pattern—can compress a full orthogonal update down to $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ learnable numbers while preserving dense expressivity and stability benefits inherited from OFT. More importantly, the **graph view** reveals that butterflies are merely one point in a broader design space defined by how information travels across layers.

By treating sparsity patterns as communication topologies we inherit powerful insights from computer networking: there may exist more efficient “highways” than butterflies for specific downstream domains. Exploring those alternatives could push parameter‑efficient adaptation even further—making it feasible today’s massive foundation models truly universal tools rather than siloed specialists.

Stay tuned: the next generation of PEFT may well be built not on butterflies alone but on whatever graph best carries knowledge from pretraining into your target task!
