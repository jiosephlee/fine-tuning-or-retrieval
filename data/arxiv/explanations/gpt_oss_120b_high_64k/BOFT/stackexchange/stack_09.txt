\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Theorem 1 claims BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size – what does “expressive” mean here?
Question:
"Expressivity" seems related to covering more elements of the orthogonal group $
 O(d)$. The theorem states that concatenating products like $\bm B_{d-1,1}(d) \bm B^{\top}_{d-1,2}(d) \dots$ can approximate any orthogonal matrix. Could someone explain intuitively why alternating forward and transposed butterfly factors expands the reachable subspace compared to just one block‑diagonal OFT?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the paper “Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization’’ *expressive* (or *expressivity*) is used as a shorthand for **the size of the hypothesis class** that a parametrisation can represent.  
For Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the hypothesis class is  

$$
\mathcal{O}_{\text{OFT}}(b)=
      \Bigl\{
        \operatorname{diag}(R_{1},\ldots ,R_{r})
        \;\big|\;
        R_{i}\in O(b),\, br=d
      \Bigr\rbrace,
$$  

i.e.\, all $d\times d$\! orthogonal matrices that are **block‑diagonal** with blocks of size $b$.  
Because every column lives inside one fixed block, none of the parameters can couple coordinates that belong to different blocks. Consequently many orthogonal transformations – for instance the Fourier transform or any rotation that mixes the first half of the vector with the second half – are completely out of reach for OFT no matter how we choose the values inside each $R_i$.  

---

### What BOFT does differently

BOFT replaces one single block‑diagonal factor by a product of *butterfly* factors  

$$
R(m,b)=
   \tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,1)}\,%
   \tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,2)}\,%
   \cdots%
   \tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,m)},
   \qquad
   \tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in O(d),
$$  

where each $\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is itself sparse: it consists of many $2\times 2$
(or $2b\times 2b$) orthogonal “rotations’’ placed according to a **butterfly pattern**.  
When $m=1$ this product collapses to one factor and we recover exactly the block‑diagonal OFT ($\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,1)}=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},…,R_{r})$).  

For $m>1$, however, the situation changes dramatically because:

| step | sparsity pattern | what new connections appear |
|------|------------------|-----------------------------|
| **first factor** ($\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,1)}$) | connects each index only with its partner dictated by the first butterfly level | mixes pairs inside small sub‑blocks |
| **second factor** ($(\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,2)})^{!\top}$) | uses a *different* pairing – essentially the transpose permutes which indices are paired | creates length‑two paths that link indices belonging to *different* first‑level blocks |
| **subsequent factors** | keep reshuffling pairings at larger scales | after $k=\log_2 d$ alternations every source node has a multi‑hop path to every destination node |

In graph terms (see Fig.~3 of the paper) each factor is a set of directed edges from level~$i$ to level~$i+1$. A *forward* butterfly uses edges $(j,\pi(j))$; its transpose uses edges $(j,\pi^{-1}(j))$. By alternating them we obtain two complementary edge sets so that **the union of all paths covers every source–target pair**. Multiplying the corresponding sparse matrices implements exactly those multi‑hop paths: whenever there exists at least one path from input node~$u$ to output node~$v$, the entry $(v,u)$ in the product becomes non‑zero (and we can adjust its value by tuning the rotation angles inside each $2\times 2$ block).

Hence even though every individual factor has only $O(d)$ trainable parameters, their product becomes *dense*: after enough alternating layers we can realise any desired pattern of non‑zero entries. Theorem 1 formalises this intuition by stating that  

$$
R^{G}=B_{d-1,1}\,
      B_{d-1,2}^{!\top}\,
      \dots\
      B_{0,1}\,
      B_{0,2}^{!\top}
$$  

with appropriately chosen butterfly matrices $B_{i,j}$ can approximate **every** element of the full orthogonal group $O(d)$. In other words  

> The set  
> $$ 
> \mathcal{O}_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)
>   = 
>   \Bigl\bigr{
>        \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)}
>        \;\big|\;
>        (\tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^\top
>        \tilde B^{\,b}_{(d,i)} = I_d
>     }\!
> $$  
> strictly contains $\mathcal{O}_{\text{OFT}}(b)$ whenever $m>1$.  

---

### Why alternating *forward* and *transpose* matters

If we were to stack several *forward* butterfly factors only (no transpose), all those factors share exactly the same pairing at each scale – they merely rotate within already‑connected pairs. Their product would still be limited to a subgroup where some coordinates never meet each other. Introducing a transpose reverses the pairing at that layer; mathematically it multiplies by a permutation matrix on one side and its inverse on the other side. This reshuffling reassigns which coordinates meet in subsequent layers and therefore creates new routes that were impossible before. The alternation therefore breaks the symmetry imposed by a single orientation and gives us full freedom over permutations – ultimately allowing us to generate arbitrary orthogonal matrices.

---

### Putting it together

So “more expressive’’ means **a larger reachable set within $O(d)$**:

* **Block‑diagonal OFT** → only matrices whose sparsity pattern is fixed (block diagonal); many rotations are impossible.  
* **BOFT with one layer ($m=1$,~$b>2$)** → still block‑diagonal – no gain in expressivity.  
* **BOFT with two or more layers ($m≥2$,~$b≤d$)** → after alternating forward/transposed butterflies every pair of dimensions can influence each other; any orthogonal matrix can be approximated arbitrarily closely as shown by Theorem  ​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​.  

Because BOFT attains essentially full expressivity while using only $\mathcal O(d\bigr.\!\log\bigr.\! d)$ parameters (instead of $\mathcal O(d^{{}^{} }{}^{} )$, i.e.\@quadratic), it occupies a sweet spot between parameter efficiency and functional richness – exactly what the authors mean when they say BOFT is “more expressive than OFT with the same block size.’’
