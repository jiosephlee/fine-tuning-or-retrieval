\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What exactly is “multiplicative dropout” for BOFT and why can’t standard dropout be used?
Question:
"Standard dropout" zeroes out activations elementwise, but BOFT updates weights multiplicatively via an orthogonal matrix product. The authors propose dropping entire butterfly components ($p_1$%) and diagonal blocks within them ($p_2$%). How is this implemented in practice (e.g., masking which entries), and what intuition justifies these two separate dropout rates?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) the trainable parameters are not added to a layer but *multiply* the frozen weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ from the left:

$$
\mathbf z=\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf R(m,b)}_{\text{product of butterfly factors}}\;\mathbf W^{0}\bigr)^{!\top}\mathbf x .
$$

Because $\mathbf R(m,b)$ is constrained to be **orthogonal**, every factor in the product must itself be an orthogonal matrix. This restriction makes the usual element‑wise Dropout inappropriate – setting individual entries of an orthogonal matrix to zero destroys its orthogonality (the resulting matrix can no longer be written as $(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ with a skew‑symmetric $\mathbf Q$). What we need is a form of stochastic regularisation that *preserves* orthogonality while still forcing the model to rely on many different configurations of the trainable part. That is exactly what “multiplicative Dropout’’ does.

---

### How it works in practice

BOFT represents $\mathbf R(m,b)$ as a product of $m$ **butterfly components**

$$
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=
\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\,\underbrace{\mathbf B^{F}_{i,1},\dots,\mathbf B^{F}_{i,d/k}}_{\text{blocks of size }k=2^{\,m-i+1}}\bigr),
$$

where each $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ can be rearranged into a set of $d/k$ independent $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal blocks (a permutation of a $2\times 2$ butterfly factor).

Multiplicative Dropout proceeds in two independent steps for every forward pass:

1. **Component‑level mask ($p_{1}$)**  
   For each $i\in\{1,\dots,m\}$ draw a Bernoulli variable $c_{i}\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{1})$.  
   If $c_{i}=0$ we replace the whole factor by the identity,
   $$
   \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \leftarrow \mathbf I_{d},
   $$
   otherwise we keep the learned factor unchanged.

2. **Block‑level mask ($p_{2}$)**  
   Inside every factor that survives step 1 we further sample a mask over its internal diagonal blocks.  
   Suppose a factor contains $L=d/k$ blocks $\{\mathbf B^{F}_{i,\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}$. For each block draw 
   $b_{i,\ell}\sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(p_{2})$.  
   When $b_{i,\ell}=0$ we overwrite that particular block with an identity matrix of size $2b\times 2b$,
   $$
   \mathbf B^{F}_{i,\ell} \leftarrow \mathbf I_{2b}.
   $$  
   The other blocks remain as they were learned.

After both masks have been applied we obtain a *stochastic* version of the orthogonal matrix,

$$
\widehat{\mathbf R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}
     \Bigl(
        c_{i}\,\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} +
        (1-c_{i})\,\mathbf I_{d}
     \Bigr),
$$

with intra‑component identities inserted according to the second mask. The forward computation then uses this sampled matrix,

$$
\mathbf z=\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf R}(m,b)\,\mathbf W^{0}\bigr)^{!\top}\mathbf x .
$$

In code this is usually implemented by building binary tensors `comp_mask` of shape `(m,)` and `blk_mask` of shape `(m, L)` and performing cheap index assignments (`torch.eye(d)` or `torch.eye(2*b)` ) before multiplying all factors together. The overhead is negligible compared with the cost of one extra matrix multiplication per training step.

---

### Why ordinary Dropout does not work

Standard Dropout drops individual entries of a tensor independently:

```python
mask = torch.bernoulli((1-p)*torch.ones_like(tensor))
tensor = tensor * mask
```

If we applied this directly to one butterfly factor or to the final product $\mathbf R(m,b)$, most rows would lose their unit‑norm property and columns would cease to be mutually orthogonal. Consequently  

* The Cayley parametrisation would no longer guarantee an exact representation,  
* Gradients would push the network away from the orthogonal manifold,  
* The desirable property exploited by OFT—preserving pair‑wise angles between neurons—would be broken,  
* Training could become unstable because spectral norms could explode.

Multiplicative Dropout avoids these problems because every masked region is **replaced by an exact identity**, which is itself an orthogonal matrix. Replacing an entire component or a whole block therefore leaves the *product* still on the Stiefel manifold (the set of all orthogonal matrices). In other words, we are performing stochastic **subsampling** of valid transformations rather than corrupting them.

---

### Intuition behind two separate probabilities

The butterfly factorisation has a natural hierarchy:

* At the top level there are $m$ factors; each factor can be thought of as a “layer’’ that mixes information across increasingly large groups of dimensions.  
* Within each factor there are many small $2b\times 2b$ blocks; these enact local rotations between pairs (or groups when $b>1$) of coordinates.

The two masks therefore regularise two distinct degrees of freedom:

* **$p_{1}$** controls how often an *entire* mixing stage is skipped. This behaves like *stochastic depth*: sometimes the network sees fewer mixing stages, which forces it to learn representations that are robust even when some global exchanges are missing.  
* **$p_{2}$** controls how often *individual local rotations* are skipped while keeping the overall stage alive. This encourages robustness at a finer granularity – even if a particular pairwise exchange is missing, other exchanges must compensate.

Having two knobs lets practitioners trade off overall capacity against regularisation more flexibly than a single scalar rate could. Empirically one typically chooses $p_{1}<p_{2}$; dropping whole stages too aggressively would drastically reduce expressivity whereas dropping many small blocks still leaves enough structure for learning rich transformations.

---

### Summary

Multiplicative Dropout in BOFT is implemented by sampling two Bernoulli masks: one at the level of butterfly components ($p_{1}$) and one at the level of their internal diagonal blocks ($p_{2}$). The masked components/blocks are replaced with identity matrices so that every sampled transformation remains exactly orthogonal. Standard element‑wise Dropout cannot be used because it would destroy this orthogonality guarantee and break the Cayley parametrisation upon which OFT/BOFT rely. The two separate rates reflect BOFT’s hierarchical architecture—$p_{1}$ provides coarse‑grained stochastic depth across whole mixing stages while $p_{2}$ supplies fine‑grained regularisation within each stage—thereby yielding effective prevention of over‑fitting without sacrificing the core mathematical properties that make Orthogonal Finetuning attractive.)
