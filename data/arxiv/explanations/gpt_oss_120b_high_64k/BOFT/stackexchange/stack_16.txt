\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Can classic transforms like DFT be exactly represented by an orthogonal butterfly matrix despite real‑valued constraints?
Question:
Butterfly structures are known to implement FFT when complex numbers are allowed. Since BOFT restricts each sparse factor to be real orthogonal, can it still recover DFT exactly (perhaps up to scaling or permutations), or only approximate it? What modifications are needed if exact recovery is desired?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – a discrete Fourier transform can be represented *exactly* as a product of real‑orthogonal butterfly factors, provided that  

1. each “twiddle‑factor’’ multiplication is realised by a **$(2\times 2)$ rotation block** (the real‑valued analogue of multiplying by $e^{-i\theta}$), and  
2. we either work in the **real‑expanded space** $\mathbb{R}^{\,2N}$ (so that one complex dimension becomes two real dimensions) or we use **block size $b\succeq 2$** in BOFT so that those rotations live inside each block.  

If these conditions are met, no scaling or permutation error remains – the resulting matrix is exactly the unitary DFT written in a purely real orthogonal form. Without them BOFT can only approximate the DFT because ordinary “scalar’’ diagonals of $\pm 1$ cannot encode arbitrary phase rotations.  

Below is an intuitive walk‑through that connects the paper’s constructions to the classic FFT algorithm.

---

### 1. What part of FFT needs complex numbers?

The radix‑$(2)$ Cooley–Tukey FFT repeatedly applies two elementary operations:

* **Butterfly addition/subtraction**

$$
\begin{pmatrix}a\\ b\end{pmatrix}
   \;\mapsto\;
   \begin{pmatrix}a+b\\[4pt]a-b\end{pmatrix},
$$  

which is just a permutation followed by multiplication with the $(2\times 2)$ matrix  

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\[4pt]1&-1\end{bmatrix}.
$$  

After normalisation this matrix becomes  

$$
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\[4pt]1&-1\end{bmatrix},
$$  

an **orthogonal** matrix.

* **Twiddle‑factor multiplication**

$$
x \;\mapsto\; e^{-i\,\theta}\,x .
$$  

In complex form this is just a scalar on each entry, but in real coordinates it corresponds to a **$(2\times 2)$ rotation**

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\Re x\\[4pt]\Im x\end{pmatrix}
   \;\mapsto\;
   \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos\theta & -\,\sin\theta\\[4pt]
        \sin\theta & ~~\,\,cos\:~%
   \end{bmatrix}
   \begin{pmatrix}\Re x\\[4pt]\Im x\end{pmatrix},
$$  

which is again an orthogonal matrix.

Hence every stage of a radix‑$(2)$ FFT can be written as a product of sparse matrices whose nonzero pattern is exactly what BOFT calls a *butterfly factor* – two blocks of size $\tfrac{k}{2}\times\tfrac{k}{2}$, each placed on opposite corners (see Eq.~(~(1)) in the paper).

---

### 2. How BOFT’s factors match those operations

$$
\btilde B(d,k)=
      {\rm diag}\!\bigl(B^{F}_{1}(k),\,B^{F}_{d/k}(k)\bigr)
$$  

consists of many independent $k\times k$ “butterfly factors’’  

$$
B^{F}(k)=
   {\small
   \begin{bmatrix}
      {\rm diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & {\rm diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) \\[6pt]
      {\rm diag}(\mathbf d_{2}) & {\rm diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
   </array>}.
$$

* setting $(d_{1},d_{3},d_{2},d_{4})=(1,0,0,\pm 1)$ yields an addition/subtraction step,
