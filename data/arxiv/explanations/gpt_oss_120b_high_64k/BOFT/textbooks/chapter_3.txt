\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 3: Limits of Conventional OFT Designs
===================================

In order to appreciate the innovations that later chapters will present, it is essential first to understand why the original **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** framework incurs a heavy parameter cost and how its most common remedy—imposing a block‑diagonal structure—trades expressive power for efficiency.  This section dissects these trade‑offs from both a geometric and an algorithmic perspective, giving the reader a clear intuition for what a “dense” orthogonal transformation buys and why its naïve implementation is impractical for today’s large foundation models.

## Dense versus Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices

The core idea of OFT is to preserve **pairwise angles** between neurons when adapting a pretrained linear layer.  Consider a pretrained weight matrix  
\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
where each column \(\mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) corresponds to one neuron’s input‑side parameters.  During finetuning OFT replaces the forward computation  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
\qquad (\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})
\]

with  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is constrained to be **orthogonal** (\(\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_{d}\)).  An orthogonal matrix implements a rotation or reflection of the ambient space; consequently every inner product \(\langle \mathbf{w}^{0}_{i}, \mathbf{w}^{0}_{j}\rangle\) – and hence every angle between two neurons – remains unchanged after multiplication by \(\mathbf{R}\).  From a geometric standpoint this means that the knowledge encoded in the pretraining phase stays intact while still allowing the model to reorient its feature space toward the downstream task.

A **dense** orthogonal matrix has no enforced zeros: all \(d^{2}\) entries may be non‑zero (subject only to the orthogonality constraints).  Such a matrix can rotate any direction into any other direction, providing maximal flexibility for adapting the pretrained features.  In contrast, a **block‑diagonal** orthogonal matrix restricts rotation to within predetermined subspaces; the matrix consists of several smaller orthogonal blocks placed along its diagonal and zeros elsewhere:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\,\mathbf{R}_{2},\,\dotsc ,\,\mathbf{R}_{r}),
\qquad
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b},
\]
with \(b r = d.\)  Each \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) only mixes dimensions that belong to its own block, leaving all cross‑block interactions untouched.

Intuitively, a dense \(\mathbf{R}\) can be thought of as a “global” reshape of the feature space, whereas a block‑diagonal \(\mathbf{R}\) behaves like many independent “local” reshapes running in parallel.  The former offers complete expressivity at the price of many learnable numbers; the latter offers drastic parameter savings but at the cost of severely limiting how information flows between groups of dimensions.


## Parameter Scaling \(O(d^{2})\) for Full Orthogonal Matrices

To parametrize an arbitrary orthogonal matrix one typically adopts a *Cayley transform*:

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf{Q}^\top = -\,\mathbf{Q}.
\]

The skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) contains all degrees of freedom of \(\mathbf{R}\).  A real \(d\times d\) skew‑symmetric matrix has exactly  

\[
|\, \{\,(i,j)\mid 1 \le i < j \le d\,\} | = \frac{d(d-1)}{2}
\]

independent entries (each off‑diagonal pair contributes one parameter while diagonal entries are forced to zero).  Hence learning an unrestricted orthogonal transformation costs  

\[
O(d^{2})
=
O(d(d-1)/2)
=
O(d^{2})
\]

trainable parameters for **each** finetuned linear layer.

When foundation models contain layers with widths ranging from several hundred up to tens of thousands, this quadratic dependence quickly becomes prohibitive: storing even a single dense orthogonal matrix may exceed GPU memory limits, and back‑propagating through hundreds of such matrices would dramatically increase training time.  Consequently, without any reduction technique OFT cannot be applied to modern large‑scale networks in a practical manner.


## Block‑Diagonal OFT: Sparsity Pattern and Reduction to \(O(b\,d)\) Parameters

The most direct way to shrink the parameter budget is to impose sparsity patterns on \(\mathbf{R}\).  The simplest such pattern—adopted in the original OFT formulation—is **block diagonal**.  By dividing the input dimension \(d\) into \(r=d/b\) equal parts and allowing each part only its own orthogonal transformation \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\), one replaces one massive \(d\times d\) rotation by many smaller rotations:

* Each submatrix \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times b}\) still requires  
\(b(b-1)/2 = O(b^{2})\) parameters via its own Cayley transform.
* Since there are \(r=d/b\) blocks, the total number of trainable scalar values becomes  

  \[
  r\,\frac{b(b-1)}{2}
   = \frac{d}{b}\,\frac{b(b-1)}{'2}
   = \frac{{d}(b-1)}{'2}
   = O(b\,d).
  \]

Thus when we set \(b \ll d,\) e.g., \(b=16,\ d=1024,\) we shrink from roughly one million parameters down to roughly sixteen thousand—a reduction by two orders of magnitude.

## Inductive Bias Introduced by Fixed Block Partitioning

A *bias* in machine learning refers not just to an asymmetry in data but also to constraints imposed by model architecture that shape which functions can be represented efficiently.  
In block‑diagonal OFT three intertwined sources of inductive bias arise:

1. **Locality Bias** – By construction each dimension group interacts only with dimensions belonging to the same group. If useful information resides across groups (as it often does in vision or language representations where features are highly entangled), those interactions must pass through indirect pathways such as deeper layers or additional residual connections, potentially slowing convergence or limiting final performance.
   
2. **Index‑Based Grouping Bias** – The groups are formed based purely on their numerical indices rather than any learned similarity between dimensions. This arbitrary partitioning may split semantically related features into different blocks or lump unrelated ones together, forcing the model either not to correlate them at all or to rely on other layers that may already be saturated.
   
3. **Expressivity Limitation** – As an extreme case consider trying to approximate a classic linear transform such as the discrete Fourier transform (DFT). The DFT mixes all frequencies globally and cannot be expressed exactly by any finite block‑diagonal product because every output coefficient depends on every input coefficient. Consequently any attempt using only block‑diagonal rotations will inevitably incur approximation error no matter how large we make \(b<D.\)

From an information‐theoretic view these biases reduce *effective capacity*: they restrict what kinds of rotations can be represented without spending additional parameters elsewhere (e.g., adding many more blocks or deeper fine‐tuning stages). Empirically this manifests as slower training or lower downstream accuracy compared with full OFT when sufficient computational budget is available.


## Motivation for a Dense Yet Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Representation

The observations above motivate seeking an architectural compromise that preserves **global mixing**—the hallmark advantage of dense rotations—while keeping *parameter counts* well below quadratic growth.  An ideal solution would satisfy three desiderata identified implicitly by the authors:

| Desideratum | Intuition |
|---|---|
| **Dense Connectivity** | Every input dimension should ultimately influence every output dimension so that global relationships can be learned directly. |
| **Parameter Economy** | Total trainable numbers should scale close­to linearly with \(d,\) ideally \(O(d\,{\rm polylog}(d)),\) rather than quadratically. |
| **Orthogonality Preservation** | The transformation must remain strictly orthogonal throughout training so that pairwise angles—and thus pretraining knowledge—are retained without extra regularization tricks.|

Achieving dense connectivity without storing all \(d^{2}\) entries necessitates *factorizing* the target dense rotation into a product of several sparse factors—a strategy reminiscent of how fast Fourier transforms compress computation via butterfly patterns. Each sparse factor contributes only \(O(d)\) nonzero entries but collectively they propagate information across every pair of dimensions after enough successive multiplications.

The key insight is that rather than imposing *hard* zero patterns at training time (as in block diagonal), one can encode *structured* zero patterns that are mathematically guaranteed—by construction—to yield full connectivity after composition while still requiring far fewer independent scalar values than storing an arbitrarily dense matrix outright.

This line of reasoning sets up subsequent chapters where such factorized structures (specifically butterfly factorizations) are introduced formally as “Orthogonal Butterfly” (**BOFT**) methods that achieve precisely this dense yet parameter-efficient goal.


--- 

In summary, conventional OFT designs suffer from an inherent tension: using an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix guarantees maximal expressive power but incurs prohibitive quadratic parameter costs; replacing it with a naive block‑diagonal sparsity saves parameters linearly but injects rigid inductive biases that limit global information flow and hamper performance on tasks requiring cross‑dimensional interaction. Understanding these limits motivates exploring structured factorizations—like butterfly matrices—that retain global mixing while dramatically curtailing trainable degrees of freedom—a theme explored in depth in later sections.
