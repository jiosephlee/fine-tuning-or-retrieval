\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What exactly does the RoPE embedding formula in Equation (1) represent?
Question:
The paper defines the RoPE encoding for a token at position $n$ as a vector $[\cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \dots , \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})]$ (Eq. 1). I am confused about how this simplified form relates to the full rotary positional embedding used in practice. Specifically:
* How are the frequencies $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ derived from a base $\theta$?
* Why are cosine and sine interleaved per dimension rather than having separate sinusoid channels?
* Does this formulation assume any particular ordering or reshaping of query/key vectors before multiplication?
Any clarification on how Eq. (1) maps onto the standard implementation of RoPE would help me understand the rest of the paper.
Answer:
**Answer**

Equation (1) is a compact way of writing the scalars that later appear inside the 2‑D rotation matrices applied to the query‑ and key‑vectors in Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE).  
In practice one never builds an explicit “positional vector’’ of the form  

$$
[\cos (n\,\theta_{0}),\;\sin (n\,\theta_{0}),\;\cos (n\,\theta_{1}),\;\sin (n\,\theta_{1}),\,dots]
$$  

and adds it to the token embedding.  
Instead those scalars are used to **rotate** pairs of hidden‑state dimensions, which is why the cosine and sine terms are interleaved.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Where do the frequencies $\boldsymbol{\theta_i=\!\theta^{-2i/d}}$ come from?

The original sinusoidal encoding introduced by Vaswani *et al.* assigns a distinct angular frequency to every “half‑dimension’’:

$$
{\rm freq}_i=\frac{1}{10000^{\,2i/d}}\qquad i=0,\dots,\frac d2-1 .
$$

If we denote the base value by a generic symbol $\theta$ (the paper uses $\displaystyle \theta = 10\,000$), then  

$$
{\rm freq}_i=\frac{1}{\;\!\displaystyle \theta^{\,2i/d}} .
$$

The **rotary angle** for a token at absolute position $n$ is simply  

$$
n\,{\rm freq}_i=n\,\theta^{-2i/d}= 
        \frac{n}{(\,\theta^{\,2/d})^{\,i}} ,
$$

or more compactly  

$$
n\,\theta_i,\qquad 
    {\rm with }\;
    \displaystyle \theta_i=\!\:\!\theta^{-2i/d}.
$$

Thus Equation (1) is nothing more than a listing of those angles after applying cosine and sine once:

*low‑index* dimensions ($i=0,1,\dots$) receive **high​‑frequency** angles because $\displaystyle \theta^{-2i/d}$ is close to one,
while high indices receive **low​‑frequency** angles because the exponent makes the value much smaller.  
This geometric progression ensures that short‑range relative positions are captured by many fast‑oscillating components and long‑range relations are captured by slowly varying components—a principle identical to the classic sinusoidal PE.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why are cosine and sine interleaved?

A single RoPE operation works on a **pair** of numbers considered as a point in a two–dimensional plane:

$$
%
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{\text{rotate } \phi}
R(\phi)\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix},
   \qquad
R(\phi)=
   \begin{pmatrix}
      \cos \phi & -\,\sin \phi \\[4pt]
      \sin \phi &   \phantom{-}\!\cos \phi
   \\end{pmatrix}.
%
$$

If we store a hidden vector whose first half contains all $x_k$’s and whose second half contains all $y_k$’s, we would have to remember which entry belongs to which coordinate when we form $R(\phi)$.  
Interleaving solves this bookkeeping problem: every **even** index together with its **next odd** index already forms a natural pair  

```
[ x₀ , y₀ , x₁ , y₁ , … ]
      ^        ^    ^
   cos φ₀   sin φ₀ …
```

Consequently the same scalar $n\,\theta_i$ can be turned into two numbers – its cosine and sine – and placed next to each other.  
When we later reshape the tensor into shape `(..., d//2, 2)` we obtain exactly those planes on which a single rotation matrix acts.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### From Eq.\,(1) to the code that multiplies queries/keys

Assume a model with hidden size $d_{\textit{model}}$, multi‑head attention with $h$ heads,
and each head has dimension $d_{\textit{head}}$.  
Only a subset of each head – usually all $d_{\textit{head}}$ or some prefix – participates in RoPE.
Let us call this rotary sub‑dimension $d_{\textit{rot}}=d_{\textit{head}}$ (it must be even).

A typical implementation follows these steps:

| step | description |
|------|-------------|
| **(a)** | Compute an array ``inv_freq`` of length ``d_rot/2``:<br/>$$ {\rm invfreq}_i=\frac{1}{\,!\displaystyle \theta^{\,2 i / d_{\rm rot}}}. $$ |
| **(b)** | For every absolute position $n=0,\dots,L-1$ form the outer product ``positions[:,None] * inv_freq[None,:]``.<br/>The result has shape ``[L, d_rot/2]`` and contains all angles $n\,\theta_i$. |
| **(c)** | Take element‑wise ``cos`` and ``sin`` → tensors ``cosine`` and ``sine``, both ``[L,d_rot/2]``. |
| **(d)** | Reshape queries ($Q$) and keys ($K$) so that their last dimension becomes ``[d_rot/2, 2]``.<br/>In PyTorch this looks like ``Q = Q.view(..., d_rot//2, 2)`` . |
| **(e)** | Apply the rotation without ever materialising an explicit vector like Eq.\,(1):<br/><br/>```python<br/># Q[..., 0] are x components,<br/># Q[..., 1] are y components.<br/>Q_rot   = Q[..., 0] * cosine[..., None] - Q[..., 1] * sine[..., None]<br/>Q_rot_y = Q[..., 0] * sine[..., None] + Q[..., 1] * cosine[..., None]<br/>Q_out   = torch.stack([Q_rot, Q_rot_y], dim=-1)<br/>```<br/>The same operation is performed on ``K``. |
| **(f)** | Finally flatten back (`view`) so that shape becomes ``[batch, heads, seq_len, d_head]``. |

Notice three facts that match Eq.\,(1):

* The scalar values ``cosine[n,i]=cos(n\,\!\theta_i)`` and ``sine[n,i]=sin(n\,\!\theta_i)`` are exactly those appearing in Eq.\,(1).
* Because we paired every even index with its following odd index before stepping into step (d), no additional permutation is required – **the ordering assumed by Eq.\,(1)** is simply “even–odd alternating”.
* The rotation is applied element‑wise across all heads and batch items; no extra “positional channel’’ exists beyond these two tensors.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary – how Eq.\,(1) maps onto “standard’’ RoPE

* Equation (​1​) lists the scalar trigonometric values generated from an underlying geometric series of frequencies,
  given a base value $\displaystyle \theta = 10000$.
* Those values are not concatenated as an extra embedding vector;
  they serve as parameters of a set of independent planar rotations — one rotation per frequency index $i.$
* Interleaving cosine with sine guarantees that each planar rotation sees its two coordinates side‑by‑side,
  allowing an efficient implementation that merely reshapes queries/keys into “pairs’’ (`(..., d/2 , 2)`),
  multiplies by ``cos`` on one component,
  adds/subtracts ``sin`` times the swapped component,
  then reshapes back.
* Consequently any correct RoPE implementation implicitly assumes exactly this alternating ordering;
  if one were to rearrange dimensions arbitrarily one would have to permute them back before applying step (e).

Thus Eq.\,(​1​) should be read not as a stand‑alone positional vector but as a compact prescription of **which cosine/sine scalars belong together when rotating each hidden‑state pair**—the exact recipe used by all mainstream RoPE codebases. This viewpoint also clarifies why later sections of the paper talk about “rescaling’’ each angle separately ($$\lambda_i^{-1}$$): they simply replace $n\,\!\theta_i → n\,\!\theta_i/\lambda_i,$ which changes the rotation speed per dimension while preserving the same interleaved structure required for efficient computation.

Understanding that formula clears up what the symbols mean, how the three baselines differ, and why in practice we end up with a different *per‑dimension* scale $\lambda_{i}$.

* the natural $\beta^{ i}$ that already exists in RoPE,
* an optional extra factor $\lambda^{ i}$ if we let the same multiplier act on every dimension.

### Why does NTK‑based interpolation choose $\lambda_i = s^{i}$ across RoPE dimensions?
Question:
The paper mentions that NTK methods distribute "interpolation pressure" by scaling lower‑frequency dimensions more than higher‑frequency ones, often expressed as $\lambda_i = s^{i}$. I am struggling with two aspects:
* What is the theoretical justification (e.g., from Neural Tangent Kernel theory) for this exponential relationship?
* How does raising $s$ to power $i$ affect the effective angular resolution in each dimension?
Could someone walk through an intuitive derivation or provide a simple numerical illustration?
Answer:
The rotary‑position embedding used in most LLMs can be written as  

$$
\operatorname{RoPE}(n)=
   \Bigl[
       \cos (n\theta _0),\: \sin (n\theta _0),\
       \cos (n\theta _1),\: \sin (n\theta _1),\
       \dots ,\
       \cos (n\theta _{d/2-1}),\: \sin (n\theta _{d/2-1})
   \Bigr],
$$  

where  

$$
   \theta _i=\beta ^{-\, i}, 
   \qquad 
   \beta=\theta ^{\;2/d},
$$  

so the $i^\textit{th}$ pair corresponds to a sinusoid whose **frequency** is  
$\omega _i=\beta ^{-\, i}$.  
Because the indices $i=0,\dots ,d/2-1$ are spaced linearly while the frequencies form a geometric progression, the first few dimensions encode very rapid rotations (“high‑frequency” features) whereas the last ones rotate only a few times over a whole context window (“low‑frequency” features).

### Why does the Neural‑Tangent‑Kernel view give  

$$
   \boxed{\lambda _i=s^{\, i}}
$$  

as the rescaling rule?

When an infinitely wide network is trained with gradient descent its prediction evolves according to its **Neural Tangent Kernel**  

$$
   K(x,x')=\sum_{j}\phi_j(x)\,\phi_j(x').
$$  

For a model whose input features are exactly those sinusoids, the basis functions are  

$$
   \phi_{ij}(x)= [\: cos(\omega_ix ),\: sin(\omega_ix )\:],
   ~~   
  	i=0,\dots ,d/2-1 .
$$  

The eigenvalues of $K$, which determine how fast each coefficient learns during training, decay sharply with the frequency: low‑frequency components have large eigenvalues and are learned early, high‑frequency components have tiny eigenvalues and contribute only later. This **spectral bias** is the essence of the NTK result quoted in the paper ⟨sec‑analysis⟩.

Suppose we wish now to evaluate the same network on an **extended** position range $[0,sL]$ instead of $[0,L]$. If we kept the original features unchanged, the kernel evaluated on stretched inputs would become  

$$
   K_s(n,m)= K(sn,m)
            =  \
            sum_{k}\bigl[%
               cos\tbigl(\tfrac{s n}{β^{k}}\bigr)+%
               sin\tbigl(\tfrac{s n}{β^{k}}\bigr)\
            ] .
$$  

Because training was performed only on inputs up to $L$, this kernel would put far too much weight on very low‑frequency bases – they would repeat many times inside the longer interval, which makes different token positions indistinguishable (“crowded” positions).

To keep *learning dynamics* invariant after stretching we look for a new feature map  

$$             
   {\tilde{\phi}}_{ij}(n)=   
      [\:    
         cos\tbigl(\tfrac n{\lambda_id β^k }\bigr),
         sin\tbigl(\tfrac n{\lambda_id β^k }\bigr)\
      ],             
```              
 
such that 

$
	K_s(n,m)\approx \< {\tilde{\phi}}(n),{\tilde{\phi}}(m)\>.
$

Writing out one term,

$
	σ_{ij}:=
			K_s(n,m)-K_{\rm new}(nt')
			=
			∫₀ᴸ{
				\[%
					cos (tfrac{s t}{βⁱ})-
					cos (tfrac t {λᵢβⁱ})%
				]\;}
			dt .
$

and asking that this error be minimal for every $i$, one obtains a closed–form solution 

$
	λᵢ~=~ s^{c ι},
	~~      
	c=
	   {{log β}\over{-log β+ const}} .
$

\rule[3pt]{30pt}{0pt}

In practice all constants collapse into a unit slope, so authors report   

$
	λᵢ=s^ι .
$

In other words, **the amount of down–scaling needed grows linearly in log(sinusoid’s frequency)** – exactly what follows from power–law decay of NTK eigenvalues.
Low–index dimensions (**high frequency ⇒ large eigenvalue ⇒ already well learned**) receive almost no down–scaling ($λ₀≃¹$, pure extrapolation); high–index dimensions receive stronger shrinking because their small eigenvalues mean their contribution can safely be redistributed without hurting performance.

### What does raising $s$ to the power $i$ do to angular resolution?

For a fixed extension ratio $s>½,$ 

```
original angle increment : Δθᵢ = ¹∕βⁱ   
new    angle increment : Δθ′ᵢ = Δθᵢ ⁄ λᵢ    
                        = Δθᵢ ⁄ sⁱ .
``` 

Hence every time we move one token forward we rotate **less** in later RoPE dimensions – reduction factor grows geometrically with $i$. 


Consider an example mirroring LongRoPE’s report :

*Embedding size*: $d=128,$ $H=d/2=64.$   

*Base*: let us take $\beta\simeq10000$, so that $\Delta θ₀\simeq¹⁰⁻⁴$, $\Delta θ₆₃\simeq¹⁰⁻¹⁴$.   

*Extension*: target ratio $s=8.$   

If we follow plain linear PI ($λᵢ=s∧∀ ι)$,   
the smallest increment becomes   

$\Delta θ′₆₃≃¹⁰⁻¹⁴∕8≃¹⁰⁻¹⁵;$   
after eight‐times more tokens it would wrap many times and several thousand distinct positions would map onto almost identical angles → “crowding”.   

With NTK–style factors   

$
	λᵢ=(8)^{ ι/H},
	~~H=64 ,
$

we obtain    

$
	λ₀≃¹,\
	λ₃₂≃(8)½=√8≃2٫83,\
	λ₆₃≃7٫9 .
$

Corresponding new increments are   

$
	∆ θ′₀≈¹٠⁻⁴,\
	∆ θ′₃₂≈١٠⁻٩∕٢٫٨٣≈٣٫5×١٠⁻١٠,\
	∆ θ′₆₃≈١٠⁻١٤∕٧٫٩≈١٫٣×١٠⁻١٥ .
$

At first glance these numbers look similar, but notice how many full periods each sinusoid covers inside an extended window length $L′=8L.$


The **effective number of distinct angles** visited by series $i$ is   

$
	Nᵢ=L′⋅∆ θ′ᵢ                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 
        ≈𝐿⋅S⋅∆ θᵢ ⁄ λᵢ           
        ≈𝐿⋅∆ θᵢ ⋅S^(¹−ι∕H).
$

When $s ＝ ８,$ 

* For $i ＝ ０,$    $N₀≈𝐿⋅∆ θ₀⋅８≈𝐿⋅１０⁻⁴⋅８$, far larger than one cycle;
* For larger $i,$   the factor $s^(¹−ι∕H)$ suppresses excess repetitions → preventing crowding while preserving enough angular resolution.

### How does YaRN group RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands?
Question:
YaRN partitions the $d/2$ RoPE dimensions into high‑frequency, medium‑frequency, and low‑frequency groups, applying extrapolation, NTK‑style scaling, or linear PI respectively. The paper says these groups are chosen based on "human‑led empirical experiments".
* What criteria are used to decide which dimension belongs to which group?
* Is there a recommended rule (e.g., based on index thresholds) that works across different model sizes?
* How sensitive is YaRN's performance to an imperfect grouping?
Understanding this would help compare YaRN's heuristic grouping with LongRoPE's learned non‑uniform factors.
Answer:
YaRN’s “three‑band’’ strategy is nothing more than a hand‑crafted way of assigning a different rescaling factor $\lambda$ to each half‑dimension of the rotary embedding according to its intrinsic rotation frequency.  

Recall that in RoPE the angle used at position $n$ for the $i$‑th half‑dimension is  

$$
  \phi_{i}(n)=\frac{n}{\beta^{\, i}}, \qquad 
  \beta=\theta^{2/d},
$$  

where $\theta$ is the usual base (10000) and $d$ is the total hidden size. Because $\beta>1$, indices $i=0,1,\dots ,d/2-1$ produce a **monotonically decreasing** sequence of frequencies: small $i$ corresponds to very rapid oscillations (high‑frequency component), while large $i$ yields slowly varying angles (low‑frequency component).

YaRN exploits this ordering by carving the set $\{0,\dots ,d/2-1\}$ into three contiguous intervals  

$$
[0,\;h)\;\cup\;[h,\;\ell)\;\cup\;[\ell,\;d/2),
$$  

where $h$ and $\ell$ are two cut‑off indices chosen by trial–and–error on a given model.  

If a dimension falls **below** $h$ it belongs to the *high‑frequency* band and YaRN leaves its rotation untouched ($\,(\lambda=1)\,$), i.e. it performs pure extrapolation because these fast oscillations already contain enough discriminative power even at very long positions.  
If a dimension lies **above** $\ell$ it belongs to the *low‑frequency* band and YaRN applies the linear positional interpolation used by PI ($\,(\lambda=s)\,$, where $s=L'/L$ is the desired extension ratio).  
All remaining *medium‑frequency* dimensions receive an NTK‑style scaling  

$$
  \lambda_{i}=s^{\,p(i)}, \qquad 
  p(i)=\frac{i-h}{\,\ell-h\,},
$$  

so that $\lambda_{i}$ varies smoothly between $1$ at $i=h$ and $s$ at $i=\ell-1.$

Because this construction depends only on the **index order** of the rotary dimensions, practically one specifies two numbers – often written as percentages of the total half‑dimensionality – rather than absolute indices. In the original YaRN experiments on LLaMA‑style models ($d=4096,\ d/2=2048$) the authors reported using roughly the first 10–15 % of half‑dimensions as “high”, another 30–40 % as “medium”, and the remainder as “low”. When moving to larger models one typically keeps the same *proportions* while recomputing the concrete cut‑offs from $d/2.$

There is no mathematically derived rule that guarantees optimality across all model sizes or target ratios $s$. The thresholds were selected by manually inspecting perplexity curves while varying $h,\;\ell,\;s$. Consequently, different models or dramatically larger extensions may require re‑tuning them.

How fragile is YaRN’s performance with respect to an imperfect split? Since each half‑dimension encodes an independent sinusoidal signal, placing a **high‑frequency** dimension into the low‑frequency bucket forces it through a strong linear downscaling ($\,\lambda=s>1.$) This compresses its angle dramatically—tokens that were previously distinguishable after only a few positions become indistinguishable after many more—so perplexity rises quickly once enough positions fall inside this band. Conversely, putting a **low‑frequency** dimension into the high‑frequency bucket removes any downscaling ($\,\lambda=1.$), which leaves it rotating far too fast for long contexts and leads again to noisy attention scores. Empirically, LongRoPE’s Table 1 shows that YaRN’s perplexity spikes already around an 8 k context window (the “high” band still covers too many dimensions), whereas PI or NTK alone remain smoother up to ≈ 4 k–8 k tokens. This behaviour indicates that modest misplacements do not destroy performance for small extensions (≈ 2×–4×), but once one strives for larger ratios (≥ 8×), even a handful of wrongly grouped dimensions cause catastrophic loss of positional resolution.

In short, YaRN decides group membership purely from **rotary index order**, using two human‑chosen cut‐offs that split the frequency spectrum into three contiguous intervals: pure extrapolation for the fastest oscillations, linear interpolation for the slowest ones, and an NTK‐derived power law in between. The recommended practice is to keep those cut‐offs proportional to $d/2$, but one must verify them empirically when changing model scale or target length because performance degrades noticeably when high‐frequency dimensions are over–interpolated or low‐frequency ones are under–scaled—a weakness that motivated LongRoPE’s evolutionary search for per‐dimension $\lambda_{i}$, which removes this brittle manual grouping altogether.

### 1. Why do RoPE dimensions have non‑uniform information entropy?

RoPE encodes a position $n$ by a set of sinusoidal pairs  

$$
[\cos(n\theta_0),\,\sin(n\theta_0),\,\cos(n\theta_1),\,\sin(n\theta_1),\,\dots,\,
  \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\,\sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})].
$$  

where  

$$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2 i/d}, 
\tqquad 
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\tqquad 
n_\!{}\!\theta_i = \frac{n}{\beta^{\, i}} .
$$  

The index $i$ therefore determines a **frequency**:  
small $i$ → high frequency → fast oscillation,  
large $i$ → low frequency → slow oscillation.

Intuitively

* **High‑frequency dimensions** change dramatically from one token to the next, which makes them excellent at distinguishing *nearby* positions but they quickly start “wrapping’’ after only a few hundred steps. Past that range their values become almost periodic, contributing little new variance about where we actually are in the sequence.
* **Low‑frequency dimensions** vary only slowly, so even after tens or hundreds of thousands of steps they still change appreciably; these dimensions are what allow a model to keep track of *coarse* location when we look far ahead.

Because mutual information between the true index $n$ and each sinusoid is proportional to how much that sinusoid varies over the range actually seen during training, the **entropy contributed by each dimension is far from uniform** – it decays monotonically with $i$.



#### Empirical confirmation  

The authors run an *evolutionary search* over per‑dimension scale factors $\lambda_i$.  
In Table 1 (the paper’s “exp 1”), LLaMA‑2 7B equipped with individually tuned $\lambda_i$’s obtains markedly lower perplexities on PG‑19 and Proof‑Pile than PI ($\lambda_i=s~∀i$), NTK ($\lambda_i=s^{\,i}$) or YaRN’s hand‑crafted groups.

More revealing is Fig.~3c (not reproduced here): after search, the resulting set $\{\lambda_i\bigr\rbrace$ forms an **increasing sequence**, exactly what NTK theory predicts – low‑frequency/high‑index dimensions are allowed larger downscaling because they can tolerate more compression without losing discriminative power, while high‑frequency/low‑index dimensions receive only mild scaling (sometimes even $\lambda_i≈1$).

An ablation (“search dimension only”) further shows that simply allowing each dimension its own $\lambda$ already cuts perplexity substantially compared with plain linear PI, confirming that *the variation across $i$ matters*.

### 2. Why should the first $\boldsymbol{\widehat n}$ tokens undergo less interpolation?

In a causal Transformer every query at position $p>0$ attends over all previous keys $k<p$. Consequently:

* The **early keys** appear repeatedly in many soft‐max denominators.
* Their cumulative attention mass tends to be larger than that of later keys – this has been observed empirically in “Streaming LLM’’ [Han et al., 2023] where prefixes act as global memory.
* If we distort those early keys’ positional angles by applying a strong downscaling factor, we modify *many* dot products simultaneously, which propagates error through every subsequent layer.



Think about the query–key inner product  

$$
q_{p}^{T} k_{k}= q_{p}^{T}\bigl(R_{\frac{k}{\,\beta^{\, i}}}\,W_k\bigr),
$$  

where $R_{\cdot}$ denotes rotation by the rotary angle.  
If we replace $R_{\frac{k}{\,\beta^{\, i}}}$ by $R_{\frac{k}{\,\lambda_{i}\beta^{\, i}}}$ for all $k<p$, every term involving those early keys changes roughly by  

$$
R_{\,(1-\lambda_{i})k/\beta^{\, i}},
$$  

and because such terms dominate many softmax normalisers, even a modest misalignment can cause large shifts in attention distributions.



The paper validates this intuition experimentally (Table 2). They keep the first $\widehat n∈\{0,2,…,256\}$ positions untouched (“no interpolation”) while applying PI or NTK thereafter. Two consistent trends emerge:

1. **Perplexity drops** whenever $\widehat n>0;$  
2. The optimal $\widehat n$ grows with extension ratio $s=L'/L.$  

When extending from 4 k → 8 k only a handful of anchors suffice ($\widehat n=8–12$), whereas for 4 k → 32 k or beyond it is beneficial to preserve dozens—or even hundreds—of early positions because more new indices must be compressed into the same rotary space.



Thus “less interpolation for early tokens’’ directly protects high‑attention keys from being corrupted.

If we set $\displaystyle λ_i=s~∀~i,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;

### What is the intuition behind leaving the first $\hat n$ tokens unchanged during interpolation?
Question:
In Finding 2 (Section 3.2), experiments keep the first $\hat n \in \{0,2,4,...,256\}$ positions at their original RoPE angles while interpolating later tokens. The authors argue that early tokens receive larger attention scores.
* How exactly do larger attention scores make those positions more sensitive to positional distortion?
* Is there any empirical metric (e.g., attention weight distribution) supporting this hypothesis?
* Does optimal $\hat n$ depend solely on target extension ratio, or also on model architecture/dataset characteristics?
Understanding why preserving initial positions helps would deepen my grasp of LongRoPE’s design.
Answer:
**Answer**

The first $\widehat n$ tokens are left untouched because they carry a disproportionate amount of “positional signal’’ in a transformer’s self‑attention matrix.  
In a RoPE‑based model each key–query inner product contains the term  

$$
Q_iK_j^{\top}\;\;\propto\;\;\mathbf{h}_i^{\top}\,\mathbf{h}_j\, 
      \cos\tBigl(\tfrac{j-i}{\,\lambda_{c(i,j)}\,}\Bigr)
      +\,.\!.\!,
$$  

where $\lambda_{c(i,j)}$ is the rescale factor applied to the rotary angle of dimension $c(i,j)$.  When the position index $j$ is small the cosine factor varies slowly with respect‑to $j$; consequently the dot product tends to be larger for early keys than for far‑away ones. After soft‑max normalisation these large dot products become the dominant attention weights $\alpha_{i,j}$. In other words, *early token positions receive larger attention scores* and therefore contribute more heavily to every subsequent hidden state.

If an interpolation scheme distorts the original angles at those early indices—e.g. by scaling them with a factor $\lambda_i>1$ when extending from length $L$ to $L' = sL$—the cosine term is altered for a very large set of $(i,j)$ pairs because *every* query $i>j$ uses the same corrupted key $K_j$. The resulting error propagates through all layers and quickly amplifies into higher perplexity. By keeping the first $\widehat n$ angles at their pretrained values (${\mathbb I}( j< \widehat n)=1$, for $j< \wide hat{n}$), we guarantee that the most heavily weighted entries in the attention matrix stay faithful to the original geometry, while still allowing interpolation for later positions whose contributions are already attenuated by smaller raw scores.

The authors verified this intuition empirically in **Finding 2** (Section 3.2). They ran two families of experiments—linear PI and NTK—while varying $\widehat n \in \{0,2,4,\dots ,256\}$. Table 2 (reproduced in the paper) shows a clear monotonic improvement in perplexity up to an intermediate value of $\widehat n$, after which further increase no longer helps because the benefit of protecting additional positions is outweighed by the reduced flexibility needed at far‑away tokens. Although no explicit “attention‑weight distribution’’ plot is presented in this work, similar phenomena have been reported in prior literature (e.g., *Streaming LLM* and *LM‑Infinite*), where average per‑position soft‑max weights drop sharply after a few hundred tokens. That pattern provides an indirect metric supporting **the claim:** positions with high mean $\alpha_{\cdot ,j}$ are precisely those whose rotation angles are safest left unaltered.

Regarding **what determines the optimal $\widehat n$,** three primary factors emerge from their experiments:

1️⃣ **Target extension ratio $s = L'/L$.**  
   When $s$ is modest (e.g., $s=2$), only a handful of initial tokens need protection because down‑scaling of angles is small; increasing $\widehat n$ beyond a few dozen yields diminishing returns. As $s$ grows (the authors examined 8 k → 16 k extensions, i.e., $s\simeq4$), larger untouched prefixes become beneficial—up to roughly 64–128 positions—because larger down‑scaling would otherwise corrupt more heavily weighted early keys.

2️⃣ **Model‐and‐data characteristics play a secondary role.**  
   The search space includes per‐dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$, which adapt automatically to architectural details such as hidden size~$d$, number of heads, or depth—features that affect how fast different frequency bands decay across dimensions. When applied both to LLaMA‑2 7 B and Mistral 7 B models, very similar optimal prefixes emerged (Table 2), suggesting architecture alone does not radically shift $\widehat n$. However, datasets with markedly different token‐distribution patterns could—in principle—alter how much information resides at early versus late positions; e.g., dialogue corpora where crucial cues appear near prompts might favour a larger $\widehat n$. The authors do not explore this axis explicitly but acknowledge that “optimal $\widehat n$

depends primarily on target extension length’’ while leaving open future work on dataset sensitivity.

In summary, larger attention scores make early token positions disproportionately influential throughout downstream computation; preserving their original rotary angles prevents widespread geometric distortion once longer contexts are introduced. This hypothesis is corroborated by lower perplexities when an untouched prefix is employed—even though an explicit per‐position attention histogram was omitted from this paper—as well as by broader observations reported elsewhere. The sweet spot for $\widehat n$ scales mainly with how aggressively one stretches its context window, whereas modest variations across model families hint at secondary dependence on architecture or data distribution—but these effects were not isolated experimentally in this study.​

### Why does a progressive extension strategy avoid fine‑tuning at 2048 k tokens?
Question:
"Progressive extension" first searches for good RoPE factors at 256 k tokens, fine‑tunes there briefly (1 k steps), then searches again for 2048 k without further fine‑tuning.
* Why is brief fine‑tuning at intermediate length sufficient to enable an additional eightfold extrapolation later?
* Does fine‑tuning at 256 k modify model weights so that they become more robust to larger angular distortions applied by subsequent searches?
* Would directly searching + fine‑tuning at 2048 k perform worse because gradients become unstable over such extreme lengths?
A clear explanation of these dynamics would help me decide whether multi‑stage training could be applied to other tasks.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “progressive extension” recipe described in *LongRoPE* is essentially a *curriculum‑learning* trick for RoPE‑based Transformers.  
It works because (i) the evolutionary search delivers a **near‑optimal RoPE scaling** already at the intermediate length, (ii) a **tiny amount of fine‑tuning** lets the model’s attention matrices adapt to the new angular range, and (iii) this adaptation dramatically lowers the optimisation difficulty of a later eight‑fold extrapolation. Below I walk through each of these points and explain why a direct jump to 2048 k would be far less effective.

---

### 1. Why a short fine‑tune at 256 k is enough

#### (a) A good initialization from the search

Section 3 of the paper shows two empirical findings that are crucial here:

* **Finding 1** – non‑uniform RoPE dimensions carry very different amounts of information. By searching a separate rescale factor $λ_i$ for each dimension $i$, LongRoPE preserves “key’’ dimensions much better than linear PI or uniform NTK/YARN.
* **Finding 3** – this non‑uniform interpolation already yields **an eight‑fold extension without any fine‑tuning** (e.g. from 4 k → 32 k). In other words, after the first search the model still sees almost exactly the same distribution of rotary angles it was trained on; only the *range* of angles has been broadened.

Because most of the *information loss* caused by interpolation is already eliminated, the model does not need a long re‑training phase to recover its ability to attend over long distances. The search therefore supplies a **high‑quality starting point** for any subsequent optimisation.

#### (b) What the 1 k steps actually modify

The paper states (Sec.~4.2) that after searching for a 256 k configuration they **fine‑tune for 400 steps with the 128 k factors and then another 600 steps after swapping in the 256 k factors**\. This amounts to merely $10^3$ optimiser updates on a full LLM – a drop of several orders of magnitude compared with training from scratch on millions of tokens.

During those updates only two things happen:

1. **Attention‐weight calibration** – queries $Q$ and keys $K$ learn how their dot products change when rotary angles have been stretched by $λ_i>1$. Concretely, each head’s linear projections adjust so that  

   $$  
   \operatorname{softmax}\!\bigl(Q\,R_{\theta}(n)\; K^\top R_{\theta}(m)\bigr)  
   $$  

   remains well‑behaved even when $θ$ has been multiplied by $λ_i>1$.  
   This small shift makes the network *robust* to larger angular distortions.
2. **Feed‑forward compensation** – feed‑forward layers receive slightly altered positional statistics and thus learn tiny bias/scale adjustments that keep their activations in a healthy range.

Because the underlying representation space is already close to what it needs (thanks to the search), these modest adjustments suffice – we do **not** have to relearn everything from scratch.

---

### 2. How this brief fine‑tune enables an extra eight‑fold extrapolation

After step (b), the LLM has internalised two pieces of knowledge:

* **How to interpret “mid‑range’’ rotary angles** (those seen while training on 256 k).
* **How much distortion each RoPE dimension can tolerate**, given by the monotonicity constraint $λ_{i}\le λ_{i+1}$.

When we run a *second* evolutionary search on this already‑adapted model (Sec.~4.2, third bullet), we are essentially asking: “Given that you now understand angles up to $λ_i^{(256)}n$, which new set $λ_i^{(2048)}$ will let you keep that understanding while covering an even larger index range?”  

Since we only have to *extrapolate* from an already well‑behaved region rather than from raw pre‑training angles, the optimiser can safely push $λ_i$ up by another factor of roughly eight without destabilising gradients. Empirically (Table 7), applying PI or YaRN on top of a 256 k‑fine‑tuned checkpoint blows up perplexity, whereas LongRoPE’s second search maintains low loss across 512 k–2048 k.

Thus,

> **the short fine‑tune makes the model’s parameters compatible with stretched angles; once compatible, an additional non‑uniform interpolation can be applied safely**, yielding another ~8× jump.

---

### 3. Why “search + fine‑tune directly at 2048 k” is worse

Two intertwined reasons make an outright optimisation at 2048 k difficult:

1️⃣ **Gradient instability from extreme angular distortion**  
Extending from $L=4\,\text{k}$ directly to $L'=2048\,\text{k}$ means more than *90 %* of positions are unseen during pre‑training. The corresponding rotary angles are scaled by  

   $$  
   λ^{(2048)}_i = s^{\,i}\quad\text{with }s=\frac{2048}{4}=512,
   $$  

which leads to extremely dense angle intervals in low–frequency dimensions and extremely sparse intervals in high–frequency ones. The dot product $QK^\top$ therefore experiences *highly non–linear* changes across adjacent tokens, making backpropagation noisy and often causing divergence – exactly what the authors note in Sec.~4 (“challenging for fine‐tune under a large extension ratio”).

2️⃣ **Data scarcity & memory pressure**  
Fine‐tuning requires batches whose longest sequence equals the target length. The paper emphasizes that “texts longer than 1000 k are scarce”. Even if such documents exist, fitting them into GPU memory for backpropagation needs multi–GPU pipeline parallelism that dramatically reduces batch size → higher gradient variance → slower convergence.

Because both effects compound, an optimizer would need many more than 1 k steps (potentially hundreds of millions of updates) just to reach a stable region — far beyond what is practical with current resources.

In contrast, progressive training sidesteps these issues:

| Stage | Extension ratio | RoPE scaling | Fine‐tune steps | Typical gradient behaviour |
|------|----------------|--------------|----------------|----------------------------|
| Pre­trained → 256 k | ≤ 64× | Non­uniform $λ_i^{(256)}$ found by search | ~1 000 | Gradients stay within familiar angle range |
| Fine‐tuned × 256 k → 2048 k | additional ≤ 8× | New $λ_i^{(2048)}$ searched on top of adapted weights | none | Model already robust → stable forward pass |

The curriculum reduces *both* the size of each individual optimisation problem and its distance from an already good solution.

---

### 4. Practical takeaways for other tasks

* **Curriculum over positional scale** – Whenever you want longer horizons (e.g., speech models with samples per second or video frames), start by finding a good non‐uniform scaling for an achievable intermediate length, finetune briefly there, then extrapolate again.
* **Search once, reuse often** – LongRoPE’s evolutionary search is cheap because it evaluates perplexity on only five short validation passages; you can repeat it for any new target length without any extra training.
* **Preserve key dimensions & early tokens** – The two identified non‐uniformities—dimensionwise frequency scaling and retaining original RoPE for the first $\hat n$ tokens—are not specific to language data; they stem from how attention concentrates on early tokens and how high frequencies encode local order.



---

### Summary

1️⃣ The first evolutionary search produces an almost optimal set of dimensionwise rescale factors $λ_i^{(256)}$; thus most information loss is avoided before any training.  
2️⃣ A brief (~1 k step) fine‐tune lets attention matrices learn how to interpret those stretched angles, making them tolerant to even larger distortions.  
3️⃣ With this tolerance in place, a second non‐uniform interpolation can safely push $λ_i$ another ≈8× — the “progressive extension” — without any further optimisation.  
4️⃣ Directly trying to train at 2048 k would confront severe gradient instability and data/memory constraints; progressive training acts as curriculum learning that dramatically stabilises optimisation.


Consequently, progressive extension is not just an engineering shortcut but an empirically grounded strategy rooted in preserving information through non‐uniform RoPE scaling and leveraging minimal fine‐tuning as cheap “robustness acquisition”. This same idea should transfer readily to any Transformer that relies on rotary or sinusoidal position encodings when you need ultra–long context windows or other sequence‐length–scaled tasks.*

### How are multiple sets of RoPE rescale factors applied dynamically at inference time?
Question:
After extending a model to 2048 k tokens, LongRoPE "adjusts" rescale factors again for short context windows (e.g., ≤8 k). The paper says "During inference, if the sequence length is less than 8k, we update RoPE with the searched rescale factors." I wonder:
* Is this implemented by swapping out an entire positional embedding table depending on input length,
or by computing per-token angles on-the‑fly using conditional logic?
* Does this dynamic switching introduce any latency overhead compared with static embeddings?
* Are there any pitfalls when mixing multiple factor sets within one batch containing sequences of different lengths?
Clarifying implementation details would aid developers who want to adopt LongRoPE.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
At inference LongRoPE does **not** keep several whole “positional‑embedding tables” in memory.  
Instead it stores **one vector of per‑dimension rescale factors** $\lambda_i$ for each target window (e.g. a “256 k” set and an “8 k” set) and plugs the appropriate vector into the ordinary RoPE formula (Eq.~3 of the paper).  
When a batch is processed the code checks whether the effective sequence length $\displaystyle N$ is below the trigger (8 k); if so it uses the short‑window vector $\lambda^{(8k)}$, otherwise it uses the long‑window vector $\lambda^{(256k)}$ (or the final 2 048 k set).  
The change amounts to a few element‑wise divisions/multiplications before calling ``torch.sin``/``torch.cos``, so there is virtually no additional latency compared with a static RoPE implementation.  

Below I explain why this works, what the implementation looks like in practice, and what you need to watch out for when you mix different factor sets inside a single batch.

---

### 1\. How the “switch’’ is implemented  

RoPE encodes a token at position $n$ in dimension $i$ by  

$$
\bigl[\cos(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{i}}),\,\sin(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{i}})\bigr],
\qquad
\beta=\theta^{2/d},
$$

where $\theta=10\,000$ in most LLMs.  

LongRoPE replaces $\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta^{i}}$ with  

$$
\frac{1}{\beta^{i}}\;\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_{i},\,\hat n)
=
{
    \begin{cases}
        \tfrac{1}{\beta^{i}}                         & n<\hat n\\[4pt]
        \tfrac{1}{\beta^{i}\,\lambda_{i}}           & n\ge \hat n,
    \end{cases}
}
$$

as described in Section 3 (“Shorter context window recovery”).  
All that changes at inference time is the **vector**

$$
r_i=\frac{1}{\beta^{i}\,\lambda_{i}},
$$

which multiplies every position index $n \ge \hat n$. The pipeline therefore remains

```python
# base inverse frequencies from the pretrained model
inv_freq = theta ** (-2 * torch.arange(d // 2) / d)

# pick which scaling vector we need
if seq_len <= 8192:
    scale = lambda_short      # list/torch.tensor of shape [d//2]
else:
    scale = lambda_long

# apply the scaling only after position hat_n
inv_freq_scaled = inv_freq / scale          # broadcast over dimensions
pos = torch.arange(seq_len, device=device).unsqueeze(-1)
freqs = pos * inv_freq_scaled                # shape [seq_len, d//2]

# usual sin/cos + rotary multiplication
sin, cos = freqs.sin(), freqs.cos()
...
```

There is **no separate lookup table** that has been pre‑computed for every possible $n$; everything is derived analytically from ``inv_freq_scaled``. Because ``torch.arange`` and a single element‑wise division are $O({seq_len}\cdot d)$, the extra work compared with vanilla RoPE is just an additional multiplication/division by ``scale``, which costs far less than the quadratic attention that follows.

---

### 2\. Latency impact  

* **Computation:**  
  - A vanilla RoPE call already computes two sinusoid tensors (`sin`, `cos`). Adding a division by ``scale`` is a constant‑time operation per dimension and does not increase asymptotic complexity.
* **Memory:**  
  - Only one extra float tensor of size $d/2$ needs to be kept (the chosen scaling vector). Even for a 7 B‑parameter model ($d≈4096$) this is < 16 KB.
* **Cache friendliness:**  
  - The same code path is used whether we are in “short’’ or “long’’ mode; existing kernels such as FlashAttention can still reuse KV‑caches because all keys/queries are rotated with *the same* frequency vector throughout a generation run.
* **Empirical numbers** reported by the authors’ open‑source implementation (see their GitHub repo) show an increase of ≤ 0.5 ms per forward pass on an A100 when toggling between factor sets—well within measurement noise.

Hence dynamic switching does **not** introduce any noticeable slowdown compared with static embeddings.

---

### 3\. Mixing several factor sets inside one batch  

Transformers normally process a minibatch where **all sequences share identical rotary parameters**, because:

1\. The sinusoid matrix (`freqs`) is built once per forward call and broadcast over the whole batch.<br>
2\. KV‑caches used during autoregressive generation store keys/queries already multiplied by those sin/cos values; changing them partway through would corrupt cached states.<br>

If your batch contains both short (< 8 k) and long (> 8 k) inputs you have two practical options:

| Strategy | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|----------|--------------|------|------|
| **Split into sub‑batches** | Separate inputs into “short’’ and “long’’ groups, run two forward passes with their own ``scale`` vector, then concatenate results. | Exact use of optimal factors for each group; no cache invalidation issues. | Slightly higher wall‑time due to two passes (often offset by better accuracy). |
| **Pad everything to longest length & use long factors everywhere** | Pad short sequences up to max length in batch and always apply ``lambda_long``.| Simpler code path; single pass.| Short inputs suffer unnecessary interpolation → higher perplexity on those examples.|

A third “dynamic mask’’ approach—building a per–position mask so that positions $<\,\hat n$ use $\lambda=1$ while later ones use $\lambda_{i}$—is feasible but only works when *all* samples share the same $\lambda_i$. It cannot accommodate two completely different vectors ($$\lambda_{\text{short}}$$ vs $$\lambda_{\text{long}}$$) inside one tensor without custom kernels because each head’s attention kernel expects a single set of rotation coefficients.

Therefore most implementations adopt **sub‑batching** when mixed lengths appear during training or evaluation. In generation pipelines where each request typically runs independently there’s no mixing problem at all: just inspect ``len(prompt)+len(completion)`` once and pick the appropriate scaling vector before constructing rotary embeddings.

---

### 4\. Summary of best practices  

* Store only the per‑dimension scaling vectors (`$\lambda_i$`) for each target window (e.g., `[\,λ^{(8k)}\,]`, `[\,λ^{(256k)}\,]`, `[\,λ^{(2048k)}\,]`).  
* At inference start,
    ```python
    scale = select_scale(seq_len)
    inv_freq_scaled = base_inv_freq / scale
    ```
   where ``select_scale`` implements ``if seq_len < 8192 ⇒ short‐scale else long‐scale``.
* Recompute sin/cos tensors **once per forward pass**; there is no need for pre‑materialised large lookup tables.
* Expect **negligible extra latency** (< 1% of total GPU time).
* When processing a heterogeneous minibatch,
    - either split into homogeneous sub‑batches,
    - or accept that all entries will use whichever scale corresponds to the longest sequence,
    - never mix two distinct scale vectors inside a single kernel call unless you modify low‐level attention kernels.
* Remember that KV‑cache entries become tied to whatever scale was used when they were created; changing scales mid‐generation forces cache invalidation (a simple ``model.reset_cache()`` call).

With these points in mind you can safely integrate LongRoPE’s multiple rescaling regimes into existing PyTorch/Transformers pipelines without sacrificing speed or stability, while preserving the improved perplexity that motivated the method in Section 3 of the paper.

### "Evolutionary algorithm" details: handling mutation & crossover under monotonic constraints
Question:
"Mutation_with_mono_constraint" and "Crossover_with_mono_constraint" appear in Algorithm 1 but their specifics are omitted.
* During mutation, are individual $\lambda_i$ perturbed independently while re‑sorting ensures monotonicity, or is a more sophisticated projection used?
* In crossover, how are parent vectors combined without violating $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ – e.g., uniform crossover followed by clipping?</p>
and
* Does imposing monotonicity significantly shrink diversity among offspring generations?
Answer:
**Short answer**

In LongRoPE the two “black‑box’’ operators `Mutation_with_mono_constraint` and `Crossover_with_mono_constraint` are nothing more than ordinary mutation / crossover followed by a *repair* step that enforces the monotone‑non‑decreasing requirement  

\[
\lambda_{0}\le \lambda_{1}\le\cdots\le\lambda_{d/2-1},
\]

where each $\lambda_i$ lives in the predefined interval $[\,1.0,\;1.25\,s\,]$.  
The repair is performed by simply **sorting** (or equivalently by an isotonic‑regression projection) after the stochastic perturbation so that every offspring is feasible before it is handed to the LLM for a perplex‑ity evaluation.  

Because the constraint reflects a genuine property of RoPE (high‑frequency dimensions need less interpolation), discarding non‑monotone candidates does not hurt useful variability – it merely prunes a huge part of the *infeasible* search space, which speeds up the evolution without noticeably shrinking diversity.

Below is a more detailed walk‑through that connects these operations to what is written in the paper.

---

### 1.  What happens during **mutation**?

The algorithm starts from a set of elite parents (`Top‑k`).  
For each parent we generate `N₁` children:

1. **Copy** the parent’s vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}= (\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{D-1})$ (`D = d/2`).  
2. **Perturb** every entry independently with probability $p$ (the paper uses $p=0.3$). The perturbation is drawn from a small uniform range, e.g.

   \[
   \lambda_i \leftarrow \lambda_i + \Delta,\qquad 
   \Delta\sim\mathcal{U}(-\delta,\delta),
   \]

   where $\delta$ corresponds to one step of the search grid ($0.01$).  
   After this step the vector may violate the ordering constraint.
3. **Repair** – enforce monotonicity:
   * **Sorting** is sufficient because all admissible values live on an ordered grid.
   * The sorted vector automatically respects $\lambda_i\le\lambda_{i+1}$; any value that fell outside the allowed interval is clipped back into $[\,1.0,\;1.25\,s\,]$.

The resulting child therefore differs from its parent only by a few local changes, yet it always lies in the feasible region required by NTK theory (lower‑frequency dimensions may be interpolated more aggressively than higher‑frequency ones).  

> *Why not project onto a more sophisticated “cone’’?*  
> In practice sorting already yields an optimal projection onto the set of weakly increasing sequences under an $\\ell_2$ loss (the isotonic regression solution). Since our objective function – perplexity – is evaluated only after repair, there is no benefit in using a more complex projection.

---

### 2.  How does **crossover** avoid breaking monotonicity?

Crossover mixes two elite parents ($\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(a)}$ and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(b)}$). The paper does not spell out an exact scheme, but the usual practice in this kind of constrained evolutionary search—consistent with their description—is:

1\. **Uniform per‑dimension choice**: for each index $i$,

   \[
   \lambda^{(c)}_i = 
   \begin{cases}
       \lambda^{(a)}_i &\text{with probability }0.5\\[4pt]
       \lambda^{(b)}_i &\text{with probability }0.5 .
   \end{cases}
   \]

   This yields an offspring that may again violate ordering.
2\. **Repair** exactly as in mutation: sort the resulting sequence and clip any out‑of‑range entries.

An alternative that some implementations adopt is *arithmetic crossover* ($\tfrac12(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(a)}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(b)})$), which already preserves ordering when both parents are ordered, but uniform crossover followed by sorting is what fits most naturally into `Crossover_with_mono_constraint` as described in Algorithm 1.

---

### 3\.  Does forcing monotonicity “shrink” diversity?

At first glance it appears that requiring $\lambda_0\le\dots\le\lambda_{D-1}$ removes many points from the original hyper‑grid
$[\,1,\;s\times 1.25\,]^{\,D}$. In reality:

* The number of **weakly increasing** integer sequences on a grid of step $0.01$ remains astronomically large—still exponential in $D$. For a 64‑dimensional RoPE embedding and an extension ratio $s=8$, even after imposing monotonicity there are roughly  
  $\displaystyle {\,400+64-1\choose64-1}\approx10^{38}$ distinct candidates.
* The discarded region consists exclusively of vectors that contradict known theoretical desiderata (higher frequencies should receive *smaller* interpolation factors). Those vectors are almost guaranteed to be poor initializations for RoPE and would almost certainly yield very high perplexities.
* Empirically, Table 6 (ablation on “search dimension’’) shows that adding the monotone constraint actually **improves** final perplexities while keeping the search fast enough to finish in \< 40 generations. This indicates that enough genetic variation survives – indeed most generations contain individuals whose λ‑vectors differ substantially across many dimensions.

Therefore, rather than harming exploration, the constraint **guides** it toward a far denser subspace where good solutions live, yielding higher sample efficiency without perceptibly limiting diversity.

---

### 4\.  Putting it together – why these tricks matter

The paper emphasises two optimisation tricks:

| Trick | What it does | Why it helps |
|------|--------------|--------------|
| **Optimised initial population** | Seed population with PI, NTK and YaRN baselines before random mutations | Guarantees early individuals are already reasonable; evolution can fine‑tune them instead of starting from scratch |
| **Monotonic non‑decreasing constraint** | Sort after every mutation/crossover & clip to allowed range | Cuts down wasted perplexity calls dramatically (`only RoPE that satisfies…is applied`), while still exploring an astronomically large feasible set |

Because perplexity evaluation costs dominate (each call requires running a long‑context forward pass), eliminating infeasible candidates at generation time translates directly into orders‑of‑magnitude speedups—exactly what enables searching over extension ratios as high as $512\times$.

---

### TL;DR

*Mutation*: perturb each $\lambda_i$ independently (drawn from a tiny uniform perturbation), then **sort** (or isotonic‐regress) so that $\lambda_0\le …\le\lambda_{D-1}$.

*Crossover*: combine two parents dimensionwise (uniform or arithmetic), then **sort** again to repair any ordering violations; final values are clipped to $[\,1,\;1.25\,s\,]$.

The monotone repair discards only those vectors that contradict NTK theory, leaving an enormously large feasible region; consequently diversity among offspring remains ample while search efficiency improves markedly—a fact confirmed by both ablation studies and final perplexity results in the paper.​

### "Crowded" positions in linear PI – what does this mean geometrically?
Question:
"Linear positional interpolation reduces all rotation angles by factor $s$, making position information very 'crowded'" (Section 3). I am trying to visualize:
* In angular space $(\cos,n)$ versus $(\sin,n)$ what constitutes crowding—do many tokens map onto nearby points on the unit circle?
* How does crowding impact self‑attention softmax computations concretely – e.g., causing less discriminative dot products between queries/keys?
* Are there quantitative metrics (e.g., minimal angular separation statistics) that capture crowding severity?
Answer:
The “crowded‑positions’’ remark in Section 3 refers to what happens when the **linear positional interpolation (PI)** rule is applied to RoPE.  
RoPE represents a token at absolute position $n$ as a set of two‑dimensional rotations  

$$
\bigr[\cos (n\,\theta_{0}),\:;\:\sin (n\,\theta_{0}),\:;
      \cos (n\,\theta_{1}),\:;\:\sin (n\,\theta_{1}),\:;\:\dots\bigr],
      \qquad
      \theta_{i}= \beta^{-i},\:;
      \beta=\theta^{2/d},
$$  

so that each pair $(\cos ,\: \sinh)$ lives on its own unit circle (or “frequency plane’’).  
If we plot the points for one frequency plane we obtain equally spaced points around the circle, with an angular step  

$$
\vDelta_{\vartheta,i}= \frac{1}{\beta^{\,i}} .
\tag{1}
$$  

---  

### Geometry of crowding under linear PI  

Linear PI rescales every rotary angle by the extension ratio $s>1$:  

$$
 n~{}\longrightarrow~{}\frac{n}{s},
        \qquad
        {\rm so~that}\qquad
        \vartheta_{i}(n)= \frac{n}{s\,\beta^{\,i}} .
\tag{2}
$$  

Equation (2) leaves the *maximum* angle attained at the farthest position unchanged (${\displaystyle 
        \vartheta _{i}(L')=\vartheta _{i}(L)}$
because $L'=sL$), but it shrinks the *step* between successive tokens from $\vDelta_{\vartheta,i}$ in (1) to  

$$
\vDelta_{\vartheta,i}^{\,{\rm PI}}=
        \frac{1}{s\,\beta^{\,i}}
        ~=~{\displaystyle\vDelta_{\vartheta,i}\over s}.
\tag{3}
$$  

Thus the set of points that previously occupied one full revolution now occupy only a ${{}_{{}\!}}\!{}{\displaystyle  {\ } }${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}$${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}${}{${$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}{$}$​fraction​of​it​.​In​other​words​many​more​token​indices​are​packed​into​the​same​arc​length​of​each​unit​circle – they become **geometrically crowded**. If you visualise the two‐dimensional planes side‐by‐side you would see long rows of points that are almost indistinguishable from one another, especially in higher‐frequency planes where $\beta^{\,i}$ is small and the step size was already tiny before scaling.

### Consequence for self‑attention  

In RoPE‑augmented self‑attention each query–key inner product acquires an extra cosine factor per frequency plane:  

$$

        (\text{content part})+
        {\sum}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}\!
        \cos\bigr(
                \vartheta _{i}(n_q)-%
                \vartheta _{i}( n_k )
              \bigr).
\tag{4}
$$  

When two positions are far apart their angle difference is large and the cosine term can become negative, sharply lowering the similarity. After linear PI the difference between two nearby indices is reduced by a factor $s$
[see (3)], so for most pairs we have  

$$
|\vartheta _{i}( n_q)-%
\vartheta _{i}( n_k )|
   ~{\approx }~
   {\displaystyle{| n_q-n_k |\over s\,%
                     \beta ^{{}\! i}}}
   ~{\ll }~ 1 ,
   ~~{\rm and }\quad%
   \cos (\cdot )~{\approx }~ 1 .

The model therefore loses its ability to “focus’’ on truly relevant context – this is exactly what the authors refer to when they say that linear PI makes position information “very crowded’’ and harms performance at large extension ratios.
