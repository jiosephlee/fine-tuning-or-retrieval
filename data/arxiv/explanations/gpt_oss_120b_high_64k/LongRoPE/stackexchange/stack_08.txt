\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the intuition behind leaving the first $\hat n$ tokens unchanged during interpolation?
Question:
In Finding 2 (Section 3.2), experiments keep the first $\hat n \in \{0,2,4,...,256\}$ positions at their original RoPE angles while interpolating later tokens. The authors argue that early tokens receive larger attention scores.
* How exactly do larger attention scores make those positions more sensitive to positional distortion?
* Is there any empirical metric (e.g., attention weight distribution) supporting this hypothesis?
* Does optimal $\hat n$ depend solely on target extension ratio, or also on model architecture/dataset characteristics?
Understanding why preserving initial positions helps would deepen my grasp of LongRoPE’s design.
Answer:
**Answer**

The first $\widehat n$ tokens are left untouched because they carry a disproportionate amount of “positional signal’’ in a transformer’s self‑attention matrix.  
In a RoPE‑based model each key–query inner product contains the term  

$$
Q_iK_j^{\top}\;\;\propto\;\;\mathbf{h}_i^{\top}\,\mathbf{h}_j\, 
      \cos\tBigl(\tfrac{j-i}{\,\lambda_{c(i,j)}\,}\Bigr)
      +\,.\!.\!,
$$  

where $\lambda_{c(i,j)}$ is the rescale factor applied to the rotary angle of dimension $c(i,j)$.  When the position index $j$ is small the cosine factor varies slowly with respect‑to $j$; consequently the dot product tends to be larger for early keys than for far‑away ones. After soft‑max normalisation these large dot products become the dominant attention weights $\alpha_{i,j}$. In other words, *early token positions receive larger attention scores* and therefore contribute more heavily to every subsequent hidden state.

If an interpolation scheme distorts the original angles at those early indices—e.g. by scaling them with a factor $\lambda_i>1$ when extending from length $L$ to $L' = sL$—the cosine term is altered for a very large set of $(i,j)$ pairs because *every* query $i>j$ uses the same corrupted key $K_j$. The resulting error propagates through all layers and quickly amplifies into higher perplexity. By keeping the first $\widehat n$ angles at their pretrained values (${\mathbb I}( j< \widehat n)=1$, for $j< \wide hat{n}$), we guarantee that the most heavily weighted entries in the attention matrix stay faithful to the original geometry, while still allowing interpolation for later positions whose contributions are already attenuated by smaller raw scores.

The authors verified this intuition empirically in **Finding 2** (Section 3.2). They ran two families of experiments—linear PI and NTK—while varying $\widehat n \in \{0,2,4,\dots ,256\}$. Table 2 (reproduced in the paper) shows a clear monotonic improvement in perplexity up to an intermediate value of $\widehat n$, after which further increase no longer helps because the benefit of protecting additional positions is outweighed by the reduced flexibility needed at far‑away tokens. Although no explicit “attention‑weight distribution’’ plot is presented in this work, similar phenomena have been reported in prior literature (e.g., *Streaming LLM* and *LM‑Infinite*), where average per‑position soft‑max weights drop sharply after a few hundred tokens. That pattern provides an indirect metric supporting **the claim:** positions with high mean $\alpha_{\cdot ,j}$ are precisely those whose rotation angles are safest left unaltered.

Regarding **what determines the optimal $\widehat n$,** three primary factors emerge from their experiments:

1️⃣ **Target extension ratio $s = L'/L$.**  
   When $s$ is modest (e.g., $s=2$), only a handful of initial tokens need protection because down‑scaling of angles is small; increasing $\widehat n$ beyond a few dozen yields diminishing returns. As $s$ grows (the authors examined 8 k → 16 k extensions, i.e., $s\simeq4$), larger untouched prefixes become beneficial—up to roughly 64–128 positions—because larger down‑scaling would otherwise corrupt more heavily weighted early keys.

2️⃣ **Model‐and‐data characteristics play a secondary role.**  
   The search space includes per‐dimension rescale factors $\lambda_i$, which adapt automatically to architectural details such as hidden size~$d$, number of heads, or depth—features that affect how fast different frequency bands decay across dimensions. When applied both to LLaMA‑2 7 B and Mistral 7 B models, very similar optimal prefixes emerged (Table 2), suggesting architecture alone does not radically shift $\widehat n$. However, datasets with markedly different token‐distribution patterns could—in principle—alter how much information resides at early versus late positions; e.g., dialogue corpora where crucial cues appear near prompts might favour a larger $\widehat n$. The authors do not explore this axis explicitly but acknowledge that “optimal $\widehat n$

depends primarily on target extension length’’ while leaving open future work on dataset sensitivity.

In summary, larger attention scores make early token positions disproportionately influential throughout downstream computation; preserving their original rotary angles prevents widespread geometric distortion once longer contexts are introduced. This hypothesis is corroborated by lower perplexities when an untouched prefix is employed—even though an explicit per‐position attention histogram was omitted from this paper—as well as by broader observations reported elsewhere. The sweet spot for $\widehat n$ scales mainly with how aggressively one stretches its context window, whereas modest variations across model families hint at secondary dependence on architecture or data distribution—but these effects were not isolated experimentally in this study.​
