\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What are the two forms of non‑uniformity identified in LongRoPE?
Question:
Section 3.2 claims that effective positional interpolation must consider (a) varying RoPE dimensions and (b) token positions within a sequence. I would like clarification on:
* How exactly do "different RoPE dimensions" exhibit non‑uniform information entropy? Is there empirical evidence supporting this claim?
* Why do early token positions ($\hat n$ tokens) benefit from less interpolation, and how is this related to attention scores in practice?
* Do these two non‑uniformities interact multiplicatively or additively when constructing $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i, \hat n)$?
A concrete description would help me grasp why simply using a single uniform scaling fails for very long contexts.
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper *LongRoPE* points out that a naïve “one‐size‑fits‑all’’ rescaling of Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) is ineffective once we try to push an LLM far beyond its original context length.  
Two orthogonal sources of **non‑uniformity** must be taken into account:

1. **Along the RoPE dimension axis** – different sinusoidal frequencies carry different amounts of positional information.  
2. **Along the token index axis** – the first $\widehat{n}$ tokens act as privileged “anchor’’ keys for most subsequent queries and therefore should be disturbed as little as possible.

Below I explain why these phenomena arise, what empirical evidence supports them in the paper, how they are reflected mathematically by $\mathbb I(\widehat{\lambda}_i,\widehat n)$, and why a single uniform scaling fails for extremely long contexts.

---

### 1. Why do RoPE dimensions have non‑uniform information entropy?

RoPE encodes a position $n$ by a set of sinusoidal pairs  

$$
[\cos(n\theta_0),\,\sin(n\theta_0),\,\cos(n\theta_1),\,\sin(n\theta_1),\,\dots,\,
  \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}),\,\sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})].
$$  

where  

$$
\theta_i = \theta^{-2 i/d}, 
\tqquad 
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\tqquad 
n_\!{}\!\theta_i = \frac{n}{\beta^{\, i}} .
$$  

The index $i$ therefore determines a **frequency**:  
small $i$ → high frequency → fast oscillation,  
large $i$ → low frequency → slow oscillation.

Intuitively

* **High‑frequency dimensions** change dramatically from one token to the next, which makes them excellent at distinguishing *nearby* positions but they quickly start “wrapping’’ after only a few hundred steps. Past that range their values become almost periodic, contributing little new variance about where we actually are in the sequence.
* **Low‑frequency dimensions** vary only slowly, so even after tens or hundreds of thousands of steps they still change appreciably; these dimensions are what allow a model to keep track of *coarse* location when we look far ahead.

Because mutual information between the true index $n$ and each sinusoid is proportional to how much that sinusoid varies over the range actually seen during training, the **entropy contributed by each dimension is far from uniform** – it decays monotonically with $i$.



#### Empirical confirmation  

The authors run an *evolutionary search* over per‑dimension scale factors $\lambda_i$.  
In Table 1 (the paper’s “exp 1”), LLaMA‑2 7B equipped with individually tuned $\lambda_i$’s obtains markedly lower perplexities on PG‑19 and Proof‑Pile than PI ($\lambda_i=s~∀i$), NTK ($\lambda_i=s^{\,i}$) or YaRN’s hand‑crafted groups.

More revealing is Fig.~3c (not reproduced here): after search, the resulting set $\{\lambda_i\bigr\rbrace$ forms an **increasing sequence**, exactly what NTK theory predicts – low‑frequency/high‑index dimensions are allowed larger downscaling because they can tolerate more compression without losing discriminative power, while high‑frequency/low‑index dimensions receive only mild scaling (sometimes even $\lambda_i≈1$).

An ablation (“search dimension only”) further shows that simply allowing each dimension its own $\lambda$ already cuts perplexity substantially compared with plain linear PI, confirming that *the variation across $i$ matters*.

### 2. Why should the first $\boldsymbol{\widehat n}$ tokens undergo less interpolation?

In a causal Transformer every query at position $p>0$ attends over all previous keys $k<p$. Consequently:

* The **early keys** appear repeatedly in many soft‐max denominators.
* Their cumulative attention mass tends to be larger than that of later keys – this has been observed empirically in “Streaming LLM’’ [Han et al., 2023] where prefixes act as global memory.
* If we distort those early keys’ positional angles by applying a strong downscaling factor, we modify *many* dot products simultaneously, which propagates error through every subsequent layer.



Think about the query–key inner product  

$$
q_{p}^{T} k_{k}= q_{p}^{T}\bigl(R_{\frac{k}{\,\beta^{\, i}}}\,W_k\bigr),
$$  

where $R_{\cdot}$ denotes rotation by the rotary angle.  
If we replace $R_{\frac{k}{\,\beta^{\, i}}}$ by $R_{\frac{k}{\,\lambda_{i}\beta^{\, i}}}$ for all $k<p$, every term involving those early keys changes roughly by  

$$
R_{\,(1-\lambda_{i})k/\beta^{\, i}},
$$  

and because such terms dominate many softmax normalisers, even a modest misalignment can cause large shifts in attention distributions.



The paper validates this intuition experimentally (Table 2). They keep the first $\widehat n∈\{0,2,…,256\}$ positions untouched (“no interpolation”) while applying PI or NTK thereafter. Two consistent trends emerge:

1. **Perplexity drops** whenever $\widehat n>0;$  
2. The optimal $\widehat n$ grows with extension ratio $s=L'/L.$  

When extending from 4 k → 8 k only a handful of anchors suffice ($\widehat n=8–12$), whereas for 4 k → 32 k or beyond it is beneficial to preserve dozens—or even hundreds—of early positions because more new indices must be compressed into the same rotary space.



Thus “less interpolation for early tokens’’ directly protects high‑attention keys from being corrupted.

### 4\. Why does a single uniform scale fail for very long contexts?

If we set $\displaystyle λ_i=s~∀~i,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;
̂ n=0,$   
every sinusoid—high frequency included—is compressed by exactly the same factor.
Two detrimental consequences follow:


| Effect | High‑frequency dims ($i≈0)$ | Low‑frequency dims ($i≈d/2−1)$ | Consequence |
|--------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|-------------|
| Uniform downscaling | Rotations become so rapid that distinct nearby indices collapse onto nearly identical vectors (“crowding”). | Rotations already change slowly; further compression narrows their reachable range dramatically – fewer distinguishable values inside an astronomically large window.| Strong loss of locality → attention cannot differentiate close neighbours → exploding perplexity after modest extensions |
| No anchor preservation | Early keys lose their exact geometric relationships → every downstream query suffers from mismatched dot products.| Same issue amplified because low frequencies dominate long‑range discrimination.| Attention scores become noisy throughout entire sequence.|

By allowing **different $\displaystyle λ_i$ along $i,$ LongRoPE keeps high frequencies almost intact (small $λ$, preserving fine local discrimination) while letting low frequencies be stretched more aggressively (large $λ$, supplying enough “room’’ for faraway indices without squeezing them into indistinguishable points). By additionally **excluding** the first $\displaystylê n>0,$ anchors retain their original geometry, preventing systemic drift throughout deep layers.

## Summary

* **Non‑uniformity across RoPE dimensions:** Each dimension encodes position at a distinct frequency; therefore its *information capacity* varies monotonically from fine‐grained/high‐entropy early dims to coarse‐grained/lower‐entropy later dims. The paper’s evolutionary search uncovers an increasing schedule $λ_0≤λ_1≤⋯≤λ_{d/2-1}$; ablations demonstrate substantial perplexity gains compared with any method using one universal scale.
* **Non‑uniformity across token indices:** Early tokens serve repeatedly as keys receiving large attention masses; distorting their rotary angles harms many subsequent queries. Empirically preserving an initial block ($̂ n>0)$ improves language modeling—optimal block size grows with how far we stretch the context window.
* **Combination rule:** The effective rescaling factor is given by Eq.(4). It applies either identity or $λ_i^{-1}$ depending on whether $n<̂ n$. This piecewise product—not an additional cross term—captures both axes jointly.
* **Why uniform scaling collapses:** A single $λ$ squeezes all frequencies equally *and* eliminates anchor preservation simultaneously, leading quickly to “crowded’’ positional codes and degraded attention scores once extensions exceed roughly four–eight times beyond training length.

Consequently, LongRoPE’s dual–nonuniform treatment provides an initialization that preserves critical positional information across both axes, enabling extensions up to two million tokens while retaining performance on short contexts—a feat unattainable with any uniform positional interpolation scheme alone.
