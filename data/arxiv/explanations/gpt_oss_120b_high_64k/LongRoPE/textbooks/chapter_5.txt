\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 5: 5. Formulating Positional Interpolation as an Optimization Problem

# Problem Statement

Extending a large language model (LLM) beyond its pretrained context window requires us to modify the rotary positional embedding (RoPE) so that it can accommodate new token indices without degrading the model’s ability to predict the next token.  From a learning‑theoretic viewpoint this requirement can be expressed as an optimization problem: given a target context length \(L'\) (for example \(L' = 2048\,\text{k}\) tokens), choose a modification of the RoPE such that the expected next‑token loss—measured by perplexity—on sequences of length at least \(L'\) is minimized.  Formally,

\[
\underset{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X},\,|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'}{\operatorname*{arg\,min}}\;
\mathcal{L}\!\left(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE}),\mathbf{x}\right),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\mathbf{X}\) denotes a collection of long documents used for evaluation,  
\(\mathcal{L}\) denotes the standard negative log‑likelihood loss whose exponentiation yields perplexity, and  
\(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE})\) represents the language model equipped with a potentially altered RoPE mapping.  

Intuitively, Eq. (1) asks us to find those changes to the positional encoding that keep the model’s predictions as accurate as possible even when it processes sequences many times longer than those seen during pre‑training.

# Variables

The only degrees of freedom available in our formulation are (i) a per‑dimension rescale factor \(\lambda_i\) applied to the rotary angle of RoPE’s \(i^{\text{th}}\) sinusoid and (ii) a token‑position threshold \(\hat n\) indicating how many initial tokens should retain their original embedding without any scaling.  

For a token at position \(n\) and RoPE dimension index \(i\in\{0,\dots,\tfrac d2-1\}\), let \(\beta = \theta^{2/d}\), where \(\theta=10\,000\) is the default base frequency used by most LLMs. The original rotary angle before any extension is \(\frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\). After applying our rescaling scheme we obtain

\[
\Bigl[\cos\bigl(\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{i}}\bigr),
      \sin\bigl(\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\tfrac{n}{\beta^{i}}\bigr), \dots\bigr],
\tag{2}
\]

where

\[
\mathbb I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)=
   \begin{cases}
      1                      & \text{if } n < \hat n ,\\[4pt]
      \displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i}
                           & \text{if } n \ge \hat n .
   \end{cases}
\]

Thus \(\lambda_i>1\) corresponds to interpolating (compressing) angles so that distant positions fit inside the original angle range, whereas \(\lambda_i=1\) leaves the angle untouched (pure extrapolation).  
The vector \((\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1})\) captures **non‑uniformity across dimensions**, while \(\hat n\) captures **non‑uniformity across token positions**—both phenomena were empirically observed in Section 3 of the LongRoPE paper.

# Search Space Definition

To make Eq. (1) tractable we must bound each variable’s admissible region:

* **Per‑dimension rescale factors** – For a given extension ratio 
\(s=\frac{L'}{L}\)
we allow 
\( \lambda_i \in [\,1.0,\; s\times1.25\,] \)
with granularity \(0.01\).  
The lower bound \(1.0\) corresponds to pure extrapolation (no compression), while the upper bound permits modest over‑interpolation beyond what naïve linear interpolation would suggest (\(s\times1.25 > s\)).  

* **Token‑position threshold** – The set 
\( \hat n \in
   \{0,\,1,\,2,\,4,\,8,\,12,\,16,\,20,
    24,\,28,\,32,\,64,\,128,\,256\} 
   \)
covers plausible numbers of initial tokens whose embeddings are left unchanged.  
When \(\hat n=0\) every position undergoes scaling; larger values progressively protect more early tokens—a design motivated by findings that early tokens receive disproportionately high attention weights during inference.

* **Monotonicity constraint** – Theory from Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) analysis suggests that low‑frequency dimensions (\(i\) large) can tolerate more compression than high‑frequency ones (\(i\) small). Accordingly we enforce

\[
    \lambda_0
    \;\leq\
    \lambda_1
    \;\leq\
    \dots
    \;\leq\
    \lambda_{\tfrac d2-1},
    \tag{3}
\]

which dramatically reduces admissible configurations while preserving an ordering consistent with information‑encoding intuition: higher‑frequency components retain finer granularity of positional resolution.

Together these specifications carve out a **search space** containing all candidate interpolation schemes that respect both empirical non‑uniformities and theoretical desiderata.

# Objective Function

During search we cannot afford to evaluate full training loss on massive corpora; instead we approximate Eq. (1)’s objective by measuring **perplexity** on a tiny validation subset (\(5\bigr.\) random documents from PG19 or similar long‑text benchmarks). Perplexity \(P(\theta)\) for a candidate set of parameters \(\theta=(\{\lambda_i\},\hat n)\) is defined as

\[
P(\theta)=
   \exp
   {\Bigl(
       -\,%
       {\tfrac1N}
       {\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(w_t|w_{<t})
       }
     {\Bigr)},
     %{\;}
   ,
   %\\
   %where N counts all tokens in evaluation sequences,
   %and p_\theta denotes the model’s predictive distribution under
   %the modified RoPE.
}
{
}
%
]%
,
}
%
Here \(w_t\) denotes token \(t,\)
\(N=\sum_{x∈S}|x|\)` is total token count over the chosen sample set \(S,\)
and \(p_\theta(w_t|w_{<t})`\)`is computed by feeding each sequence through LLM after applying Eq.\,(2).
Because perplexity directly exponentiates the average negative log‑likelihood,
minimizing \(P(\theta)\)
is mathematically identical to minimizing J in Eq.\,(1).
Thus our **objective function** for any candidate solution simply returns its perplexity on this validation slice; lower values indicate better preservation of next‑token prediction quality after extending context window length to \(L'\).

# Why Exhaustive Search Is Infeasible

Even after imposing monotonic ordering (3), enumerating every admissible configuration remains astronomically expensive due to combinatorial explosion:

* With step size \(0.01,\)
the interval 
\( [\,1.0,s\times1.25]  (s=4)\)
contains roughly 
\(400  =  (s\times125)\)
distinct values for each \(λ_i.\)

* A typical transformer encoder such as LLaMA 2‑7B uses 
\(d/2 = 64  .\)

Thus just counting assignments satisfying monotonicity yields roughly

\[
400^{64}
   ≈
   4\times10^{167}
   .
   %This figure stems from treating each λi independently;
   %the monotone constraint reduces possibilities but only marginally.
]\]

Multiplying by all choices for \( ̂n ∈14 {\rm options}\)
produces on the order of

\(4\times10^{167}\times14 ≈ 6\times10^{168}\)

candidate settings—a number far beyond any conceivable compute budget.
Even evaluating one candidate requires feeding several thousand tokens through an LLM and recording its likelihoods—a process that may take seconds on modern GPUs; scaling this up leads immediately to impractical runtimes measured in millennia or more.

Consequently **exhaustive enumeration** cannot be used as a practical optimisation strategy.
Instead LongRoPE introduces an *evolutionary search* procedure (Algorithm 1 in the paper), which leverages population‑based mutation and crossover operators together with early pruning via monotonic constraints.
This approach reliably discovers high‑quality interpolation schemes within dozens of iterations rather than exploring an astronomically large space blindly.


--- 

In summary, framing positional interpolation as an explicit optimization problem isolates precisely which quantities must be tuned (\(\{\lambda_i,\hat n\\}), defines their feasible region grounded in both NTK theory and empirical observations about early‐token importance, provides a concrete surrogate objective measurable via perplexity on short validation slices, and demonstrates why naïve brute force was out of reach—thereby motivating LongRoPE’s efficient evolutionary search pipeline described later in this work.
