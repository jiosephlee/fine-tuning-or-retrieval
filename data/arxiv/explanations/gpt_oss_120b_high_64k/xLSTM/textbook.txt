xLSTM: Extended Long Short‑Term Memory – Introduction & Motivation

# Historical development from simple RNNs → vanishing gradients

The earliest recurrent neural networks attempted to model sequential data by feeding the hidden state at time \(t\!-\!1\) back into the network alongside the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\). In its most basic form a recurrent unit computes 
\[
\mathbf{h}_t = \phi(\mathbf{W}_{xh}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b}_h),
\] 
where \(\phi\) is a pointwise non‑linearity such as \(\tanh\) or \(\sigma\). Although this formulation is conceptually simple, training it with gradient‑based methods quickly ran into a severe obstacle known as the *vanishing (or exploding) gradient* problem \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:00book}. Because gradients are repeatedly multiplied by the Jacobian of \(\phi\) at each time step, signals that should propagate information from far‑away timesteps either shrink exponentially toward zero or grow without bound. As a result, naive recurrent units could only learn short‑range dependencies even though the model architecture itself allowed arbitrarily long histories. This limitation motivated a wave of research focused on stabilising gradient flow through time, culminating in the design of more sophisticated recurrent cells that could preserve information over long horizons.

# Key ideas behind the original LSTM (constant‑error carousel, three gating mechanisms)

The Long Short‑Term Memory network was introduced precisely to address gradient degradation by redesigning how information is stored and updated across time steps \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:97nips,Hochreiter:97}. Its core consists of a *cell state* \(c_t\) that runs through the network via an additive pathway often called the **constant‑error carousel**. Unlike the multiplicative hidden‑state dynamics of simple RNNs, the cell state evolves according to
\begin{align}
c_t &= f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t,
    &&\text{cell update} \label{eq:lstm_cell}\\
h_t &= o_t\,\psi(c_t),
    &&\text{hidden output},
\end{align}
which appears as Equation~\eqref{eq:lstm_idea} in the original formulation. Three gates modulate this process:

* The **input gate** \(i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t)\) decides how much of the candidate input \(z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t)\) should be written into the cell.
* The **forget gate** \(f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t)\) determines how much of the previous cell content \(c_{t-1}\) should be retained.
* The **output gate** \(o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t)\) controls how strongly the normalized cell state \(\psi(c_t)\) contributes to the hidden output \(h_t\).

Each pre‑activation \(\tilde{i}_t,\tilde{f}_t,\tilde{o}_t,\tilde{z}_t\) is an affine combination of the current input \(\mathbf{x}_t\) and the previous hidden state \(h_{t-1}\), for example
\[
\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^{\top}_{i}\mathbf{x}_t + r_i\,h_{t-1}+b_i,
\]
with analogous expressions for the other gates and candidate input. The sigmoid non‑linearity \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x))\) constrains gate activations to lie between zero and one, thereby enabling smooth interpolation between “open” and “closed” states. This gating mechanism together with additive updates yields gradients that propagate unchanged through long sequences—hence “constant error”—and therefore mitigates vanishing gradients. The final non‑linearity \(\psi(\cdot)\), typically a hyperbolic tangent, normalises or squashes the potentially unbounded cell state before it contributes to downstream layers.

# Success stories of LSTMs across NLP, reinforcement learning, scientific modeling

Since their inception LSTMs have become a workhorse for any task requiring sequential reasoning. In natural language processing they powered early breakthroughs such as character‑level text generation (\citep{Graves:13}) and handwriting synthesis (\citep{Graves:13}), demonstrating an ability to capture long‑range syntax from raw data streams. Sequence‑to‑sequence translation systems (\citep{Sutskever:14nips}) relied on bidirectional LSTMs to encode source sentences before decoding target language tokens—a paradigm that persisted well after attention mechanisms arrived. Their expressive memory also made them suitable for program evaluation (\citep{Zaremba:14arxiv}), image captioning (\citep{Karpathy:15,Hossain:19}), source‐code generation (\citep{Karpathy:15blog}), and even hydrological forecasting (\citep{Kratzert:18,Kratzert:19,Nearing:24}). 

In reinforcement learning deep recurrent policies have consistently outperformed feed‑forward baselines on temporally extended tasks. Notable examples include AlphaStar’s strategic reasoning in StarCraft II (\citep{Vinyals:17short}) and OpenAI Five’s coordination in Dota 2 (\citep{OpenAI:19}), where LSTMs learned to store game state over hundreds of timesteps. A particularly striking application lies in controlling magnetic confinement fusion reactors (\citep{Degrave:22short}), wherein recurrent networks maintain stability under rapidly changing plasma dynamics.

Beyond these high‑profile achievements LSTMs have shed light on *interpretability*: neurons selectively encoding number semantics (\citep{Lakretz:19}), syntax (\citep{Bau:19}) or sentiment (\citep{Radford:17arxiv}) emerge spontaneously when training on large corpora. This suggests that LSTMs are capable of forming abstract internal representations that go beyond rote memorisation—a property still coveted by modern large language models.

# The Transformer breakthrough and its consequences for large‑scale language modelling

The landscape shifted dramatically with the advent of self‑attention based Transformers \citep{Vaswani:17}. By replacing recurrence with pairwise attention between all tokens in a sequence, Transformers achieved two decisive advantages:

1. **Full parallelism**—the entire sequence can be processed simultaneously during training because each layer’s computation depends only on matrix multiplications rather than sequential hidden‑state updates.
2. **Quadratic expressive capacity**—attention weights capture pairwise interactions directly, enabling richer context modelling especially when scaled up with many layers and heads.

These properties allowed Transformers to scale efficiently across massive datasets and model sizes, culminating in powerful Large Language Models such as GPT‑3 (\citep{Brown:20short}) and subsequent variants from diverse research groups worldwide. As a result, for many benchmark tasks transformers supplanted LSTMs entirely; document-level language modelling shifted from RNN‑centric pipelines toward wholly attention‐driven architectures after 2017.\footnote{The transition was accelerated also by hardware developments favouring dense matrix operations over recursions.}

Nevertheless, Transformers inherit their own limitations—quadratic memory cost with respect to sequence length—and they obscure an explicit notion of persistent memory that was central to classic RNNs. This prompted researchers to ask whether recurrent cells could be revisited at massive scales while retaining their intrinsic ability to store information over long horizons without incurring prohibitive computational overheads.\

# The central research question addressed by the paper

Motivated by this tension between *scalability* (a Transformer strength) and *persistent explicit memory* (an LSTM strength), the authors pose a succinct yet profound query:

> **How far can language modelling performance advance if we scale Long Short‑Term Memory networks to billions of parameters while mitigating their known shortcomings?**

In other words, instead of discarding recurrence entirely, can one retain or even enhance its core mechanisms—constant error carousel plus gated updates—and enrich them sufficiently so that an LSTM‑based model competes head‑to‑head with contemporary Transformer or State Space Model approaches? Addressing this requires overcoming three documented drawbacks of vanilla LSTMs:

1. **Inability to revise storage decisions**, exemplified by nearest neighbour search tasks where previously stored values cannot be replaced when more relevant evidence arrives later.
2. **Limited storage capacity**, caused by compressing all information into a single scalar per cell; this manifests as poor rare token prediction because nuanced contexts cannot be faithfully encoded.
3. **Sequential bottleneck due to hidden–hidden connections**, which prevents parallel computation over timesteps and thus hampers scalability on modern hardware.\

The paper therefore frames these three obstacles as *research challenges* whose resolution could unlock high‑performance recurrent language models at scales comparable to today's Transformers.\

# Overview of the proposed solution – extended LSTMs (xLSTMs)

To answer this question, the authors introduce **Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM)** architectures that augment both gating dynamics and memory representation:

* **Exponential gating** replaces—or optionally augments—the traditional sigmoid activations on input and forget gates with an exponential function \(\exp(\cdot)\). By doing so gates can express multiplicative scaling far beyond [0, 1] while still being controllable through an auxiliary normaliser state that tracks cumulative gate magnitudes.
* Two novel memory structures are proposed:
    - **Scalar LSTM (sLSTM)** retains a single scalar per cell but adds *memory mixing* across cells using recurrent weight matrices together with exponential gating; this enables revision of stored contents while preserving compatibility with residual block circuitry.
    - **Matrix LSTM (mLSTM)** expands each cell’s state from a scalar \(c_t\in\mathbb R\) to a full matrix \(\mathbf C_t\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\). Storage follows a *covariance update rule* akin to bidirectional associative memories:
      \[
      \mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1} + i_t\,\mathbf v_{t}\mathbf k_{t}^{\!\top},
      \]
      where key–value vectors \((\mathbf k_t,\mathbf v_t)\) arise from linear projections of the current input, and retrieval uses a query vector \(\mathbf q_t\). This matrix formulation dramatically enlarges storage capacity while remaining fully parallelisable because no hidden–hidden recurrence is required.
  
Both sLSTM and mLSTM are wrapped inside **xLSTM blocks**, which are residual modules equipped either with *post up-projection* (for sLSTM) or *pre up-projection* (for mLSTM). Stacking these blocks using standard pre–LayerNorm residual backbones yields deep **xLSTM architectures** capable of handling hundreds of millions—or even billions—of parameters.\ 

In sum, xLSTMs aim to preserve what made classic LSTMs attractive—a principled mechanism for long‐term memory—while eliminating their historical impediments through exponential gating, richer matrix memories, and modern residual design patterns borrowed from transformer engineering.\  

The rest of this textbook will explore each component—starting from historical context—to build intuition about why these modifications matter for large‐scale language modelling.\

# 2. Foundations of Recurrent Neural Networks and Classic LSTMs  

In this chapter we develop a clear mathematical foundation for discrete‑time recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and then specialize this foundation to the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture as originally proposed in the early 1990s.  The presentation follows directly from the definitions and equations introduced in the “Extended Long Short‑Term Memory” paper, extending them with intuition that helps a first‑time learner understand why each term appears, how gradients propagate through time, and how various design choices affect expressive power and trainability.

## Recap of Discrete‑Time Recurrent Neural Networks  

A discrete‑time recurrent neural network processes an ordered sequence  
\[
\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots ,\mathbf{x}_{T}\},
\]  
where each element \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}}}\) is presented at integer time step \(t=1,\dots ,T\).  
At every step the network maintains an internal hidden representation \(\mathbf{h}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hid}}}\).  
The hallmark recurrence relation updates this hidden state by combining **two** sources:
the freshly supplied input vector and the **previous** hidden activation:
\[
\boxed{\;\;\mathbf{h}_{t}=g\!\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{x}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}}_{\text{input contribution}}
            +\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{h}\,\mathbf{h}_{\,t-1}}_{\text{recurrent contribution}}
            +\mathbf{b}\bigr)\;}
\tag{2}
\]
Here \(g(\cdot)\) denotes a pointwise non‑linearity such as \(\sigma(\cdot)\) or \(\operatorname*{tanh}(\cdot)\);  
the learnable parameters consist of an *input* weight matrix \(\mathbf W_x\), a *recurrent* matrix \(\mathbf W_h\), and a bias vector \(\mathbf b\).  

Equation (2) captures precisely what is repeated throughout every subsequent inference step,
and it provides an explicit map from past context (\(\mathbf h_{\,t{-}1}\)) to future context (\(\mathbf h_{\, t}\)).
When unrolled over all steps this recurrence defines a directed computational graph whose depth equals
the sequence length \(T\).  

In classical vanilla RNNs all gates share a **single** hidden state that both
stores information **and** determines how new data influences that storage.
Because both operations rely on identical parameters,
the network quickly encounters numerical difficulties when learning long‑range dependencies,
as explained next.



## Gradient Flow Through Time Steps and the Vanishing/Exploding Problem  

Training an RNN amounts to minimising a loss function \(L=\sum_{k=1}^{T}\ell_k(y_k,\hat y_k)\)
by back‑propagation through time (BPTT).
During BPTT gradients propagate backwards over every recurrence,
and at time step \(k<t\) they appear multiplied by successive Jacobian factors:
\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf h_k}
=
\underbrace{\Bigl( 
          \prod_{i=k+1}^{T}
          \frac{\partial \mathbf h_i}{\partial 
                 \mathbf h_{\,i-1}}\Bigr)}_{\displaystyle 
          J_{k+1:T}}
      \frac{\partial L}{\partial 
            \mathbf h_T}.
      \tag{3}
\]
The Jacobian  
\(J_i=\partial {\bf h}_i/\partial {\bf h}_{\,i-1}\)
encodes how much a small perturbation at one time influences later activations.

## Common Architectural Variations  

While Equations (5)–(11) capture “vanilla” LSTMs,
researchers quickly explored extensions aimed at either enlarging storage capacity or providing extra decision signals.

### Vectorised Cell States  

Early work treated `cₜ` as a single real number.
Later formulations aggregated several scalar cells into a vector 
`\boldsymbol cₜ∈ℝ^{d_c}` .
All four gates operate elementwise on this vector;
meanwhile additional *recurrent matrixes* ${R}` couple distinct dimensions:
```
   {\boldsymbol c}_ [t] = f[t] ⊙ {\boldsymbol c}[ [t−   ]]
                     + i[t] ⊙ z[t] .
```
These coupling matrices enable information stored in one dimension
to influence updates elsewhere—a mechanism called *memory mixing*
that proved essential when tackling tasks requiring nuanced state tracking ([Greff et al., 2015]; see Appendix B).

### Peephole Connections  

A second family inserts direct pathways from the current
cell value `cₜ` back into every gate’s preactivation:
```
˜ iₜ ← … + pᵢ ⊙ c₍ₜ−¹〉 ,
˜ fₜ ← … + p_f ⊙ c₍ₜ−¹〉 ,
˜ oₜ ← … + p_o ⊙ c₍ₜ〉 .
```
Here `{pᵢ,p_f,p_o}` are learned vectors (“peepholes”).
By exposing exact memory magnitude when deciding whether
to write (`i`) or erase (`f`) information,
peepholes sharpen timing behavior especially on periodic tasks.
Although not part of Eq.(6)’s minimal specification,
most textbooks regard them among “common architectural variations”.

### Multi‑Head / Multi‐Cell Design  

Within `sLSTM`—a descendant introduced later—the authors allow several heads.
Each head contains its own collection of scalar memories;
mixing occurs *within* heads but never across heads.

of parallelism reminiscent \

## LayerNorm Placement Conventions in Modern RNN Stacks  

Training very deep stacks (dozens or hundreds of layers) requires stabilising statistics across layers.
Two prevailing strategies exist concerning Layer Normalisation (`LN`):

### Pre‑LayerNorm  
`LN` is applied **before** any residual addition:
```
yᶦ = LN(fᶦ(xᶦ))         # feedforward/submodule  
outᶦ = xᶦ + yᶦ           # residual connection
```
The normalization therefore sees only freshly computed sublayer output,
insulating subsequent layers from internal covariate shifts.
The `xLSTM` paper reports that all large experiments (“pre‑LayerNorm residual backbones”) follow exactly this pattern,
mirroring practice adopted throughout today’s transformer‑based language models such as GPT‑3 or PaLM.
Pre‑LN has been shown empirically to enable deeper stacking without requiring fine‐grained learning‐rate schedules.

### Post‑LayerNorm  
An older convention places `LN` **after** adding the skip connection:
```
outᶦ = LN(xᶦ + yᶦ).
```
While mathematically equivalent under some conditions,
post‑LN normalises a sum containing both old activations (`xᶦ`) and fresh updates (`yᶦ`).  
When `yᶦ` has much larger magnitude than `xᶦ`,
post‑LN may amplify noise originating from earlier layers—a difficulty evident especially when scaling models past hundreds of millions parameters.



#### Why Modern Stacks Prefer Pre‑LN  

From an optimization viewpoint ``pre’’ normalisation guarantees that each submodule receives inputs whose mean≈0 and variance≈1 regardless of depth.
Consequently gradient magnitudes entering backward passes stay balanced throughout extremely deep networks such as those required for billions of parameters—the very setting explored by `xLSTM`.
Even though `mLSTM` eliminates explicit hidden‐to‐hidden mixes making parallelisation easier,
its designers still embed it inside pre‑LN blocks so that training dynamics remain comparable with contemporary transformer baselines.

3. Fundamental Limitations of Classical LSTMs for Language Modeling  
# Inability to Revise Storage Decisions  

One of the earliest design goals of the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) was to circumvent the vanishing‑gradient problem that plagued vanilla recurrent neural networks.  The core mechanism—an additive update of a single scalar cell state  
\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t},
\]  
coupled with sigmoid‑gated input, forget, and output signals—does indeed preserve gradients over long horizons.  However, this simplicity also creates a *rigidity* in how information can be overwritten.  Once a value has been written into the cell, the only way to change it later is through the forget gate multiplying the existing content by a factor $f_{t}\in(0,1)$.  If $f_{t}$ is small, the old value decays quickly; if it is close to one, it persists almost unchanged.

The consequence of this irreversible storage policy can be observed on a synthetic **Nearest‑Neighbor Search** task (see Appendix \ref{sec:appNearestNeighborSearch} of the original paper).  The problem statement is as follows: a reference vector $\mathbf{r}$ is presented first; then a long sequence of candidate vectors $\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\}$ arrives, each paired with an attached label; at the end of the sequence the model must output the label belonging to the candidate most similar to $\mathbf{r}$.  Solving this task requires **revising** an earlier decision whenever a new candidate is found that is closer (in Euclidean or cosine distance) than any previously observed one.  

When a conventional LSTM processes this stream, its scalar cell can store only one “best‑so‑far” similarity score and its associated label.  Because revisions rely solely on multiplicative forgetting, any mistake made early in the sequence cannot be undone without completely erasing the memory—an operation that would also discard useful information about earlier candidates that might still be needed later.  Empirically, Figure \ref{fig:lstmProblems} in the paper shows that the mean squared error of an LSTM remains high even after many training steps, whereas an architecture equipped with **exponential gating** (the central idea behind xLSTM) quickly learns to replace stale information with newer, more relevant values, dramatically reducing error.

In short, classical LSTMs lack a *revision* operator that can increase stored values (or switch stored symbols) without attenuating everything else.  This limitation hampers their ability to perform dynamic search or tracking tasks where the most relevant datum evolves over time.


# Limited Scalar Memory  

The original LSTM cell stores a single real number $c_{t}$ per hidden dimension.  While this scalar suffices for many sequence modelling problems—especially those where information can be compressed into a low‑dimensional summary—it imposes a **capacity ceiling** when confronted with diverse or rare lexical items.  

The phenomenon becomes apparent in a **Rare‑Token Prediction** experiment on the widely used Wikitext‑103 benchmark \citep{Merity:17}.  The dataset contains both frequent function words and infrequent content words (e.g., entity names or technical terms).  The authors partitioned tokens according to their unigram frequency and measured perplexity separately for each bucket.  The left panel of Figure \ref{fig:lstmProblems} reveals that vanilla LSTMs achieve higher perplexities on rare token bins compared with more frequent ones; this degradation persists even as model size grows.

Intuitively, imagine trying to memorize an encyclopedic fact (“the capital of *X* is *Y*”) while simultaneously tracking grammatical agreement across hundreds of intervening words.  A single scalar per dimension cannot allocate dedicated subspaces for distinct facts; instead it must merge all signals into one linearly additive trace.  When many rare events occur sporadically throughout a long context, they interfere destructively—the gradient signal attempting to store a rare token gets overwritten by subsequent updates that are driven primarily by common tokens.

The xLSTM addresses this bottleneck by promoting the cell state from a scalar $c_t\in\mathbb{R}$ to a *matrix* $\mathbf{C}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ (the **mLSTM** variant).  The matrix update follows a covariance rule  
\[
\mathbf{C}_{t}=f_{t}\,\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_{t}\,\mathbf{v}_{t}\,\mathbf{k}_{t}^{\!\top},
\]  
where $\mathbf{k}_t$ and $\mathbf{v}_t$ act as key–value pairs analogous to those in Transformers.  By storing outer‑product information rather than a single number, each dimension can preserve distinct patterns corresponding to different tokens, thereby expanding effective storage capacity without increasing parameter count.


# Sequential Bottleneck  

Unlike self‑attention mechanisms whose pairwise interactions can be computed in parallel across time steps—a feature that makes Transformers highly scalable—the classic LSTM retains **hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent connections**:
\[
h_{t}=o_{t}\,\psi(c_{t}), \qquad
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t}.
\]
The hidden state $h_{t-1}$ appears explicitly in every gate pre‑activation (e.g., $\tilde{i}_t=\mathbf w_i^{\!\top}\mathbf x_t+r_i h_{t-1}+b_i$).  Consequently, computation at time $t$ depends on the exact value of $h_{t-1}$ produced at time $t-1$, creating an *inherently sequential* data path.

When training language models on billions of tokens—a regime where modern hardware benefits immensely from parallelism—this sequential dependency becomes a performance bottleneck.  Even though each step involves only matrix–vector multiplications (which are cheap compared with quadratic attention), GPU kernels cannot launch many timesteps simultaneously because they would need future hidden states that are not yet available.  

In contrast, **mLSTM** removes hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence entirely: its gates depend only on the current input $\mathbf x_t$, not on $h_{t-1}$.  This design enables *parallel scan* algorithms similar to those used in FlashAttention \citep{Dao:22} or Gated Linear Attention \citep{Yang:23arxiv}, allowing all timesteps of a sequence to be processed concurrently once the matrix memory updates are expressed as prefix sums over $\{\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t,i_t,f_t\}$.  Thus mLSTM retains linear time complexity *and* enjoys parallel execution—a critical advantage for scaling.


# Expressiveness Viewpoint – Formal Languages and Memory Mixing  

Beyond quantitative performance metrics, there exists a *qualitative* distinction between models capable of **state tracking** and those that are not.  Formal language theory provides an intuitive lens: certain regular or context‑free languages require an internal counter or stack that must evolve deterministically based on past inputs (e.g., parity checking or balanced parentheses).  

The authors evaluate xLSTM variants on extended versions of formal language tasks introduced by \citet{Deletang:23}.  These tasks include:
* **Parity** – determining whether an odd number of ones has been seen,
* **Dyck languages** – checking correctly nested brackets,
* **Copy** – reproducing an earlier subsequence after an intervening delimiter.

Crucially, these benchmarks expose whether a model possesses *memory mixing*: the ability for multiple cells or heads to interact via recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf R$ so that information from earlier positions can be recombined later.  Classical LSTMs already contain such mixing through hidden‑to‑hidden connections; however they suffer from inability to revise values and limited capacity as described above.

More importantly, **Transformers** and many recent **State Space Models (SSMs)** lack explicit memory mixing because their recurrence equations are linear and homogeneous across dimensions; they essentially compute linear filters applied independently per channel.  As shown in Figure \ref{fig:formal-main}, these models achieve near‑random accuracy on parity-type tasks even after extensive training—a result aligned with theoretical findings that linear recurrent units cannot implement non‑trivial counting without extra non‑linear feedback loops \citep{Merrill:24}.  

By contrast, sLSTM introduces *exponential gating* together with controlled normalizers ($n_t$) and retains hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence across multiple heads within each head’s cell group ("memory mixing across cells within each head").  Empirically this enables sLSTM—and consequently xLSTM architectures containing sLSTM blocks—to solve parity and other state‑tracking problems reliably.  Hence exponential gating combined with memory mixing upgrades classical LSTMs from being merely gradient­stable recurrent units into truly expressive sequence recognisers capable of encoding complex formal languages.


# Empirical Evidence from Prior Work Showing Performance Gaps  

The theoretical limitations discussed above translate directly into observed performance gaps when classical LSTMs are compared against modern architectures on large‑scale language modelling benchmarks:

| Model family | Typical parameter regime | Representative validation perplexity (SlimPajama) |
|--------------|--------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Vanilla LSTM | ~350 M | Significantly higher than Transformers; exact numbers omitted for brevity but consistently trailing GPT‐3 scale baselines |
| Transformers (e.g., GPT‑3) | ~350 M | Baseline reference perplexity |
| State Space Models (e.g., S4, Mamba) | ~350 M | Comparable or better than vanilla LSTMs but still above Transformers |
| xLSTM variants (sLSTM + mLSTM) | ~350 M | **Best among all families** – lower perplexity than both Transformers and SSMs (Table \ref{tab:spaj15b_model_results}) |

These results—reproduced across multiple experimental setups (15 B tokens in Section \ref{sec:ExpComparison}, 300 B tokens in Section \ref{sec:ExpLanguage})—confirm that simply scaling up an unmodified LSTM does not close the gap introduced by its three core deficiencies:

1. **Revision incapability** leads to higher error on nearest‐neighbor type retrieval tasks.
2. **Scalar memory restriction** inflates perplexity especially for rare tokens.
3. **Sequential bottleneck** limits throughput at long context lengths; Transformers enjoy quadratic attention while maintaining faster wall-clock time thanks to parallelism.

Prior literature cited throughout the introduction (\citet{Hochreiter:01,Hochreiter:07}, \citet{Vaswani:17}) already documented how Transformers overtook LSTMs around 2017 for text generation tasks once model sizes entered tens or hundreds of millions of parameters. The empirical results presented here reaffirm those observations while showing how targeted architectural upgrades—exponential gating and matrix memories—can recover much of that lost ground.

Together these points build a compelling case: any effort aimed at scaling classical LSTMs towards billions of parameters must first address revision capability, expand storage beyond scalars, and remove hidden‑to‑hidden sequential dependencies.   The subsequent sections of this textbook will demonstrate how xLSTM implements precisely these remedies via exponential gates, sLSTM/mLSTM memory structures, and residual block backbones that enable efficient large‐scale training.

4. Design Goals Behind Extended LSTMs (xLSTMs)

The classic Long Short‑Term Memory network was invented to solve the vanishing‑gradient problem by means of the **constant error carousel** and three sigmoid gates – input $i_{t}$, forget $f_{t}$ and output $o_{t}$ – as expressed in the original update
\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t},\qquad 
h_{t}=o_{t}\,\psi(c_{t}) ,
\tag{1}
\]
where $c_{t}$ is the cell state, $h_{t}$ the hidden state and $\psi$ a squashing function such as $\tanh$.
When LSTMs were first deployed this formulation already allowed gradients to flow over long horizons,
but three structural weaknesses quickly became apparent when trying to scale them toward modern large language models:
(1) **Inability to revise stored information**, leading the network to get stuck once an early decision was written into $c_{t}$;
(2) **Limited storage capacity**, because each cell carries only a scalar or, at best, a low‑dimensional vector;
(3) **Absence of parallelism**, caused by the hidden–to–hidden recurrent coupling ($h_{t-1}\!\rightarrow\!h_{t}$) that forces strict sequential evaluation.
The design goals behind Extended LSTMs (xLSTMs) are precisely aimed at eliminating these three bottlenecks while preserving the desirable gradient properties of Equation (1).  
Below we examine each desideratum systematically and show how the two complementary extensions—*exponential gating* and *novel memory structures*—realize them.



# Linear scalability vs quadratic attention trade‑off analysis

A central motivation for rethinking recurrent architectures is computational cost.
Standard Transformers compute self‑attention through pairwise interactions among all $T$ tokens,
which yields a time complexity $\mathcal{O}(T^{2})$ and a space requirement proportional
to $T^{2}$ when naïvely storing attention scores.
For short sentences this overhead is negligible,
but when language models are tasked with contexts exceeding several thousand tokens
the quadratic term dominates both training wall‑clock time and GPU memory consumption.
In contrast, an RNN‐style recurrence updates its internal representation from one step
to the next using only information available at the current time,
so every forward pass costs $\mathcal{O}(T)$ operations,
and the amount of additional activation storage grows only linearly,
$\mathcal{O}(T)$.
From a theoretical standpoint this means that *the amount of computation needed per token
does not increase when extending the context window*,
which directly satisfies a “linear scalability” requirement for ever larger corpora
such as those used during BERT or GPT training runs.


The price paid by early‐stage RNNs is loss of explicit pairwise interaction;
however, xLSTMs retain an expressive summarization capability through their
high‑dimensional *memory* rather than explicit token–token scores.
Because the recurrent update implicitly mixes past inputs,
the model follows Cover’s theorem – a non‑linear embedding into a higher dimensional
space increases the chance that different histories become linearly separable.
Thus xLSTMs keep **linear run time**, yet compensate
for missing explicit attention by learning powerful compressed representations.
Empirically this trade‑off shows up in generation benchmarks:
Figure 13 of the original work demonstrates perfectly linear scaling
of generation latency for xLTM variants,
while equivalent Transformer baselines incur superlinear growth.
Consequently, linear scalability emerged as a primary architectural goal:
maintain $\mathcal{O}(T)$ operations **and** $\mathcal{O}(T)$ auxiliary memory,
thereby allowing language models whose context windows stretch well past $10\,000$ tokens without prohibitive hardware costs.



# Higher‑capacity representations through richer memory structures

The original LSTM stores its entire history within a scalar cell $c_{t}\in\mathbb R$
(or at most an elementwise vector $\mathbf c_{t}\in\mathbb R^{d}$).
Although this representation suffices for many sequence tasks,
the limited dimensionality becomes apparent when predicting rare tokens.
Figure 2(b) illustrates how standard LSTMs struggle on low‐frequency vocabulary items:
the perplexity rises sharply because there simply isn’t enough representational bandwidth
to retain detailed distinctions among rarely observed patterns.


To address this bottleneck xLSTMs introduce **two distinct memory augmentations:**  

*The scalar extension* (**sLSTM**) keeps one scalar per cell but augments it
with a *normalizer state* $n_{t}$ which tracks cumulative gate products:
\[
n_{t}=f_{t}\,n_{t-1}+i_{t}.
\]
Dividing $c_{t}$ by $n_{t}$ yields an online weighted average,
thereby allowing more nuanced weighting between old and new information without sacrificing scalar size.
While s­LSTM does not increase raw dimensionality,
this extra bookkeeping already improves rare‐token performance compared with vanilla LSTMs,
as demonstrated empirically on Wikitext‑103.



*The matrix extension* (**mLSTM**) dramatically expands storage capacity.
Instead of a scalar order parameter,
m ​​​​​​​​​​​ ​stores an entire $d\times d$ matrix 
$\mathbf C_{t}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ .
When processing input $\mathbf{x}_{t}$ it constructs
a *key* $\mathbf{k}_{t}\in\mathbb R^{d}$ 
and a *value* $\mathbf{v}_{t}\in\mathbb R^{d}$ through learned projections:
\[
\mathbf{k}_{t}= \frac{1}{\sqrt d}\,\mathbf W_{\!k}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+ \mathbf b_{\!k},
\qquad 
\mathbf{v}_{t}= \mathbf W_{\!v}\,\mathbf{x}_{t}+ \mathbf b_{\!v}.
\]
These vectors are stored jointly via an outer product,
a classical **covariance update rule**
\[
\mathbf C_{t}=f_{\, t}\,\mathbf C _{\, t-1}
+ i_{\; t}\,\mathbf v _{\, t}\,\mathbf k _{\, t}^{\!\top}.
\tag{2}
\]
Equation (2) mirrors established associative–memory mechanisms such as bidirectional
associative memories (Kohonen 1972)
and Fast Weight Programmers (Schmidhuber 1992).
Because each key–value pair contributes rank one growth,
the effective rank of $\mathbf C$ grows with the number of distinct associations memorized,
providing **quadratically more degrees of freedom** than an equivalent vector‐based cell.
A normalizer $\mathbf n _{\, t}=f _{\, t}\,\mathbf n _{\, t-1}+i _{\, t}\,\mathbf k _{\, t}$ records the cumulative strength
of stored keys so that retrieval uses
\[
\tilde{\mathbf h}_{ t}= 
\dfrac{\;\mathbf C _{\, t}\,\mathbf q _{\, t}}{\max \bigl(|\,\mathbf n _{\, t}^{\!\top }\!\mathbf q _{\, t}|,\ 1\bigr)},
\qquad 
h _{\, t}=o _{\, t}\odot \tilde{\mathbf h}_{ t},
\tag{3}
\]
where $\mathbf q _ {\, t}= \mathbf W_{\!q}\,\mathbf{x}_{ t}+ \mathbf b_{\!q}$ is a query vector.
Thus retrieval operates analogously to key–value attention:
a query dot product selects relevant entries without constructing pairwise token interactions.
Empirically this richer structure enables non‐Transformer models such as x[0] :L m (m=0)
to solve Multi‑Query Associative Recall tasks at parity with Transformer baselines
(even when storing up\-to 256 pairs).

Therefore “higher–capacity representations” turned out to require **richer internal memories**
that move beyond simple scalars;
the combination of normalizer‐augmented scalars (s­LSTM) and full covariance matrices (m ­S​​​​​​.﻿​‌‌​​​​‍ ​​א​​​‎21)
meets this objective while preserving upstream differentiability.

practical effect illustrated in Figure 2(a): during Nearest Neighbor Search an early match gets lodged into $c$,

# Two complementary extensions: exponential gating AND novel memory structures

The preceding sections highlight **four pillars**—linear cost, higher capacity,
revisability, and ease-of-parallelisation—as guiding criteria around which x𝐿𝑆𝓉𝗠 was constructed.
Realising all four simultaneously would have been impossible if relying on a single modification alone;
instead two tightly coupled innovations work together:

| Goal | Achieved primarily by | Supporting effect |
|------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Linear computational scaling | **Matrix Memory + Scan‐friendly Recurrence** | Exponential gating introduces no extra sequential dependence |
| High storage density | **Matrix Memory (`C`)** | Normalizer ensures stable retrieval |
| Ability to overwrite old facts | **Exponential gated `i` / optionally `f`** | Normalizer rescales division \(c/n\) yielding stable averages |
| Parallel execution | **Removing hidden–hidden links (`m̂ʟ𝐒ᴛᴍ`)** | Faster CUDA kernel synchronises remaining dependence loops |

Exponential gates afford unrestricted magnitude control without sacrificing differentiability;
when combined with stabilizers they remain numerically well behaved even under billions’
parameter regimes.
Matrix memories provide exponentially richer embeddings while maintaining associative access patterns similar to self-attention yet at linear cost.
Memory mixing across multiple heads—a feature unique to sɭᵐᴏ𝐚𝗀𝗲——blends these mechanisms further:
heads can specialize either toward fine-grained revocable scalars or toward massive key/value storages depending on depth within the stack.



In practice architects configure an entire model as a mixture `x⁢[a:b]`,
i.e., allocating proportion `a/(a+b)` layers specialized around matrix memories (`m  …`) and proportion `b/(a+b)` around scalar-with-mixing cells (`s…`).  
Experiments show even modest proportions favoring `m` outperform pure transformer baselines on both validation perplexity (§5)
and downstream benchmarks (§7),
while inserting occasional `s` layers restores finegrained state tracking absent from “purely feedforward’’ SSM designs.”

Consequently these two extensions do not merely coexist—they reinforce one another:
exponential gating improves both revisit capability **and** numerical stability necessary when accumulating many outer products inside \(C_\!{}\);
conversely rich memories benefit from every gate being able either amplify relevant associations (`i`) or purge irrelevancies (`f`).  
Together they embody precisely those design aims enumerated at the start:
linear runtime even at extreme context lengths,
dramatically expanded latent bandwidth,
flexible overwrite dynamics crucial for search/retrieval scenarios,
and GPU-friendly recursions compatible with industrious large language model tool chains.



In summary, extending classical LSTM units along these carefully delineated objectives yields
the Flexible Long Short-Term Memory family termed ***x𝐿𝑆𝔗𝑀***—
a framework capable now—by construction—to operate at billions‑scale parameter regimes while meeting all four foundational desiderata outlined above.
