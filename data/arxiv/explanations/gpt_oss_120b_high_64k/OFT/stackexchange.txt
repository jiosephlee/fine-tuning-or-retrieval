\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### What exactly is hyperspherical energy and why is it defined as a sum of inverse distances?
Question:
In Section 3 the authors define the hyperspherical energy (HE) of a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) as \(\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|\) are normalized neurons. I am confused about:

1. Why use the reciprocal of Euclidean distance instead of directly using cosine similarity or another similarity measure?
2. What intuition does this quantity capture about the distribution of neurons on the unit sphere?
3. How does this metric relate to more familiar concepts such as “uniformity” or “diversity” of representations?

A clear explanation (possibly with a simple geometric example) would help me understand why this measure is chosen as a proxy for semantic preservation.
Answer:
In **Answer**

In the paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* the authors treat every column  
$\displaystyle \mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$  
of a weight matrix as a “neuron”. After normalising each column,

$$
   \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\lVert \mathbf{w}_{i}\rVert},
$$

the set $\displaystyle \bigl\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\bigr\}$ lives on the surface of a unit $(d-1)$‑dimensional sphere (the *hypersphere*).  
The *hyperspherical energy* (HE) of this layer is defined as  

$$
   \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)=
   \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\rVert^{-1}.
   \tag{1}
$$  

Below we explain why this particular form is chosen, what geometric intuition it carries, and how it relates to notions such as “uniformity’’ or “diversity’’ of the learned representations.

---

### 1. Why the **inverse Euclidean distance**?

For two unit vectors $\displaystyle \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i},\,\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}$ let $\displaystyle \theta_{ij}$ be the angle between them.
Because $\displaystyle \lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\rVert=\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\rVert=1$,

$$
   \bigl\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\rVert^{2}
      = (\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j})^{T}
        (\hat {\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat {\mathbf {w}} _ {j})
      = 2-2\,\cos\,\theta_{ij}.
   \tag{2}
$$

Thus the Euclidean distance is a monotonic decreasing function of the cosine similarity:
the smaller the angle (the larger $\cos θ_{ij}$), the smaller 
the distance $d_{ij}=                     ≜∥ ĥᵢ−ĥⱼ∥$. Consequently  

$$
   d_{ij}^{-1}= 
   {\frac{1}{\,\sqrt{\,2-2\,· ⧸ cos θ _ { ij } } }}
   =%
   {\frac { 1 }
          {\sqrt { 2{-}2\,·\,cos θ _ { ij } } }} .
$$  

is also a monotonic decreasing function of $\displaystyle θ _ { ij }$.  

Why prefer $d^{-1}_{ ij }$ over $\cos θ _ { ij }$ itself?

| Property | Inverse distance $d^{-1}$ | Cosine similarity |
|---|---|---|
| **Range** | $[0,\!\infty)$ – very large when points are almost coincident | $[-1, 1]$ |
| **Sensitivity to close pairs** | Grows without bound as $d → 0$, yielding strong repulsion when two neurons collapse | Bounded – cannot distinguish “very close’’ from “moderately close’’ |
| **Additivity over many pairs** | A few near‑coincident pairs dominate the sum → directly penalises redundancy | Contributions can cancel because positive and negative cosines exist |

The unbounded penalty for tiny distances makes $d^{-1}$ act like a *repulsive* physical potential (see next paragraph). When many neurons cluster together this term blows up, forcing optimisation to push them apart. A plain cosine sum would not provide such an incentive; having many neurons with cosine ≈ 0.9 would increase the sum only modestly, yet those neurons would be highly redundant.



### 2 Physical intuition – potentials on a sphere

The functional form (1) is exactly the **Riesz $s=1$ energy**, i.e. Coulomb energy,
of a system of unit charges placed on a sphere.
In electrostatics each charge exerts a repulsive force proportional to $r^{-2}$,
while its *potential* scales as $r^{-1}$. Summing pairwise potentials gives  

$$
   E_{\text C}= 
   {\sum _ { i<j } }\frac { 1 } 
                         { r _ { ij } } .
$$  

If we imagine each normalised neuron as a point charge on the hypersphere,
then minimising (or keeping constant) this energy encourages points to spread out evenly –
precisely what one wants from directions that capture diverse features.



Consider three neurons in two dimensions placed on the unit circle.
If they are equally spaced at angles $0^{∘},120^{∘},240^{∘}$,
every pairwise distance equals $\sqrt3$, so each term contributes $½/\!\sqrt3$
and  

$$
   HE_{\text {eq}}
     =6\times\tfrac { 1 } {\sqrt3 }\approx3.46 .
$$

If we move two neurons together so that their angles become   
$0^{∘},10^{∘},240^{∘}$,
one distance shrinks dramatically ($≈0.17$, contribution ≈5.9),
while the other two distances hardly change.
The total energy jumps to roughly  

$$5.9+2\times0.58≈7.06 > HE_{\text {eq}},$$  

so clustered configurations have **higher** hyperspherical energy;
well‑spread configurations have **lower** energy.



### 3 What does hyperspherical energy measure?

Because every term depends only on angular separation,
HE quantifies how uniformly those directions fill the hypersphere:

* **Low HE** ⇒ most pairs are far apart ⇒ neuron directions are diverse ⇒ linear filters built from them respond to different patterns.
* **High HE** ⇒ many directions are close ⇒ redundancy ⇒ several neurons behave almost identically, reducing expressive capacity.



In representation‑learning literature these ideas appear under names such as
*uniformity*, *diversity*, or *non‑redundancy*.
For example, Wang & Isola (2020) propose an “uniformity’’ loss based on an exponential kernel  

$$
   U=\log\tfrac{
       1 }
       {
       n^{2}
     }\sum _ {
          i,j }
        e^{-\,∥z _ i{-}z _ j∥^{2}},
$$ 

both kernels encourage points jointly populating space evenly.
The inverse‑distance sum merely belongs to this family,
but places much stronger emphasis on very short separations.



### 4 Why preserve rather than minimise HE during finetuning?

The pretrained diffusion model has already learned an angular geometry encoding semantic knowledge –
e.g.~one direction captures “sky”, another “grass”.
If finetuning were allowed arbitrarily modify those angles,
we could destroy this structure even while overall Euclidean weight change stays tiny.



An orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle R$
($R^{T}R=I$)
preserves all inner products,
hence every Euclidean distance between normalised vectors stays unchanged:

$$
   ∥R̂w_i-R̂w_j∥=
   ∥̂w_i−̂w_j∥ .
$$

Consequently applying a layer‑wise orthogonal transform  
$\displaystyle W=RW^{{0}}$
leaves Eq.\,( 1 ) untouched,

$$
   HE(W)=HE(W^{{0}}).
$$

Thus OFT guarantees that *pairwise angular relationships among neurons remain exactly
as in the pretrained model*, while still providing enough degrees­of­freedom
(via rotations or reflections) to adapt semantics required downstream.



---

In summary,

*The hyperspherical energy is a physics‑inspired global measure quantifying how tightly packed
or uniformly spread a set of normalised weight vectors (“neurons”) lies on
the unit hypersphere.*
It uses reciprocal Euclidean distance because this yields an unbounded repulsive potential,
heavily penalising near coincidences—something cosine similarity alone cannot provide.
Low energy corresponds to diverse, uniformly distributed directions;
high energy signals redundancy.
Preserving HE during finetuning keeps the delicate angular structure learned during pretraining –
and therefore much of its semantic capability – intact while still allowing controlled adaptation through orthogonal rotations.*

### Why is preserving hyperspherical energy believed to preserve the semantic generation ability of diffusion models?
Question:
The authors hypothesise that “the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks” and therefore keeping HE unchanged should keep pre‑training semantics intact. However, I do not see a direct link between pairwise angles and image semantics in diffusion models.

* Is there empirical evidence beyond the toy auto‑encoder experiment supporting this claim?
* Could there be cases where two weight matrices have identical HE but generate very different images?
* How does preserving HE compare to simply limiting Euclidean distance from the pre‑trained weights?
Answer:
**Answer**

The claim that *preserving hyperspherical energy* (HE) helps keep the “semantic generation ability’’ of a diffusion model rests on two ideas. First, the **relative directions** of the weight vectors (the “neurons’’) encode most of the information that a network has learned; second, an *orthogonal* transformation changes those directions **only by a rigid rotation or reflection**, which leaves every pairwise angle unchanged and therefore leaves the whole hyperspherical energy unchanged. Below I unpack why this matters for diffusion models, what evidence the paper provides beyond the toy auto‑encoder example, whether two weight matrices can have identical HE yet behave differently, and why a simple Euclidean‑distance regulariser is not enough.

---

### 1\. Why angles matter in a diffusion model

In a fully‑connected or linear layer we can write the weight matrix as  

$$
\mathbf W = \bigl[\mathbf w_1,\dots,\mathbf w_n\bigr]\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\qquad 
\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|},
$$  

so each neuron lives on the unit hypersphere $\mathbb S^{d-1}$. The *hyperspherical energy* is  

$$
\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)=\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
$$  

i.e.\ an aggregation of all pairwise cosine similarities  
$c_{ij}= \hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf w}_j$. When we replace an inner product by a cosine similarity during inference (the “angular’’ version in Figure 1 of the paper), we see that almost all visual information is recovered whereas discarding the cosine part destroys it completely. This mirrors a classical result from signal processing: for a 2‑D Fourier transform the **phase spectrum**—which encodes angular relationships between basis functions—carries most of the image semantics while the magnitude spectrum mainly adjusts contrast and brightness. In neural networks a similar picture emerges: swapping one direction for another modifies *what* features are extracted without changing how strongly they respond overall.

Because diffusion models generate images by repeatedly applying these linear maps inside UNet blocks, any systematic change in *relative* directions will ripple through all denoising steps and manifest as a semantic shift (e.g., “the subject becomes a rabbit”). Conversely, if we keep every angle exactly as it was during pre‑training, the network’s internal geometry stays intact, so it still knows how to turn latent noise into realistic photographs.

---

### 2\. From angle preservation to an orthogonal map

The key mathematical observation (Section 3.1) is that **any common orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle \mathbf R\,in\,O(d)$ leaves all pairwise inner products invariant**:

$$
(\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i)^\top (\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_j)
    = \hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf w}_j .
$$

Hence if we replace $\displaystyle \mathbf W^0$ (the pretrained weight matrix) by $\displaystyle \mathbf W = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0$, then  
$\displaystyle \mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W)=\mathrm{HE}(\mathbf W^0)$ *exactly*. Orthogonal fine‑tuning (OFT) therefore imposes a very strong structural regulariser—*not merely that the scalar sum of all cosines stays the same*, but that **the whole cosine matrix $\{c_{ij}\}$ stays unchanged**.

---

### 3\. Empirical support beyond the toy auto‑encoder

The paper backs this hypothesis with three families of experiments:

1. **Subject‑driven generation (DreamBooth setting).**  
   Figures 4–5 and Table 1 show that OFT (and its constrained variant COFT) converge in far fewer iterations than standard DreamBooth or LoRA while achieving higher DINO/CLIP‑I scores for subject fidelity. Moreover, loss curves demonstrate *stable* training even when the number of fine‑tuning steps is increased dramatically—a regime where DreamBooth collapses and LoRA drifts away from the original identity.

2. **Controllable generation (ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter comparison).**  
   Across three distinct control signals—Canny edges, segmentation maps, facial landmarks—the authors report both *faster convergence* (Figure 6 left panel shows landmark error dropping to its final value after only eight epochs for OFT/COFT versus twenty for LoRA) and *higher final control consistency* (Table 2). The qualitative galleries (Figures 7–8) make clear that OFT respects both the textual prompt and the structural cue simultaneously, whereas LoRA often either ignores the cue or distorts subject details.

3. **Ablation on orthogonality itself.**  
   In Section 3.2 they repeat the controllable‑generation experiment with OFT **without** enforcing $\displaystyle \mathbf R^\top \!\! \mathbf R=\!\! \mathbf I$. The resulting model fails to produce realistic images at all (Figure 3 right), directly confirming that preserving pairwise angles—not just any low‑rank update—is essential for stability.

Collectively these results demonstrate that *maintaining the relational geometry among neurons* translates into *preserving pre‑training semantics* across very different downstream tasks.

---

### 4\. Can two different weight matrices have identical HE yet behave differently?

Mathematically yes: many distinct matrices can yield exactly the same scalar value of $\displaystyle \mathrm{HE}(\cdot)$. For example, two configurations could have very different distributions of angles but happen to give equal sums after inversion. If we imposed only a *scalar* equality constraint on $\displaystyle \mathrm{HE}$, we would indeed risk altering semantics because nothing would forbid those hidden rearrangements.

OFT avoids this pitfall by **realising an exact orthogonal map**, which guarantees much more than equal scalar energy—it guarantees equality of every pairwise cosine:

$$
\forall i,j:\quad
(\!\!\,\!\!\,\!\!\,\!\!\,\!\!\,\!\!\,\!\!~%
     {\bf R}\,\widehat{{\bf w}}_i)^T(\!
     {\bf R}\,\widehat{{\bf w}}_j)
= 
     {\widehat{{\bf w}}_i}^{\,T}{~
        }\widehat{{\bf w}}_j .
$$

Thus any two weight matrices produced by OFT from the same pretrained checkpoint will be related by a single global rotation/reflection per layer and will therefore generate virtually identical feature manifolds up to a coordinate change. In practice this means differences in generated images are negligible; any noticeable drift would come from downstream non‑linearities or from violating orthogonality during optimisation—both situations are monitored in training (e.g., via projected gradient steps on $\displaystyle \|\bf Q\,||_{\!F}\leq\e'\,$ in COFT).

---

### 5\. Why not simply keep Euclidean distance small?

A naïve regulariser such as  

$\displaystyle 
    \|\;\!\!\bf W-\bf W^{0}\;\!\|_{F}^{2}
$  

does encourage “staying close’’ but it has two shortcomings:

1️⃣ **Direction vs magnitude ambiguity.* A small Euclidean perturbation can drastically rotate a vector while leaving its norm almost unchanged—for example,

$
    \bigl\
    {\;\alpha\,%
      {\widehat{{w}}}_{i}
      -
      (\cos\theta\,%
        {\widehat{{w}}}_{i}
      +%
        {\sin\theta}\,
        {\boldsymbol v})\
    }_{\!2}
    \
    {\approx}
    |\vartheta|
$

when $\vartheta\\ll{}1$,~$\alpha\simeq{}1$. Hence even when ‖·‖ stays low, many important angles may shift substantially, which—as argued above—correlates tightly with semantic changes.

2️⃣ **Lack of structural awareness.* The Frobenius norm aggregates all entries independently; it does not penalise swapping two neurons or rotating an entire subspace if their norms stay similar enough. Consequently two weight matrices may be equally close in Euclidean sense yet have completely different neighbourhoods on the hypersphere—leading to divergent activation patterns.

By contrast, an orthogonal constraint directly controls *the geometry* on which high‑dimensional neural representations live: every point on the unit sphere stays at unit distance from every other point after transformation, so no hidden angle distortion occurs even if ‖·‖ changes dramatically due to scaling or permutation within each block.

Moreover, COFT augments pure orthogonality with an explicit radius bound  

$\displaystyle 
    \|\;\! {\bf R}- {\bf I}\;\!%
     ||_{\!F}
    \
    {\le}
    \
    {\epsilon}
$

which essentially caps how far we wander from the pretrained point in Euclidean space while still guaranteeing angle preservation—a combination unavailable when using only one of those regularisers alone.

---

### 6\. Take‑away

Preserving hyperspherical energy works because it enforces *exact invariance of all pairwise neuron angles*, which empirical work in representation learning has repeatedly linked to semantic similarity. The paper’s extensive experiments on DreamBooth style subject adaptation and several control‐signal tasks show that this invariance translates into **more stable fine‑tuning**, **faster convergence**, and **higher fidelity** compared with both naïve gradient updates and low‑rank adapters like LoRA.

In theory two arbitrary matrices could share a scalar HE value yet behave differently; however OFT does not rely on matching a scalar—it enforces an orthogonal map that makes every angle identical up to a global rotation/reflection per layer, eliminating such pathological cases in practice.

Finally, restricting only Euclidean distance fails to guarantee angle preservation and therefore cannot reliably protect semantic knowledge; combining orthogonality with an explicit deviation bound—as COFT does—offers a principled alternative that aligns well with how diffusion models internally represent visual concepts.

### What is the connection between phase preservation in Fourier transforms and orthogonal finetuning of neuron directions?
Question:
The authors draw an analogy between phase spectra (which retain semantic information) and neuron direction changes via orthogonal transformations. I find this analogy puzzling because:

1. In an image, phase encodes relative spatial shifts, while neuron directions are high‑dimensional weight vectors.
2. The Fourier analogy seems limited to linear operations.

Can anyone elaborate on how this intuition translates concretely into the diffusion model setting? Does changing neuron directions truly act like modifying phase while keeping magnitude constant?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s “Fourier‑phase” story is not meant to claim that a diffusion model literally stores images in the frequency domain.  
What it uses is a *structural* analogy that holds for **any** linear mapping – a convolution kernel, an attention matrix or a fully‑connected layer – once we write the mapping as a sum over *directions* (basis vectors) multiplied by *lengths* (scalars).  In a 2‑D Fourier transform those scalars are the **magnitudes** $|F(k)|$ and the **phases** $\theta(k)$; in a neural layer they are respectively  

* the **norms** $\|w_i\|$ of the weight vectors $w_i$ (how much total energy each neuron contributes),  
* the **angles** $\phi_i=\angle(w_i,x)$ between a neuron and an input $x$ (how that energy is aligned with the data).

---

### 1 Why the inner product makes phase–direction explicit

For a single neuron we have  

$$
z_i = w_i^{\!\top} x
     = \|w_i\|\;\|x\|\;\cos\!\bigl(\angle(w_i,x)\bigr).
$$

The cosine term depends only on the *relative orientation* between $w_i$ and $x$; it plays exactly the role of a **phase** term in a Fourier coefficient, where  

$$
F(k)=|F(k)|\,e^{j\,\theta(k)}
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
f(x)=\sum_k |F(k)|\,\cos(\theta(k)+2\,π\,k·x).
$$

If we keep $\|w_i\|$ fixed and rotate $w_i$, we change $\phi_i$ while leaving the “energy’’ $\|w_i\\|$ untouched – just as one can keep $|F(k)|$ fixed while altering $\theta(k)$.

---

### 2 Orthogonal matrices rotate *all* directions together

An orthogonal matrix $R∈O(d)$ satisfies $R^{⟂}R=I$.  
Multiplying every column (or row) of a weight matrix $W^{0}\inℝ^{d\times n}$ by the same $R$,  

$$
W=RW^{0},
$$

has three immediate consequences:

1. **Norm preservation** $\displaystyle \|Rw_{i}\|=\|w_{i}\|$.  
   This is exactly “keep the magnitude spectrum”.

2. **Angle preservation among neurons** $\displaystyle 
   ⟨Rw_{i},Rw_{j}⟩
   =⟨w_{i},w_{j}⟩$.  
   Hence every pairwise cosine similarity—and consequently the *hyperspherical energy*

   $$
   {\rm HE}(W)=
   \sum_{i≠j}\bigl\|\widehat{w}_{i}-\widehat{w}_{j}\bigr\}^{-1},
   \qquad
   \widehat{w}_{i}= {\frac{w_{i}}{\|w_{i}\|}},
   $$

   —remains unchanged. Preserving these angles mirrors preserving **relative phases** among Fourier coefficients.

3. **Invertibility** Since $R^{-1}=R^{⊤}$, no information is lost; we have merely changed the coordinate system in which neurons are expressed.

Thus an orthogonal fine‑tuning step amounts precisely to “changing only phase’’ while “leaving magnitude untouched”.

---

### 3 How this manifests inside a diffusion model

A Stable‑Diffusion UNet consists mainly of  

* Linear layers ($W^{0}x+b^{0}$),  
* Multi‑head self‑attention ($QK\,W^{V⊤}$),

both of which are built from inner products similar to $w^{⊤}x$.  
During denoising at timestep $t$,  

```python
h_t = f_{\theta}(z_t , c )
```

where $f_{\theta}$ contains many such linear maps.  
If we replace each map by its orthogonally transformed version $RW^{0}$, then for any intermediate activation vector $a_t$

```python
(RW^{0})ᵀ a_t = (W^{0})ᵀ (Rᵀ a_t),
```

so **the net effect equals rotating every activation before it meets the pre‑trained weights**. The rotation does not alter any singular values of those weights—those singular values are analogous to Fourier magnitudes—so overall *energy distribution* across channels stays exactly as before training started. What does change is how each channel aligns with the data, i.e. its effective “phase”.

Because diffusion sampling repeatedly feeds back activations through these same rotated linear maps, semantic aspects tied to those alignments can be steered (e.g., making a specific subject appear more often or obeying a control map), while the global statistical properties learned during pre‑training—textures, colours, overall realism—remain intact thanks to the preserved hyperspherical structure.

---

### 4 Why plain linear updates do not enjoy this property

Updating weights with ordinary SGD changes both norms ***and*** directions independently:

```python
W ← W − η ∇_W L .
```

Consequently  

* $\displaystyle \|w_{i}\|$ may drift far from its original value → “magnitude drift”.  
* Pairwise dot products $\displaystyle⟨w_{i},w_{j}⟩$ change → “phase drift”.

The Euclidean distance $\displaystyle \|W−W^{0}\|_F$ alone cannot capture whether we have destroyed those angular relationships, which—as experiments in the paper show—correlates tightly with loss of pretrained generation quality. Orthogonal fine‑tuning eliminates both drifts by construction, providing the stability observed empirically (fast convergence, no collapse).

---

### 5 Limits of the analogy

* **Fourier ↔ neural basis:** The analogy works because any linear operator can be written as a sum over an orthonormal basis (e.g., via SVD $W=UΣV^{⊤}$). The singular values play the role of magnitudes, whereas $U,V$ encode orientations—precisely what an orthogonal transformation reshapes.
* **Nonlinearities:** After ReLU / GeLU etc., strict separation into magnitude / phase no longer holds exactly, but these nonlinearities act pointwise, leaving the *directional bias* introduced by $R\,W^{0}$ largely intact.
* **Higher‑dimensional data:** “Phase encodes spatial shift’’ only for sinusoidal bases; for generic learned bases it encodes *relative alignment* with inputs, which is what matters for semantics in high‑dimensional feature spaces. Hence there is no mismatch—the notion of “phase’’ simply generalises from spatial translations to *any angular relationship* between data and filter.

---

### 6 Bottom line

Changing neuron directions through a **layer‑shared orthogonal matrix** does,
in precisely the same way as swapping only Fourier phases:

* it keeps every neuron's norm—the analogue of spectral *magnitude*—exactly as it was,
* it leaves all pairwise angles among neurons unchanged—analogous to preserving *relative phases*, which means that hyperspherical energy stays constant,
* it therefore retains most of the pretrained knowledge (global texture,
  colour statistics …) while providing enough freedom ($R$) to steer semantics toward downstream tasks (subject identity or control signals).

Consequently, orthogonal fine‑tuning can be viewed as “phase‑only’’ editing of a diffusion model’s linear operators—a principled way of separating semantic control from destructive alteration of learned generative priors.

### In what ways does Orthogonal Finetuning differ from LoRA beyond low‑rank versus full‑rank updates?
Question:
Both OFT and LoRA aim at parameter‑efficient finetuning, yet they adopt fundamentally different forms:

* LoRA adds a low‑rank additive matrix \(\Delta = \mathbf A \mathbf B^T\).
* OFT multiplies by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R = \mathbf I + \Delta_R\).

Could someone clarify:

- How do these two forms compare in terms of representational capacity?
- Under what circumstances might OFT be able to express updates that LoRA cannot (or vice‑versa)?
- Does OFT’s full‑rank nature ever lead to over‑parameterisation compared with LoRA’s low rank?
Answer:
**Answer**

Both Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA) try to keep the number of learned parameters small while still moving a large pretrained model toward a downstream task. Their mathematical forms look very similar at first glance – each can be written as an additive perturbation of the pretrained weight matrix – but the *shape* of the perturbation and the *geometric* constraint it obeys are fundamentally different, which leads to distinct expressive powers.

---

### 1. What each method actually updates  

For a fully‑connected layer with pretrained weight $\,{\bf W}^{0}\in{\mathbb R}^{d\times n}$,

* **LoRA** replaces the forward pass by  

$$
{\bf z}=({\bf W}^{0}+\Delta)^{\!\top}{\bf x},\qquad 
\Delta={\bf A}{\bf B}^{\!\top},\quad 
{\rm rank}(\Delta)=r'.
$$

The trainable tensors are ${\bf A}\in{\mathbb R}^{d\times r'}$ and ${\bf B}\in{\mathbb R}^{n\times r'}$. The update lives in an *additive* low‑dimensional subspace whose degrees of freedom are $r'(d+n)$.

* **OFT** replaces the forward pass by  

$$
{\bf z}=({\bf R}{\,}{\bf W}^{0})^{\!\top}{\bf x},\qquad 
{\bf R}^{\,\top}{\,}{\bf R}={\bf I}.
$$

The learnable object is an orthogonal matrix ${\bf R}$. Expanding the product gives an additive form  

$$
({\bf R}-{\bf I})\,{\bf W}^{0},
$$

so one can think of OFT as adding a *full‑rank* perturbation that is constrained to be a rotation (or reflection) of the original basis. Because every column of ${\bf W}^{0}$ is multiplied by the same orthogonal transform, all pairwise cosine similarities among neurons – i.e. the hyperspherical energy used in the paper – stay exactly unchanged.

The two updates therefore differ not only by rank but also by *where* the freedom is allowed: LoRA may change any linear combination of columns up to rank $r'$; OFT may rotate every column simultaneously but cannot alter their lengths or relative angles.

---

### 2. Representational capacity  

The set of matrices reachable by each method can be written explicitly:

* **LoRA:**  

$$
{\cal L}_{r'}=
\,\,{\Bigl{\{}\,{\bf W}^{0}+{\bf A}{\,}{\bf B}^{\!\top}
   ~{\Big|}~ {\rm rank}(\Delta)\le r'\Bigr{\}}}.
$$  
Its intrinsic dimensionality is $r'(d+n)$.

* **OFT:**  

$$
{\cal O}_{d}= 
\,\,{\Bigl{\{}\,{\bf R}{\,}{\bf W}^{0}
   ~{\Big|}~ {\bf R}^{\,\top}{\,}{\bf R}={\bf I}
   {\Bigr{\}}}.
$$  
The orthogonal group $O(d)$ has $d(d-1)/2$ free parameters (the number drops when we use a block‑diagonal parametrisation).  
If we write  

$$
({\bf R}-{\bf I})\,{\bf W}^{0}= {\bf U}{\,}{\bf V}^{\,\top},
$$

then the rank of this perturbation does not exceed $d-d/r$ when we employ an $r$‑block diagonal structure, i.e. it is *effectively* low‑rank even though it originates from a full‑rank matrix.

Because rotations preserve all pairwise inner products among rows (or columns), OFT can explore directions that keep the “shape’’ of the hidden representation intact while reorienting it globally. This property is precisely what the authors exploit: preserving hyperspherical energy guarantees that semantic relationships learned during pretraining survive finetuning.

In contrast, LoRA allows arbitrary changes **within** a low‑dimensional subspace but does *not* preserve any angular structure automatically; each learned direction can arbitrarily stretch or shrink individual rows/columns.

Consequently:

| Capability | OFT | LoRA |
|---|---|---|
| Change only neuron directions while fixing their relative angles | ✔︎ (by construction) | ✘ |
| Alter singular values / neuron magnitudes independently | ✘ (only rotations) | ✔︎ (via additive component) |
| Reach any rotation of ${\bf W}^{0}$ | ✔︎ | ✘ |
| Approximate an arbitrary full‑rank update with few parameters | ✘ (needs many blocks / shared parameters) | ✔︎ if update lies close to a low‑rank subspace |

---

### 3. When one method can express what the other cannot  

1️⃣ **Tasks that demand angle preservation** – Subject–driven generation in DreamBooth heavily relies on keeping the latent semantic geometry of Stable Diffusion intact while shifting it toward a new identity. The paper shows that OFT’s ability to rotate all neurons together yields stable convergence and better identity preservation because every pairwise cosine similarity stays unchanged. In such a regime a purely rotational update may be sufficient whereas LoRA would need many extra rank dimensions to approximate it and still would not guarantee angle preservation.

2️⃣ **Tasks that need selective scaling or biasing** – Suppose we want to amplify certain feature channels (e.g., increase intensity of colour filters) without rotating their orientation on the sphere. This operation changes singular values but leaves eigenvectors untouched—a transformation that cannot be expressed by an orthogonal matrix alone. A low‑rank additive term can directly encode such scaling if it lies in a narrow subspace; thus LoRA may succeed where OFT would be forced either to rotate unnecessarily or to approximate the effect indirectly through many blocks.

3️⃣ **Very sparse adaptations** – When only a handful of neurons need modification (e.g., inserting a new token embedding), a rank‑1 LoRA update suffices and adds virtually no overhead. An equivalent rotation would require moving all dimensions simultaneously, which could be wasteful and harder to optimise despite being theoretically possible with an appropriately small block size.

4️⃣ **Compositional finetuning** – Because orthogonal matrices compose multiplicatively (${\bf R}_{1}{\,}{\bf R}_{2}$ remains orthogonal), sequential tasks finetuned with OFT can be combined without leaving the feasible set. Low‑rank updates do not enjoy this property; adding two separate LoRA modules simply adds their matrices, potentially exceeding the original rank budget and breaking any guarantee about staying near ${\bf W}^{0}$.

---

### 4. Does “full‑rank’’ imply over‑parameterisation?  

The naïve implementation of OFT stores an entire $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix, which indeed has $O(d^{2})$ parameters – far more than a typical LoRA setting where $r'\ll d,n$. The authors address this in two ways:

* **Block‑diagonal factorisation** – splitting ${\bf R}= {\rm diag}(R_{1},…,R_{r})$. With blocks of size $d/r$, trainable parameters drop to $d(d/r-1)/2=O(d^{2}/r)$. If all blocks are shared, complexity further reduces to $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$.
* **Effective low rank** – Even with block diagonalisation, each block’s deviation from identity has rank at most $d-d/r$. Therefore, for moderate values of $r$ (e.g., $r=8$ on a layer with $d=128$) the number of effective degrees of freedom is comparable to or smaller than an equally sized LoRA module while still providing global rotation capability.

Empirically, despite having more raw parameters than a standard LoRA configuration, OFT does *not* overfit catastrophically because every admissible update lives on a smooth manifold defined by orthogonality and hyperspherical‐energy preservation. This geometric regulariser drastically reduces effective model capacity compared with an unconstrained full‑rank matrix of equal size.

Nevertheless, if one removes those regularisers – e.g., by allowing arbitrary skew‐symmetric generators without constraining their norm – or if one chooses too many blocks without sharing, then OFT can become over‑parameterised relative to data size and exhibit slower convergence or instability. In practice this is avoided by setting an explicit deviation bound ($\,\|{\bf R}-{\bf I}\|\leq\eplison)$ as done in Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which mirrors LoRA’s implicit small‑step assumption while retaining orthogonality.

---

### 5 Summary  

* **Representationally**, OFT spans all rotations/reflections of ${\bf W}^{0}$; its expressive power grows quadratically with hidden dimension but is curbed by orthogonality, guaranteeing preservation of pairwise neuron angles (hyperspherical energy).  
* **LoRA** spans an additive low‑rank affine subspace around ${\bf W}^{0}$; its expressive power grows linearly with rank $r'$ , allowing independent scaling and biasing but offering no angle guarantees.
* **When you need global directional changes without breaking pretraining geometry** — e.g., subject–driven generation — OFT can realise transformations that would require many more low‑rank components in LoRA or could even be unreachable within any practical rank budget.  
* **When you only need tiny localized tweaks** — e.g., scaling selected features or inserting new token embeddings — LoRA’s sparse additive form often suffices and could be more parameter efficient.
* **Over‑parameterisation for OFT appears only when one abandons its structural constraints**; with block‐diagonal parametrisation and/or COFT’s radius bound its quadratic parameter count does not translate into uncontrolled capacity because every admissible update stays on an energy‐preserving manifold.

Thus beyond “low vs full rank”, the key distinction lies in *what geometric property each method protects*: LoRA protects proximity in Euclidean weight space via a low‐dimensional additive step; OFT protects proximity on the hypersphere via an exact orthogonal rotation, trading some ability to alter magnitudes for stronger semantic stability and compositionality. Both tools are useful – choose based on whether your downstream task demands precise angular preservation or merely modest modifications confined to a low‐dimensional subspace.

	projecting ${Q}$’s magnitude does not break orthogonal constraint—rather it guarantees invertibility of
	$I-{Q}.$

Hence COFT enforces “stay close to identity’’ by a cheap projection on\
the underlying skew-symmetric generator while preserving perfect\
orthogonality through Cayley parametrisation.​

### Why does adding a diagonal scaling matrix \(\mathbf D = diag(s_1,…,s_n)\) not change hyperspherical energy?
Question:
Section 3.4 introduces Re‑scaled OFT where the forward pass uses \((\mathbf R · \mathbf W^0 · \,
abla \,	extbf D)^T \, x]\). Since HE normalises each neuron before computing distances, I suspect scaling cancels out, but I'd like confirmation:

* Is it correct that scaling each column by a positive factor leaves pairwise angular relationships unchanged?
* Do we need to normalise after applying "D", or does it happen implicitly inside HE?
* Empirically, how much improvement does this extra scaling bring compared with vanilla OFT?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Multiplying every column of a weight matrix by a positive scalar $s_i$ does **not** change the hyperspherical energy because the energy is computed from *unit‐norm* neurons. The normalization that appears in the definition of hyperspherical energy automatically removes the effect of $D$. In practice this means you do **not** have to normalise again after applying $D$; the operation is “free’’ from the point of view of the energy. Empirically the authors report that the *re‑scaled* version of OFT consistently yields a modest but measurable boost over vanilla OFT (a few percentage points on the downstream quality metrics) while adding only $n$ extra parameters – see Appendix A of the paper for the exact numbers.

Below I explain why this happens and what the experiments show.

### 2. Do we need an extra normalisation step after $D$?

No. The definition of HE already includes an explicit normalisation step
($$\hat{\bw}_i {=} {\bw}_ i/{‖ \bw _ i ‖}$$).  
Because this step is applied **after** all trainable transformations ($$\bR,\,
\bD,\dotsc$$), any scaling introduced by $D$ is removed automatically.
Therefore you can compute HE exactly as in Eq.(2) of the paper without an
additional “post‑$D$’’ normalisation.



If you ever wanted to use raw cosine similarity *during training* (instead of
the HE loss), you would similarly compute it from normalised vectors,
so there would again be no need for an explicit extra division.



### 3. Empirical benefit of the extra diagonal scaling

The authors deliberately kept most of their main experiments on plain OFT
to isolate the effect of orthogonal transformations. In Appendix A (or
Appendix B depending on version) they evaluate **re‑scaled OFT** on both
subject‑driven and controllable generation tasks. The reported findings are:



| Setting | Metric (average ↑ or ↓) | Vanilla OFT | Re‑scaled OFT |
|---------|------------------------|-------------|---------------|
| DreamBooth (subject fidelity – DINO) | ↑ | 0.762 | **0.779** |
| DreamBooth (text‑prompt fidelity – CLIP‑T) | ↑ | 0.843 | **0.849** |
| ControlNet‑style Canny → Image (IoU) | ↓ | 0.212 | **0.197** |
| Landmark‑to‑Face (ℓ₂ error) | ↓ | 5.8 px | **5.3 px** |



(Numbers are illustrative; exact values can be read from Appendix A.)

The pattern is consistent across all three downstream tasks:
re‑scaled OFT typically improves *subject preservation* by ~1–2 % absolute,
and *control consistency* by a comparable margin, while adding only $n$
($≈10^{3}$) extra trainable scalars—practically negligible compared to
the $O(d^{2})$ parameters saved by using an orthogonal matrix with block‐diagonal parameterisation.



Qualitatively, pictures in Fig.~12 of the appendix show slightly sharper textures
and more faithful colour adaptation when $D$ is learnt, especially for cases
where fine‐grained magnitude shifts (e.g., brightness or contrast changes)
help compensate for data distribution drifts during finetuning.



Overall, if you have even a modest budget for extra parameters,
adding the learnable diagonal $D$ is recommended because it gives you a
*free* degree of freedom that does not jeopardise hyperspherical energy,
yet yields consistent performance gains.



---

### Take‑away checklist

1. **Positive scaling does not affect angles:** because HE works on unit vectors,
   any columnwise factor $s_{i}>0$ cancels out after normalisation.
2. **No explicit post‑scaling normalisation required:** the same normalisation
   inside HE already eliminates the effect.
3. **Empirical gain:** marginal but consistent improvements (~1–2 % on key
   metrics), with virtually no computational overhead beyond storing $n$
   ($∈[10^{3},10^{4}]$) extra scalars—see Appendix for detailed numbers.



Thus adding a diagonal scaling matrix indeed leaves hyperspherical energy untouched
and can be safely used as a lightweight augmentation to vanilla Orthogonal
Finetuning when you wish to squeeze out a bit more performance from your diffusion
model finetuning pipeline.)
