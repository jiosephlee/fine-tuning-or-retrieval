Foundations of Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

# The Forward Diffusion Process and Noise Schedules  

A modern text‑to‑image diffusion model treats an image as a random variable that is gradually corrupted by Gaussian noise.  Let \(x_{0}\in\mathbb R^{H\times W\times3}\) denote an original photo‑realistic image drawn from a data distribution \(q(x_{0})\).  A forward Markov chain produces a sequence 
\[
x_{1},x_{2},\dots ,x_{T}
\]
by repeatedly applying a small Gaussian perturbation at each step \(t=1,\dots ,T\).  Formally  
\[
q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal N\!\Bigl(x_{t};\,\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\;x_{t-1},\,\beta_{t}\mathbf I\Bigr),
\tag{1}
\]  
where \(\beta _{t}\in(0,1)\) is a *noise schedule* that controls how much variance is injected at step \(t\).  By repeatedly applying (1) we obtain a closed‑form marginal distribution  
\[
q(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\mathcal N\!\Bigl(x_{t};\,\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,x_{0},
(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t})\mathbf I\bigr),
\qquad 
\bar{\alpha}_{t}:=\prod_{s=1}^{t}(1-\beta_s).
\tag{2}
\]  
When \(t=T\) the cumulative product \(\bar{\alpha}_{T}\) becomes very small and \(x_T\simeq {\cal N}(0,\mathbf I)\); thus the forward process transforms any natural image into an isotropic Gaussian “noise canvas”.  

The choice of the schedule \(\{\beta _{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}\) critically influences both training stability and sample quality.  Empirically researchers use linear schedules, cosine schedules or variants where early timesteps receive little noise while later timesteps dominate the corruption (\cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score}).  Intuitively this mimics an annealing process: early steps preserve most low‑frequency structure whereas later steps destroy finer detail, giving the model ample opportunity to learn how structure disappears under controlled perturbations.


# Reverse Denoising as a Learned Conditional Score Function  

Training reverses the forward chain by teaching a neural network to predict how an image corrupted at step \(t\) should be “denoised”.  Let \(\mathbf c\data{}\) denote a conditioning signal derived from a textual prompt (or any other modality).  A parameterized model \(\epsilon_{\theta}(\cdot)\), often implemented as a UNet (see Section 3), takes \(x_{t}\), the timestep index \(t\), and the conditioning vector \(\mathbf c\data{}\), and outputs an estimate of the added Gaussian noise \(\epsilon\: (\sim {\cal N}(0,\mathbf I))\):
\[
\widehat{\epsilon}= \epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}, t,\mathbf c\data{}).
\]  
Training minimizes a simple mean‑squared error loss  
\[
{\cal L}_{simple}= 
\E_{x_0,\epsilon,t}\!\bigl[\, 
   \|\, \epsilon-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}, t,\mathbf c\data{})\, 
   \|^{2}
\bigr],
    \qquad
   x_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,\epsilon,
    \tag{3}
\]  
so that the network learns how to recover the removed noise component given its corrupted observation (\cite{ho2020denoising}).  

Once trained, sampling proceeds by approximating the posterior reverse transition  
```math
p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,\mathbf c\data{})=
    {\cal N}\!\bigl(
       x_{t-1};\, \mu_{\theta}(x_t,t,\mathbf c\data{}), 
       \sigma^{2}_{t}\mathbf I
    \bigr),
```  
where \(\mu_{\theta}\) is obtained from the predicted noise according to standard denoising formulas derived from Bayes’ rule (\cite{ho2020denoising,song2021denoising}).  Crucially, because **the model receives both \(x_t\) and the conditioning vector**, it learns a *conditional score function* 
\(s_\theta(x_t,t|\mathbf c\data{}) = -\,\nabla_{x_t}  \log p_\theta(x_t|\mathbf c\data{}) ,   
``
which directs stochastic trajectories toward images consistent with both visual priors learned during pretraining and semantic cues supplied by language.

# 2 The Need for Controlled Generation  

Modern text‑to‑image diffusion models—such as Stable Diffusion, DALL‑E 2, or Imagen—have demonstrated an astonishing ability to turn a free‑form textual description into a high‑fidelity photograph‑like image.  In practice, however, naïve prompting often falls short when an application demands *fine‑grained* or *repeatable* control over particular aspects of the output.  Two concrete downstream scenarios illustrate this limitation:

1. **Subject‑driven generation**, where one wishes to keep the visual identity of a specific object (a particular dog, a favorite toy, or a person’s face) while changing its pose, background, or clothing.
2. **Controllable generation**, where an external signal such as an edge map, a semantic segmentation mask, or facial landmarks should dictate low‑level structure while the textual prompt governs high‑level semantics.

Both scenarios share the same core problem: how to adapt an already powerful pretrained diffusion model *without* erasing its rich knowledge about visual diversity and realism.  The remainder of this chapter formalizes each task, spells out their learning objectives, and highlights why maintaining pretrained diversity during adaptation is especially challenging.



## Subject‑driven Generation (DreamBooth Scenario)

### Problem Statement  

In subject‑driven generation we are given only a *few* reference photographs of a target entity—call this set \(\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\), where \(N\) typically ranges from one to ten—and a textual token describing the desired context (e.g., “a photo of **[V]** riding a bicycle”).  The goal is to produce new images \(\mathbf{\hat{x}}\) that contain **the same visual identity** as the reference set—capturing subtle details such as fur pattern or facial features—while simultaneously obeying novel textual instructions (“in a park”, “wearing sunglasses”, etc.).

This setting was popularized by *DreamBooth*.  DreamBooth augments the vocabulary of the pretrained language–image model with a *custom token* (often denoted \([V]\)) associated implicitly with the subject’s appearance.  During finetuning it optimizes two losses simultaneously:

* A **reconstruction loss** that forces the diffusion model to regenerate each reference photograph when asked “a photo of \([V]\)”.  
* A **class‑specific prior preservation loss** that discourages the model from drifting away from its original distribution over the broader class (“dog”, “person”, …), thereby protecting overall realism.

Formally one may write an overall objective of the form  

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SB}} = 
\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!\! \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}(\mathbf{x}_i,\;\![V])}_{\text{reconstruction}}
+ 
\lambda_{\mathrm{pp}}\,
\underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}}\bigl(p_{\theta}(\cdot|\text{"a photo of }[V]\right)}_{\text{prior preservation}},
\]

where \(\theta\) denotes all trainable parameters after adaptation and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{pp}}\) balances fidelity to subjects versus adherence to the original class distribution.

The essential difficulty lies in learning *how* to change model weights so that \([V]\) consistently triggers the desired identity across all possible contexts expressed by arbitrary prompts.



## Controllable Generation with External Signals

### Problem Statement  

In many applications users possess an auxiliary representation \(c\) that conveys desired spatial layout or geometry:

* **Edge maps** \(c_{\mathrm{edge}}\) extracted by Canny detectors,
* **Segmentation masks** \(c_{\mathrm{seg}}\) indicating object categories per pixel,
* **Facial landmark sets** \(c_{\mathrm{lm}}\) representing keypoint coordinates on faces,

and they wish `diffusion( t , c ) → \mathbf{\hat{x}}` where \(t\) remains a textual description (“a cat sitting on a couch”) and \(c\) enforces structural constraints (“the cat’s silhouette follows this edge map”).  

Existing approaches such as *ControlNet* and *T2I‑Adapter* achieve this by attaching an auxiliary network branch that processes \(c\) and injects its representation into intermediate diffusion layers during finetuning.  In our work we adopt precisely this setting—same data splits, same base architecture (Stable Diffusion v1.5)—but replace their weight‑update scheme with orthogonal finetuning (OFT/COFT).  

During training these methods typically minimize an expectation over paired data \((c,\mathbf{x})\):

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CT}} = 
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(c,\mathbf{x})}\!\bigl[\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{diff}}\bigl(\mathbf{x},\,t,c\bigr)\bigr]}_{\text{diffusion denoising loss}}
+\lambda_{\mathrm{ctrl}}
\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(c,\mathbf{x})}\!\bigl[\!\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\bigl(c,\;\!g(\mathbf{\hat{x}})\bigr)\bigr]}_{\text{control consistency}},
\]

where \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm {diff}}\) is the usual score‑matching objective underlying diffusion training, \(g(\cdot)\) denotes a deterministic extractor producing an estimated control signal from an image (e.g., applying Canny again), and \(\lambda_{\mathrm {ctrl}}\) balances fidelity to textual semantics against adherence to visual constraints imposed by \(c\).

The second term encodes exactly what our newly introduced *control consistency metric* evaluates at test time: after generating \(\mathbf{\hat{x}}\), we compute \(g(\mathbf{\hat{x}})\) and compare it against the original input \(c\) using suitable pixel‑wise measures such as Intersection‑over‑Union (IoU), F1 score, or landmark‐wise \(\ell_2\) distance.



## Formal Objectives for Each Task  

Both downstream problems can be cast under a unified optimization lens:

> Find adapted parameters \(\theta^\star = \arg \min_\theta \Bigl( 
>   \underbrace{\lambda_{t}\,\mathcal L_{t}(\theta)}_{\text{textual alignment}}
>   + 
>   \underbrace{\lambda_{s}\,\mathcal L_{s}(\theta)}_{\text{subset‐specific regularizer}}
>   + 
>   \underbrace{\lambda_{c}\,\mathcal L_{c}(\theta)}_{\text{control fidelity}}
>   \Bigr).\]

*For subject‑driven generation*:  
- \(\mathcal L_{t}\) encodes likelihood under the diffusion forward process conditioned on prompts containing the custom token – effectively guaranteeing that generated images obey any textual instruction containing “\[V]”.  
- \(\mathcal L_{s}\) corresponds exactly to DreamBooth’s reconstruction + prior‐preservation terms described above (\(\lambda_{s}=1,\lambda_{c}=0\)).  

*For controllable generation*:  
- \(\mathcal L_{t}\) remains the ordinary diffusion denoising loss ensuring general image quality conditioned on text alone.  
- \(\mathcal L_{c}\) implements alignment between generated imagery and auxiliary signal (\(c_{\mathrm {edge}}, c_{\mathrm {seg}}, c_{\mathrm {lm}}\)). – This term may be instantiated as pixel‑wise cross‑entropy between predicted segmentation masks versus ground truth masks, or mean squared error between predicted versus provided landmarks after projection through detector \(g(\cdot)\).

Crucially these objectives are *joint*: any viable adaptation must satisfy both semantic alignment (what should appear according to language) **and** structural guidance (how it should appear according to external cues).  Isolating one side leads either to “creative” but uncontrolled outputs (pure prompting), or perfectly matching geometry yet nonsensical content (solely feeding edges without respecting prompt semantics).



## Challenges in Preserving Pretrained Diversity While Adapting New Semantics  

### 1️⃣ Maintaining High‑Fidelity Diversity  

Large pretrained diffusion models have absorbed billions of image–caption pairs; consequently they encode an enormous variety of textures, lighting conditions, viewpoints, and style variants across all object categories.  When finetuned on merely five photographs of one dog (“subject”), naïve parameter updates tend either **overfit**—the model collapses into reproducing those five poses repeatedly—or **erase**—the subtle characteristics distinguishing that dog from other dogs disappear because weight changes disturb intricate inter‑neuron relationships learned during pretraining.

Traditional finetuning reduces this risk by applying an exceptionally small learning rate so that Euclidean displacement
\[
\|\boldsymbol M - \boldsymbol M^{0}\|
\]
between current weights \(\boldsymbol M\) and pretrained weights \(\boldsymbol M^{0}\) stays minimal.  Yet Euclidean proximity does not guarantee preservation of semantic knowledge: two weight matrices may be close in Euclidean norm while having drastically different *pairwise angles* among neurons—a quantity tightly linked to how information flows through deep layers.



### 2️⃣ Controlling Structural Fidelity Without Breaking Semantic Richness  

In controllable generation one injects additional conditioning pathways stemming from edge maps or segmentation masks into intermediate layers of the diffusion backbone.  If these extra pathways are trained aggressively without constraints they can dominate gradient flow and force hidden representations into regimes never seen during pretraining—all other cues from text are ignored leading either to blurry images or severe artifacts around controlled regions.

Furthermore many controllable generation methods introduce dedicated adapter modules (*ControlNet*, *T2I‑Adapter*) whose parameters are trained separately from the main backbone.  Although this isolates new parameters structurally,
the underlying main weights still drift when backpropagation reaches them through skip connections.
Consequently you observe “sudden emergence’’ phenomena reported in ControlNet experiments: after many epochs almost no improvement appears until abruptly performance spikes—a sign that unregularized updates have finally managed nevertheless to re-align previously broken representations.



### 3️⃣ Quantifying Preservation of Generative Ability  

A central difficulty highlighted in recent research is *the lack of an intrinsic measure* reflecting how well generative capacity survives finetuning.  
The paper argues that **hyperspherical energy**
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\boldsymbol W)=
   \sum_{i\neq j}
   \bigl\|\widehat{\boldsymbol w}_i-\widehat{\boldsymbol w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
   ~~ 
   \widehat{\boldsymbol w}_i = 
       {\boldsymbol w_i}/{\|\boldsymbol w_i\|}
       ,
\]
(where each row/column vector describes one neuron within layer weight matrix \(\boldsymbol W\)) provides exactly such structural information:
it aggregates angular similarity across all neuron pairs on their unit hypersphere.
Empirically preserving hyperspherical energy correlates strongly with retaining diverse semantic content after adaptation,
whereas naïve Euclidean regularization fails because it does not enforce angle preservation.



### 4️⃣ Balancing Flexibility Versus Regularity  

The ultimate goal of controlled finetuning therefore lies at an intersection:

* **Flexibility** – sufficient degrees of freedom so that new semantics (*new identity*, *new geometry*) can be injected.
* **Regularity** – enough structural discipline so that underlying knowledge encoded in pairwise neuron directions remains intact and does not destabilize training dynamics.

Standard full‑parameter updates grant maximal flexibility but sacrifice regularity → instability & collapse.
Conversely extreme regularization—freezing all weights—yields perfect stability yet zero flexibility.
Orthogonal finetuning/OFT occupies precisely this middle ground:
by constraining updates into layer‑wise orthogonal matrices,
the pairwise neuron angles remain unchanged,
hence hyperspherical energy stays constant while still allowing every neuron direction vector to rotate jointly.
Adding an explicit radius constraint—as done in COFT—further limits how far orthogonal matrices stray from identity,
producing even smoother convergence especially when only tiny training datasets are available.



### 5️⃣ Sample Efficiency & Training Stability  

Because orthogonal transformations inherently preserve intra‑layer geometry,
they discourage catastrophic forgetting even when training data are scarce.
Experimental evidence in subjects ranging from few‑shot DreamBooth adaption
to large-scale controllable synthesis shows:
* Faster convergence (significant quality gains after just a handful of epochs),
* Reduced sensitivity to hyperparameters such as learning rate or total iteration count,
* No additional inference overhead—the learned orthogonal matrix can be pre‑multiplied into pretrained weights producing an equivalent single weight tensor for deployment.



---  

In sum, plain prompting ignores both *who/what* needs precise replication (subject identity) and *where/how* visual structure must conform (external controls).  
Subject‑driven generation extends prompting by embedding individualized visual concepts via few-shot fine­tuning,
while controllable generation augments prompting with explicit geometric priors.
Both demand principled optimisation objectives that jointly respect textual intent,
control fidelity, and above all preserve–as much as possible–the rich diversity baked into large pretrained diffusion models.
Understanding these requirements sets the stage for Orthogonal Finetuning (§ 3), which explicitly addresses how best to navigate this delicate trade­off between flexibility required for downstream tasks and stability needed for preserving pretrained knowledge.

3. Existing Finetuning Strategies and Their Shortcomings  

In order to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation, researchers have explored a number of straightforward adaptation techniques.  Although each of these methods can achieve some degree of specialization, the original paper observes that they often suffer from either unstable training dynamics or a degradation of the pretrained semantic capabilities.  In this chapter we critically examine the most common approaches—plain gradient finetuning with a small learning rate, low‑rank additive updates (LoRA), auxiliary conditioning networks such as ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter, and the class‑specific prior preservation losses introduced by DreamBooth—highlighting both their underlying intuitions and their empirical shortcomings.

# Fine‑tuning with a Small Learning Rate  

The most direct way to adapt a pretrained diffusion model is to continue training its parameters $\bm{M}$ using stochastic gradient descent (or one of its variants) while initializing from the pretrained weights $\bm{M}^{0}$.  The update rule is typically written as  
\[
\bm{M} \leftarrow \bm{M} - \eta\,\nabla_{\bm{M}} \mathcal{L}(\bm{M}) ,
\]  
where $\eta$ denotes a **small learning rate** and $\mathcal{L}$ is the task–specific loss (for instance a reconstruction loss in DreamBooth).  By choosing $\eta$ very low—often orders of magnitude smaller than those used during pretraining—the optimizer is forced to stay close to $\bm{M}^{0}$ in Euclidean space, i.e. it implicitly seeks to minimize  
\[
\|\bm{M}-\bm{M}^{0}\| .
\]  

Intuitively this makes sense: if only a few examples are available for a downstream task, one would like to perturb the model only slightly so as not to erase its broad visual knowledge.  However, as emphasized in the paper, **Euclidean distance alone does not capture semantic structure**.  Two weight matrices that are close in $\ell_{2}$ norm may differ substantially in how their neurons interact pairwise on a unit hypersphere—a representation known as **hyperspherical similarity**.  Since diffusion models generate images by repeatedly applying linear transformations followed by non‑linearities across many layers, even modest Euclidean drift can inadvertently alter angles between neurons and thereby change how high‑level concepts are encoded.  

Empirically, plain finetuning with a small learning rate sometimes succeeds early in training but later **collapses** when more epochs are performed.  For example, Figure 1(b) of the original work shows that after roughly $2\,000$ iterations DreamBooth—a method built on plain gradient updates—produces unstable or degenerated samples despite starting from a well‑behaved point after $400$ iterations.  The instability stems from an implicit lack of any structural regularizer beyond the Euclidean proximity term; the optimizer may wander into regions where pairwise neuron relationships no longer reflect those learned during pretraining, causing a loss of fidelity and diversity.

# Low‑rank Weight Updates (LoRA) – Formulation and Intuition  

Low‑rank adaptation (LoRA) was introduced as an alternative to unrestricted gradient updates by constraining the form of the weight change.  Instead of updating each entry of $\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ directly, LoRA parameterizes the deviation as a product of two smaller matrices:
\[
\Delta\bm{W}= \bm{B}\bm{A},
\qquad
\text{with } 
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{r'\times d},\
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r'},
\]  
where $r'\ll \min(d,n)$ denotes the rank hyperparameter chosen by the practitioner.  The finetuned weight therefore takes the form
\[
\bm{W}= \bm{W}^{0}+ \Delta\bm{W}
        = \bm{W}^{0}+ \bm{B}\bm{A}.
\]  

The intuition behind LoRA is twofold. First, **parameter efficiency**: only $r'(d+n)$ scalar parameters need to be learned rather than $dn$, which dramatically reduces memory consumption when $d$ and $n$ are large (as is typical for diffusion models). Second, **regularization via low rank**: restricting $\Delta\bm{W}$ to lie in a low–dimensional subspace limits how far $\bm{W}$ can deviate from $\bm{W}^{0}$ while still allowing expressive modifications aligned with task‐specific directions.

From an algebraic perspective LoRA’s additive update can be interpreted as nudging each row vector (or column vector) of $\bm W^{0}$ along directions spanned by a few basis vectors defined by $\bm A$ and scaled jointly by rows of $\bm B$.  Because these basis vectors are shared across all neurons within a layer, LoRA preserves much of the original **pairwise geometry** among neurons—if $r'$ is very small—while still permitting meaningful adaptation (e.g., injecting new style cues).  

Nonetheless, LoRA does not directly control how **angles between neurons** evolve during training because its constraint focuses solely on rank rather than hyperspherical geometry.  Consequently several practical drawbacks emerge:

* Even though $\Delta\bm W$ has limited rank, it may nonetheless rotate neurons arbitrarily within that subspace, potentially breaking the “hyperspherical energy” that encodes semantic relationships.
* When $r'$ is chosen too large for stability reasons (“increase expressivity”), LoRA begins behaving like unrestricted finetuning: it can overly distort neuron directions while still satisfying its own low‑rank constraint.
* Empirical results reported in Section 4 demonstrate that LoRA frequently fails to preserve subject identity in DreamBooth‐style experiments (“the model completely loses subject identity” for certain prompts) despite converging quickly on training losses.

Thus while LoRA offers computational benefits relative to naïve gradient finetuning, it lacks an explicit mechanism for safeguarding **semantic preservation**, which becomes particularly critical when only few examples are available for downstream tasks.

# Extra Networks that Inject Control Signals  

Beyond modifying weights inside the pretrained diffusion backbone itself, another family of approaches augments the model with **auxiliary conditioning networks** designed specifically to ingest additional spatial or structural cues at inference time.  ControlNet \citep{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter \citep{mou2023t2i} exemplify this paradigm: both start from a frozen pretrained text‑to‑image diffusion model and attach extra trainable modules that consume an auxiliary signal—such as Canny edges, segmentation masks, or dense pose maps—and inject processed features into multiple layers of the diffusion pipeline.

Mathematically such methods define an extra function $\Phi_{\theta_{\text{aux}}}$ parameterized by auxiliary weights $\theta_{\textbf aux}}$, which maps an auxiliary input $\mathbf{s}$ into an embedding vector $\mathbf{s}_{l}$ at layer $l$.  This embedding is then combined additively or multiplicatively with the original latent representation before each denoising step:
\[
\mathbf{x}_{l}' = \mathbf{x}_{l}+ f_{l}\bigl(\Phi_{\theta_{\text aux}}(\mathbf{s}) ,\, \mathbf{x}_{l}\bigr),
\]  
where $f_{l}$ denotes a simple linear mixing operation specified per layer.  
Crucially, both ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter keep all **core diffusion weights frozen**, thereby avoiding any direct perturbation of $\{\boldsymbol W^{0}\}_{l}$ across layers.

The advantage of this approach is that it isolates task‐specific capacity within dedicated parameters; one can train on comparatively few paired examples without risking damage to general knowledge stored in $\boldsymbol W^{0}$.  Moreover it permits *test–time* flexibility because different control signals can be swapped without retraining an entire diffusion backbone.

However this strategy introduces several limitations directly relevant to stability and semantic fidelity:

1. **Additional inference overhead** – During sampling every forward pass must compute both the main diffusion step *and* pass through every auxiliary network branch(s), leading to higher latency compared with pure backbone evaluations.
2. **Limited expressivity for subtle semantic changes** – Because only an additive bias is injected while core weights remain untouched, achieving detailed subject manipulation (e.g., injecting a specific person’s face across varied backgrounds) can be difficult; empirical comparisons show that despite controlling Canny edges well enough for coarse shapes,
   ControlNet’s generated images sometimes lack fine–grained realism compared with OFT’s orthogonal transformations.
3. **Potential overfitting when data are scarce** – When only a handful of supervision pairs exist (as is typical for personalized generation), even modestly sized auxiliary networks may memorize training samples rather than learning robust mappings between control signals and visual semantics.
4. **No guarantee of preserving hyperspherical relationships** – Since auxiliary branches influence activations post hoc rather than transforming neuron directions themselves,
   they do not maintain pairwise angles among original weights,
   leaving open possibilities for subtle drift that harms long‑term generative diversity.

Thus although extra conditioning networks provide a clean modular approach,
their reliance on additional parameters at test time and lack of structural regularization prevents them from fully addressing stability concerns observed in practice.

# Class‐specific Prior Preservation Losses Used by DreamBooth  

DreamBooth represents another line of work that focuses on *preserving* specific semantic attributes while enabling new compositions via textual prompts \citep{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  Its training objective mixes two components:

1. A **reconstruction loss** encouraging generated samples conditioned on novel textual tokens (e.g., “[V]”) together with image‐level guidance from a few subject photographs;
2. A **class–specific prior preservation loss**, which penalizes divergence from images belonging to a broader class sharing visual characteristics unrelated to individual identity (for example general dog images when personalizing a particular dog).

Formally let $\mathcal L_{\text {recon}}$ denote pixel/feature reconstruction error between generated output $G(\mathbf p)$ under prompt $\mathbf p$ containing personalized tokens
and its reference image,
and let $\mathcal L_{\text {prior}}$ be defined over a set
$\{\tilde{\mathbf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}$ drawn from the same generic class:
\[
\mathcal L_{\text {prior}} = 
    \frac1K\, \sum_{i=1}^{K}
    D_{\text {sim}}\bigl(G(\mathbf p_{\text {class}}),\, \tilde{\mathbf x}_{i}\bigr),
\]  
where $D_{\text {sim}}$ measures feature similarity via CLIP embeddings or other perceptual metrics,
and $\mathbf p_{\text {class}}$ denotes prompts describing generic class attributes without personalized tokens.
The total objective reads
\[
\mathcal L = 
    \lambda_{\text {recon}}\;\mathcal L_{\text {recon}}
    + 
    \lambda_{\text {prior}}\;\mathcal L_{\text {prior}},
\]  
with hyperparameters $(\lambda_{\text {recon}},\, \lambda_{\text {prior}})$ balancing fidelity against overfitting.\footnote{
The exact losses employed follow DreamBooth’s original implementation;
the present chapter does not add new terms beyond what appears in those references.
}

This formulation explicitly tries to keep generated images anchored within their broader feature manifold while allowing optional personalization through learned token embeddings added into
the text encoder’s vocabulary.
Consequently it mitigates catastrophic forgetting—an issue severe for naïve finetuning—but does so **only through direct supervision** rather than through structural invariants such as hyperspherical energy conservation.

Experimental findings described in Section 4 reveal that despite employing class‐preserving regularization,
DreamBooth still displays later instability:
after several thousand iterations newly minted subjects begin losing distinctive traits,
and artifacts appear when prompts request uncommon contexts (“yellow shirt” vs “yellow fur”).
These symptoms indicate that controlling Euclidean proximity via priors does not fully protect inter-neuron angular geometry,
which appears essential for long‑term semantic coherence according to later OFT analysis.

# Empirical Observations on Collapse or Loss of Identity  

Across all three families discussed above—plain small‐LR finetuning,
low‑rank adapters,
and auxiliary conditioning networks—the authors report consistent failure patterns when evaluating subject–driven generation or controllable generation tasks under realistic resource constraints:

* **Model collapse under prolonged training** – When fine‑tuned for many hundred updates,
the naïve gradient approach exhibits severe degradation manifesting as distorted textures,
mode collapse toward monochrome blobs,
or outright failure to generate any realistic picture.
This phenomenon was illustrated experimentally where after roughly $2\,000$ iterations DreamBooth’s outputs became “collapsed”.

* **Loss of subject identity** – In personalized generation experiments,
methods relying solely on additive updates either fail entirely or produce inaccurate variations.
For instance LO​RA often generates yellow fur instead of yellow shirts,
or “completely loses subject identity” when tasked with rendering an object placed inside a bowl.
Even though LO​RA’s rank restriction improves stability relative to bare gradients,
the resultant images sometimes bear little resemblance to target subjects because directional changes among neurons destroy key encoding patterns learned during pretraining.

* **Instability within conditional adapters** – While ControlNet retains overall visual quality thanks largely to its frozen backbone,
its reliance on added branches leads sometimes abrupt emergence of correct control signals only after many epochs;
before those points performance remains erratic.
T2I‑Adapter likewise displays slow convergence given limited data,
with quantitative metrics lagging behind both OFT variants even after comparable numbers of training epochs.

* **Insensitivity to iteration count for some methods vs OFT’s robustness** –
The paper emphasizes that OFT exhibits “insensitivity” towards fine‐grained scheduling:
even very large numbers ($>400$) iteration counts do not degrade output fidelity.
Conversely baseline techniques demand careful early stopping: otherwise they fall into poor local minima evidenced by drastic drops in DINO/CLIP-I metrics during later stages.
These empirically underscored weaknesses motivate introducing orthogonal constraints:
by preserving hyperspherical energy—the sum over inverse distances between normalized neurons—
the authors guarantee all pairwise angular relationships remain exactly unchanged under orthogonal transformation.
Consequently semantic information embedded across layers persists throughout finetuning leading directly to more stable convergence."

In summary, existing adaptation tactics either rely upon weak Euclidean proximity assumptions (plain finetuning),
impose only low‐rank structure without safeguarding neuron orientations (LoRA),
or delegate task specificity entirely onto extra modules while leaving underlying geometry untouched
(Auxiliary networks).
Moreover even sophisticated supervision such as class‐specific priors fails to prevent later degradation.
Through systematic experimental inspection these shortcomings become apparent across diverse downstream scenarios—
prompted me­thods suffer either instability or loss of subject fidelity,
thereby setting up motivation for Orthogonal Finetuning which explicitly addresses both robustness
and semantic preservation through mathematically provable invariance properties.

4. Geometry of Neuron Representations – Hyperspherical Energy  

In modern deep networks, especially large text‑to‑image diffusion models, the weight matrices of each layer contain thousands of individual neurons.  Understanding how these neurons relate to one another can reveal why a pretrained model remains capable of generating photorealistic images after it is adapted to a new task.  A powerful way to expose these relationships is to view each neuron as a point on a geometric object—the unit hypersphere—and then quantify the collective arrangement of all points with a single scalar called *hyperspherical energy*.  This chapter develops the geometric intuition behind this construction, defines the relevant quantities rigorously, and explains why preserving hyperspherical energy becomes a core principle for the orthogonal finetuning (OFT) method introduced in the paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*.

# Normalizing Neuron Weight Vectors onto the Unit Hypersphere  

Consider a fully‑connected layer (or an equivalent linear mapping in any architecture) with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \{\mathbf{w}_{1},\dots ,\mathbf{w}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where each column $\mathbf{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the vector of parameters that forms one neuron’s receptive field.  In raw form these vectors have arbitrary length and direction.  A common analytical trick—adopted throughout the paper—is to *normalize* every neuron:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|_{2}}, \qquad i=1,\dots ,n.
\]

Normalization projects each $\mathbf{w}_{i}$ onto the **unit hypersphere** $S^{d-1}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid \|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}=1\}$.  Geometrically this means that instead of caring about how far each point lies from the origin (the magnitude), we retain only its direction—its *angle* relative to any reference axis.  By working on $S^{d-1}$ we make explicit that many recent works (e.g., SphereNet~\cite{liu2017deep}) have observed comparable or even superior performance when neurons are constrained to lie on this sphere.  The reason is intuitive: two neurons that point in almost the same direction compute very similar features regardless of their absolute scale; conversely, distinctly oriented neurons encode qualitatively different information.  Thus normalizing strips away irrelevant scaling factors and leaves us with a clean geometric picture where every neuron occupies a point on an intrinsically bounded surface.

# Cosine Similarity as Angular Similarity  

When vectors are placed on $S^{d-1}$ their pairwise relationship reduces naturally to an *angular* one—how large is the angle $\theta_{ij}$ between $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}$?  The cosine of this angle,

\[
\cos(\theta_{ij}) = \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j},
\]

is called **cosine similarity**.  Because each vector has unit norm, the inner product equals exactly this cosine value; no further normalization is required.  Cosine similarity ranges from $-1$ (exactly opposite directions) through $0$ (orthogonal directions) up to $+1$ (identical directions).  

Why is cosine similarity meaningful for semantics? Empirical studies cited in the paper~\cite{liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular} demonstrate that angular discrepancies between feature vectors correlate strongly with semantic differences in visual tasks.  When two neural activations have a high cosine similarity they tend to encode similar visual concepts (e.g., “dog” vs “cat”).  Conversely, low cosine similarity indicates distinct concepts.  Hence analyzing all pairwise cosines among neurons provides an angular fingerprint of what knowledge a layer has captured.

A convenient alternative view replaces cosine similarity with Euclidean distance on $S^{d-1}$.  For unit vectors,

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\|_{2} = \sqrt{2(1-\cos(\theta_{ij}))}.
\]

Thus distances and cosines are monotonic transformations of each other: smaller distances imply larger cosines and more aligned directions.  This equivalence will be crucial when we define hyperspherical energy.

# Definition of Hyperspherical Energy  

The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) quantifies how uniformly—or conversely how redundantly—the set of normalized neurons populates $S^{d-1}$.  Formally,

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = 
    \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i \neq j}}^{n}
    \bigl\|\,
        \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
        -
        \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}
    \,\bigr\|_2^{-1}.
    \tag{1}
\]

Each term $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\|_2^{-1}$ can be interpreted as an *inverse repulsive potential*: pairs of neurons that are close together on the sphere contribute a large amount to the total sum because their distance denominator is small.  Conversely, well‑separated pairs contribute little.  Summing over every unordered pair yields a global scalar that simultaneously captures two complementary aspects:

* **Redundancy:** If many neurons cluster around similar directions, many terms become large → high HE.
* **Uniformity:** If neurons spread evenly across all possible directions, distances increase → low HE.

In practice we rarely compute an exhaustive sum for extremely wide layers; nonetheless Eq.~(1) provides an exact definition used throughout the paper for theoretical reasoning and for measuring empirical changes during finetuning.

Because $\operatorname{HE}(\cdot)$ uses only normalized vectors, it ignores magnitude entirely—it operates purely on angles—and therefore aligns perfectly with our earlier discussion about cosine similarity being an appropriate semantic proxy.

# Empirical Connection Between Energy Levels and Generalization / Semantic Richness  

Why should we care about whether $\operatorname{HE}$ is low or high?  
The paper draws several lines of evidence linking hyperspherical arrangements to downstream performance:

1. **SphereNet Observation** – Training networks whose neurons are explicitly constrained on $S^{d-1}$ yields comparable capacity but noticeably better *generalization*~\cite{liu2017deep}.  The hypothesis is that forcing neurons onto the sphere discourages pathological scaling while preserving rich angular relationships.

2. **Toy Auto‑Encoder Experiment** – In Figure~2 (referred to as Fig.~\ref{fig:toy_ae} in the paper), a standard convolutional auto‑encoder trained with ordinary inner products was evaluated at test time under three alternatives:
   - Using both magnitude *and* angle (the original inner product);
   - Using only magnitude;
   - Using only angle via cosine activation.
   
   The results showed that retaining **only angular information** almost perfectly reconstructed inputs, whereas magnitude alone contributed essentially no useful signal.  
   This demonstrates empirically that *the direction* of learned filters carries most semantic content—mirroring the intuition behind hemispherical energy as an indicator of meaningful structure.

3. **Finetuning Behaviour** – When conventional finetuning updates weights via small Euclidean steps (e.g., DreamBooth or LoRA), there is no guarantee that pairwise angles among neurons stay similar to those pretraining has established.  Consequently generated images may drift away semantically even though Euclidean distance from the original weights appears small—a phenomenon observed in Fig.~4 where LoRA sometimes collapses identity preservation despite modest weight changes.
   
   By contrast, when OFT enforces $\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)=\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W^0)$ exactly—where $\mathbf W^0$ denotes pretrained weights—the resulting models retain their high‑fidelity generation ability across downstream tasks such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation.
   
Collectively these observations suggest:  
- A *low* hyperspherical energy compared with random initialization often correlates with better generalization because neurons are forced onto diverse directions early on.
- Maintaining *the same* level of energy during finetuning safeguards *semantic richness*: it keeps the relative angular layout learned during pretraining intact while still allowing controlled directional rotations through orthogonal transforms.

# Invariance Property: Pairwise Angles Are Preserved Under Orthogonal Transformations  

The crucial mathematical fact enabling Orthogonal Finetuning is that any **orthogonal matrix** $\mathbf R$—satisfying $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I$—preserves all pairwise angles among vectors it multiplies:

Take two normalized neurons $\hat{\mathbf w}_i$, $\hat{\mathbf w}_j$. After applying $\mathbf R$, they become $\tilde{\mathbf w}_i=\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i$ and $\tilde{\mathbf w}_j=\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_j$. Their inner product transforms as

\[
\tilde{\mathbf w}_i^\top \tilde{\mathbf w}_j 
     = (\hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \mathbf R^\top)\,
       (\mathbf R\, \hat{\mathbf w}_j)
     = \hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top (\underbrace{\mathbf R^\top \!\! \mathbf R}_{=\;\mathbf I}) 
        \hat{\mathbf w}_j
     = \hat{\mathbf w}_i^\top \hat{\mathbf w}_j .
\]

Because inner products are unchanged, cosines—and consequently Euclidean distances on $S^{d-1}$—are unchanged:

\[
\bigl\|\tilde{\mathbf w}_i-\tilde{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|_2
 = 
   \bigl\|\,
         \hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j
      \,\bigr\|_2 .
\]

Plugging these identities into Eq.~(1),

\[
	HE(\tilde {\bf W}) 
     = 
	HE(\bf R {\bf W}^{0})
     = 
	HE({\bf W}^{0}) .
   % note
%
Thus any orthogonal transformation leaves hyperspherical energy exactly invariant.\footnote{
Formally one may write ${W}=R\,W^0$ where $W^0$ comprises columns 
$\bigl(w^0_1,\dots,w^0_n\bigr)$. Then ${HE}(R\,W^0)=HE(W^0)$ follows from
orthogonal invariance of inner products.
}

This invariance explains why OFT can “rotate” all neurons within a layer while guaranteeing that *the entire geometric fingerprint remains identical*. From a learning perspective it means we may modify each neuron's direction arbitrarily—as long as we apply one global rotation or reflection per layer—without altering any pairwise angular statistic deduced from pretraining data.

A practical corollary emerges for finetuning design:  
if we restrict ourselves to transformations parameterized by orthogonal matrices,
the update step automatically satisfies $\left| HE(\bf W)-HE(\bf W^0)\right|=0$, yielding perfect preservation of semantic structure according to our hypotheses above.  Consequently OFT reduces finetuning to learning an orthogonal matrix per layer—a dramatically smaller search space compared with arbitrary full‑rank weight updates—while still providing enough flexibility to encode new task‑specific directional cues (for instance adjusting a subject’s pose or aligning generated images with control signals).

Moreover, since orthogonal matrices can be expressed as rotations or reflections ($det(\bf R)=+1$ or $-1$), they induce smooth changes on the underlying manifold ($S^{d-1}$). This smoothness contributes further toward stable optimization dynamics—a claim reinforced experimentally in the paper where OFT converges faster than naive finetuning methods despite using far fewer trainable parameters.

### Summary  

In this chapter we have built up from first principles why treating neural weights as points on a unit hypersphere uncovers essential semantic information hidden in their directions. Normalization discards irrelevant magnitudes and brings every neuron onto $S^{d-1}$. Cosine similarity then measures angular agreement—the geometric proxy for semantic relatedness—while hyperspherical energy aggregates all pairwise distances into a single statistic indicating either redundancy or uniform coverage across directions. Empirical findings within the paper demonstrate that low or preserved energy correlates strongly with good generalization and faithful image generation after finetuning. Finally, because all pairwise angles remain invariant under any orthogonal transformation \( {\bf R}^\top {\bf R}= {\bf I}\), applying such transformations constitutes an elegant finetuning strategy: Orthogonal Finetuning can reshape neuronal directions while guaranteeing zero change in hyperspherical energy, thereby protecting pretraining knowledge while acquiring new controllability capabilities.

5. Orthogonal Transformations – Theory and Parameterizations  

In modern text‑to‑image diffusion models, finetuning must preserve the delicate relational structure that was learned during pre‑training.  This relational structure is captured by **hyperspherical energy**, a quantity that aggregates pairwise angular relationships among neurons within a layer.  An elegant way to keep hyperspherical energy unchanged while still allowing the model to adapt to a new task is to restrict finetuning updates to **orthogonal transformations** of the weight matrices.  In this chapter we develop the mathematical foundations of orthogonal matrices, explain why they preserve angles, distinguish between rotations and reflections, and then present practical parameterizations—most notably the Cayley transform and block‑diagonal constructions—that make learning such transformations feasible at scale.

---

## Orthogonal Group \(O(d)\)

Let \(d\) denote the dimensionality of a neuron space (for example, the input dimension of a fully‑connected layer).  A real square matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) belongs to the **orthogonal group** \(O(d)\) if it satisfies  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I},
\]

where \(\mathbf{I}\) is the \(d\times d\) identity matrix and \((\cdot)^{\top}\) denotes matrix transpose.  This condition implies that \(\mathbf{R}\) preserves Euclidean inner products:

\[
\langle \mathbf{R}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}\rangle
    = (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{u})^{\top}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v})
    = \mathbf{u}^{\top}\underbrace{\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}}_{=\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{v}
    = \langle \mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{v}\rangle,
\]

for any vectors \(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\).  Consequently an orthogonal matrix acts as a rigid rotation or reflection of space—it does not stretch or compress vectors, nor does it alter angles between them.  In finetuning we exploit precisely this property: applying an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) to every neuron in a layer changes their directions uniformly while leaving all pairwise angular relationships intact.
