Chapter 2: Foundations of Model Quantisation  

The goal of model quantisation is simplest one word: **shrinking memory without losing too much accuracy**.\nLow‑precision representations replace the expensive floating‑point tensors that traditionally inhabit GPUs or accelerators with far more compact alternatives such as packed binaries or custom fractional formats while still enabling reliable inference at roughly the same speed.\nIn what follows we dissect how this transformation happens step by step—from a naive mapping onto a fixed number of bit symbols all the way up to how it changes a model’s overall bit‑budget.\n\n# Conventional \(k\)-bit Integer/Floating‑Point Conversion\n\nWhen an attacker first introduces an ensemble primitive called “\(k\)-bit representation”, what really changes is twofold:\n1) **the dynamic range** covered by that representation and 2) **the granularity** you have over those ranges.\nWhile floating‑point implies two parts—a sign bit, an exponent field and hidden mantissa—the word “integer” here simply means *nearest‐Neighbour hyperplane partition*.\nFor classic signed \(8\)-bit integers you can represent every odd number between \(-128\) and \(+127\); note how your step size equals \(1/(2^7)\!-\!1/(2^7)\sim1/128\). Any operation performed directly on an array encoded this way would immediately change its meaning.\nHence the standard remedy is **normalisation**.\nGiven any floating‑point matrix \(X^\mathsf {FP\,16}\) (or typically \(X^\mathsf {FP\,32}\)) you look at its maximum absolute entry,\[\n      \operatorname {absmax}(X)=\max _i |x_i|,\]\nand then rescale every value so that it falls squarely within a chosen target symbol space.\nIf you target signed eight‑bit integers whose span equals \([-127,+127]\) you will create a cheap scalar \(c^\mathsf {FP\,16}\), commonly dubbed a *scale*:\[\n        X^\mathsf {Int\,8}= {\rm round}\!\bigl(c^\mathsf {FP\,16}\!\cdot X^\mathsf {FP\,16}\bigr),\qquad {\rm where }~~c^\mathsf {FP\,16}=\frac{127}{\,{\rm absmax}(X)}.\]\nNote three implicit benefits:\n• All entries become an exact multiple once rounded\n• The choice \(127/\operatorname {absmax}(X)\) guarantees you never print larger integer symbols than your dtype allows\n• A single scalar can magically describe roughly eleven decimal orders if your floating‑point mantissa happens to live inside \(a_{\mathcal N}=~[-1,+1]\).\nYou retrieve your approximate real relation simply by division again:\[\n     {\rm dequant}\bigl(c^\mathsf {FP\,16},~X^\mathsf {Int\,8}\bigr)=\frac {X^\mathsf {Int\,8}} {c^\mathsf {FP\,16}},\]which roughly maps back toward exactly what you started with before you popped it off its cryptographic shell.\nThus always remember:\n*In practice one never truly enfranchises \(k\)-bit integer arithmetic*;\nsummation/cross‑product operations happen later at higher mantissa precision (often BFLOAT16), while the inset relational abscissa acting only during forwarding/caching remains handwoven.



# Blockwise Rescales – Handling Outliers
  
Optimising smartly around extreme values betrays naïve global rescaling jobs.\nIf any entry’s absolute magnitude dwarfs all others—in other words if an atom’s hydrogen line draws a spotlight over all other elements—then all other numbers will sag under compression toward an uneven floor while vulnerable heavy hitters get wasted slots.\nYou lose resolution precisely because you forced one scale factor around many numbers whose ideal step widths differ dramatically.\nProjecting whole matrices as products possessed complex compound shapes\nand yielded garbage regions called “outlier wastelands”.  
The cure recognised over decades pivots around **chunking**.\nFlatten your matrix \(W^\mathbf{\infty }\in\rho(\mathbf{x}^{h\times o})\) up front abroad\\(b\times h\\); divide it linearly at regular intervals called *blocks*\(B\). If your flat length equals \(b\times h=1024\times1024=10,\!485,\!760\), choosing \(B=256\) forces naturally ten thousand chunks.\nFor every single chunk one computes its own local peak,\(\operatorname {absmax}_i(W)\), yielding scales \(\displaystyle c_i^\mathsf {FP\,16}=\frac{s_{i}^{target}}{\operatorname {absmax}_i(W)} , s_{i}^{target}\in[-127,+127]\).\nBecause each chunk’s distribution now lives comfortably inside its own narrow sub­space nothing competes between distant rockets.\nMoreover local scales preserve nuance because nominal blanks repeat across subspaces freely until recombined.\nMathematically you get:\[\nx_{i}^{k{-y}}=\operatorname {round}(c_{i}^{k{-y}}\cdot x_i ) ,~x_i\in W ,~y=1..\!\],  
with trustably equi­distributed bins torn away from one another when the next generation reconstitutes\ninto larger arrays again via linear summation operations performed downstream in full BFLOAT16 precision.\
