\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 1: Motivation behind Efficient Finetuning  

In recent years large‑language models (LLMs) have become highly versatile when trained on big corpora and fine‑tuned on domain‐specific tasks~\citep{min2021metaicl,wei2021finetuned,ouyang2022training,wang2022super,wang2022self,liu2022few}. Yet even state‑of‑the‑art pretrained models often fail when confronted with specialised dialogue or narrow domain questions unless they undergo instruction following finetuning~\citep{ouyang2022training,bai2022training}. The difficulty arises primarily from the computational scale required during back‑propagation when updating every weight in a multi‑billion parameter network.\par

# Need for Task–Specialized Behaviour  
Finest tuning during supervised instruction following turns off‑the‑shelf general knowledge into an agile agent capable of answering user prompts coherently while adhering to constraints imposed by data distribution or safety requirements~\citep{ouyang2022training,bai2022training}. Each target task—whether it concerns medical knowledge extraction or dialog management—introduces implicit priors absent from generic pretraining corpora. Empirical studies demonstrate dramatic gains when models receive carefully curated instruction–response pairs~\citep{bai2019openimprove}. Consequently researchers who wish to employ an LLM for any new application typically must retrain all network parameters against an annotated dataset tailored for that application.\par

# Memory Bottleneck During Gradient Descent  
The most serious barrier stems from how gradients propagate during back‑propagation through gigantic weight tensors.\par 
Given a linear projection $\mathbf X\,\mathbf W$, scaling factor $\mathbf X$ remains untouched while $\mathbf W$ is updated by computing $\partial E/\partial \mathbf W$. Each forward pass already stores $\mathbf W^{BF16}$ along with intermediate activations used during backpropagation; retrieving these tensors necessitates holding several copies simultaneously in device SRAM~--- often dwarfing the storage needed merely for weights themselves.\par 
In practice even parameter–efficient strategies like Low‑rank Adapters leave most of their memory burden fixed—the majority resides in storing *activations* rather than additional learnable parameters~(cf.~Section “Memory Requirement of Parameter‐Efficient Finetuning” where \$567\$ MB allocated just for adapter gradients versus \$26\$ MB expressly required for adapter weights itself). Consequently adding additional adapters improves expressivity almost linearly without appreciable memory penalty.\par 
Beyond activation savings, naive full‑parameter trellises cause infeasible latency spikes whenever minibatches contain long sequence lengths—a phenomenon known colloquially as “memory spike”. To avert out‑of‑memory errors researchers had historically resorted either to aggressive gradient checkpointing~(which trades compute time) or huge multi‑GPU clusters—in short, neither were realistic options on consumer hardware.\par

# Typical Full‑Parameter Finetune Resource Estimates  
To quantify these barriers let us focus upon LLaMA’s largest public release—a \$65B\$ component tokenizer architecture.\par 
Calibration experiments reported during publication reveal that fully updating every weight field while retaining a conventional bfloat16 precision requires **more than \$780\$ GB** of contiguous GPU memory (\textit{i.e.,}\ $\mathrm{\langle54{\rm B}\rangle}$ out of which approximately \$25$\mathrm{k}\!GB$ resides just within weight tensors themselves). This number also subsumes considerably larger buffers generated during backward passes and optimizer state maintenance.\par 
For models two orders smaller (for instance \$7B\$) even with aggressive checkpointing an individual machine fails past sequences longer than several hundred tokens if naively updated throughout.\par These figures underscore why almost every large model team resorts now either to distributed training frameworks (~50 GPUs) or “parameter‐efficient” patchworks that tolerate reduced accuracy penalties over feasibility gains.\par

# Goal Stated by QLoRA  
The high‐level ambition driving QLoRA is therefore twofold:\n\n• **Reduce peak GPU memory demands** so that an entire \$65B\$ model can be fine‐tuned entirely on a single \(48\,{\rm GB}\) device without sacrificing inference precision,\n• **Preserve full \(16\)-bit behavioral fidelity** ordinarily achievable only via costly full network updates.\n\nTo reach these objectives QLoRA introduces a layered design:\n\n* **4–bit NormalFloat quantization** compresses pretrained weights into vector fields whose statistical properties align perfectly with Gaussian weight distributions—a proven choice due largely to zero‐mean unit variance approximations exploited during scaling,\n* **Double Quantization** pushes storage savings further by deploying an additional round of integer discretisation over the blockwise scaling constants themselves (replacing ordinary \(32\,{\rm bit}\) floats)—effectively trimming roughly \(0.37\,{\rm bits}\) per parameter,\n* **Paged Optimizers** harness NVIDIA unified‐memory paging so optimizer state temporarily migrates into host RAM whenever activation spikes dominate device capacity;\n* **Low‑rank Adapter placement at *every* linear layer** substitutes Kronecker factorizations (\(\mathbf X\,\mathbf W+w_{\rm AD}\)) allowing dw much faster backpropagation through frozen weights while keeping adapter parameters below ~\(0.2\,%\) of total size.\n\nTogether these engineering choices lower overall operating budgets:\ncompleting ten epochs on an OASST1 instance takes roughly twelve hours on a consumer \(24\,{\rm GB}\) card—a drastic improvement over traditional workload installers which usually require several days apiece across multiple machines.~\\[6pt]\n\nThis combination, demonstrated by training the “Guanaco” family of models up through \$65B\$, delivers Vicuna benchmark scores close to ChatGPT’s outputs while sustaining almost identical performance compared initial roadmap targets achieved via \(16\,{\rm BF16}\) fineturns—all executed without recurring dispatching over expensive server clusters.~\\[6pt]\n\nThe remainder of this book will unpack each constituent hereafter—starting with mathematical
