# Metrics and settings
MMLU (Massively Multitask Language Understanding), 5-shot: used as a broad NLU proxy for instruction-following performance. The paper reports 5-shot accuracy on the $57$ MMLU tasks. MMLU is used especially to compare models finetuned on instruction datasets (FLAN v2, Alpaca) across model sizes.

Vicuna benchmark (automated and human evaluations): the Vicuna prompts are fed to each system and responses are evaluated either by GPT-4 (automated scoring relative to ChatGPT) or by human annotators. The automated protocol includes scoring both ChatGPT and the candidate response on a $0$–$10$ scale and computing a percentage relative to ChatGPT’s score.

OA benchmark (OASST1 validation): used as a second, more conversational evaluation set. Prior turns are included in the prompt when available to preserve dialog context.

Generation sampling settings: nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ are used consistently for generative evaluations. This ensures comparable stochasticity across systems.

Why the choices? MMLU provides a controlled, multiple-choice accuracy metric widely used for instruction-finetuned models; Vicuna and OA provide real-world, generation-based evaluations that reflect chatbot utility. The sampling hyperparameters fix randomness across comparisons so differences in responses reflect model behavior rather than differing generation heuristics.

# Hyperparameter search and pitfalls of default LoRA settings
A key empirical finding in the paper is that default or commonly used LoRA configurations are insufficient to replicate full finetuning, particularly at larger model scales. Two interrelated points are central.

1) Amount and placement of adapters matter more than adapter parameter count. The authors find that applying LoRA only to the query and value attention <|endoftext|> (a common practice) fails to match full-finetuning performance on many tasks and scales. The most critical LoRA hyperparameter turned out to be how many adapters are used across the network: matching 16-bit finetuning required adding LoRA adapters to all linear layers in each transformer block, not just a small subset. Intuition: while LoRA parameter count is small, the <|endoftext|> capacity needed to approximate the update of a full model can be distributed across many submodules; restricting adapters to few <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> adaptation pathways and hurts final performance.

2) Adapter dimension and tiny parameter percentages are less sensitive. The projection rank $r$ (LoRA’s <|endoftext|> dimension) and the fact that LoRA parameters are a small fraction (commonly about $0.2\%$ of the original model parameters) were not the limiting factors. In controlled experiments the value of $r$ had little effect compared to the total number and placement of adapters. This supports the approach of placing many small adapters rather than a few larger ones.

Memory intuition and concrete footprint comparisons clarify why this approach is feasible. Even though LoRA parameters are small in absolute size <|endoftext|> on one setup LoRA parameters were only $26\ \mathrm{MB}$), the dominant memory cost during finetuning comes from activation <|endoftext|> For example, in a <|endoftext|> <|endoftext|> model trained with batch size $1$ on FLAN v2, the LoRA input <|endoftext|> consumed $567\ \mathrm{MB}$ while LoRA parameters were $26\ \mathrm{MB}$. With <|endoftext|> checkpointing the input <|endoftext|> average down to $18\ \mathrm{MB}$ per sequence. Meanwhile, storing a 4-bit base model consumed roughly $5048\ \mathrm{MB}$. The practical consequence is that increasing the number of adapters has only a small effect on total memory, so adding adapters in more places is an effective way to recover the <|endoftext|> capacity lost by freezing the majority of the model.

# Evaluation protocols: GPT-4 scoring, pairwise comparisons, and handling ordering effects
The paper uses a mix of automated (GPT-4) and human evaluations, combined by tournament-style aggregation, to produce robust <|endoftext|> comparisons.

Automated scoring versus ChatGPT (relative scoring): On the Vicuna prompts the authors use GPT-4 to score two responses (ChatGPT’s and a candidate model’s) on a $0$–$10$ scale along with an explanation. The candidate model’s performance is reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score (so a model matching ChatGPT would be $100\%$; a model scoring higher than ChatGPT can exceed $100\%$). Important practical observation: GPT-4 displayed an ordering effect, assigning higher scores to the response that appeared earlier in the prompt. To mitigate this bias the authors recommend reporting the mean score over both input orders (model A first then model B, and vice versa), which controls for ordering artifacts.

Pairwise head-to-head comparisons (three-class labeling): For more direct comparisons the authors use pairwise evaluations in which GPT-4 (or human annotators) are prompted to choose the better response or declare a tie. This is reduced to three outcomes: win/loss/tie. Pairwise judgments are then used to build tournaments.

Human evaluation details: Human annotators were recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk. For comparisons against ChatGPT the paper collected two human ratings per comparison; for pairwise comparisons among systems the study collected three human annotators per comparison. These judgments are used both for absolute comparisons and for Elo aggregation.

Nucleus sampling and consistency: All generative outputs in these evaluations used nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ to maintain consistent stochasticity across systems.

Elo rating aggregation and tournament protocol: The authors aggregate pairwise results into Elo scores. The specific choices are: initial Elo rating of $1000$, $K=32$ (the update factor), and the tournament sampling procedure is repeated $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds to control for ordering and sampling noise. Repeating many times yields distributions over Elo values and helps ensure reported rankings are not due to sampling artifacts.

Agreement and reliability diagnostics: The paper reports several inter-rater and inter-method agreement statistics that illuminate the strengths and limits of model-based evaluation:

- Moderate <|endoftext|> agreement between GPT-4 rankings and human rankings: Kendall Tau $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman correlation $r=0.55$. This shows GPT-4 and humans often agree on which systems are better, but not perfectly.

- Example-level agreement between GPT-4 and human annotator majority vote is weaker: Fleiss $\kappa = 0.25$. This indicates that at the <|endoftext|> level there is notable disagreement.

- Human annotator inter-rater agreement was moderate: Fleiss $\kappa = 0.42$ for human raters in the human-only comparisons.

- A concrete bias observation: GPT-4 assigned significantly higher Elo to its own outputs than humans did (Elo $1348$ vs $1176$), suggesting GPT-4 may be more favorable to outputs similar to its own generation style.

Practical consequences and mitigation strategies documented in the paper:
- Because automated scorers (GPT-4) can have biases (ordering effects, self-favoring), the authors combine automated and human evaluations and recommend controlling ordering by averaging over both presentation orders.
- Pairwise comparisons and Elo aggregation reduce the problems of absolute scoring scales (what does a $7$ out of $10$ mean?) by relying on relative judgments, which are more stable and easier for both humans and model evaluators to make.
- Repeating the tournament aggregation many times ($10{,}000$ seeds) reduces variance induced by sampling and ordering.
