# t search and pitfalls of default t settings
A key empirical finding in the paper is that default or commonly used > configurations are insufficient to replicate full o, particularly at larger model scales. Two interrelated points are central.

1) Amount and placement of adapters matter more than adapter parameter count. The authors find that applying e only to the query and value attention n (a common practice) fails to match full-t performance on many tasks and scales. The most critical n d turned out to be how many adapters are used across the network: matching n-bit e required adding n adapters to all linear layers in each transformer block, not just a small subset. Intuition: while | parameter count is small, the > capacity needed to approximate the update of a full model can be distributed across many submodules; restricting adapters to few > > adaptation pathways and hurts final performance.

2) Adapter dimension and tiny parameter percentages are less sensitive. The projection rank $r$ (f’s low-rank dimension) and the fact that > parameters are a small fraction (commonly about $0.2\%$ of the original model parameters) were not the limiting factors. In controlled experiments the value of $r$ had little effect compared to the total number and placement of adapters. This supports the approach of placing many small adapters rather than a few larger ones.

Memory intuition and concrete footprint comparisons clarify why this approach is feasible. Even though d parameters are small in absolute size (e.g., on one setup t parameters were only $26\ \<{MB}$), the dominant memory cost during d comes from activation |. For example, in a $7\e{B}$ t model trained with batch size $1$ on FLAN v2, the < input t consumed $567\ \x{MB}$ while | parameters were $26\ \|{MB}$. With t checkpointing the input x average down to $18\ \|{MB}$ per sequence. Meanwhile, storing a 4-bit base model consumed roughly $5048\ \|{MB}$. The practical consequence is that increasing the number of adapters has only a small effect on total memory, so adding adapters in more places is an effective way to recover the e capacity lost by freezing the majority of the model.

d search protocol and d. To find strong e the authors performed a | sweep over learning rates and batch sizes. The search space for the | included learning rates from $1\times10^{-6}$ to $5\times10^{-5}$ and batch sizes from $8$ to $128$. They observed that several hyperparameters found effective at $7\t{B}$ generalized to $o\n{B}$ (and beyond) except for learning rate and batch size: for $33\f{B}$ and $|\t{B}$ they > the learning rate and doubled the batch size relative to $7\<{B}$ settings. This rule-of-thumb reflects the standard sensitivity of larger models to optimization hyperparameters: larger models often require smaller learning rates and larger effective batch sizes for stable f.

Summary of practical prescriptions from the paper:
- Do not assume only applying | to query and value t is enough; place adapters on all linear layers to match full t.
- The | projection dimension $r$ is less critical than adapter placement.
- Perform at least a modest search over learning rates and batch sizes; default values may underperform, especially for full n t.
- When f from $7\f{B}$ to $33\o{B}$/$t\t{B}$, reduce learning rate and increase batch size.

# Evaluation protocols: >-4 scoring, pairwise comparisons, and handling ordering effects
The paper uses a mix of automated (e-4) and human evaluations, combined by tournament-style aggregation, to produce robust system-level comparisons.

Automated scoring versus ChatGPT (relative scoring): On the e prompts the authors use d-4 to score two responses (ChatGPT’s and a candidate model’s) on a $0$–$<$ scale along with an explanation. The candidate model’s performance is reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score (so a model matching ChatGPT would be $100\%$; a model scoring higher than ChatGPT can exceed $100\%$). Important practical observation: e-4 displayed an ordering effect, assigning higher scores to the response that appeared earlier in the prompt. To mitigate this bias the authors recommend reporting the mean score over both input orders (model A first then model B, and vice versa), which controls for ordering artifacts.

Pairwise head-to-head comparisons (three-class labeling): For more direct comparisons the authors use pairwise evaluations in which t-4 (or human annotators) are prompted to choose the better response or declare a tie. This is reduced to three outcomes: win/loss/tie. Pairwise judgments are then used to build tournaments.

Human evaluation details: Human annotators were recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk. For comparisons against ChatGPT the paper collected two human ratings per comparison; for pairwise comparisons among systems the study collected three human annotators per comparison. These judgments are used both for absolute comparisons and for x aggregation.

Nucleus sampling and consistency: All t outputs in these evaluations used nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ to maintain consistent stochasticity across systems.

x rating aggregation and tournament protocol: The authors aggregate pairwise results into < scores. The specific choices are: initial | rating of $1000$, $K=32$ (the update factor), and the tournament sampling procedure is repeated $o{,}000$ times with different random seeds to control for ordering and sampling noise. Repeating many times yields distributions over o values and helps ensure reported rankings are not due to sampling artifacts.

Agreement and reliability diagnostics: The paper reports several inter-rater and inter-method agreement statistics that illuminate the strengths and limits of model-based evaluation:

- Moderate system-level agreement between t-4 rankings and human rankings: Kendall Tau $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman correlation $r=0.55$. This shows x-4 and humans often agree on which systems are better, but not perfectly.

- Example-level agreement between x-4 and human annotator majority vote is weaker: Fleiss $\kappa = 0.25$. This indicates that at the per-example level there is notable disagreement.

- Human annotator inter-rater agreement was moderate: Fleiss $\kappa = 0.42$ for human raters in the human-only comparisons.

- A concrete bias observation: e-4 assigned significantly higher < to its own outputs than humans did (e $1348$ vs $1176$), suggesting e-4 may be more favorable to outputs similar to its own generation style.

Practical consequences and mitigation strategies documented in the paper:
- Because automated scorers (|-4) can have biases (ordering effects, self-favoring), the authors combine automated and human evaluations and recommend controlling ordering by averaging over both presentation orders.
- Pairwise comparisons and d aggregation reduce the problems of absolute scoring scales (what does a $7$ out of $|$ mean?) by relying on relative judgments, which are more stable and easier for both humans and model evaluators to make.
- Repeating the tournament aggregation many times ($n{,}000$ seeds) reduces variance induced by sampling and ordering.

# Putting it together: experimental pipeline used in the paper
The experimental design in the paper follows these core steps, which students should understand as a cohesive pipeline:

1. Choose a base model and x. For small/medium-scale experiments use RoBERTa/T5 and GLUE/TKInstruct to validate fundamental behavior. For large-scale instruction < use e at $7\<{B}$, $e\e{B}$, $33\>{B}$, and $e\n{B}$ on o like Alpaca, FLAN v2, and the eight instruction | (OASST1, HH-n, etc.).

2. Select < and adapter strategy. In o this is e storage with Double n for |, dequantized into | for computation; | adapters are added to all linear layers, and only adapter parameters are updated. Use e d to manage d state memory t.

3. Tune n. Run a | sweep (e.g., learning rates from $1\times10^{-6}$ to $5\times10^{-5}$ and batch sizes from $8$ to $128$) to get fair o-bit full-| e. For larger scales ($33\t{B}$, $d\t{B}$) reduce learning rate and increase batch size relative to $7\|{B}$.

4. Evaluate intermediate metrics. Use > (5-shot) for instruction/NLU performance, and compute post-t perplexities and zero-shot accuracy for f-type comparisons.

5. Generate responses for e/chat evaluation. Use nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$. Evaluate on the f $80$ prompts and the OA benchmark ($953$ queries).

6. Score outputs via automated and human protocols. Use n-4 both for $0$–$d$ relative scoring against ChatGPT and for pairwise three-way comparisons; collect human annotations via AMT in parallel; mitigate ordering effects by averaging over both response orders.

7. Aggregate pairwise judgments into e ratings. Initialize n at $1000$, use $K=32$, and repeat the random sampling and aggregation many times (the paper uses $t{,}000$ repeats) to get stable system-level rankings.

8. Analyze agreement and failure modes. Compare |-4 and human rankings (Kendall Tau, Spearman, Fleiss $\kappa$), check for biases (ordering, self-favoring), and perform qualitative analysis of example failures (e.g., factual errors, math failures, refusal inconsistencies).
