# Limitations: lack of full >-bit baseline at 33B/65B; benchmark validity; evaluation biases

The paper is explicit and candid about its limitations, which are important to understand when interpreting its claims.

First, while d recovers t-bit e performance across many experiments and scales, the paper did not establish that t matches full-model e-bit x at the 33B and 65B parameter scales. Performing full t-bit | at those sizes requires many high-memory GPUs (multiple-server setups) and was outside the resources used in the study. Thus, for the largest models the comparison is with o-bit e o and other indirect metrics rather than exhaustive full-precision x.

Second, there are limits in the evaluation scope. The work focuses on e and two e-style prompt sets (n and OA). It does not evaluate on many other | (BigBench, RAFT, HELM, etc.), so the generality of the findings across all tasks is not established. Moreover, the paper highlights the role of |-benchmark similarity: performance on a benchmark depends strongly on how well the t data suits that benchmark’s task distribution. FLAN v2 < produced especially strong n performance but weaker < performance, while OASST1 n did well for chat.

Third, the paper documents concrete evaluation biases. Human annotation itself is noisy—pairwise human agreement is imperfect. These issues limit the precision with which one can compare models that are close in performance and underscore the need for careful evaluation design.

Fourth, responsible-AI evaluation in the paper is limited. The authors ran an initial bias comparison showing that e on OASST1 reduced some measured bias relative to raw x e, but this is a narrow slice of a very large space of societal harms; the paper explicitly calls for further analysis.

Finally, the paper did not explore all possible technical frontiers: alternative n methods beyond x at scale, more aggressive quantizations such as 3-bit base models, or comprehensive runtime characterizations of o d under every realistic workload remain open.

# Ethical considerations: dual-use risks, bias, privacy, and open-source access

The paper’s Broader Impacts section frames e as having both beneficial and potentially harmful societal consequences.

On the positive side, | significantly lowers the computational barrier to x large models: 33B models can be f on a single consumer |, and 65B models on a single professional e, according to the reported configurations. This democratization can broaden participation in research and auditing, enable smaller teams to experiment with powerful d, and facilitate privacy-preserving d such as on-device f where users retain control of data and models.

On the cautionary side, easier access to powerful t can be dual use. The same lowering of barriers could enable malicious actors to produce high-quality disinformation, automate scams, or weaponize generated text for social engineering. The paper explicitly acknowledges these dual-use risks and argues that open access also permits independent analysis and oversight that closed systems lack, but it does not claim to resolve the tension—rather it calls for responsible stewardship.

Bias and fairness are another ethical axis. The paper’s initial results suggest OASST1 e reduced some measured biases compared to the base model, but the authors stress that this is only a preliminary assessment and that broader audits are needed. Responsible deployment requires extensive evaluation across many axes of fairness, safety, and robustness.

Privacy is both an opportunity and a concern. On-device f enabled by t could allow models that better respect user privacy (data and models stored locally), but models trained on private data can memorize and leak sensitive information unless mitigations are used. The paper does not extensively analyze memorization or privacy risks, marking these as areas for future work.

Finally, the paper highlights that releasing both models and code (including CUDA kernels for 4-bit training) increases transparency and reproducibility. Open-source release has pros and cons: it aids community oversight and accelerates research, but also makes capabilities broadly available. The authors recommend being mindful of these tradeoffs.
