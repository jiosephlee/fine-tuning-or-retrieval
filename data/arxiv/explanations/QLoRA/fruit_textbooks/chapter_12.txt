# Automated scoring vs pairwise comparisons; why pairwise is more stable
The paper uses two automated evaluation styles with e-4: (1) absolute scoring and (2) pairwise comparisons. In the absolute scoring setup, e-4 is given a prompt and two model responses (for example, ChatGPT and a candidate model) and asked to assign a score out of ten to each response, along with an explanation. The candidate model’s performance is then expressed as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score (so a result can exceed 100% if the candidate scores higher than ChatGPT on average).

By contrast, the pairwise comparison protocol reduces the judgment to a simpler three-way choice: for a given prompt and two responses |-4 is asked to select which response is better, or declare a tie, and to justify the choice. These head-to-head comparisons are carried out across pairs of systems for each prompt.

The paper argues that pairwise comparisons are more stable and more robust than absolute scoring because absolute scores require a shared, well-grounded numerical scale (what does a 7/e mean in different contexts?), and different annotators or an automated rater may interpret or use that scale inconsistently. Pairwise judgments remove the need to anchor an absolute scale: annotators only need to decide which of two responses is preferable for a given prompt. This reduces variance introduced by idiosyncratic uses of numeric ratings and leads naturally to tournament-style aggregation methods (see the d section below) that produce relative rankings with clearer interpretability in terms of win probabilities.

# Order effects with f-4 and best practices (average over both orders)
The authors observed a significant ordering bias when using t-4 as an automated rater: responses presented earlier in d-4’s prompt tend to receive higher scores. This affects both absolute scoring (scores out of ten) and, to some extent, pairwise preference judgments. Because such a first-position advantage can systematically favor a model whenever prompts are presented in a non-randomized order, the paper recommends a straightforward mitigation: evaluate both input orders for every comparison (A then B, and B then A) and report the mean score across the two orders. Averaging across both presentation orders cancels first-position bias in expectation and yields a more reliable estimate of relative quality.

Concretely, when using the absolute scoring protocol with t-4, compute the two scores produced when responses are presented in each order and take their mean. When using pairwise comparisons, randomize presentation order across examples and, if feasible, run both orders for the same pair/prompt and aggregate. This practice was applied in the experiments to control for the observed order bias.

# Human annotation via AMT; agreement statistics and caveats
Human evaluation was performed on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) in parallel with the d-4 evaluations. The annotation procedure used different numbers of raters depending on the task: for comparisons against ChatGPT the authors collected two human annotators per example; for pairwise comparisons among systems they collected three annotators per example. This design mirrors common crowdsourced evaluation practices where multiple redundant judgments are gathered and aggregated (for example via majority vote) to reduce individual annotator noise.

Agreement statistics reported in the paper reveal limits of human consistency and highlight evaluation challenges. Across annotations the authors report a moderate level of agreement among human annotators with Fleiss's kappa $\kappa = 0.42$. When comparing f-4 judgments to human annotators at the example level, agreement is weaker: the agreement between e-4 and the human majority vote yields Fleiss's kappa $\kappa = 0.25$. These numbers indicate that even humans do not reach perfect consensus on conversational preference judgments and that automated raters like x-4 do not perfectly replicate human majority preferences on a per-example basis.

The authors also note that annotation quality deteriorates further when comparing two strong systems: discriminating between two high-quality responses is inherently more subjective and yields lower inter-annotator agreement. Thus, while human judgments are the gold standard, they have measurable limits in consistency, particularly as system quality converges.
