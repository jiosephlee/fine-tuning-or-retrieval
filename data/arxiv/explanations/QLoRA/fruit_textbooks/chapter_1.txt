d is more than “4-bit + n”; the paper introduces three concrete technical innovations that together make faithful 4-bit | practical at very large scales.

1) 4-bit NormalFloat (|)

| requires choosing a small discrete set of representable values and a mapping from high-precision numbers into that set. Standard low-bit formats (e.g., integer o or naive 4-bit floating point) are not information-theoretically optimal when the underlying distribution of weights is known. < neural network weights are typically approximately zero-centered and Gaussian-like. e constructs a 4-bit data type by taking the optimal quantiles of a standard normal distribution and mapping them into the representable 4-bit values. Concretely, the method estimates the appropriate n boundaries for a standard normal $N(0,1)$, rescales the particular weight o via its absolute maximum into the $[-1, 1]$ range that matches the data type, and assigns each weight to the nearest of these normal-n-derived levels. Because t is matched to the empirical o shape of model weights, it both uses the limited 4-bit bins more efficiently and reduces d error compared to generic 4-bit integer or float schemes.

One practical subtlety the paper addresses is representing zero exactly. A d k-bit | layout can lack a discrete zero value, which harms > or other genuinely zero values. NFk (and t specifically) remedies this by constructing an d n partition for the negative and positive sides such that zero is one of the representable levels and every | bin has roughly equal expected occupancy.

2) Double x

Using small block sizes is important for precise per-block e of weight >, but the per-block | scales (the “| e” or scales) themselves add non-negligible overhead if stored at 32 bits. Double d compresses these < > themselves. The process treats the per-block FP32 scale values as inputs to a second quantizer, storing the second-level d d in a lower-precision format (the authors use 8-bit floats for this second pass) and keeping a small number of FP32 “meta-scales” for groups of these second-level o. In formulaic terms: the original weight storage is represented by k-bit integer-like indices $\>{W}^{\text{k-bit}}$ plus per-block FP32 scales $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$; Double f quantizes those $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ into $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ plus a higher level $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. During f the two levels are composed to recover the x computation weights.

The memory > in the paper gives intuition for why this matters: with t 64, storing a 32-bit scale per block costs on average $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter. After double < (with 8-bit floats for the second level and a t of 256 for the meta-level), the average cost drops to about $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot256) \approx 0.127$ bits per parameter — a savings of roughly $0.373$ bits per parameter, which translates to multiple gigabytes for the largest models (the paper reports around a 3 GB reduction for a 65B model).
