Chapter Title: Model-Based Evaluation of Chatbots in QLoRA: GPT-4 as a Proxy for Human Judgments

# Introduction

Instruction finetuning of large language models requires careful evaluation to determine whether a model meaningfully improves on tasks such as question answering and dialogue. In the paper “QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs,” the authors introduce an efficient method for 4-bit finetuning that enables the training of very large models on a single GPU while preserving 16-bit finetuning performance. To make credible claims about conversational quality, they complement standard academic benchmarks with chatbot evaluations that more closely resemble real-world use. Within this evaluation program, a central question is how to compare systems at scale and at reasonable cost.

This chapter explains the methodology proposed and used in the paper for evaluating chatbots, focusing on the use of a model-based evaluator—specifically GPT-4—as a low-cost but imperfect substitute for human raters. The authors find that GPT-4 and human evaluators largely agree on system rankings in tournament settings, despite noticeable biases and instance-level disagreements. The result is a practical recommendation: model-based evaluation by GPT-4 is a reasonable alternative to human evaluation when cost and scalability are primary concerns, provided its limitations are acknowledged and mitigated where possible.

# Evaluating Chatbots: Tasks, Data, and Setup

The authors adopt a two-pronged evaluation strategy. First, they report results on a standardized, multiple-choice academic benchmark, MMLU, evaluated in a 5-shot setting. Second, and more pertinent for assessing chatbot quality, they conduct generative evaluations using curated prompt sets and side-by-side comparisons of system outputs.

For generative evaluation, they use two datasets. The first is the Vicuna prompts, a set of 80 diverse queries previously used to compare conversational systems. The second is a larger Open Assistant (OA) validation set with 953 user queries derived from multi-turn, crowd-sourced dialogues. In both cases, they standardize decoding with nucleus sampling at parameter choices $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$.

This combined setup allows them to analyze not only conventional language understanding but also open-ended generation quality, coherence, helpfulness, and stylistic properties as perceived by evaluators. However, direct generative assessments are expensive if conducted with human raters at scale. The authors therefore explore GPT-4 as an automated judge, comparing it directly to humans across two complementary protocols.

# Automated Evaluation with GPT-4

The authors employ GPT-4 in two ways. In the first protocol, GPT-4 assigns numerical scores out of ten to a model’s response and to a ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 Turbo) response for each Vicuna prompt. They then compute the model’s performance as a percentage relative to ChatGPT. This framing mirrors prior practice and enables direct comparability with existing reports. The authors identify a significant ordering effect whereby GPT-4 increases the score of the response presented first. To reduce this bias, they recommend averaging over both possible orders of presentation.

In the second protocol, the authors design head-to-head comparisons in which GPT-4 is asked to select the better response between two systems or to declare a tie. These pairwise labels are obtained for all permutations of model pairs across both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks. This design supports tournament-style aggregation, which, as discussed below, can yield more stable system-level rankings than absolute scoring.

Both automated protocols produce large quantities of judgments at low marginal cost, enabling comprehensive, pairwise coverage among many systems and supporting robust aggregation methods.

# Human Evaluation

To calibrate and validate GPT-4 as an evaluator, the authors run parallel human studies on Amazon Mechanical Turk. For the “score against ChatGPT” protocol, two human annotators judge each comparison. For the head-to-head pairwise protocol, three annotators judge each pair. These human evaluations follow the same datasets and prompt/response structures as the automated evaluations, enabling a direct comparison between human and GPT-4 judgments at both the system and example levels.

The authors report that agreement among human annotators is only moderate, and that agreement erodes further when comparing strong systems whose outputs are closely matched in quality. This finding underscores the intrinsic difficulty of the task and suggests that even human-based evaluations carry substantial uncertainty, particularly at the top end of model performance.

# Tournament-Style Aggregation and Elo Ratings

To convert many pairwise judgments into a coherent ranking of systems, the authors employ an Elo rating framework. A “match” comprises a single prompt for which two systems produce candidate responses; a judge (human or GPT-4) decides the winner or declares a tie. Elo ratings then update based on the expected outcome of the match, with upsets driving larger changes and expected outcomes driving smaller changes. This process produces an ordinal system ranking that reflects comparative performance across many pairings.

Concretely, the authors adopt the following settings. They initialize each model with an Elo score of $1{,}000$ and use $K=32$ as the update constant. To control for sensitivity to match order, they repeat the tournament aggregation $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. Elo offers an intuitive interpretability: an Elo difference corresponds to an expected win rate in head-to-head play. For example, an Elo of $1{,}100$ versus $1{,}000$ implies an expected win probability of approximately $65\%$ for the higher-rated model, whereas two equally rated models at $1{,}000$ have an expected $50\%$ win rate against each other.

This tournament framework addresses two issues present in absolute scoring. First, it avoids the need for a universal scale, which is especially problematic when different prompts vary in difficulty and when judges differ in how they use rating scales. Second, it amortizes noise in individual match outcomes across a large number of comparisons, producing a stable system-level ranking.

# Agreement Between GPT-4 and Human Evaluators

The central question is whether GPT-4 can stand in for human raters. The authors address this at two levels: system rankings and individual examples.

At the system level, GPT-4 and human Elo rankings largely agree, though not perfectly. On the Vicuna benchmark, they report a Kendall tau of $\tau=0.43$ and a Spearman rank correlation of $r=0.55$ between GPT-4–based and human-based system rankings. These values indicate moderate concordance: GPT-4’s judgments, when aggregated across many matches, produce broadly similar orderings to those derived from human judgments. This is the key observation underpinning the recommendation that GPT-4 can serve as a low-cost alternative for ranking chatbots.

At the example level, however, agreement is weaker. The authors report a Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.25$ between GPT-4 and human majority votes on individual comparisons, indicating only fair agreement. This reveals that GPT-4 and humans frequently diverge on specific prompts, even if the aggregate effect across many prompts yields similar system rankings. Furthermore, among the human annotators themselves, agreement is moderate (Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.42$), and declines when comparing high-quality systems, highlighting the intrinsic subjectivity and difficulty of the task.

The authors also identify systematic biases in automated evaluation. Beyond the ordering effect in absolute scoring, they observe that GPT-4 assigns significantly higher Elo scores to its own outputs than do human raters. Specifically, GPT-4’s ratings yield an Elo of $1{,}348$ for GPT-4 itself versus $1{,}176$ under human ratings, roughly implying an additional $\sim 20\%$ win probability advantage in head-to-head matches against opponents. These findings underscore that while GPT-4 is useful as an evaluator, it is not neutral or unbiased, necessitating procedural safeguards and careful interpretation.

# Practical Merits and Limitations of GPT-4 as an Evaluator

The authors’ results support a pragmatic position. On the one hand, GPT-4 provides a scalable and inexpensive means to generate large volumes of pairwise labels, enabling comprehensive tournament-style comparisons across many systems and prompts. At the system level, GPT-4–based Elo rankings correlate moderately with human-based rankings, suggesting that model-based evaluation can approximate human preferences well enough to guide research and development, especially when cost or speed constraints preclude extensive human studies.

On the other hand, the authors emphasize the imperfections of GPT-4 as a judge. There are marked order effects in absolute scoring, necessitating averaging over both presentation orders. At the example level, agreement with human majority votes is only fair, indicating that GPT-4’s judgments frequently diverge on a prompt-by-prompt basis. Moreover, GPT-4 appears to favor its own outputs in tournament rankings relative to human judgments. These limitations argue for caution: when relying on GPT-4 as an evaluator, researchers should deploy bias controls, prefer pairwise tournaments over absolute scoring, and, when important decisions hinge on fine distinctions among top systems, incorporate human judgments.

# Implications for Ranking Chatbots

Within this evaluation design, the authors build a ranking of competing chatbots, including both open-source models trained via QLoRA and proprietary systems. At a high level, the GPT-4–based and human-based Elo rankings agree well enough to identify the same top-tier systems, reinforcing the feasibility of using a model-based evaluator to rank chatbots. At the same time, they also observe wide confidence intervals in absolute score–based evaluations and partial disagreements in tournament rankings, especially for smaller models, consistent with the previously described uncertainties and biases.

The authors therefore recommend pairwise, tournament-style procedures with Elo aggregation for chatbot evaluation, and they present GPT-4–based judgments as a reasonable, low-cost proxy for human judgments under this framework. This approach mitigates scale-anchoring problems, amortizes noise across many matches, and aligns sufficiently with human rankings to be actionable for research, while acknowledging that it is not a perfect substitute.

# Conclusion

The paper’s evaluation program leads to a clear takeaway: GPT-4 can serve as a low-cost, scalable, and reasonably reliable evaluator for ranking chatbots when used with careful protocols. In tournament-style settings with Elo aggregation, system-level rankings derived from GPT-4 largely track those derived from human annotators, as evidenced by a Kendall tau of $\tau=0.43$ and a Spearman correlation of $r=0.55$ on the Vicuna benchmark. However, model-based evaluation is imperfect. The authors document ordering effects, weaker example-level agreement (Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.25$), and evidence that GPT-4 inflates its own standing relative to human raters. Consequently, best practice entails controlling for order, preferring pairwise comparisons with Elo aggregation, and using human evaluations to adjudicate fine-grained distinctions or to audit biases.

Within these constraints, the authors’ findings justify the practical conclusion that GPT-4 is a reasonable alternative to human evaluation for ranking chatbots, particularly when resources are constrained and large-scale comparisons are necessary.
