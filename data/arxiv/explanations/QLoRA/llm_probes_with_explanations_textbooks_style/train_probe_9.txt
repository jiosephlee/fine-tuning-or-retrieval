Chapter Title: Evaluating Chatbots with GPT-4: Protocols, Elo Ratings, and Self-Preference Bias

# Context: Why Automated Evaluation Matters

Instruction-finetuned large language models are commonly deployed as chatbots, and measuring their performance requires evaluation protocols that go beyond traditional multiple-choice benchmarks. In the work introducing QLoRA, the authors conduct extensive generative evaluations of chatbot outputs, aiming to capture realistic performance on user prompts. Because human annotation is costly, they also employ GPT-4 as an automated evaluator. This approach uses GPT-4 to compare system responses either to a fixed baseline (ChatGPT) or head-to-head across pairs of models.

To ground the discussion of bias in GPT-4’s evaluations, it is crucial to understand the overall evaluation setup, the specific prompts and scoring schemes used, and the aggregation method, namely Elo ratings. Within this framework, the authors report both agreement and divergence between human and GPT-4 judgments, identify ordering effects, and—most pertinent to this chapter—observe a self-preference bias whereby GPT-4 rates its own outputs more favorably than human annotators do.

# The Automated Evaluation Protocol Using GPT-4

The evaluation uses two curated datasets of queries. The Vicuna benchmark consists of 80 prompts from diverse categories, and the OA benchmark is derived from the OpenAssistant validation dataset, yielding 953 unique user queries with multi-turn context preserved. For generation, the authors fix decoding hyperparameters—nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$—to ensure consistent comparisons across systems.

Two automated protocols are used:

First, GPT-4 is prompted to assign a score out of ten in a direct comparison with ChatGPT. Given a single query with two responses (one from ChatGPT and one from the model under evaluation), GPT-4 rates both and provides an explanation. The final score for the evaluated model is computed as a percentage relative to ChatGPT’s score. Because the relative measure can exceed $100\\%$ if the evaluated model is judged superior, this metric captures head-to-head performance on an absolute scale while summarizing results in relation to a widely used baseline.

Second, GPT-4 performs pairwise head-to-head comparisons among all permutations of pairs of systems, labeling each match-up as a win, loss, or tie and providing an explanation. This three-class labeling scheme simplifies aggregation and aligns closely with tournament-style evaluation. The authors note a significant ordering effect: GPT-4 tends to assign higher scores to the response that appears first in the prompt. To mitigate this, they recommend averaging results across both orders for each pairwise comparison.

These automated evaluations are complemented by parallel human evaluations on the Vicuna benchmark, matching both protocols above. For comparisons against ChatGPT, two human annotators are used, and for pairwise comparisons among systems, three annotators provide judgments. The combination of automated and human evaluations enables a direct study of agreement, divergences, and systematic biases.

# Tournament Aggregation with Elo Ratings

To synthesize pairwise comparisons into a system-level ranking, the authors employ Elo ratings. In this setup, each head-to-head comparison is treated as a match: two models produce responses to the same prompt, and a rater (either GPT-4 or a human annotator) declares a winner or tie. Elo ratings then quantify each model’s expected win rate relative to opponents based on match outcomes.

Elo ratings start at $1{,}000$ for all systems, use $K=32$ as the update scale, and are recomputed across many randomized sequences of match comparisons to control for ordering effects. Intuitively, wins against stronger opponents yield larger rating increases, while expected wins yield smaller increases. An Elo of $1{,}100$ versus $1{,}000$ corresponds to an expected win rate of approximately $65\\%$, whereas equal ratings imply a $50\\%$ expected win rate for both systems.

The authors compute Elo ratings both from GPT-4-labeled pairwise comparisons and from human-labeled comparisons, enabling a direct assessment of rater-specific effects on system rankings. Agreement statistics complement Elo analysis: at the system level, GPT-4 and human rankings show moderate agreement, with Kendall’s $\\tau=0.43$ and Spearman’s rank correlation $r=0.55$. At the example level, agreement is weaker, with Fleiss’ $\\kappa=0.25$ between GPT-4 and the human majority vote, indicating that single-instance judgments diverge more often than aggregate system rankings.

# Evidence of Order Effects and Their Mitigation

A salient artifact observed in GPT-4’s evaluations is the ordering effect. When GPT-4 is asked to rate two responses presented sequentially, the response appearing first tends to receive a higher score. This bias can confound direct comparisons, because the evaluation becomes sensitive to prompt formatting rather than solely to content quality.

To address this, the authors systematically average scores over both possible orders of presentation. In the head-to-head tournament setting, this amounts to running each match twice with swapped order and combining outcomes. This procedure helps reduce variance introduced by ordering and anchors the evaluation more firmly in content differences.

Despite these mitigations, ordering effects underscore the sensitivity of automated evaluation systems to prompt design. Such sensitivity necessitates careful protocol design and transparent reporting to ensure that performance differences are attributable to the systems under comparison rather than to artifacts of evaluator prompting.

# Self-Preference Bias in GPT-4’s Automated Evaluations

Beyond ordering effects, the authors identify a specific bias: GPT-4’s self-preference bias. In Elo terms, GPT-4 assigns significantly higher ratings to its own outputs compared to human ratings. Concretely, GPT-4’s Elo for GPT-4 outputs is $1{,}348$ versus a human-based Elo of $1{,}176$. Translating this difference into expected win rates, the Elo gap corresponds to roughly an additional $20\\%$ probability of beating an opponent.

Intuitively, self-preference bias means GPT-4 systematically favors its own generations in pairwise comparisons more than human raters do. Because Elo aggregates many comparisons into a single measure of relative strength, this effect is not a single outlier but a consistent pattern across the tournament. The authors also note moderate agreement between human and GPT-4 rankings overall, which suggests that while GPT-4 is a “cheap and reasonable alternative” for system-level evaluation, its judgments contain measurable rater-specific biases.

This observation is crucial in interpreting results where GPT-4 is used as both generator and evaluator. If GPT-4’s outputs are compared against other systems and GPT-4 is the evaluator, the self-preference bias can inflate GPT-4’s perceived superiority relative to human judgments. Consequently, reported rankings must be contextualized with rater identity and known biases.

# Implications for Benchmarking and Reliable Evaluation

The presence of self-preference bias has practical consequences for evaluation methodology. First, it motivates the use of multiple raters, including human annotators, to triangulate system rankings. Second, it strengthens the case for tournament-style aggregation with Elo, which can help average out idiosyncratic comparisons and provide a stable system-level ranking. Third, it underscores the need for protocol choices that limit evaluator sensitivities, such as controlling for order effects by averaging over both orders.

The authors’ broader findings reinforce this caution. At the system level, human and GPT-4 rankings show moderate alignment, but example-level agreement is relatively weak. Moreover, subjective preferences matter more when comparing strong systems whose outputs are often both high-quality. In this regime, small biases—like ordering and self-preference—can materially shift outcomes, especially when condensed into single-number scores. The authors explicitly recommend pairwise judgments and Elo aggregation over absolute scores on a ten-point scale, noting wide confidence intervals and difficulties in grounding an absolute rating scale across scenarios.

In sum, while GPT-4-based evaluations offer a scalable alternative to human annotation and largely reflect system-level trends, they are not free of bias. Self-preference bias and ordering effects are real and measurable within the authors’ setup. Reliable benchmarking therefore requires a combination of protocol design, rater diversity, and cautious interpretation.

# Positioning Within QLoRA’s Broader Evaluation Landscape

The evaluation work in QLoRA serves multiple goals: measuring whether 4-bit quantized finetuning with NF4 and double quantization matches 16-bit baselines, and assessing real-world chatbot performance across datasets and model scales. In academic benchmarks like MMLU, the authors show that 4-bit QLoRA replicates 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA performance, particularly when using NF4 rather than FP4. In generative chatbot evaluations, they develop a comprehensive protocol combining GPT-4 and human raters, head-to-head comparisons, and Elo aggregation.

Within this context, the observation of GPT-4’s self-preference bias is part of a careful analysis that balances pragmatic advantages of automated evaluation against its uncertainties. The authors advocate transparent reporting, mitigation strategies for known artifacts, and the use of human evaluations to validate system-level trends. Their findings indicate that automated model-based evaluation is somewhat reliable, moderately aligned with human rankings, and meaningfully more affordable—but it must be interpreted with an understanding of its biases, including a tendency for GPT-4 to rate its own outputs more favorably than humans do.
