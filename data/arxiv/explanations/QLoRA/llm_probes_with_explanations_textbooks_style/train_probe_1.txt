Controlling Ordering Effects in Elo-Based Chatbot Evaluation

# Elo Rating in Tournament-Style Model Evaluation

This chapter explains how the paper “QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs” uses repeated Elo computations with many random seeds to control ordering effects in tournament-style chatbot evaluations. To understand this decision, we first review the Elo rating procedure as it is described in the paper and how it is applied to compare language models.

Elo rating is introduced as a way to aggregate pairwise comparisons between systems into a single measure of performance. Each “match” consists of two models generating responses to the same prompt, and an annotator—either a human or GPT-4—selects the better response or declares a tie. The Elo rating update uses the expected outcome of a match as a function of the two players’ current Elo scores. For instance, an Elo of $1100$ versus $1000$ corresponds to an expected win-rate of approximately $65\\%$ for the higher-rated player, while two equal players (e.g., $1000$ vs $1000$ or $1100$ vs $1100$) have an expected win-rate of $50\\%$. After each match, Elo ratings change proportionally to the unexpectedness of the result—an upset yields a large change, a predicted outcome yields a small change. In the paper’s setup, all models start at $1000$ Elo and the update scale is set with $K=32$.

The tournament is constructed by sampling from the set of labeled pairwise comparisons to generate a sequence of matches. Over time, as matches accumulate and ratings are updated, Elo provides a ranking that “approximately matches the skill of each player,” in the paper’s words. Because the comparisons are conducted for many pairs across benchmarks (Vicuna and OA) and with both human and GPT-4 judges, this procedure consolidates diverse head-to-head evidence into a single, interpretable scale of relative performance.

# What Ordering Effects Are and Why They Matter

Ordering effects arise when the order in which events, inputs, or comparisons occur influences outcomes. In the context of the paper, ordering effects are documented in two related places.

First, when using GPT-4 to rate each system’s response against ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark, the authors “find a significant ordering effect with GPT-4 increasing the score of the response occurring earlier in the prompt.” This is a direct prompt-order bias: simply placing one system’s response first can inflate its score. To mitigate this, the authors recommend reporting the mean score over both orders. This demonstrates that order can introduce systematic bias into evaluation.

Second, ordering effects can also impact tournament-style Elo estimation. Elo ratings are sensitive to the sequence in which matches occur because ratings are updated incrementally after each match. If a strong model happens to face weaker models early, it may quickly accrue a high rating; if it faces stronger models first, early losses may depress its rating before it has many opportunities to recover. The paper highlights this with the example that “the effect of which model pairs compete with each other first” can produce ordering effects. In a finite sample of matches, any fixed match ordering may conflate skill with sequence, thereby injecting variance or bias into the final Elo scores.

In both cases—prompt ordering for GPT-4 judgments and match ordering for Elo updates—the paper emphasizes that ordering is not an inherent property of model quality. Rather, it is an artifact introduced by the evaluation setup. Therefore, controlling for ordering effects is necessary to make model comparisons more reliable.

# The Role of Random Seeds in Elo Computation

To address ordering effects in Elo-based evaluation, the paper repeats the entire Elo computation “10,000 times with different random seeds to control for ordering effects.” The random seed determines the random sampling that selects and orders matches from the set of available labeled comparisons. By changing the seed, the procedure generates a different match ordering and potentially a different trajectory of rating updates.

Repeated runs with many seeds serve two purposes. First, they average out idiosyncrasies from any single ordering. If a particular ordering favored a model due to early matchups, the effect will be diluted when many alternative orderings are considered. Second, they reveal the robustness of the resulting rankings. If Elo scores are stable across seeds, one can be more confident that the rankings reflect underlying comparative performance rather than quirks of a specific sequence.

Concretely, the authors implement this by sampling from the pool of pairwise judgments—both human-labeled and GPT-4-labeled—and conducting the Elo update process starting from the same initial score of $1000$ with $K=32$. Each run uses a different random seed, thereby producing a different sequence of matches. The aggregation over $10{,}000$ random seeds controls for the effect of which model pairs meet first, which is explicitly noted as an ordering effect in the paper.

# Connecting Ordering Control to Broader Evaluation Reliability

The paper’s broader evaluation analysis provides additional context for why controlling ordering effects is critical. With generative model evaluations, several sources of variance and bias can influence outcomes:

The GPT-4 prompt-order sensitivity. GPT-4 tends to assign higher scores to the system shown first, making pairwise ratings dependent on presentation order. The authors counter this at the prompt level by averaging scores across both orders.

The moderate agreement between human and GPT-4 judgments. At the system level, the paper reports a Kendall Tau of $\\tau=0.43$ and a Spearman correlation of $r=0.55$ between GPT-4 and human rankings on the Vicuna benchmark. At the example level, agreement is weaker, with Fleiss $\\kappa=0.25$. These results suggest variation in preferences and potential biases, reinforcing the need for procedures that reduce evaluation artifacts.

The sensitivity of tournament aggregation to match sequences. Elo updates depend on match order because ratings evolve cumulatively. The same set of pairwise labels can yield different Elo trajectories if matches are ordered differently.

Given these considerations, repeating Elo computations across $10{,}000$ random seeds directly targets one of the controllable sources of variability: ordering effects in match sequences. This repetition increases the reliability of the aggregated Elo rankings and aligns with the paper’s broader strategy of mitigating order-related biases wherever they arise, whether in prompting or in tournament aggregation.

# Implications for Interpreting Elo Scores in the Paper

By controlling ordering effects through repeated Elo computations, the paper supports its conclusions about relative chatbot performance with a more robust aggregation. The reported findings—such as Guanaco 33B and 65B outperforming all open-source models on Vicuna and OA benchmarks besides GPT-4, and Guanaco 65B achieving $99.3\\%$ of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna benchmark—are grounded in tournament evaluations that explicitly reduce sensitivity to match sequence artifacts.

The paper further notes differences between benchmarks, indicating that dataset suitability affects performance: models finetuned on FLAN v2 perform well on MMLU but poorly on chatbot benchmarks, while models trained on datasets like OASST1 excel in chatbot evaluations. In this context, robust Elo aggregation helps separate true comparative capability from procedural noise and makes cross-benchmark comparisons more trustworthy.

Finally, the authors’ observations about biases in automated evaluators, moderate human–model agreement, and reliance on relative judgments (instead of absolute scales) underscore that carefully designed evaluation protocols are essential. Repeating Elo computations with many random seeds is one principled step within that design: it directly addresses the ordering effect identified in the paper and strengthens the validity of the tournament-style conclusions.
