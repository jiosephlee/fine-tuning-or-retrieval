\title{A Textbook about the Paper: QLoRA (4 chapters)}

Why QLoRA: Motivation, Background, and Problem Formulation

# The finetuning bottleneck: GPU memory costs and accessibility
Finetuning large pretrained language models is one of the most effective ways to adapt them to new tasks or to add/remove behaviors, but it is also extremely resource intensive. A concrete illustration from the work we study is that regular 16-bit finetuning of a 65 billion parameter model (LLaMA-65B in the paper) requires more than 780 GB of GPU memory. That number captures the combined memory needs for stored model parameters, optimizer states, activations, and gradients during training. For most researchers and practitioners this is prohibitive: having to coordinate many high-memory GPUs or access large cloud instances dramatically narrows who can run experiments and iterate quickly. The consequence is that large-model finetuning has become concentrated in well-resourced labs and companies, and many interesting empirical questions (how does instruction-tuning behavior scale across datasets and model sizes, which large models are most amenable to low-cost finetuning, etc.) become effectively infeasible to investigate.

The core motivation behind QLoRA is therefore accessibility: reduce the memory footprint of finetuning so that very large models become finetunable on a single, modest GPU (the paper demonstrates finetuning a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU) while preserving the predictive performance and runtime characteristics of conventional 16-bit finetuning. Achieving this requires handling two opposing constraints simultaneously: (1) drastically shrink the storage size of the frozen pretrained weights, and (2) preserve numerical fidelity during forward and backward passes so that the finetuned model attains the same quality as a full 16-bit finetuning baseline.

# Inference-time quantization vs training-time stability
Quantization—storing model parameters in fewer bits than 16 or 32—is an established strategy for making inference cheaper. Many recent methods successfully quantize large language models to 8-bit or even 4-bit for inference while still producing high-quality outputs. However, training (or even adapter-based finetuning) is fundamentally more demanding than inference for two reasons.

First, training requires backpropagation through the network. Gradient computations are sensitive to numerical precision and to how the forward activations are computed. Techniques that are sufficient for inference can break during backpropagation: the paper notes that many quantization methods that work for inference "break down during training" because they do not preserve the necessary numerical behavior for accurate gradient computation.

Second, inference quantization often tolerates small, systematic biases or rounding choices because weights stay fixed; during training those biases can change the optimization dynamics and the ability of adapters to correct for quantization error. In short, inference-time quantization focuses on forward-pass accuracy for a fixed model, while training-time quantization must preserve both forward- and backward-pass numerical properties in a way that permits reliable gradient-based adaptation.

QLoRA's design addresses these concerns by (a) storing the base model in a very low-precision format but (b) dequantizing to a higher-precision compute format when performing forward and backward passes, and (c) only updating small adapter modules (LoRA) in high precision so that learning is stable.

# LoRA fundamentals: low-rank adapters and gradient flow through frozen weights
LoRA (Low-Rank Adapters) is a parameter-efficient finetuning technique that adds a small set of trainable parameters to a frozen pretrained model. Instead of updating the full weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$ inside a linear projection $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{Y}$, LoRA augments the projection by an additional low-rank term. Concretely, the LoRA-modified projection computes

\[
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\]

where $\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ and $\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$ are low-rank matrices with $r \ll \min(h,o)$, and $s$ is a scalar multiplier. The pretrained weight $\mathbf{W}$ remains frozen: gradients are propagated through $\mathbf{W}$ to the LoRA parameters, and only $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$ are updated.

Two intuitive implications follow. First, LoRA drastically cuts the number of learned parameters and optimizer state because $r$ is small compared to the full matrix dimensions. Second, because gradients flow through the frozen base weights, the base model's numeric fidelity during forward/backward computation matters: if the base weights are stored or used in a way that breaks derivative computation, the LoRA updates cannot correctly optimize. Thus effective low-precision finetuning with LoRA requires a scheme that preserves accurate forward and backward computations even when the base model is stored in very few bits.

The placement of LoRA adapters also matters. Earlier practical LoRA recipes often apply LoRA only to certain projection matrices (for example, the query and value projections in attention). The QLoRA work empirically finds that applying LoRA to all linear layers in transformer blocks (i.e., using adapters more widely across the network) is crucial to fully match full-model finetuning performance; the memory cost of doing so is small relative to the overall activation/gradient cost, so this is a favorable tradeoff.

# Block-wise k-bit quantization: outliers, per-block scales, and dequantization
A core building block of practical quantization is block-wise rescaling and rounding. Naively quantizing an entire tensor into a $k$-bit integer range can fail when the tensor contains outliers: a few large-magnitude elements expand the dynamic range, causing most quantization bins to be unused and increasing relative error for the majority of values. To mitigate this, quantization is typically performed in blocks: the tensor is chunked into contiguous blocks of size $B$, and each block is independently scaled into the representational range of the $k$-bit datatype. This yields per-block quantization constants (scales) that reduce the effect of outliers and improve fidelity.

A standard block-wise quantization step (to 8-bit integer for illustration) rescales by the absolute block maximum and rounds:

\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}} = \text{round}\!\left( \frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})} \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}} \right) = \text{round}(c^{\text{FP32}}\cdot \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}),
\]

where $c^{\text{FP32}}$ is the per-block quantization constant (scale). Dequantization is the inverse:

\[
\operatorname{dequant}(c^{\text{FP32}}, \mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}) = \frac{\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}}{c^{\text{FP32}}} \approx \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}.
\]

Block size $B$ is a tradeoff: smaller blocks give better local scaling (reduce quantization error) but increase the number of per-block scale constants, which increases storage overhead. Because quantization constants themselves are typically stored in 32-bit floats, a small block size can add nontrivial average bits per parameter just from the scales. QLoRA addresses this later with Double Quantization, which compresses the quantization constants themselves.

# Memory anatomy of finetuning: activations and gradients dominate vs adapter parameter size
To reason about where memory is spent during finetuning, it helps to decompose the major components: (1) frozen model parameter storage (weights), (2) optimizer states for parameters being updated, (3) activations stored for backpropagation, and (4) gradients and parameter updates. For parameter-efficient finetuning with LoRA, the added trainable parameters are tiny compared to the full model, and their optimizer states are modest. However, activations and their gradients remain large because they scale with batch size and sequence length and are proportional to model width. The paper gives a concrete example: for a 7B LLaMA model finetuned on FLAN v2 with batch size 1, LoRA weights (about 0.2% of model weights in typical recipes) occupy only 26 MB, but LoRA input gradients occupy 567 MB, while storing the 4-bit base model takes 5,048 MB. When gradient checkpointing is used, the average per-sequence gradient storage falls to an average of 18 MB, but the point remains that activation and gradient memory, not adapter parameter size, are the main drivers of peak GPU memory use.

This observation has a direct practical implication: because LoRA parameters are so small, one can afford to place adapters densely (e.g., at every linear layer) in order to recover performance—without substantially increasing peak memory usage. Instead, to reduce memory footprint and make large-model finetuning possible on a single GPU, the highest leverage changes are in how and where model weights and optimizer states are stored and how to avoid large spikes during backpropagation. QLoRA addresses these by using 4-bit storage for the base model (dramatically reducing the stored weights) and by introducing Paged Optimizers to handle occasional optimizer-memory spikes via unified memory.

# Problem statement: achieve 16-bit finetuning quality with a 4-bit base model
The central technical problem that QLoRA aims to solve can be stated succinctly: can we finetune a pretrained language model stored in a 4-bit representation such that the finetuned performance matches that of conventional 16-bit finetuning? The challenge is twofold. First, how to store the huge base model compactly in very low precision while preserving enough numerical information for correct forward and backward computations. Second, how to perform training with stable optimization dynamics so adapter updates recover the information lost to quantization and yield the same final task performance as 16-bit finetuning.

QLoRA sets out a design goal: reduce average finetuning memory requirements sufficiently to finetune a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, while attaining the same predictive performance as 16-bit finetuning. Meeting this goal requires innovations in the quantization datatype, compression of quantization metadata, and memory management during optimizer updates.

# QLoRA at a glance: storage dtype vs compute dtype; dequantize-on-use; adapter-only gradient updates
QLoRA is built around a simple but powerful dichotomy between storage precision and compute precision. The base pretrained model is stored in a compact low-precision format (by default a 4-bit NormalFloat format, NF4). However, whenever the model is used in computation (both forward and backward passes), the stored tensors are dequantized into a higher-precision compute dtype (the paper uses BFloat16, abbreviated BF16, for computation). In other words, QLoRA stores weights tightly but dequantizes them to a 16-bit compute format on-the-fly.

Only the LoRA adapter parameters are trainable and have their gradients computed and stored in BF16. The base weights remain frozen; gradients with respect to them are not computed or stored. This reduces optimizer state memory substantially.

For a single linear layer with a LoRA adapter, QLoRA's forward pass can be written as

\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \, \operatorname{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) \;+\; \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}.
\]

Here the function $\operatorname{doubleDequant}$ performs a two-stage dequantization (explained below) to reconstruct a BF16 copy of the weight block from its compact stored representation and its quantization constants:

\[
\operatorname{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \operatorname{dequant}(\operatorname{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}.
\]

Because dequantization happens at runtime into BF16, matrix multiplications and gradient computations occur in a numerically stable compute format even though storage is tiny. During backpropagation we only need gradients with respect to the adapter parameters $\mathbf{L}_i$; we do not need gradients for the frozen 4-bit weights $\mathbf{W}$. Nevertheless, computing those adapter gradients requires using the dequantized $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$ to compute derivatives, so the dequantization path must be accurate.

This dequantize-on-use strategy separates the goals of storage compression and numerical stability in computation, enabling heavy compression without sacrificing the arithmetic fidelity of forward/backward passes.

# Key contributions overview: NF4, Double Quantization, Paged Optimizers
QLoRA marries three main technical ideas to meet its design goals. Each addresses a different part of the bottleneck.

4-bit NormalFloat (NF4). Quantization datatypes can be designed to minimize information loss for the class of values being quantized. Empirically and theoretically, pretrained neural network weights are often approximately normally distributed with mean near zero and a characteristic standard deviation. NF4 constructs a 4-bit datatype that is information-theoretically optimal for zero-mean normal distributions. The construction proceeds by computing quantiles of a standard normal $N(0,1)$ and placing codebook values at midpoints of these quantiles. Formally, for $k$ bits we compute $2^k$ code values $q_i$ via

\[
q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(Q_X\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big) + Q_X\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big),
\]

where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function of $N(0,1)$. Because a symmetric quantization with an even number of code values may not have an exact zero codepoint (important for padding and exact zeros), NFk uses a slightly asymmetric construction that ensures a discrete zero representation while preserving equal expected bin counts. NF4 is the 4-bit instance of this NormalFloat family. Intuitively, NF4 matches the codebook to the weight distribution and thereby reduces quantization error compared to naive FP4 or Int4 choices.

Double Quantization. Block-wise quantization requires storing per-block quantization constants (scales). For small block sizes (which improve per-block fidelity), storing these constants in 32-bit float form can add a non-negligible overhead. Double Quantization compresses the quantization metadata by treating the per-block constants themselves as inputs to a second quantization stage. Concretely, the first quantization produces per-block scales $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ together with quantized weight blocks. Double Quantization quantizes the $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ into a lower-precision format (the paper uses 8-bit floats, FP8, for these constants) producing $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$, and then stores a small extra set of second-level constants $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ to enable reconstruction. Because the scales are all positive, they are mean-centered before symmetric quantization in the second stage. The paper reports that for a weight blocksize of 64, the naive storage overhead from 32-bit constants is $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter on average; with Double Quantization (using FP8 and a blocksize of 256 for the second level) the overhead falls to

\[
\frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} = 0.127 \text{ bits per parameter},
\]

a net reduction of roughly $0.373$ bits per parameter (about 3 GB saved on a 65B model in their setup). Crucially, this extra compression of the quantization metadata does not measurably degrade downstream performance.

Paged Optimizers. Even when the base model is stored compactly, optimizer states and occasional activation/gradient spikes during training can still cause out-of-memory errors. QLoRA uses "Paged Optimizers" that rely on NVIDIA's unified memory feature to allow automatic paging of optimizer state pages between GPU and host memory. When occasional spikes occur (for example due to long sequences creating large activation checkpoints), optimizer pages can be evicted to CPU RAM and paged back into GPU memory when needed for updates, preventing hard out-of-memory failures. The unified-memory paging behaves like standard OS-level paging but for CUDA allocations; it is used judiciously to eliminate rare but problematic peaks in memory use during finetuning on a single GPU.

These three components—an evidence-based 4-bit datatype, compression of quantization metadata, and pragmatic memory management—combine into a system that stores the bulk of the model in 4 bits, computes in BF16, and updates only compact LoRA adapters in BF16.

# QLoRA formal view and training-time behavior
Putting the pieces together, QLoRA has (1) a storage data type (NF4 for the base weights), (2) a compute data type (BF16), and (3) LoRA adapters trained in BF16. Each time a block of the base model is needed for a forward or backward computation it is double-dequantized into BF16 using the compressed quantization metadata, then used in a standard matrix multiplication:

\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \, \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} + \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}.
\]

During backpropagation, gradients are not accumulated for $\mathbf{W}$; only gradients with respect to $\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2$ are computed and used to update the adapter parameters. Because dequantization provides a high-fidelity BF16 representation of $\mathbf{W}$ for use in both forward and backward passes, the computed adapter gradients are numerically similar to what would be obtained if the entire model were stored and computed in BF16 (or FP16). Practically, this means QLoRA recovers the same finetuning performance as 16-bit methods in the paper's experiments.

# Preview of outcomes: dramatic memory savings and high-quality models
The paper demonstrates that with QLoRA it is possible to finetune a 65B parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU while matching 16-bit finetuning performance on evaluated tasks. The authors reduce average finetuning memory usage from over 780 GB (for standard 16-bit full finetuning of a 65B model) to less than 48 GB using the combination of NF4 storage, Double Quantization, LoRA adapters, and Paged Optimizers. This reduction transforms what was previously a multi-server problem into one that can be done on a single consumer or professional GPU.

Using QLoRA, the authors trained a family of finetuned models called Guanaco. The best Guanaco model (65B, finetuned on a variant of the OASST1 instruction dataset) achieves competitive chatbot performance: it reaches 99.3% of the performance level of ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark in their automated evaluation setup, and several Guanaco variants can be finetuned in a few hours on a single GPU (for example, a 33B model in about 12 hours on a 24 GB GPU, or 24 hours for the largest model on a single professional GPU). The smallest Guanaco (7B) requires about 5 GB of memory for deployment and substantially outperforms a 13B Alpaca baseline on Vicuna, showing that QLoRA empowers both research-scale experiments and practical deployment.

# Closing perspective
The QLoRA proposal reframes finetuning of large language models as a problem of separating storage efficiency from compute fidelity. By choosing an information-aware 4-bit storage format (NF4), compressing quantization metadata (Double Quantization), and managing rare optimizer-memory spikes (Paged Optimizers), QLoRA enables stable backward passes through a quantized frozen base model and recovers 16-bit finetuning quality using only low-precision stored weights plus a small set of BF16 adapters. The result is a practical, reproducible recipe that substantially lowers the access barrier to finetuning very large models and unlocks new empirical exploration at scales that were previously inaccessible to most researchers.

The QLoRA Method: Datatypes, Algorithms, and Implementation

# NormalFloat (NF4): quantile quantization for normal weights, fixed-range normalization, zero handling via asymmetric bins

NormalFloat (NF4) is the 4-bit data type that QLoRA uses to store pretrained model weights. Its design is motivated by two observations about pretrained neural network weights: (1) they are approximately zero-centered and follow a normal-like distribution, and (2) we can treat the tensor scale (standard deviation) as a separate quantization constant. NF4 is an application of quantile quantization specialized to the standard normal distribution, implemented so that each of the $2^k$ quantization bins has equal expected occupancy for normally distributed values after a fixed-range normalization.

The construction proceeds in three conceptual steps. First, consider the standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$ and compute the $2^k+1$ quantiles of that distribution; using those quantiles, define $2^k$ representative bin values by averaging adjacent quantile points. Concretely, for $k$ bits we compute representative values
\[
q_i = \frac{1}{2}\Big( Q_X\!\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big) + Q_X\!\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big),
\]
where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function (inverse CDF) of $N(0,1)$ and $i=0,\dots,2^k-1$. Second, normalize an input weight tensor into the fixed range $[-1,1]$ by dividing by an absolute-maximum scale for that block; this makes the per-block distribution have the same support as the NF data type. Third, quantize the normalized weights using the precomputed $q_i$ values and store the resulting 4-bit indices along with the block scale.

A practical but important corner case is representing exact zero. If we were to make a symmetric $2^k$-level quantization around zero, there would not be an exact discrete representation of $0$, which hurts padding and other exact-zero elements. NF4 resolves this by constructing an asymmetric bin layout: estimate quantiles separately for the negative side using $2^{k-1}$ bins and for the positive side using $2^{k-1}+1$ bins, then unify these two sets and remove the duplicate zero. The result is a $k$-bit NormalFloat data type with an explicit discrete zero and with roughly equal expected occupancy per bin for Gaussian-distributed inputs after normalization.

Because the NF4 bin values are derived from exact quantiles of $N(0,1)$, the data type is information-theoretically optimal for zero-centered normal distributions up to the rescaling constant. In practice NF4 is used with a small block size (e.g., 64 elements per block) so that the per-block scale closely matches the local standard deviation of the weights, preserving the theoretical assumptions that make NF4 effective.

# Why NF4 beats FP4/Int4: information-theoretic optimality, outlier handling, empirical precision advantages

NF4's core advantage is that it is matched to the empirical distribution of pretrained weights. Pretrained transformer weights are typically close to zero-mean Gaussians (after layerwise parameter statistics), so choosing quantization bin boundaries to equalize occupancy for that distribution minimizes the expected binning error. This is an information-theoretic statement: for a known distribution, quantile-based binning minimizes the expected quantization error when using a fixed number of bins.

There are two concrete failure modes of simpler 4-bit schemes that NF4 mitigates. Integer 4-bit (Int4) or naïve 4-bit float (FP4) formats typically assign bins uniformly over the representable numeric range. If a tensor contains outliers, a single global or coarse-grained scaling uses too wide a numeric range and many representational bins will be unused for the bulk of the distribution; conversely, if one adapts the scale tightly to the bulk, outliers will be clipped. Quantile-based NF4 avoids this misallocation of bins by placing bin centers near the regions where the normal distribution places mass. When combined with small block sizes (so the blockwise scale adapts to local dispersion), NF4 retains much more representational fidelity for the usual case of Gaussian-like weight blocks while also allowing an exact zero bin for padding.

Empirically, the paper reports that NF4 outperforms both FP4 and Int4 across language modeling and zero-shot tasks for a broad range of models (OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, Pythia) and model sizes (125M–13B), with lower perplexities and better downstream zero-shot accuracies. Those empirical gains support the theoretic intuition: matching the quantizer to the weights' distribution yields tangible precision advantages in practice.

# Double Quantization (DQ): quantizing quantization constants, memory math (0.5 → 0.127 bits/param with block sizes 64/256), mean-centering of positive scales

Block-wise k-bit quantization stores two pieces of information per block: the low-bit indices for the block elements and the block's quantization constant (scale) $c$ in high-precision (usually FP32). When the blocksize is small (e.g., 64 elements), these scale values incur a non-negligible per-parameter memory overhead: with 32-bit constants and blocksize $B$, the scale storage contributes $32/B$ bits per parameter on average. For $B=64$ that is $32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter.

Double Quantization (DQ) reduces the memory cost of storing these quantization constants by quantizing the constants themselves. Concretely QLoRA treats the first-level constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ (the per-block scales for the NF4 representation) as a vector to be quantized a second time. The second-level quantization stores quantized second-level constants $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}$ (the quantized representation of the scales) plus a second-level high-precision constant $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ for groups of these scales.

In implementation, QLoRA uses an 8-bit floating point format (FP8) for the second-level quantization of the per-block scales and groups those FP8-quantized constants in a larger block (e.g., blocksize 256) so that $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ contributes only $32/(B\cdot 256)$ bits per parameter on average. The memory math in the paper for $B=64$ and a second-level grouping of 256 yields:
\[
\text{original cost} = \frac{32}{64} = 0.5 \text{ bits/param}
\]
\[
\text{DQ cost} = \frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} = 0.127 \text{ bits/param}
\]
which is a reduction of $0.373$ bits/parameter (roughly 3 GB for a 65B-parameter model).

One practical detail is that the first-level scales $c_2$ are strictly positive. To make symmetric quantization for the second step effective, QLoRA subtracts the mean from the $c_2$ values before quantizing them—this centers the distribution of $c_2$ around zero and yields better symmetric quantization behavior for FP8. During dequantization the mean is added back as part of the double dequantization pipeline.

Double Quantization thus yields significant memory savings with negligible empirical impact to precision when FP8 is used for scale quantization and when grouping for the second-level constants is large enough (e.g., 256), as reported in the paper.

# Paged Optimizers: using NVIDIA unified memory to avoid OOM during gradient checkpointing; when paging triggers and expected trade-offs

Paged Optimizers are an engineering technique QLoRA uses to prevent out-of-memory (OOM) failures that arise from occasional memory spikes during training, primarily caused by gradient checkpointing with long sequence lengths or large batch slices. The approach leverages NVIDIA unified memory, which provides automatic page-wise transfers between GPU memory and CPU RAM. By allocating optimizer state memory as paged (i.e., managed by the unified memory system), the runtime can evict optimizer pages to host RAM when the GPU lacks space, and page them back into GPU memory when they are needed for parameter updates.

In a QLoRA training loop this means optimizer states—which can be large for some optimizers, especially when training adapters with occasional optimizer state growth—do not have to be fully resident on GPU memory at all times. The paging behavior is automatic: when a forward/backward pass creates a spike that exceeds GPU capacity, the unified memory subsystem will move pages to CPU RAM rather than raising an immediate OOM error. Later, when the optimizer update needs those pages, they are transferred back.

The paper emphasizes that paging is most likely to occur when processing a minibatch with an unusually long sequence length—these are the rare events that otherwise force conservative, memory-inefficient engineering. They report that, for their 65B experiments on a 48GB GPU with batch size 16, paged optimizers provided the same training speed as regular optimizers in practice, meaning no observable slowdown in that configuration. The authors note, however, that they did not provide exhaustive measurements of paging overhead, and that future work should characterize when paging-induced slowdowns occur. The key practical trade-off is that paging can avoid failure at the cost of potential host–device transfer overhead; in many typical training regimes these spikes are infrequent enough that paging is an acceptable and robust solution.

# Formal QLoRA layer: storage vs compute dtype (4-bit → BF16), double dequantization, adapter path, gradients only for LoRA parameters

A single QLoRA layer fuses a frozen, block-wise quantized base weight with a trainable LoRA adapter. The base weight is stored in NF4 (4-bit) with double-quantized scales; the computation is performed in a higher-precision computation dtype (the paper uses BrainFloat16 / BF16). The forward pass for one linear projection with one LoRA adapter is:

\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \,\text{doubleDequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\big)
\;+\; \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \mathbf{L}_1^{\text{BF16}} \mathbf{L}_2^{\text{BF16}}.
\]

The double dequantization operation composes the two dequantization steps and yields a BF16 tensor for the base weight:

\[
\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) 
= \text{dequant}\big(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}\big)
= \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}.
\]

Operationally, QLoRA stores $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ and the compact second-level constants on GPU, and when the layer is executed the NF4 indices are dequantized to BF16 (using the recovered per-block scales after double dequantization) and the BF16 matrix multiply is performed.

Crucially for finetuning, the pretrained base weights $\mathbf{W}$ remain frozen; gradients are not computed or accumulated for $\mathbf{W}$. Instead, QLoRA learns small low-rank adapter matrices $\mathbf{L}_1, \mathbf{L}_2$ (in BF16) and only computes parameter updates for those adapter weights. During backpropagation we still need to propagate gradients through the dequantization path because the adapter gradients depend on the derivatives of the output with respect to the base-weight path. Concretely, for adapter gradients $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i$ we require the derivative $\partial \mathbf{Y}/\partial \mathbf{W}$ which is computed using the dequantized BF16 $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$ during the backward pass. But after computing these derivatives we never form or store $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{W}$ as trainable state; only the adapter parameters receive optimizer updates.

This storage-vs-compute split—4-bit storage and BF16 computation—allows the full forward and backward passes to be performed with high numerical fidelity while keeping the parameter storage requirements minimal.

# Adapter design and placement: placing adapters in all transformer linear layers vs default Q/V only; scaling factor s; rank r considerations

LoRA augments a linear projection $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ with a low-rank residual computed as $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ scaled by a scalar $s$, giving the full projection
\[
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\]
where $\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$, $\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$, and $r$ is the adapter rank.

A central empirical finding reported in the paper is that the standard practice of applying LoRA only to the query and value projection matrices in attention layers is insufficient to recover full fine-tuning performance when working from aggressive (4-bit) quantization. Instead, placing LoRA adapters on every linear projection across transformer blocks—effectively adding adapters at every layer—was necessary to match 16-bit full fine-tuning performance in their experiments (for example, LLaMA 7B finetuned on Alpaca). The number of adapter locations (i.e., placement at all linear layers) is therefore a critical hyperparameter; in contrast the choice of projection rank $r$ proved to be less sensitive in their hyperparameter sweeps.

The scaling factor $s$ is included in the LoRA formulation to control the magnitude of the adapter contribution relative to the frozen base projection. While the paper does not provide a prescriptive $s$ value, it emphasizes careful tuning of adapter placement (quantity) rather than rank $r$ or other LoRA hyperparameters to recover full-finetuning performance. Because LoRA parameters are small relative to the model, the authors point out that increasing the number of adapters (by placing them more widely) does not materially increase overall training memory—the dominant memory costs are activation gradients and the (paged) optimizer state.

# Block sizes and dtypes in practice: W blocks of 64 for precision; FP8 for second-level constants with blocks of 256

In the QLoRA implementation choices matter for the precision–memory trade-off. The paper reports the following practical defaults that balance quantization precision against memory:

- Base-weight blocks: use NF4 with a blocksize of $B=64$. Small block sizes increase precision because the per-block scale better matches local weight dispersion; empirically, a blocksize of 64 gave the desired quantization quality.

- Second-level constants: quantize the per-block scales to an 8-bit floating point format (FP8) and group these FP8 constants into larger blocks for the second-level scale (e.g., groups of 256), i.e., use blocksize 256 for the second quantization. This combination yielded large memory savings while not degrading empirical performance.

Thus QLoRA stores NF4 indices per weight block of 64 and stores second-level FP8-quantized scales grouped at 256, along with a sparse set of FP32 second-level grouping constants. This configuration is what yields the representative memory-compression math cited in the paper and the empirical fidelity comparable to higher-precision baselines.

# Training pipeline: dequantize-on-use, BF16 matmuls, gradient checkpointing, optimizer states with paging

The training pipeline for QLoRA follows the principle of dequantize-on-use. Weight storage is optimized (NF4 + double quantized scales), but whenever a forward computation requires the base weight, the indices and quantized scales are dequantized into the BF16 compute format and then used for matrix multiplications. The LoRA adapter path is kept in BF16 for both forward and backward computations.

Training proceeds with the usual forward/backward pass and gradient checkpointing to trade compute for lower memory usage of activation storage. Because activation gradients are the major consumer of memory during finetuning, gradient checkpointing remains crucial; the paper reports that with gradient checkpointing the memory devoted to LoRA input gradients becomes comparable to or larger than the LoRA parameters themselves. The small memory footprint of LoRA parameters allows placing adapters everywhere without blowing up memory use.

Optimizer states—potentially large and subject to spikes as checkpointed activations are recomputed—are allocated as paged memory so that the unified memory subsystem can spill optimizer state pages to host RAM if a GPU memory spike occurs. This pipeline ensures that: (1) the forward and backward passes use BF16 arithmetic after dequantization for numerical fidelity, (2) the frozen base weights never need to be updated (so no optimizer state or gradient accumulation is required for them), and (3) only the adapter parameters carry optimizer state, which is paged when necessary.

The practical consequence is that QLoRA can finetune very large models (33B on a single 24GB consumer GPU, 65B on a single 48GB GPU) without sacrificing the precision of BF16 computation in the forward/backward passes while keeping storage costs very low.

# Engineering notes: CUDA kernels for 4-bit training, integration in Hugging Face Transformers/bitsandbytes, reproducibility tips

Implementing QLoRA efficiently requires low-level support for fast dequantization and BF16 matrix multiplication from 4-bit indices, and efficient CUDA kernels to perform these steps without becoming a compute bottleneck. The authors release CUDA kernels for 4-bit training as part of their codebase and integrate QLoRA into existing tooling (Hugging Face Transformers and bitsandbytes), allowing practitioners to reuse the optimized kernels and higher-level APIs.

For reproducibility and practical experiments the paper highlights a few operational recommendations that practitioners should follow to replicate the reported performance:

- Use NF4 for the base-weight storage with blocksize 64, and use double quantization with FP8 for the second-level quantized scales grouped at 256. This configuration balanced precision and memory in the experiments.

- Place LoRA adapters in all linear transformer layers rather than only query/value projections; the number and placement of adapters matters more than adapter rank $r$ in matching full finetuning performance.

- Use BF16 as the computation dtype after dequantization so that matrix multiplies and backward passes have higher numerical fidelity than low-bit storage would imply.

- Enable gradient checkpointing to reduce activation storage. Combine this with paged optimizers so occasional spikes caused by checkpoint recomputation do not produce OOM failures.

- For larger models (33B, 65B), adjust hyperparameters found at smaller scales: the authors found that learning rate should be halved and batch size doubled when moving from 7B to 33B/65B to generalize their hyperparameter choices.

Finally, because QLoRA recovers 16-bit finetuning performance despite 4-bit storage, practitioners can experiment freely with base model size trade-offs: in many of the authors' experiments it was more effective to increase base-model parameter count while decreasing numeric precision (4-bit) and applying QLoRA, than to keep smaller models at higher precision.

By following the datatypes, block sizes, adapter placement, and paging recommendations above and using the provided CUDA kernels and integrations, researchers can reproduce the memory and performance characteristics reported in the paper and run large-scale finetuning experiments on modest hardware.

Validation and Ablations: Matching 16-bit with 4-bit QLoRA

# Experimental setup: architectures, scales, and tasks
To validate that QLoRA (quantized LoRA) can recover the performance of 16-bit finetuning, the authors evaluated across multiple model families, sizes, and standardized tasks. Architectures included encoder-only (RoBERTa), encoder–decoder (T5), and decoder-only (LLaMA) models. Model scales ranged from small research models (125M parameters) through medium sizes (few hundreds of millions to a few billion) up to very large publicly available models (33B and 65B). The tasks chosen for evaluation reflect three common finetuning regimes:

- GLUE for standard supervised classification evaluation with RoBERTa as the base model.
- Super-NaturalInstructions (referred to as TKInstruct in the paper) for instruction‑following behavior with T5 models.
- 5‑shot MMLU (Massively Multitask Language Understanding) for few‑shot multi-domain knowledge evaluation, applied after finetuning LLaMA on instruction datasets such as FLAN v2 and Alpaca.

Two complementary experimental goals were pursued. First, for small-to-moderate models (up to about 3B parameters) the paper directly compared QLoRA to both 16‑bit full-model finetuning and 16‑bit LoRA (adapter) finetuning on GLUE and TKInstruct to see whether accuracy is preserved. Second, for larger models (7B through 65B) where full 16‑bit finetuning is impractical on single machines, the authors compared 4‑bit QLoRA against 16‑bit LoRA baselines on MMLU to check whether 4‑bit adapter tuning can match the established 16‑bit adapter results.

Across these setups the practical quantization and finetuning configuration was the same conceptual pipeline: store the frozen pretrained weights in a low‑precision k‑bit format (in practice NF4 = 4‑bit NormalFloat), dequantize to a 16‑bit computation format (BFloat16) for forward/backward passes, and only update LoRA adapter parameters (in 16‑bit) via backpropagation through the dequantized base weights. Experiments also tested alternative 4‑bit formats (FP4, Int4) and ablated Double Quantization and paged‑optimizer behavior.

# NF4 vs FP4/Int4: post‑quantization perplexity and zero‑shot accuracy trends
A central technical contribution is the 4‑bit NormalFloat data type (NF4). NF4 is constructed to be information‑theoretically optimal for tensors whose distribution is approximately zero‑mean normal. Concretely, NFk (for k bits) is obtained by taking quantiles of a standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$ and mapping those quantiles into a $[-1,1]$ range; the per‑tensor rescaling (absolute maximum) then matches the actual tensor standard deviation to the data type range before quantization. The paper formalizes the quantile centers as

\[
q_i  = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\!\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\!\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right),
\]

where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function of $N(0,1)$, and then constructs an asymmetric variant to ensure an exact discrete zero value while keeping equal expected occupancy of the bins.

Empirically, the authors compared NF4 against FP4 (4‑bit floating representations) and Int4 (4‑bit integers) across model families including OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, and Pythia for sizes 125M up to 13B (and also evaluated larger models for downstream finetuning tasks). The reported trends were clear:

- NF4 consistently produced lower post‑quantization perplexity and higher zero‑shot accuracy than FP4 and Int4. That is, when the frozen model weights were quantized and used for evaluation without further tuning, NF4 retained language modeling quality better than the other 4‑bit formats.
- The improved fidelity of NF4 carried over into the finetuning regime: QLoRA using NF4 (plus the other QLoRA machinery) outperformed QLoRA variants that used FP4, and FP4 lagged behind the 16‑bit LoRA baseline by roughly one percentage point on some MMLU evaluations.

Intuitively, NF4 works better because many pretrained weight tensors closely approximate centered normal distributions. By designing a k‑bit representation whose bins match the quantiles of a normal distribution, NF4 spreads representational power across the region where most weight values lie, instead of wasting bins on extreme outliers or on intervals with very few values.

# Parity results: matching 16‑bit finetuning and 16‑bit LoRA
The core validation claim is that QLoRA with NF4 (and the supporting techniques) matches the performance of full 16‑bit finetuning and 16‑bit LoRA finetuning across the studied tasks and scales.

For GLUE and Super‑NaturalInstructions (T5), experiments on models from 125M up to 3B parameters showed that adapter finetuning in 4‑bit (with NF4) recovered the performance of the fully finetuned 16‑bit baselines. In other words, when comparing the standard metrics on GLUE and instruction‑following accuracy on TKInstruct, the 4‑bit NF4 QLoRA adapters achieved parity with:

- 16‑bit full‑model finetuning (where all model weights are updated), and
- 16‑bit LoRA adapter finetuning (where only adapter parameters are trained but model storage/computation remains 16‑bit).

For the larger LLaMA models (7B through 65B) evaluated on 5‑shot MMLU, NF4 QLoRA with Double Quantization matched the 16‑bit LoRA MMLU performance. The paper reports that NF4 + Double Quantization fully recovered the 16‑bit LoRA MMLU accuracy, whereas FP4 QLoRA lagged by about 1 percentage point. Thus, across the major academic benchmarks and across a wide range of scales, 4‑bit adapter finetuning using NF4 showed no detectable degradation versus 16‑bit adapter or full finetuning baselines.

Two practical implications follow: (1) the information lost to aggressive 4‑bit storage of frozen base weights can be recovered by training a small set of adapter parameters, provided the quantization is high quality (NF4) and adapters are placed appropriately; (2) using QLoRA lets researchers access much larger base models for finetuning with the same compute budget, without compromising downstream accuracy.

# Double Quantization ablation: memory savings without detectable loss
Block‑wise quantization requires storing a quantization constant (scale) for each block. For example, with 32‑bit constants and a blocksize of $B=64$ for a weight tensor $\mathbf{W}$, the per‑parameter overhead from constants is $32/B = 32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter. This overhead becomes material at the scale of tens of billions of parameters.

Double Quantization addresses this by quantizing the quantization constants themselves. Formally, if the first quantization produces per‑block constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$, Double Quantization treats the vector of these constants as input to a second quantization step, producing quantized constants $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ and a second‑level dequantizer $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. In practice the authors use 8‑bit floats for the second quantization, with a larger blocksize (e.g., 256) for the constants. Because the first‑level constants are positive, they subtract the mean before symmetrically quantizing, which improves fidelity.

The arithmetic in the paper illustrates the savings. With blocksize $B=64$, the original overhead is

\[
\text{overhead}_{\text{orig}} = \frac{32}{64} = 0.5 \text{ bits/parameter}.
\]

After Double Quantization (8‑bit FP for $c_2$ with blocksize 256 for the second quantization) the average overhead becomes approximately

\[
\text{overhead}_{\text{dq}} = \frac{8}{64} + \frac{32}{64\cdot 256} = 0.127 \text{ bits/parameter},
\]

which is a reduction of about $0.373$ bits per parameter. For a 65B‑parameter model this corresponds to several gigabytes saved in total storage. Importantly, empirical ablations showed that Double Quantization reduced memory footprint substantially without detectable performance degradation in downstream tasks when used with NF4. Thus Double Quantization is an effective, low‑risk trade that favors memory efficiency.

# LoRA hyperparameters: coverage matters, rank less so
LoRA introduces a factorized low‑rank update to selected linear projections. Typical prior practice placed adapters on the query and value projection matrices inside attention blocks. The paper reported an important ablation: using the standard, limited LoRA placement (Q/V only) was not sufficient to replicate full‑finetuning performance for larger LLaMA base models. The most critical LoRA hyperparameter discovered was not the projection rank $r$, but adapter coverage — i.e., how many linear layers receive LoRA adapters.

Several observations emerged from the experiments:

- Adapter coverage: Placing LoRA adapters on every linear layer (every transformer block linear projection) was necessary to match full 16‑bit finetuning performance. This means extending beyond Q/V attention projections to feedforward and other linear layers in each transformer block.
- Rank sensitivity: The projection dimension $r$ (the inner rank in the factorization) had limited sensitivity. Reasonable choices of $r$ work, and ramping $r$ up or down did not produce large performance swings in these experiments.
- Memory tradeoffs: Because the bulk of the training memory footprint comes from activation gradients during the backward pass rather than from storing LoRA parameters themselves, increasing the number of adapters (coverage) imposes only a small incremental memory cost. For example, in a 7B LLaMA finetuning scenario, the LoRA parameters were tiny (tens of MB), while input gradients and the 4‑bit base model dominated memory usage. Therefore, using more adapters to recover accuracy is an economical choice.

In practice this means that to obtain parity with full finetuning, practitioners should prioritize placing adapters widely across the model rather than aggressively minimizing adapter size. The authors also noted that default hyperparameters for fully finetuned baselines were undertuned and performed modest hyperparameter sweeps (learning rate, batch size) to find robust baselines for fair comparison.

# Paged Optimizers runtime notes
Large models and long sequences cause memory spikes during gradient checkpointing: intermediate activations needed for the backward pass can briefly exceed available GPU memory. Paged Optimizers mitigate this by leveraging the NVIDIA unified memory facility, which automatically pages memory between GPU and host RAM when the GPU is short on space. The optimizer state tensors are allocated in pageable memory: when a memory spike occurs they can be evicted to host RAM and paged back in automatically when needed for optimizer updates.

A few practical points from the paper:

- Paging is used only for optimizer states; the frozen base weights remain stored in the compact 4‑bit representation.
- Paging events are relatively rare under typical batching and sequence lengths, so paged optimizers do not generally introduce perceptible slowdown.
- For a 65B model running on a 48 GB GPU with a batch size of 16, the authors report that paged optimizers delivered the same training speed as regular optimizers in their runtime analysis. They caution that paging performance depends on workload and sequence lengths and that future work should more systematically characterize runtime tradeoffs across scenarios.

Paged Optimizers thus make single‑GPU finetuning of very large models reliable by avoiding hard out‑of‑memory failures produced by occasional activation spikes, while keeping runtimes competitive in typical settings.

# Resource economics: memory footprints and training times
One of the most consequential practical claims is that QLoRA dramatically reduces the GPU memory required to finetune extremely large models, bringing previously prohibitive experiments within reach of single‑GPU setups.

Key quantitative figures from the paper include:

- Full 16‑bit finetuning of a LLaMA 65B model requires more than 780 GB of GPU memory (so it cannot be done on a single server with standard GPUs).
- QLoRA reduces the average memory requirement for finetuning a 65B LLaMA model to under 48 GB, enabling finetuning on a single high‑end 48 GB GPU.
- A 7B Guanaco model at deployment requires roughly 5 GB of memory and thus can fit on modern phones; despite the small footprint it outperforms larger open models that have greater memory usage.
- For 33B models, the 4‑bit storage makes the memory footprint about 21 GB, which is lower than some earlier 13B full‑precision models (e.g., a 26 GB Alpaca 13B model), while producing better benchmark performance.
- Training time examples: Guanaco 33B can be finetuned in under 12 hours on a 24 GB consumer GPU (using QLoRA). Guanaco 65B required roughly 24 hours on a single professional GPU to achieve its reported performance (99.3% of ChatGPT on Vicuna).

These resource numbers illustrate a practical economic point: by compressing the base model to 4 bits and training only a small set of adapters, researchers can substantially increase the effective scale of the models they can finetune within fixed hardware constraints, reducing monetary and energy costs relative to distributed 16‑bit pipelines.

# Scaling insight: larger low‑precision models can beat smaller high‑precision ones
An important empirical trend observed in the paper is that within a fixed finetuning and inference budget, it is advantageous to increase the number of base parameters while decreasing their storage precision. The MMLU and Elo tournament experiments suggested that a larger model stored at 4‑bit precision and finetuned via LoRA provides better downstream performance than a smaller high‑precision model finetuned to the same degree.

This effect has an intuitive explanation: model capacity (parameter count) often improves representational power more than incremental reductions in numeric precision degrade it — provided the quantization is done carefully. QLoRA enables practitioners to trade precision for size cheaply: because adapter finetuning recovers the critical task‑relevant derivatives and NF4 preserves the essential structure of the frozen weights, the overall downstream behavior benefits from extra model capacity even when stored at 4 bits.

The paper therefore recommends that, when constrained by compute/memory budget, the practitioner consider scaling base model size and using QLoRA rather than insisting on full 16‑bit finetuning of a smaller model.

# Open questions and future directions
Although the empirical results are encouraging, the paper highlights several directions that remain open and should be investigated in future work:

- Precision boundary: QLoRA demonstrates parity at 4 bits, but where exactly the precision–performance boundary lies beneath 4 bits is unresolved. The paper suggests the possibility that even more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3‑bit bases combined with adapters) might still recover 16‑bit finetuning performance, but systematic study is needed.
- Other PEFT methods: The experiments used LoRA adapters. There exists a broad family of parameter‑efficient finetuning techniques (prompt tuning, IA^3, bias tuning, etc.). It remains to be seen which of these adapt best to the quantize‑then‑finetune paradigm at very large scales.
- Runtime characterization of paging: paging behavior depends on batch sizes, sequence lengths, and host/GPU bandwidths. The paper reports that paging did not slow training for their main 65B runs (batch size 16), but a full cost‑and‑latency characterization across workloads would be valuable.
- Broader evaluation: while QLoRA matched 16‑bit baselines on GLUE, TKInstruct, and MMLU, additional benchmarks (e.g., BigBench, HELM, specialized robustness evaluations) and other downstream tasks may reveal weaknesses or strengths not captured by the evaluated suites.
- Interaction with RLHF and other training protocols: the reported Guanaco models were trained by supervised cross‑entropy finetuning only. How QLoRA pairs with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or AI‑generated preference signals (RLAIF) at scale remains to be fully explored.

These open questions point to a research agenda that builds on the QLoRA toolkit: push precision lower, try alternative adapter strategies, measure runtime behaviors, and broaden the validation suite. The guiding practical insight from the paper is clear: careful 4‑bit quantization (NF4) plus Double Quantization and appropriate adapter placement enables researchers to finetune very large models on modest hardware without conceding accuracy, unlocking new empirical studies and applications that were previously prohibitive.

Instruction Tuning at Scale and Chatbot Results: The Guanaco Case Study

# Instruction datasets surveyed: OASST1, HH-RLHF, Alpaca, Self-Instruct, Unnatural Instructions, FLAN v2, Chip2, Longform

A central empirical axis of the QLoRA study is a comparison across eight recent instruction‑tuning datasets. These datasets were chosen to represent a diversity of provenance (crowd-sourced, model‑distilled, aggregations, hybrids), sizes, languages and intended use-cases. Briefly:

- OASST1 (Open Assistant) is a crowd-sourced, multi-turn conversational dataset intended for assistant training. It is multilingual and was collected with explicit guidelines forbidding use of GPT model outputs. The authors find it to be comparatively small (on the order of thousands of curated examples in the variant they used) but high quality for instruction‑following dialog.

- HH-RLHF is a dataset derived from Anthropic’s Helpful‑Harmless human preference collection used for RLHF research. It contains human comparisons and responses reflecting curated human preferences.

- Alpaca, Self‑Instruct, and Unnatural Instructions are examples of datasets created by distillation or programmatic generation. Alpaca is distilled from an instruction‑tuned source model, Self‑Instruct generates many instruction–response pairs by bootstrapping, and Unnatural Instructions contains a broad collection of synthetic instruction tasks. These datasets are useful for large‑scale instruction coverage but may differ in quality and style from human‑crafted dialogs.

- FLAN v2 is an aggregated instruction‑tuning collection curated for general instruction following across many task families; it is large (hundreds of thousands of examples in the subsampled variant used) and tends to be well aligned with benchmarks like MMLU.

- Chip2 (OIG / LAION format) and Longform are hybrid or specialized corpora: Chip2 aggregates multiple public instruction-style sources and Longform focuses on longer-form responses. These datasets sample different tradeoffs of breadth, format (single-turn vs multi-turn), and style.

The paper’s experiments show that dataset provenance and quality strongly affect downstream chatbot behavior. In particular, the relatively small OASST1 dataset produced the best Guanaco chatbots, indicating that data quality and conversation-style match to the target evaluation are often more important than raw dataset size.

# Training setup: supervised cross-entropy only (no RLHF), response-only vs full conversation finetuning, LR/batch scaling with model size

All Guanaco variants in the study were trained using supervised cross‑entropy (standard sequence modeling) rather than reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). This decision isolates the instruction‑finetuning effect of the data and adapters without confounding changes from reward models or policy optimization. When datasets contained explicit human rankings or multiple candidate responses (e.g., OASST1, HH‑RLHF), the authors selected the top response at each node in the conversation tree and finetuned on the selected complete conversation, including prompts and preceding turns. For datasets that clearly separate instruction and response text, they sometimes finetuned on the response only; the paper notes ablations on this choice in appendices but uses NF4 QLoRA with double quantization and paged optimizers uniformly.

Hyperparameter scaling with model size was simple but important. The 7B hyperparameters were broadly reused at larger scales, with two adjustments: for the 33B and 65B LLaMA models they halved the learning rate and doubled the batch size relative to the 7B settings. This choice stabilizes optimization at scale and was sufficient to produce strong performance without extensive per‑size re‑tuning.

# Baselines: Vicuna, Open Assistant, commercial systems (GPT-4, GPT-3.5-turbo, Bard)

To contextualize Guanaco, the authors compare against both open research baselines and commercial chat systems. Open baselines include Vicuna (a distilled LLaMA‑13B chatbot trained on ShareGPT transcripts) and Open Assistant (a LLaMA‑33B model finetuned with RLHF on OASST1). Commercial systems used as upper references are GPT‑4, GPT‑3.5‑turbo (ChatGPT family), and Bard. These baselines provide a practical ladder: Vicuna and Open Assistant represent the state of released open chatbots; GPT‑3.5 and GPT‑4 establish contemporary commercial performance levels.

# Evaluation protocols: MMLU (5-shot), Vicuna prompts (80), OA benchmark (~953 prompts), generation settings (p=0.9, T=0.7)

Evaluation uses both a standard multiple‑choice academic benchmark and curated generative dialog benchmarks.

- MMLU: Massively Multitask Language Understanding is used in a five‑shot configuration to measure general multi‑domain knowledge. It is treated as a diagnostic of knowledge and reasoning aligned to classroom/scholarly tasks.

- Vicuna prompt set: an 80‑prompt suite drawn from multiple categories used in prior work to measure open conversational ability. Vicuna-style evaluation has become a standard for comparing open chatbots.

- OA benchmark (Open Assistant validation): for multi-turn dialog evaluation the authors used all user messages in the Open Assistant validation set, producing approximately 953 queries when previous turns are included in prompts. This benchmark is particularly relevant to conversational behavior shaped by OASST1 training.

For generation, the experiments use nucleus (top‑p) sampling with p = 0.9 and temperature T = 0.7 for all model outputs in the generative benchmarks. These sampling parameters create diverse but high‑quality responses consistently across evaluations.

# Automated evaluation: GPT-4 scoring vs ChatGPT references; pairwise head-to-head with tie option; order effects and mitigation

Automated evaluation leverages GPT‑4 in two complementary ways.

First, to reproduce and extend the Vicuna evaluation approach, GPT‑4 is prompted to assign numeric scores (0–10) to ChatGPT (usually GPT‑3.5‑turbo in the reference comparisons) and the evaluated model’s response for each prompt, and to provide explanations. The model score is reported relative to ChatGPT: a model receives the percentage of ChatGPT’s total score (this relative score can exceed 100% if the model outperforms ChatGPT on average).

Second, the authors use direct pairwise head‑to‑head comparisons. For each pair of model responses to the same prompt, GPT‑4 is asked to pick the better response or declare a tie, and to explain the choice. This three‑class outcome (A wins / tie / B wins) maps cleanly to Elo tournament updates.

A nontrivial empirical artifact was discovered: GPT‑4 shows a strong order effect, favoring the response that appears earlier in its prompt. To mitigate this, the authors recommend and use averaging over both presentation orders (A first / B first). For the numeric scoring setup they report mean scores across both orderings to reduce positional bias.

# Human evaluation: AMT studies mirroring automated protocols; inter-annotator agreement and comparison to GPT-4 judgments

Human evaluation was conducted on Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) following the same two protocols as the automated setup: (1) scoring against a ChatGPT reference, and (2) pairwise comparisons. For the ChatGPT-reference scoring, two human annotators rated each example; for pairwise comparisons three annotators rated each match. The authors aggregated human judgments (majority vote for pairwise, averaging for numeric scores) and report inter‑annotator agreement metrics:

- Overall inter‑annotator agreement among humans is described as moderate, with Fleiss’ κ ≈ 0.42 reported for human annotators on the Vicuna-style tasks.

- The sample‑level agreement between GPT‑4 judgments and human majority vote is weaker: Fleiss’ κ ≈ 0.25. At the system ranking level the correlation between GPT‑4 and human rankings is also only moderate (Kendall τ = 0.43, Spearman ρ = 0.55). These numbers quantify that although GPT‑4 is a useful automated rater, it does not perfectly match human preferences and can disagree often on hard samples.

The human study validates that GPT‑4‑based automated evaluation is a cost‑effective proxy but one that must be interpreted with caution.

# Elo rating tournaments: methodology, repeated sampling, interpretation of Elo differences as win probabilities

To aggregate pairwise comparisons into a global ranking, the paper uses Elo rating. The tournament setup and aggregation are as follows:

- Each model starts with an initial rating of 1000 and a K‑factor of K = 32.

- Each labeled pairwise match (from human or GPT‑4 judgments) is treated as a game update. The expected score for player A against player B is computed using the standard Elo formula:
  \[
    E_A = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}.
  \]
  Thus, for example, a 1100 vs 1000 matchup corresponds to an expected score for the stronger player close to 0.65 (a roughly 65% win probability).

- To reduce sensitivity to the order of processed matches and sampling noise, the procedure is repeated 10,000 times with different random seeds and the Elo results are aggregated.

Elo provides an interpretable, pairwise‑consistent system ranking: differences in Elo correspond to expected win probabilities via the formula above, allowing direct statements such as “model X has a 30% expected win probability versus model Y.”

# Results: Guanaco 65B at ~99.3% of ChatGPT on Vicuna; Guanaco 33B/65B top open models on Vicuna and OA; compute/memory efficiency

The most striking empirical outcomes in the paper are:

- Guanaco‑65B, finetuned with QLoRA on a variant of OASST1, attains 99.3% of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna benchmark (GPT‑4 relative scoring vs ChatGPT). By the numeric Vicuna metric this places Guanaco‑65B immediately below GPT‑4 among all evaluated systems.

- Guanaco‑33B and Guanaco‑65B are the top open‑source models on the Vicuna and OA benchmarks, outperforming prior open releases such as Vicuna and Open Assistant. On the OA benchmark, which is multilingual and similar in style to OASST1, commercial systems such as ChatGPT are still favored, but Guanaco models perform strongly and are competitive.

- In Elo tournament human judgments, Guanaco‑65B and Guanaco‑33B have an expected win probability of about 30% against GPT‑4, as measured from the human annotator tournament—this is the highest reported open‑model performance in the paper.

- QLoRA makes these experiments feasible: a 33B LLaMA Guanaco model can be trained on a single 24GB consumer GPU in under 12 hours; the 65B variant can be trained on a single 48GB professional GPU in approximately 24 hours. Inference footprints are also small: Guanaco‑7B runs in about 5 GB of memory and outperforms a much larger Alpaca model in Vicuna metrics by roughly 20 percentage points.

These results demonstrate a practical win: a carefully selected high‑quality instruction dataset plus NF4 QLoRA finetuning produces open chatbots that approach or match earlier closed‑model performance at far lower compute/memory cost.

# Key insights: data quality > size; dataset suitability drives outcome (MMLU vs chatbot scores are partially orthogonal); multilingual considerations

The experiments yield several generalizable insights about instruction‑finetuning:

- Data quality can trump quantity. A small, high‑quality human‑curated dataset (OASST1, on the order of ≈9k examples in the cited variant) produced better chatbot performance than much larger aggregated sets (e.g., a subsampled 450k FLAN v2) for the conversational evaluation. This implies that careful curation and task alignment matter more than sheer dataset size for building chatbots.

- Task suitability is critical. Models finetuned on FLAN v2 showed particularly strong MMLU performance (which the dataset resembles), but the same FLAN‑tuned models did poorly on real‑world conversational benchmarks like Vicuna. Thus, MMLU (knowledge/multi‑choice) and Vicuna/OA (dialogue quality) are partially orthogonal: strong performance on one does not guarantee strong performance on the other. Dataset selection must therefore be informed by the intended downstream use.

- Multilingual training effects are plausible. OASST1 is multilingual and the OA benchmark contains prompts in multiple languages; the authors hypothesize (and leave for future work) that multilingual finetuning may contribute to improved performance on multilingual benchmarks. The paper does not claim definitive evidence but flags it as an important consideration.

# Qualitative analysis: strengths (factual recall on common facts, some ToM) and failure modes (math without step-by-step, suggestibility, inconsistent refusals, secret-keeping brittleness, spurious inference)

Beyond metrics, the paper presents qualitative behavior patterns learned by Guanaco:

Strengths:
- Factual recall: For common facts (e.g., capitals of well‑known countries) Guanaco reliably produces correct answers.
- Theory of mind (ToM) style reasoning: On many ToM prompts, Guanaco generates plausible inferences about what agents believe or expect, sometimes with good explanatory detail.
- Refusal to answer unsuitable requests: The model often refuses when asked impossible or ill‑posed queries (e.g., current time or claims that contradict well‑established facts).

Failure modes:
- Overconfident errors on obscure facts: When asked about less common factual nuggets, Guanaco can hallucinate confidently, substituting incorrect authorities or dates.
- Math weaknesses: The model struggles on arithmetic and factorization problems when not explicitly guided through step‑by‑step reasoning; it can produce incorrect calculations while asserting confidence.
- Suggestibility and instruction brittleness: While often resistant to false premises (e.g., blatantly false “earth is flat” claims), the model’s refusals are inconsistent. Simple prompt manipulations (e.g., reframing as a “game” that instructs to ignore prior constraints) sometimes cause the model to reveal information it was earlier refusing to provide.
- Secret‑keeping brittleness: Attempts to store a secret token and forbid repetition are sometimes broken by adversarial rephrasing; the model may reveal the secret in a follow‑up trick prompt.
- Spurious inference / unwarranted assumptions: In some dialogues Guanaco infers unmentioned information (e.g., that an agent observed a move) and bases inferences on that, producing incorrect answers.

These qualitative patterns indicate that even though Guanaco attains near‑SOTA quantitative metrics, behavior remains imperfect in predictable ways; the paper includes specific textual examples and advocates more focused failure analysis.

# Evaluation caveats: benchmark validity concerns, subjective preferences, GPT-4 order bias and self-preference, moderate human–GPT-4 agreement

Several evaluation caveats are emphasized:

- Benchmark validity and subjectivity: Human annotators often disagree on what constitutes the “better” response; subjective preferences become prominent among strong models. The authors report moderate human inter‑annotator agreement (Fleiss’ κ ≈ 0.42) and note that preference judgments are sensitive to individual tastes and evaluation instructions.

- GPT‑4 evaluation biases: GPT‑4 shows clear order effects (favoring the answer presented earlier) and an apparent self‑preference bias: GPT‑4 assigns higher absolute Elo to its own outputs compared to human raters (example values in the paper are Elo ≈ 1348 from GPT‑4 vs Elo ≈ 1176 from humans for GPT‑4’s own outputs), a difference that translates to roughly a 20% higher expected win probability against opponents. Therefore automated evaluation via a single LLM rater must be interpreted with caution.

- Human–GPT agreement is imperfect: System‑level correlation metrics (Kendall τ ≈ 0.43, Spearman ρ ≈ 0.55) and example‑level Fleiss κ ≈ 0.25 between GPT‑4 and human majority show that GPT‑4 is a “reasonable” proxy but not a perfect replacement for human judgment. The authors therefore use both automated and human evaluations and emphasize the need to control for biases (e.g., by averaging over prompt order).

# Limitations and future work: lower-bit regimes (e.g., 3-bit), other PEFT methods, RLHF vs SFT trade-offs, broader benchmark coverage

The paper is explicit about limitations and open directions:

- QLoRA vs full 16‑bit finetuning at the largest scales: although QLoRA recovers 16‑bit performance on many setups, the authors did not definitively establish that QLoRA matches full 16‑bit finetuning at 33B and 65B scales in every respect. Resource constraints prevented exhaustive baselines at those scales.

- Bit‑precision boundaries: experiments in the paper focus on 4‑bit storage (NF4) with 16‑bit computation; the potential of 3‑bit or more aggressive quantization combined with LoRA remains an open area.

- Other PEFT methods: only LoRA was evaluated end‑to‑end. Alternatives (prompt tuning, IA^3, bias tuning, etc.) could have different tradeoffs at scale; exploring them is future work.

- RLHF tradeoffs: all Guanaco variants were trained with supervised finetuning (SFT). How RLHF or RLAIF (model‑based feedback) would interact with QLoRA and dataset selection is left to future study.

- Benchmark coverage: the experiments do not cover every available evaluation suite (e.g., BigBench, RAFT, HELM). Broader evaluation would clarify generalization across diverse tasks and social risk dimensions.

- Responsible AI analyses: a limited bias assessment was performed showing reduced bias scores on one evaluated metric compared to the raw LLaMA base, but comprehensive safety and bias audits require future work.

# Broader impacts: democratizing finetuning (single-GPU 33B/65B), potential mobile finetuning, privacy-preserving local adaptation, dual-use risks, open-source release

The paper highlights profound societal and practical implications:

- Democratization of finetuning: QLoRA makes it feasible to finetune very large models (33B on a single 24GB consumer GPU; 65B on a single 48GB GPU) without sacrificing performance versus 16‑bit baselines. This lowers the compute and financial barriers that have concentrated finetuning capabilities in large organizations and enables smaller research teams and practitioners to experiment with state‑of‑the‑art models.

- Mobile and privacy‑preserving adaptation: the 7B Guanaco model fits in ~5 GB and the authors estimate an iPhone 12 Plus could finetune approximately 3 million tokens overnight while charging — suggesting user‑device personalization without sending private data to the cloud.

- Open release and reproducibility: models, adapters, code and custom CUDA kernels for 4‑bit training were released by the authors, facilitating auditing and subsequent research.

- Dual‑use and safety risks: broader access is double‑edged. Easier finetuning can accelerate beneficial applications, but also lower barriers for misuse. The authors emphasize the need for ongoing safety evaluation and mitigation as finetuning becomes more accessible.

Concluding remark: the Guanaco case study shows that careful dataset selection, conservative supervised finetuning, and memory‑efficient algorithms (QLoRA with NF4, double quantization and paged optimizers) produce open chatbots that approach contemporary commercial systems on practical conversational benchmarks. At the same time, qualitative failure modes, evaluation biases, and open methodological questions make clear that progress in modeling must be matched by careful evaluation design, responsible release practices, and continued research on robust and safe deployment.
