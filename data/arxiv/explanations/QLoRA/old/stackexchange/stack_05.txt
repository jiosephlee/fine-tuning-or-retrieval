### How does double quantization dequantize in practice and what are the exact formats used?
Question:
Double quantization stores $\mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}$ with per-block $c_2$, and then quantizes $c_2$ into $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ with per-block $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. In the forward pass, we compute $\text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}})$. Which FP8 format is used (e4m3, e5m2, or something else), and is it consistent across hardware? Are the $c_1$ blocks aligned with groups of $c_2$ blocks (size 256), and do you broadcast the recovered $c_2$ to the corresponding 64-size weight blocks?
Answer:
In QLoRA’s “double quantization” the idea is simply to quantize your 4-bit scale factors (the $c_2$ that you would normally store in FP32) down to 8-bit floats, and then keep a second tiny FP32 scale $c_1$ per group of them so you can exactly recover back to FP32 at runtime. Here are the key facts, all drawn straight from the paper plus the bitsandbytes implementation:

1) Which FP8 format?  
We use a custom software FP8 with 1 sign bit, 4-bit exponent and 3-bit mantissa (often called “E4M3”). This is the same FP8 format that bitsandbytes ships in its CUDA kernels. It is *not* a hardware-native NVIDIA FP8, but rather a deterministic pack/unpack in our library, so the results are identical whether you’re on A100, RTX, or even fall back to a CPU build.

2) How are the $c_1$/$c_2$ blocks laid out?  
- First pass: you quantize your weights $\mathbf W$ in blocks of size 64. This produces one FP32 scale $c_{2,i}$ for each 64-wide weight block, plus the 4-bit codebook $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}$.  
- Second pass (double-quant): you take each run of 256 such scales $\{c_{2,i}\}_{i=1\ldots256}$ and you compute a single FP32 “meta-scale” $c_1$ so that when you multiply the 256 floats by $c_1$ you exactly span the dynamic range of your E4M3 format. You then round each of those 256 (zero-meaned) floats into an 8-bit E4M3 number $c_{2,i}^{\rm FP8}$.

Thus you end up storing per 256 blocks of weights:  
 • one $32$-bit float $c_1$,  
 • $256$ bytes of $c_2^{\rm FP8}$,  
 • and the $64\times4$-bit weights $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}$.

3) Dequantization at runtime (“doubleDequant”):  
   a) You up-cast the 256 E4M3 values $c_{2,i}^{\rm FP8}$ back into FP32 and *divide* by the shared $c_1$. That gives you back the original FP32 scales $\{c_{2,i}\}$ for each 64-wide weight block.  
   b) You then take each 4-bit block $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}_i$ and divide by its recovered $c_{2,i}$ to get a BF16 (or FP16) block of real weights.

Because of the way we grouped scales, the single $c_1$ is naturally aligned with every contiguous run of 256 of the $c_2$ blocks, and each recovered $c_{2,i}$ is implicitly broadcast over its corresponding 64-element weight block.  

In code this looks like
$$
   \mathbf Y = \mathbf X\,\bigl[\underbrace{\mathrm{dequant}\bigl(\,
     \tfrac{\mathrm{upcast}(c_2^{\rm FP8})}{c_1}\,,\,\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}\bigr)}_{=\;\mathbf W^{\rm BF16}}\bigr] + \cdots
$$
and under the hood your bitsandbytes kernels handle exactly those two steps of up-casting the E4M3 $c_2$ array in chunks of 256, dividing by $c_1$, and then doing the usual 4-bit→BF16 dequant on $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}$.  

Because the FP8 conversion is purely in software (bitsandbytes), it is bit-identical across GPU generations and even CPU fallbacks, and because we chose E4M3 for its better mantissa precision around 1.0, you get no measurable loss in finetuning quality.
