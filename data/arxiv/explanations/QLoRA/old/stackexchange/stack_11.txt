### 3. Costs and benefits

• Memory overhead.  If you make **every** block’s scale $c$ trainable, you need to keep a gradient accumulator for it.  A 65 B-parameter model with block-size 64 has on the order of a billion such blocks—an extra few gigabytes just in optimizer state.  In contrast the NF4 code-book itself is only 16 floats, which is basically free.  
  
• Instability.  Letting scales float can lead to collapse (all $c\to0$ or $c\to\infty$) or drift, so you’d need careful regularization or clipping.  QLoRA sidesteps all that by fixing the scales to the pre-computed optimal values for a Normal$(0,\sigma)$ distribution.  
  
• Marginal benefit.  Empirically QLoRA already matches 16-bit finetuning performance, so the residual quantization error it leaves behind seems not to be the bottleneck.  In other words, most of the “action” is in the LoRA adapters, and letting the scales jiggle around buys you very little extra accuracy for a large engineering cost.
