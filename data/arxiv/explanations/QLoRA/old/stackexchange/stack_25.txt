### What is the exact algorithm for selecting responses in multi-response datasets like OASST1?
Question:
For datasets with multiple responses per prompt (like OASST1), the paper selects the top response at each conversation node. How is “top” determined—by human rating, upvotes, or some quality score? Does this introduce selection bias toward certain styles, and did you try training with all responses (e.g., sampled) to encourage diversity?
Answer:
In the QLoRA paper (and in the released Guanaco code), the authors treated OASST1 much like a branching “conversation tree” and simply pruned it down to a single linear dialogue by following what they call the “top” child at each user‐turn. Concretely, OASST1 stores for each user message $u$ a set of assistant replies $\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}$, together with metadata that indicates which reply was “accepted” by the original asker (and, in many cases, a vote‐score aggregating up‐ and down‐votes from the community). The pre‐processing script walks that tree and at every level picks the one reply that is marked accepted (or, if no explicit accept‐flag exists, the one with the highest vote score)—that becomes the “top” response. The next user turn is then appended to that branch, and so on until you flatten the whole multi‐turn session into one sequence of alternating user and “top” assistant messages.

Because the paper never reports any experiments in which they trained on *all* of the candidate replies (or sampled among them), it’s true that this procedure throws away any secondary or niche answers. In effect, you end up with a single “highest-quality” style for each prompt, which almost by definition reduces diversity. The QLoRA work does not explore mixing in the lower-rated replies (for instance by sampling proportional to their vote counts or randomly cycling through them), so we have no empirical answer on whether that would improve or hurt the final model. 

That choice was driven by two practical considerations emphasized throughout the paper: (1) *Data quality* mattered more than sheer volume—OASST1’s small but carefully curated branch of “accepted” answers already outperformed much larger instruction sets, and (2) keeping the training signal as clean as possible avoids confusing the model with lower-quality or contradictory responses. If your goal is to inject more stylistic variety or broaden the model’s expressed “voice,” you could certainly try retaining multiple replies per prompt—either by concatenating them, randomly sampling at train time, or by weighting them by their vote scores—but that falls outside the scope of the QLoRA experiments as reported. In short:

1. “Top” = the reply that OASST1 marks as *accepted* (or otherwise wins the internal vote score) at each node in the dialogue tree.  
2. Yes, by throwing away all but that single branch you introduce a selection bias toward the community-preferred or asker-selected style.  
3. The authors did *not* report any runs where they trained on *all* candidate replies (e.g. by sampling or weighting), so whether you can recover additional diversity without sacrificing quality remains an open avenue for further study.
