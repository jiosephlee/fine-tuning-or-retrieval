### How exactly is block-wise quantization applied: which dimension is blocked and how are blocks defined?
Question:
For weight matrices $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$, the paper uses blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for second-level scales. Are blocks contiguous in memory order after flattening, or aligned to rows/columns? If the tensor is transposed for GEMM efficiency (e.g., $W^T$ layout), does blocking follow the physical layout or the logical shape? How sensitive are results to the blocking orientation?
Answer:
In QLoRA, “block‐wise” quantization means exactly what it says – you take your weight tensor, flatten it into one long vector in whatever memory layout you are actually storing, and then chop that vector into consecutive chunks of size $B$ (64 for the 4-bit weights, 256 for the second-level FP8 scales). Each chunk gets its own scale (or double-quantized scale), so you never mix entries from two different blocks when you compute the absolute maximum or quantile bounds.

Concretely, if your weight matrix is stored in row-major order as  
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times o}$,  
you would flatten it as  
$$[W_{0,0}, W_{0,1}, \dots, W_{0,o-1}, W_{1,0}, \dots]$$  
and then form blocks of 64 consecutive numbers from that array. If you have re-arranged $W$ for GEMM into $W^T$ (say you store a transposed kernel for better cache locality), you still flatten the *actual* in-memory layout – in other words, you quantize along the physical ordering, not the “logical” $h \times o$ shape. The dequantization step simply undoes the same blockwise constants over exactly the same contiguous runs, so everything stays consistent.

As for sensitivity to the choice of blocking axis: the paper does not report an extensive ablation over, say, per-row vs per-column quantization, but by design the flattened approach treats every 64-element segment equally. Because transformer weight matrices tend to have roughly i.i.d. Gaussian-like distributions, you rarely get pathological outliers concentrated only in one logical dimension, so empirically they found that a flat, contiguous blocking of size 64 for the 4-bit NF4 weights (and 256 for the FP8 scales) gives a good balance of precision vs memory without being finicky about whether you’ve swapped rows and columns. In short, blocks are defined by the physical, flattened order of your tensor in memory, and for large-scale LLMs this choice is robust: changing the orientation (row-blocking vs column-blocking) would shift *which* 64 elements share a scale, but not the overall quality once you’re using NF4 + Double Quantization at that blocksize.
