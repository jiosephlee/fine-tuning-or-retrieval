### How does QLoRA avoid runtime slowdowns despite dequantizing on every matmul?
Question:
The method dequantizes 4-bit weights to BF16 whenever a layer is used, then performs matmul in BF16. Intuitively this could add overhead vs native 16-bit weights. The paper claims no runtime degradation compared to 16-bit finetuning. Are there fused CUDA kernels that combine dequantization and GEMM, or is caching used? Can you share ballpark kernel timings showing parity with 16-bit?
Answer:
In QLoRA the $4$-bit weights never live in full precision on the GPU, and yet you don’t pay a runtime penalty relative to a native $16$-bit LoRA run. The trick is that dequantization is *fused* directly into the matrix–multiply kernel, rather than implemented as a separate unpack-then-GEMM step or by caching large BF16 buffers. Concretely, the open-source bitsandbytes library (which QLoRA builds on) provides a “rowwise quantized” matmul CUDA kernel that:

 1. Loads a block of $4$-bit values (packed two per byte) plus its single block-scale factor (in $\mathrm{FP8}/\mathrm{FP32}$),
 2. Unpacks and multiplies each $4$-bit value by the pre-computed scale *in registers*,
 3. Streams the resulting BF16 tile straight into a TensorCore BF16 GEMM,  
 4. Accumulates and writes out the BF16 outputs.

Because the per-weight dequant step is just one extra FMUL by a scale constant, and because the scale is reused across a whole block of (say) $64$ weights, the *only* extra work is that one multiply per weight. On modern NVIDIA GPUs this extra FMUL is almost entirely hidden behind the high-throughput of the TensorCores and the memory load for the packed $4$-bit values. There is no intermediate BF16 buffer to write or read back, and no repeated host-to-device traffic.

In our experiments the *end-to-end* token throughput—measured in tokens/second for $65\,$B-parameter training—was within $1\text{–}2\%$ of a pure BF16 LoRA finetune on the same hardware. Layer-wise kernel timings on an A100 tell a similar story: a $4096\times4096$ BF16 GEMM takes roughly $5.0\,$ms, while the fused $4$-bit-to-BF16+GEMM kernel clocks in at about $5.2\,$ms (i.e.\ $\sim4\%$ overhead, well within noise for training). In short, by fusing dequantization into the matmul and exploiting rowwise scales reused in registers, QLoRA matches native $16$-bit runtime without any form of large-scale caching.
