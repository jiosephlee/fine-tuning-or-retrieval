According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', if $3$-bit base quantization plus LoRA worked as hypothesized, the most likely resource outcome would be a smaller memory footprint. In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', conceptually, the role of the quantization constant $c$ in mapping between low-bit and high-bit representations is to act as a scale factor. This is explicit in their quantization and dequantization definitions, where a high-precision tensor is mapped into a low-bit range by scaling and rounding, e.g., $\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{Int8}}=\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{127}{\mathrm{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{FP32}})}\,\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{FP32}}\right)=\mathrm{round}\!\left(c^{\mathrm{FP32}}\cdot \mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{FP32}}\right)$, and the inverse mapping divides by the same factor, $\mathrm{dequant}(c^{\mathrm{FP32}},\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{Int8}})=\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{Int8}}/c^{\mathrm{FP32}}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{FP32}}$, so $c$ directly scales between the real-valued dynamic range and the discrete codebook; in block-wise quantization each block $i$ has its own $c_i$ to locally rescale the block and avoid outlier underutilization, and in QLoRA’s double quantization the idea is extended with a two-stage scaling, where first-level weight codes $\mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}$ are dequantized using a (quantized) scale $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}$ that itself is dequantized by a higher-precision scale $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$, i.e., $\mathrm{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}},c_2^{\mathrm{k\text{-}bit}},\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{k\text{-}bit}})=\mathrm{dequant}(\mathrm{dequant}(c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}},c_2^{\mathrm{k\text{-}bit}}),\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{k\text{-}bit}})=\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{BF16}}$, which underscores that these constants function as hierarchical scale factors bridging storage (low-bit) and computation (high-bit) domains without changing the underlying information beyond the chosen precision.

In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', if paged optimizers were not employed during training with long sequences, the problem that would most likely recur is out-of-memory. This follows because QLoRA trains large models near the memory limit of a single GPU—reducing a 65B model’s finetuning footprint from >780 GB at 16-bit to <48 GB with 4-bit storage but still performing forward/backward in 16-bit—so transient activation and optimizer-state peaks can exceed available VRAM when sequence lengths are long; the authors explicitly note “memory spikes” during gradient checkpointing that occur when processing a mini-batch with long sequences, and introduce Paged Optimizers that allocate optimizer states in NVIDIA Unified Memory so they can be evicted to CPU RAM and paged back for the update step, preventing those spikes from causing OOM, whereas without paging the same spikes would overflow limited GPU memory and repeatedly trigger OOM errors during training.

In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', QLoRA dequantizes the stored weights to $\mathrm{BF16}$ before performing matrix multiplications to improve numerical precision. This is explicitly described in the method: QLoRA uses a 4-bit storage dtype (NF4) and a 16-bit computation dtype (usually BFloat16), so whenever a weight tensor is used it is dequantized to $\mathrm{BF16}$ and multiplied in 16-bit, as formalized by $\mathbf{Y}^{\mathrm{BF16}}=\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{BF16}}\ \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\mathrm{FP32}},c_2^{\text{k-bit}},\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}})+\mathbf{X}^{\mathrm{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_1^{\mathrm{BF16}}\mathbf{L}_2^{\mathrm{BF16}}$ and $\text{doubleDequant}(\cdot)=\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{BF16}}$, which ensures both forward and backward passes occur in 16-bit precision. Computing in $\mathrm{BF16}$ avoids the severe accumulation of rounding errors and dynamic-range issues that would arise from 4-bit arithmetic, preserves the fidelity of matrix multiplications and gradient propagation needed to optimize the LoRA parameters (since $\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\partial\mathbf{W}}$ is computed in 16-bit after dequantization), and empirically matches 16-bit finetuning performance; the paper repeatedly reports that this mixed setup (low-bit storage, 16-bit compute) recovers 16-bit baselines while dramatically reducing memory, with NF4 and double quantization affecting only storage, not the high-precision compute path.

According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', if the pretrained weights were uniformly distributed (e.g., $U(a,b)$) instead of normally distributed (e.g., $N(0,\sigma^2)$), $\text{NF4}$ would not be optimal. This is because NF4 is constructed by taking the $2^k$ quantile midpoints of a standard normal $N(0,1)$ and then normalizing into $[-1,1]$, yielding a codebook whose decision boundaries assign equal probability mass under a normal law and are therefore information-theoretically optimal for zero-mean normal weights after appropriate rescaling; in other words, its quantization levels minimize expected error for the assumed normal source by ensuring approximately uniform bin occupancy. If the true weight distribution is instead uniform, the normal-quantile codebook is misspecified: the induced bin probabilities under $U(a,b)$ are no longer equal, and the resulting rate–distortion is suboptimal compared to a quantizer tailored to $U(a,b)$ (e.g., a Lloyd–Max solution for a uniform source, which yields evenly spaced reconstruction levels and thresholds in $[-1,1]$). Since the paper’s optimality argument explicitly relies on the weights being “zero-centered normal” and on fixing the distribution up to a scale, a distributional mismatch (uniform rather than normal) breaks the equal-quantile premise and thus the information-theoretic optimality of NF4.

In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', NF4 is information-theoretically optimal under the implicit assumption about pretrained weights that they follow a $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$. This follows because NF4 is constructed by quantile quantization of a standard normal $N(0,1)$ to produce $2^k$ levels with equal probability mass per bin, which is the information-theoretically optimal codebook for a known distribution, and then normalizing these levels to the range $[-1,1]$ and rescaling weights so that their distribution matches this range; when the true weight distribution is approximately zero-mean Gaussian with variance $\sigma^2$—an empirical property of pretrained networks cited by the authors—this rescaling makes the weight quantiles align with the NF4 codebook, ensuring each bin has equal expected occupancy and thus maximizing entropy usage of the $k$-bit representation. Concretely, they compute quantizer levels $q_i=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(Q_{N(0,1)}(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1})+Q_{N(0,1)}(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1})\big)$, use an asymmetric split to include an exact zero, and apply block-wise absolute-maximum normalization so that weights drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ are mapped into the $[-1,1]$ support; under this normality assumption, quantile bins are equally populated, minimizing quantization error in an information-theoretic sense and yielding the observed empirical advantage of NF4 over FP4/Int4.

According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', when relying on paged optimizers, if long sequence mini-batches occur frequently rather than rarely, you should expect noticeable slowdowns. This follows from how paged optimizers leverage NVIDIA unified memory: when gradient checkpointing creates transient spikes in memory demand, optimizer state is automatically evicted to CPU RAM and later paged back to the GPU during updates, which entails repeated page migrations over relatively slow interconnects and page-fault handling; the authors report matching the speed of regular optimizers only in a regime where such paging “occurs when processing mini-batches with long sequence lengths, which is rare,” and explicitly note that future work should characterize when slow-downs arise, implying that if these long-sequence events—and thus paging—happen often, the cumulative overhead of frequent CPU–GPU transfers will dominate and reduce throughput.

In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', to handle rare memory spikes from long sequences, QLoRA's paged optimizers rely on a mechanism analogous to the standard operating system technique called memory paging. This is because the “Paged Optimizers” allocate optimizer states in NVIDIA Unified Memory, which performs automatic page-to-page transfers between GPU and CPU RAM when the GPU oversubscribes memory, evicting data to host memory and paging it back on demand during the optimizer update—explicitly described as working “like regular memory paging between CPU RAM and the disk.” In QLoRA’s setup, long-sequence mini-batches (especially under gradient checkpointing) cause transient activation/gradient spikes; by placing optimizer states in pageable unified memory, these spikes can be absorbed without out-of-memory errors, with the runtime cost minimized since paging is rare and localized to the spikes (the authors report equal training speed to regular optimizers for a LLaMA-65B run with batch size 16 on a 48 GB GPU). Thus, the approach mirrors OS-level demand paging semantics—temporary eviction and fault-driven reloading of memory pages—to seamlessly bridge short-lived GPU memory shortfalls.

According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', if you increase the blocksize used for storing weight scales to reduce per-parameter scale overhead without using Double Quantization, you are most likely to sacrifice quantization precision. The paper explains that block-wise quantization normalizes each block by its absolute maximum, i.e., for a block of size \(B\) with scale \(c=\frac{\alpha}{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{X}_\text{block})}\), and that a small blocksize is “required for precise 4-bit quantization” because outliers otherwise force \(\operatorname{absmax}\) to be large, compressing most values into a narrow range and leaving many bins underutilized, which increases quantization error; while increasing \(B\) does cut the scale-memory overhead from \(\frac{32}{B}\) bits/parameter, it aggregates more values (and potential outliers) per scale, amplifying this loss of precision, whereas their Double Quantization reduces the overhead to \(\frac{8}{64}+\frac{32}{64\cdot 256}\) bits/parameter without enlarging \(B\) and thus preserves the higher quantization fidelity achieved with small blocks and NF4’s optimal binning.

In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', the doubleDequant function performs an initial dequantization step that reconstructs supporting data from the first quantization level to enable the subsequent dequantization of the low-bit weight tensor $\mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}$ into $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$, namely the quantization constants. This follows directly from their definition of double dequantization: Double Quantization first stores the per-block quantization constants $c_2$ for the 4-bit weights in a low-precision form (e.g., FP8) together with higher-precision scales $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$, and then reconstructs the original scale by dequantizing $c_2^{\text{k-bit}}$ via $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$ before dequantizing the weights; formally, they define $\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$, which means the first dequantization reconstructs the blockwise quantization constants (the scales) needed to undo the 4-bit quantization of $\mathbf{W}$ in the second step and yield $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$.
