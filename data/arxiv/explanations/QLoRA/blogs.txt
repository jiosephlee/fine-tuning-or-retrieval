# QLoRA in Plain English: Fine-tuning 65B Models on a Single GPU

Large language models are huge — not just in parameters but in the hardware they traditionally require. The QLoRA paper shows a practical way to fine-tune models up to 65 billion parameters on a single 48 GB GPU while preserving the quality of full 16-bit fine-tuning. This post walks through the core ideas without getting lost in details, and explains why the trick works so well.

## The core idea, in one sentence
Store the pretrained model in very low precision (4-bit), keep those weights frozen, and backpropagate gradients through them into small, trainable LoRA adapters stored and computed in higher precision. The frozen, quantized base plus tiny adapters give you full 16-bit quality at a tiny fraction of the memory cost.

## Why this matters
Full 16-bit fine-tuning of a 65B model can need on the order of >780 GB of GPU memory. QLoRA reduces that to under 48 GB, enabling researchers and practitioners to fine-tune very large models on a single commodity (or single professional) GPU. Beyond memory, this lowers cost and time: the paper reports training a competitive chatbot family (Guanaco), and the best model reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT performance on the Vicuna benchmark after about 24 hours on one GPU.

Now the how.

## Building blocks

1. Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA)
   - LoRA keeps the large pretrained weight matrix W frozen and adds a small low-rank update. For a linear layer XW, the LoRA-augmented output is:
     $$
     Y = XW + s\;X L_1 L_2
     $$
     where $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ and $L_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$, and $r$ is small. Only $L_1, L_2$ are trained. This drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters and their memory for optimizer states.

2. 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4)
   - Standard low-bit formats (e.g., Int4, Float4) can perform poorly because neural weights have roughly normal distributions and are sensitive to outliers.
   - NF4 is a 4-bit data type designed to be information-theoretically optimal for zero-mean normally distributed weights. It assigns quantization bins according to quantiles of a standard normal, then rescales to match each block’s range. Empirically NF4 gives better post-quantization quality than FP4 or Int4 for LLM weights.

3. Double Quantization
   - When you quantize a matrix by blocks, you need to store a per-block quantization constant (scale). Those constants themselves add memory overhead.
   - Double Quantization compresses those per-block constants with a second quantization step (e.g., storing them in 8-bit floats with a larger block size), reducing the average bits-per-parameter spent on scale factors with negligible quality loss.

4. Paged Optimizers
   - Gradient checkpointing and optimizer states can create temporary memory spikes that cause out-of-memory errors.
   - QLoRA uses NVIDIA unified memory to page optimizer state between GPU and CPU as needed so occasional spikes don’t blow the GPU memory budget. This keeps training robust on a single GPU with limited RAM.

## How training actually works
- The base model weights are stored in 4-bit NF4 format. At runtime a block is dequantized to a higher-precision computation type (the paper uses BF16) for the forward and backward passes.
- Crucially, gradients are only computed for the LoRA adapter parameters. The frozen base weights are never updated, but gradients must flow through the dequantized weights into the adapters.
- Double dequantization is used to reconstruct BF16 weights from the stored NF4 plus the (compressed) scale constants when needed.
- Because adapter parameters and their optimizer states are tiny compared to the full weight matrices, memory stays small even for very large base models.

## Practical outcomes reported in the paper
- QLoRA reduces the memory requirement to fine-tune a 65B model from >780 GB (16-bit full fine-tuning) to <48 GB, enabling single-GPU fine-tuning.
- The authors trained a model family called Guanaco. Key results:
  - Guanaco-65B reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT performance on the Vicuna benchmark (as measured in their GPT-4–based automated protocol).
  - Training times are practical: some models were trained in around 12–24 hours on a single consumer/professional GPU.
- Extensive experiments (over 1,000 models) show NF4 + Double Quantization + LoRA recovers the performance of 16-bit fine-tuning across architectures and scales (7B to 65B), and NF4 outperforms naive FP4/Int4 quantization.

## Important practical details and caveats
- You still dequantize to a higher-precision compute type (BF16) for math. The storage is 4-bit; compute is higher precision.
- To match full fine-tuning quality, it’s important to place LoRA adapters broadly — the paper finds adding adapters to all linear layers (not only attention Q/V) is necessary in some setups.
- The method uses supervised cross-entropy fine-tuning in the reported chatbot experiments (no RLHF).
- Evaluation nuances: GPT-4 and human evaluation mostly agree on rankings but have systematic differences and biases (e.g., order effects when prompting GPT-4). The paper includes human and GPT-4 comparisons and discusses evaluation limitations.

## Why this technically works
- Most of the information in pretrained weights is captured well enough by the NF4 quantization for storage; the small LoRA adapters can express the fine adjustments needed to adapt the model for a task.
- Because gradients are only needed for the adapters, the big memory sink — optimizer state and parameter gradients for the full model — is avoided.
- The combination of a quantization format tailored to weight distributions (NF4), reduced overhead for scale factors (double quantization), and pragmatic memory management (paged optimizers) enables the otherwise impossible engineering trade-off: very low storage precision with high-precision training outcomes.

## Final takeaway
QLoRA is an engineering and algorithmic recipe that makes large-scale fine-tuning accessible: store the big model in 4-bit NF4, keep it frozen, add tiny trainable adapters, and carefully compress quantization metadata while using unified memory for optimizer states. The result is high-quality fine-tuning of 65B models on a single 48 GB GPU — a substantial democratization of large-model tuning that preserves the performance of full 16-bit tuning in practice.

If you want to try it, the paper’s authors released code and CUDA kernels (the repository links are in the paper), and they integrated support into common tooling so the method is reproducible.



# Meet NF4: The 4-bit format that keeps big models smart

Large language models have millions to trillions of parameters, and storing those weights in 16- or 32-bit floats is expensive. Quantizing weights to fewer bits is a powerful way to save memory, but naïve low-bit formats (e.g., Int4 or simple 4-bit float variants) often lose important precision and hurt model quality—especially for inference and training. The QLoRA paper introduces a practical 4-bit data type called NormalFloat4 (NF4) that leverages a simple empirical fact about neural network weights: they are approximately normally distributed. NF4 packs weights into 4 bits with much less information loss than other 4-bit formats, and the paper shows it is a key ingredient in finetuning quantized 65B models without degrading performance.

This post explains the intuition behind NF4, how it’s constructed, why preserving an exact zero matters, and what the experiments in the paper demonstrate about NF4 versus FP4/Int4.

## Why “normal” helps

Across many pretrained models, individual weight tensors are roughly zero-centered and bell-shaped (i.e., Gaussian-like). If you know your data follows a distribution like \(N(0,\sigma^2)\) up to a scale factor, you can design a quantization scheme that assigns the finite set of representable values to regions of the distribution so each representable value covers an equal probability mass. That is precisely the principle behind quantile quantization: choose representation points so the expected number of original values assigned to each quantization bin is the same.

Quantile quantization is information-theoretically attractive: for a known distribution, it minimizes expected quantization error under certain senses. For a normal distribution, those optimal quantiles are known analytically via the normal CDF / quantile function. NF4 leverages this by designing a 4-bit data type whose discrete representable values match the quantiles of a standard normal distribution (then rescaled to the tensor's actual scale).

Because the NF4 value points are matched to the typical shape of weight histograms, the representation uses the limited 16 value slots of 4 bits much more effectively than a uniform linear grid (as in Int4) or a tiny FP-like format that wasn’t tuned to the normal shape.

## How NF4 is built (at a high level)

The NF4 construction follows three steps:

1. Start from the theoretical standard normal \(N(0,1)\). Estimate the quantiles over that distribution that partition it into \(2^k\) bins (for 4 bits, \(k=4\), so 16 bins). A simple way to get a representative value per bin is to take the mid-point between adjacent quantiles.

   More formally, for bin indices \(i\), compute representative values
   \[
   q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\Big(Q_{N(0,1)}\!\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big) + Q_{N(0,1)}\!\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big),
   \]
   where \(Q_{N(0,1)}(\cdot)\) is the normal quantile function.

2. Normalize those representative values to the target range (the paper picks an arbitrary range like \([-1,1]\) for the datatype) so the discrete NF4 codebook matches that range.

3. When quantizing a real weight tensor, first rescale the tensor by its absolute maximum (or equivalently adjust the tensor’s standard deviation) so its distribution fits the NF4 range, then assign each element to the nearest NF4 representable value.

Because the codebook is derived from the normal distribution, the bins correspond to equal-probability slices under a normal prior, which reduces quantization error for normally-distributed weights.

## Why zero handling matters

A practical issue for symmetric quantization schemes is representing zero exactly. Neural nets commonly contain many exact zeros (padding, sparse patterns, or values that are truly zero after pruning or special processing). If your discrete set of representable values does not contain an exact 0, then zeros get rounded to some small nonzero representable value, which introduces deterministic and avoidable error.

To address this, NF4 is constructed asymmetrically so that zero is an explicit representable codepoint. The paper does this by separately estimating quantiles for the negative and positive halves, then merging them and ensuring one exact zero codepoint remains. This keeps zero-cost quantization error at zero and also uses all 16 codes effectively.

## How NF4 compares to FP4 and Int4

- Int4 (uniform integer quantization) spreads the 16 representable values linearly across the tensor range. This wastes many levels in the tails for normally distributed data (most mass is near the center), so central values are coarsely represented compared to a quantile-aware scheme.

- FP4-like formats (tiny floating formats) give more dynamic range than core Int4 but are still not tailored to a normal distribution’s shape. They can do better than linear Int4 for wide dynamic ranges, but they do not allocate representable points to match the empirical density.

- NF4 explicitly matches the normal-shaped density, allocating many representable values near zero and fewer in the tails. That leads to lower average quantization error for typical weight tensors.

The QLoRA paper reports empirical comparisons across models and sizes (OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, Pythia; 125M to 13B) and finds NF4 consistently improves post-quantization perplexities and zero-shot accuracies compared to FP4 and Int4. In short: NF4 quantizes weights to 4 bits with less damage to model quality.

## NF4 in the QLoRA pipeline

NF4 is one component of QLoRA, the full recipe that enables finetuning large (up to 65B) models on a single 48 GB GPU. In QLoRA, base model weights are stored in NF4 (plus a small set of per-block quantization constants), then dequantized to a 16-bit computation format (BFloat16) for forward and backward passes. The only parameters updated during finetuning are Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA), so gradients do not need to be accumulated for the quantized base weights. This combination preserves the numerical fidelity needed for successful finetuning while keeping memory tiny.

Two practical additions in QLoRA are Double Quantization (quantize the per-block scales to further save memory) and Paged Optimizers (use unified memory to avoid GPU memory spikes). But NF4 is the core reason the 4-bit representation itself is low-loss.

## What the experiments show

Key empirical claims in the paper that relate to NF4:

- NF4 yields better post-quantization perplexity and zero-shot accuracy than FP4 and Int4 across different models and sizes (125M–13B). Tables and figures in the paper show consistent gains.

- Using NF4 inside QLoRA, finetuning with 4-bit quantized base weights plus LoRA adapters matches the performance of 16-bit full-model finetuning and 16-bit LoRA finetuning on a range of benchmarks (GLUE, Super-NaturalInstructions, and MMLU). In particular, NF4 + double quantization recovers MMLU performance of 16-bit LoRA at scales up to 65B in the QLoRA setup.

- Empirically, NF4 is superior to a 4-bit floating format (FP4) by roughly 1 percentage point on MMLU in the LLaMA finetuning experiments, and NF4+double quantization does not degrade performance relative to using full-precision scales.

Taken together, the evidence in the paper indicates that when weights are approximately normal, designing the 4-bit codebook to match that distribution (NF4) is an effective way to reduce memory without compromising downstream accuracy.

## Takeaway

If your weight tensors look like bell curves (they usually do in pretrained LLMs), you can quantize them much more effectively by designing the low-bit codebook around that distribution. NF4 does exactly that: it is an information-aware 4-bit datatype tailored to normally distributed, zero-centered weights, includes an exact zero codepoint, and empirically preserves model quality better than FP4 or Int4.

In practice, NF4 is a practical enabler: it is a central ingredient in QLoRA, which demonstrates that 4-bit quantized weights plus adapter finetuning can match 16-bit finetuning performance even at very large scales—making high-quality finetuning of huge models feasible on dramatically smaller hardware footprints.



# Double Quantization, Explained: Compressing the scale of your scales

Quantization is the obvious lever for shrinking large neural networks: store weights in fewer bits and save memory. QLoRA goes further. It quantizes not only the model weights (to 4 bits using an NF4 scheme) but also the very numbers you use to map those low-bit values back to the original scale — the quantization constants. That second step is what the authors call double quantization, and it yields meaningful memory savings (about 0.373 bits per parameter in their setup) without measurable performance loss. This short post walks through what double quantization is, why it is safe, and how the arithmetic works — with a few simple analogies and numbers so you can see the impact on a 65B model.

## Quick refresher: block-wise quantization and quantization constants

When you quantize a floating tensor to a low-bit representation you typically:

1. Pick a block of parameters (e.g., B = 64 values).
2. Compute a scale or quantization constant c for that block (often based on the block's absolute max or some distribution-aware estimator).
3. Map the floating values to integer / low-bit bins by multiplying by that scale and rounding.

Formally, for a block we often write
- quantization: X^{Int} = round(c \cdot X^{FP32})
- dequantization: X^{FP32} = X^{Int} / c

That constant c is stored per-block. For small block sizes (needed for good precision) those c values add up in memory. For example, if c is stored in 32-bit float and blocksize is 64, the cost per parameter is 32 / 64 = 0.5 bits on average (because one 32-bit constant covers 64 parameters).

QLoRA uses a blocksize of 64 for the 4-bit weight tensors, so these quantization constants are a real contributor to memory footprint.

## The simple idea: quantize the quantization constants

Double quantization treats the per-block constants themselves as a tensor to be quantized. In other words:

- First quantize the weight block into a k-bit representation (e.g., NF4) with a 32-bit scale per block, call it c_2^{FP32}.
- Then quantize the array of c_2^{FP32} values into a lower-precision form (FP8 in QLoRA) plus a second-level scale c_1^{FP32} that lets you reconstruct c_2 when needed.

So instead of storing each c_2^{FP32} as 32 bits, you store a low-bit code (FP8) per block plus a second-level scale that itself is stored sparsely (one 32-bit c_1 per many blocks). This reduces the average bits-per-parameter devoted to scales substantially.

In QLoRA the authors use:
- Weight blocksize: 64 (for the NF4 weights).
- Quantize weight-block scales (c_2) to an 8-bit floating representation with blocksize 256 for the c_2 quantization.
- Subtract the mean from the c_2 values before quantization (they are all positive), enabling symmetric quantization around zero which improves use of the codebook.

Because the c_2 values are positive, centering them (subtracting the mean) helps make symmetric quantization more effective.

## How the arithmetic works (concise)

Double dequantization is just two steps:

1. Reconstruct the first-level scale c_2 from the second-level representation: c_2 = dequant(c_1^{FP32}, c_2^{k-bit}).
2. Dequantize the weight codes with that reconstructed scale: W^{BF16} = dequant(c_2, W^{k-bit}).

In formula form (as used in the paper):
- doubleDequant(c_1^{FP32}, c_2^{k-bit}, W^{k-bit}) = dequant(dequant(c_1^{FP32}, c_2^{k-bit}), W^{k-bit})

This lets the stored representation be very compact, but reconstructs the (approximate) full-precision scale at runtime when dequantizing weights for computation.

## A concrete bit accounting example (the paper's numbers)

Take the QLoRA configuration in the paper:

- Weight blocksize B_w = 64.
- Weight storage: 4 bits per weight (NF4).
- First-level scales (c_2) stored originally in FP32, one per block of 64 weights: cost = 32 / 64 = 0.5 bits per parameter.
- Double-quantize the c_2 values using FP8 codes with a second-level blocksize B_c = 256 and keep a c_1^{FP32} per second-level block.

After double quantization the cost per parameter for scale storage becomes:
- 8 bits per c_2 code covering 64 weights, but the c_2 codes themselves are grouped into blocks of 256 before a full-precision c_1 is stored. This gives an average cost of 8 / 64 + 32 / (64 * 256) bits per weight.

Compute that:
- 8 / 64 = 0.125 bits
- 32 / (64 * 256) = 32 / 16384 = 0.001953125 bits
- Sum ≈ 0.126953125 bits ≈ 0.127 bits per parameter.

Compare to the original 0.5 bits per parameter for full-precision c_2. The saving is
- 0.5 − 0.127 = 0.373 bits per parameter.

For very large models this adds up. On a 65B-parameter model, 0.373 bits × 65e9 ≈ 24.245e9 bits ≈ 3.03 GB. The paper reports the same ~3 GB saving estimate for a 65B model from double quantization.

All of this is done without measurable degradation in downstream performance when combined with the other QLoRA components (NF4 for weight codes, LoRA adapters for trainable parameters, and paged optimizers). In other words: you compress the things that are cheap to reconstruct (the scales), and you retain the performance-critical parts.

## Why this is safe / why it works

- The per-block scales c_2 are themselves numbers with structure — they tend to vary smoothly across blocks and are amenable to low-precision representation. Using a second-level quantizer (FP8 codes plus a large-block second-level scale) leverages that structure.
- The NF4 weight codes are already optimized for normal-like weight distributions, so the remaining precision budget is concentrated where it matters most: the per-weight codes. Slightly approximating the per-block scale (via FP8) introduces a small, structured error that can be recovered or tolerated by downstream computation and finetuning.
- QLoRA also adds LoRA adapters and performs finetuning through the frozen quantized weights. The adapters can compensate for small systematic reconstruction errors introduced by double quantization.

## Practical takeaway

If you are quantizing large models with small blocksizes (e.g., 64) the per-block scales are a non-negligible fraction of memory. Double quantization is a low-risk, high-reward trick: quantize those scale values themselves (e.g., to FP8, grouped with a larger blocksize for their own second-level scale), and you can recover several tenths of a bit per parameter. On the scale of a 65B model that translates to roughly 3 GB of saved memory for scales alone — meaningful when you are trying to fit finetuning workloads into a single 48 GB GPU.

QLoRA combines this with NF4 weight codes, LoRA adapters, and paged optimizers to provide a practical finetuning solution that retains full 16-bit finetuning performance while drastically lowering memory. Double quantization is one of the small engineering pivots that make that possible.

## Final note

Double quantization is conceptually simple but effective: quantize the thing that quantizes. When blocksize choices make your scales expensive, compress those scales with a second-level quantizer and reap real memory savings without sacrificing model quality.



# Beating Out-of-Memory Errors: How Paged Optimizers tame memory spikes

Finetuning very large language models on a single GPU is hard mostly because memory usage is spiky and unpredictable. QLoRA introduces several tricks to reduce steady-state memory (4-bit storage via NF4, Double Quantization for storage constants, LoRA adapters instead of full weight updates). But even after lowering the steady memory footprint, training can still hit short-lived but fatal out-of-memory (OOM) events — typically during gradient checkpointing when a long sequence or a particular mini-batch temporarily inflates GPU memory needs. The paper solves that last mile problem with "Paged Optimizers", a simple but powerful use of NVIDIA unified memory that makes these spikes survivable. This post unpacks how that works and why it matters.

## The problem: memory spikes during training

Two facts about LLM finetuning make memory behavior tricky:

- Most of the memory during training is not in the model parameters but in activation gradients, optimizer states, and temporary buffers used for backpropagation. Those can vary a lot with batch size and sequence length.
- Gradient checkpointing reduces peak activation memory by re-computing parts of the forward pass during the backward pass. That lowers average memory but makes memory usage burstier: during recomputation you temporarily need extra memory for activations and optimizer work.

That burstiness means small increases in sequence length or a particular mini-batch can push memory usage beyond GPU capacity and cause OOM errors, even if the average footprint would normally fit.

QLoRA already drastically reduces the baseline memory use by storing model weights in 4-bit NormalFloat and training only small LoRA adapters. But to reliably finetune 33B/65B models on a single 24/48GB GPU, the team needed a way to survive those intermittent spikes without sacrificing performance.

## The idea: use NVIDIA unified memory to page optimizer state

Paged Optimizers exploit NVIDIA unified memory (a CUDA feature) as a transparent paging layer between GPU memory and host RAM. The high-level idea:

- Allocate optimizer state (momentum, second-moment estimates, or other per-parameter buffers) in unified (paged) memory rather than strictly on GPU DRAM.
- When the GPU runs a step that requires more GPU memory than available, the CUDA runtime automatically evicts some pages from GPU memory to host RAM and brings them back when needed.
- During the optimizer update step (when those states are required on the GPU), the CUDA runtime pages them back in, so the optimizer still runs correctly.

In practice this means the large optimizer buffers are allowed to live mostly in host RAM and only occupy GPU memory when actively used. Short-lived activation spikes no longer cause OOMs because optimizer pages can be evicted to relieve the pressure.

Important details from the paper:

- Paged Optimizers are applied to optimizer state memory (not model weights). QLoRA keeps the base model stored in 4-bit and dequantizes to BF16 for compute; optimizer state would normally be large (e.g., 16/32-bit per-parameter).
- The CUDA unified memory mechanism does page-to-page transfers automatically between CPU RAM and GPU memory, analogous to virtual memory paging.
- QLoRA uses paged optimizers together with other memory reductions (NF4, Double Quantization, LoRA) to fit 33B/65B models on a single GPU.

## Why this helps QLoRA survive spikes

- Spikes are typically short: recomputation and backward passes need extra temporary memory only briefly. If the optimizer state is pageable, the runtime can evict optimizer pages to make room for those temporaries.
- By moving the large, relatively inactive memory (optimizer buffers) to pageable host RAM, the GPU can handle transient peak allocations without failing.
- After the backward pass, the optimizer needs its state for the update; unified memory pages those buffers back in automatically when the GPU accesses them.

The key point: you avoid OOM errors by letting the OS/CUDA driver decide which pages to keep on GPU and which to evict, instead of forcing everything to always live on the GPU.

## Performance: not free, but practical

Paging has costs: moving pages across the PCIe/NVLink bus is slower than staying on-device. The paper is careful about how it presents results:

- Paging only occurs when needed (i.e., during rare spikes). For many mini-batches there is no page movement and no runtime impact.
- The QLoRA authors report measured training speed for a 65B model on a 48GB GPU with batch size 16: paged optimizers provided the same training speed as regular (non-paged) optimizers. This implies that, in their setup, page transfers were infrequent enough or overlapped well enough with other compute that they did not slow training.
- The authors did not provide exhaustive timing benchmarks across many batch sizes and sequence lengths; they note that paging behaviour depends on workload and recommend more systematic study in future work.

So, in practice: paged optimizers make single-GPU finetuning far more reliable and allow researchers to run large-model experiments that would otherwise fail due to OOMs. For many realistic settings the speed cost is negligible, but the cost can vary with workload and hardware.

## How it fits into the full QLoRA recipe

Paged Optimizers are one piece of QLoRA’s memory-management stack:

- NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat) and Double Quantization reduce the storage footprint of model weights and quantization constants.
- LoRA adapters reduce the number of trainable parameters and keep weight updates small.
- Gradient checkpointing reduces steady activation memory but increases spikeiness.
- Paged Optimizers tame those spikes by paging optimizer state to host RAM when the GPU needs extra room.

Together these techniques let QLoRA finetune 33B models on a 24GB consumer GPU and 65B models on a single 48GB GPU while retaining 16-bit finetuning quality.

## Practical tips and caveats

- Paged optimizers are most useful when optimizer state is large relative to GPU memory and spikes are infrequent. If your workload constantly needs the optimizer state on GPU, paging will hurt performance.
- Be mindful of your hardware: paging performance depends on PCIe/NVLink bandwidth and host RAM speed. Systems with faster interconnects (NVLink, PCIe Gen5) will page faster.
- Keep other memory reductions (quantization, smaller blocksizes where appropriate, LoRA) in place—paged optimizers are the complement that handles spikes, not a substitute for reducing steady memory.
- Expect workload-dependent behavior. The QLoRA paper reports no speed penalty in their 65B/48GB experiment with batch size 16, but they recommend measuring runtimes for your particular setup.

## Limitations called out by the authors

The paper is transparent about limitations:

- They do not present extensive microbenchmarks showing exactly when and how much paging slows training across many configurations. Paging behaviour is rare in their runs, so detailed slow-down profiles were not provided.
- Paged Optimizers rely on the CUDA unified memory subsystem and the host–device interconnect; not all environments or older drivers will behave identically.
- The approach handles optimizer-state spikes but does not eliminate other potential OOM causes (e.g., unexpectedly large tensors in custom code).

## Bottom line

Paged Optimizers are a pragmatic, low-engineering-cost way to make single-GPU large-model finetuning robust. By allocating optimizer state in unified (paged) memory, QLoRA survives transient memory spikes from gradient checkpointing and long sequences without blowing past GPU memory limits. In the authors' experiments, that reliability came with no measurable speed cost on a 65B/48GB run, making paged optimizers a practical tool to combine with aggressive quantization and LoRA for accessible, large-scale finetuning.



# The LoRA Recipe That Matches Full Fine-tuning

Large language models (LLMs) are expensive to finetune. LoRA (Low-Rank Adapters) is a popular parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) method that injects small trainable low-rank matrices into linear layers while keeping the pretrained weights frozen. The QLoRA paper shows something important and actionable: the usual, minimal LoRA setup — attach adapters only to attention query and value projections — is often not enough to recover the performance of full 16-bit finetuning for large models. With the right placement and a few training tweaks, LoRA on a quantized 4-bit base model (QLoRA) can match 16-bit full finetuning performance across scales, up to tens of billions of parameters.

This post unpacks the paper’s key finding and gives practical guidance for getting LoRA to behave like full finetuning.

## The problem with “default” LoRA

The common LoRA practice is to add adapters only to the attention query and value projection matrices. This is attractive because it keeps the number of trainable parameters very small. But the QLoRA experiments show that, for large base models, this limited placement often fails to match full-model finetuning quality.

Why? The paper emphasizes two relevant points:

- Most of the memory footprint during finetuning comes from activation gradients (not from adapter parameters). That means you can afford to add more adapters without blowing up memory if you use techniques that reduce model storage (QLoRA’s 4-bit storage, Double Quantization) and activation-memory tricks (gradient checkpointing, paged optimizers).
- Limiting adaptation to a tiny subset of layers constrains the model’s capacity to adjust internal representations. For instruction-following and other finetuning objectives, that constriction prevents recovering the performance that comes from updating the full network.

In short: being stingy with adapter placement trades away representational flexibility that full finetuning provides.

## What worked in QLoRA: put adapters everywhere

The key change that recovered full finetuning performance was simple and robust: apply LoRA adapters to all linear layers across the transformer blocks, not just attention q/v. In the paper’s finetuning experiments (e.g., LLaMA on Alpaca), this broader placement was the most important LoRA hyperparameter to match 16-bit full finetuning.

Concretely:
- Add LoRA adapters to every linear projection inside transformer blocks (attention projections, feed-forward layers, etc.).
- Keep the base model weights frozen and train only the adapter parameters.

The paper reports that this change was crucial; other LoRA hyperparameters had much smaller effects by comparison.

## Which LoRA hyperparameters matter (and which don’t)

The paper’s experiments highlight the following observations about LoRA hyperparameters:

- Adapter placement (how many adapters / which layers get adapters): the dominant factor. Adding adapters to all linear layers is required to match full finetuning performance for large models.
- Projection rank r: surprisingly insensitive. The projection dimension r (the low-rank bottleneck size) does not materially affect whether you can match full finetuning — within the ranges tested — once you place adapters across layers. That implies you can pick modest r values and still recover performance.
- LoRA parameter memory vs activation memory: the LoRA parameter tensors themselves are small. For example, in a 7B model, LoRA parameters are tiny compared to activation gradients. Thus scaling up the number of adapters has minimal effect on total memory if you address activations and model storage separately.

So: prioritize adapter coverage first; tune r later if you need to squeeze parameter count.

## Training hyperparameters: batch size, LR, and scale-specific tweaks

The QLoRA paper also shows that baseline finetuning hyperparameters that work for smaller models are not necessarily optimal for larger scales. Two practical adjustments the authors used when scaling up:

- Number of epochs: the same number of epochs found at 7B generalized well to 13B and 33B.
- Learning rate and batch size: when moving to 33B and 65B, the authors halved the learning rate and doubled the batch size relative to the 7B settings that worked. In other words, larger models benefited from smaller LR but larger batch sizes.

These are pragmatic rules-of-thumb from the paper, not strict laws, but they illustrate that simple scale-aware adjustments can help LoRA match full finetuning.

## Why extending LoRA is affordable (memory perspective)

A common fear is that adding adapters to every linear layer will blow up GPU memory. The paper addresses this directly:

- Most finetuning memory is consumed by activation gradients and the base model parameters (storage formats), not by the LoRA parameter tensors. For a 7B LLaMA model on a single-sequence batch, the paper reports a LoRA parameter size of ~26 MB versus activation-gradient footprints in the hundreds of MBs (or more without checkpointing).
- QLoRA employs three techniques that make the “adapters-everywhere” approach feasible on single GPUs even for very large models:
  - 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization for storing pretrained weights (dramatically reduces model storage while preserving accuracy).
  - Double Quantization to shrink the memory needed for quantization constants.
  - Paged Optimizers that use system-level unified memory to avoid spikes during gradient checkpointing.
- Because the base model is stored in 4-bit and dequantized to BF16 for computation, and because the adapters are trained in BF16, adapter memory stays small while the heavy parts (the base model storage) are compressed.

Taken together, these steps let you put adapters everywhere and still finetune 33B models on a single 24GB consumer GPU and 65B models on a single 48GB professional GPU (per the paper).

## Practical checklist to match full finetuning with LoRA (based on QLoRA)

1. Apply LoRA adapters to all linear layers in the transformer blocks, not only attention q/v.
2. Use modest projection ranks r (the exact value is not critical once adapters are everywhere).
3. For large models:
   - Keep the same number of epochs that worked on smaller models as a starting point.
   - Halve the learning rate and double the batch size when moving from ~7B → 33B/65B in the paper’s experiments.
4. Use memory-saving techniques so you can afford broad adapter placement:
   - Store base weights in 4-bit NF4 (NormalFloat4) and dequantize to BF16 for compute.
   - Use Double Quantization to compress quantization constants.
   - Use gradient checkpointing and paged optimizers to avoid OOM spikes.
5. Train adapters in BF16 (as done in QLoRA) while keeping base model weights frozen.

## Takeaway

If your goal is to match full 16-bit finetuning quality with LoRA, the single most important change is adapter coverage: put adapters in all linear layers. Once you do that, you can recover full-finetuning performance with relatively modest choices for r, and using scale-aware LR and batch-size adjustments. The rest is engineering: efficient quantized storage (NF4, double quantization) plus memory-management (checkpointing, paged optimizers) makes the approach practical on single GPUs even for very large models.

The QLoRA paper demonstrates this recipe end-to-end and shows that, with these choices, LoRA can stop being merely parameter-efficient and instead become effectively equivalent to full-model finetuning in performance — at a fraction of the memory cost.



# Building Guanaco: An open chatbot that nearly matches ChatGPT in a day

## TL;DR
Guanaco is the demonstration chatbot family produced with QLoRA, a method that makes finetuning quantized large language models practical on a single GPU. Finetuned on the OASST1 instruction/dialog dataset, Guanaco reaches up to 99.3% of ChatGPT's performance on the Vicuna benchmark (Guanaco 65B). The work shows that (1) you can finetune very large models in low-precision without losing performance, (2) small, high-quality instruction data can outperform much larger but lower-quality datasets, and (3) fully useful Guanaco models can be deployed with footprints as small as ~5 GB (the 7B model).

Below I summarize how Guanaco was built, what the core results look like, and what the results imply for practitioners.

## Why Guanaco exists (and why QLoRA matters)
Training or finetuning big LLMs has traditionally required enormous GPU memory — full 16-bit finetuning of a 65B model can demand >780 GB. QLoRA changes the game by enabling backpropagation through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained model while only training small Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA) modules. The memory savings come from three main techniques: 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization, Double Quantization (quantize the quantization constants), and paged optimizers that avoid spike OOMs by leveraging unified memory.

The practical upshot: QLoRA reduces average finetuning memory so you can finetune a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU and a 33B model on a 24 GB GPU. That accessibility enabled the authors to iterate quickly and train the Guanaco models as open, competitive chatbots.

## What Guanaco was trained on
Guanaco models in the paper are finetuned primarily on a curated variant of OASST1 (Open Assistant), a crowdsourced dataset of multi-turn dialogues. For evaluation and comparisons, the authors also experimented with multiple instruction datasets (Alpaca, FLAN v2, HH-RLHF, Chip2, Longform, etc.) and found dataset suitability matters a lot.

Key training details (as presented in the paper):
- Finetuning used supervised cross-entropy (no RLHF).
- NF4 QLoRA with double quantization and paged optimizers was applied.
- Hyperparameters were tuned on smaller sizes and scaled (e.g., lower learning rate, larger batch size for 33B/65B).
- For multi-response conversation trees (OASST1, HH-RLHF), the "top" replies were selected and training included the conversation turns.

## Main results on Vicuna and related evaluations
Vicuna is a community benchmark of 80 prompts designed to test chatbot quality. The paper reports multiple evaluation modes: GPT-4 ratings against ChatGPT, pairwise comparisons judged by humans and GPT-4, and Elo-based tournament rankings aggregated from pairwise comparisons.

Highlights:
- Guanaco 65B achieves 99.3% of ChatGPT's score on the Vicuna benchmark when compared via GPT-4 scoring relative to ChatGPT.
- Guanaco 33B (4-bit weights) outperforms Vicuna 13B and is trainable on a 24 GB consumer GPU in under 12 hours.
- Guanaco 7B fits in ~5 GB memory for deployment and outperforms larger Alpaca baselines on Vicuna.
- Elo-style human evaluations place Guanaco 33B and 65B among the strongest open models, with both models showing competitive win-probabilities vs ChatGPT/GPT-4 (though GPT-4 remains top overall).

Important caveats the paper highlights about evaluation:
- GPT-4 ratings and human judgments generally agree on system-level rankings, but disagreements occur and sample-level agreement is weaker. For example, system-level Kendall Tau between human and GPT-4 ranks on Vicuna was moderate.
- GPT-4 shows an ordering bias (favoring the response shown first), so the authors average orders to mitigate that.
- Benchmarks measure different abilities: good MMLU scores don't necessarily imply strong chatbot performance (dataset suitability matters).

## The surprising power of small, high-quality data
One of the clearest empirical takeaways is that data quality often trumps quantity for instruction-following performance. In the experiments:
- A relatively small, well-curated subset like OASST1 (around 9k useful samples in some comparisons) outperformed larger but more heterogeneous instruction corpora (e.g., a subsampled FLAN v2 with ~450k samples) on chatbot benchmarks.
- Different instruction datasets produced different strengths: e.g., FLAN v2 helped MMLU more than chatbot metrics, while OASST1 improved interactive assistant performance.

The practical implication: if you want to finetune a chatbot, investing engineering effort into high-quality, dialogue-centered instruction data often yields larger gains than simply adding more noisier examples.

## Deployment footprints and latency considerations
Guanaco demonstrates that useful models can be compact for deployment:
- Guanaco 7B requires roughly 5 GB of memory and can be run on resource-constrained devices (mobile/edge) or inexpensive cloud instances.
- Larger Guanaco models (33B/65B) still benefit from 4-bit quantization for storage; training these models with QLoRA is feasible on single 24/48 GB GPUs, dramatically lowering the compute bar for building strong chatbots.

Note: the paper reports storage/runtime footprints and training time (e.g., 33B in <12 hours on a 24 GB GPU; 65B in ~24 hours on a single professional 48 GB GPU for their best model). Runtime trade-offs depend on hardware, batching, and paging behavior.

## Qualitative behavior: strengths and current failure modes
Guanaco models display many desirable chatbot behaviors but also familiar LLM weaknesses. The paper includes a concise qualitative analysis with representative examples:

Strengths
- Good factual recall for common knowledge (e.g., capitals).
- Robust refusals for impossible or nonsensical queries (“What time is it?”).
- Reasonable theory-of-mind reasoning in many prompts.

Weaknesses / failure modes
- Overconfident factual errors on obscure facts (confident hallucinations).
- Fragile refusals or "secret-keeping"—simple prompt manipulations can cause the model to reveal prior instructions.
- Math errors on even simple arithmetic or factoring when the model fails to decompose the problem or chain reasoning.
- Occasional unjustified assumptions in multi-agent reasoning.

These qualitative issues are consistent with broader findings in the field: finetuning improves many behaviors but does not eliminate hallucination, prompt-manipulation vulnerabilities, or arithmetic failure modes.

## What Guanaco shows for practitioners and researchers
- Practical finetuning of large models is now far more accessible. QLoRA enables single-GPU finetuning at scales that used to require large multi-GPU rigs.
- High-quality instruction/dialog data is a high-leverage knob. If you have limited labeling budget, prioritize dataset quality and closeness to your target interaction type.
- For model evaluation, rely on multiple methods: human annotation, calibrated automated raters (GPT-4), and pairwise/Elo evaluations to reduce scale-grounding and ordering biases.
- Quantization-aware finetuning with adapters (QLoRA + NF4 + double quantization + paged optimizers) can recover full 16-bit performance while drastically reducing memory.

## Limitations noted by the authors
The paper is careful to state limits:
- They did not exhaustively prove that QLoRA always equals full 16-bit finetuning at 33B/65B across all tasks, though experimental evidence strongly supports parity on benchmarks they ran.
- Evaluation coverage is not universal—some benchmarks (BigBench, HELM, etc.) were not evaluated.
- Responsible-AI checks are limited; while finetuning on OASST1 reduced some bias markers in their analyses, broader safety evaluations are needed.

## Where to get the models and code
The authors released code, models, and CUDA kernels (including 4-bit training kernels), and integrated their approach into common tooling. See the original repositories linked in the paper for reproducing Guanaco and QLoRA experiments.

## Bottom line
Guanaco is a practical demonstration that you can build an open, competitive chatbot quickly and cheaply if you combine: (a) aggressive but high-fidelity quantization + adapter finetuning (QLoRA), (b) a high-quality instruction/dialog dataset (OASST1), and (c) careful evaluation (human + automated pairwise/Elo). The result is a family of models that nearly match ChatGPT on a popular benchmark while being trainable on commodity GPUs and deployable with small memory footprints. This substantially lowers the barrier for building and iterating on useful chatbots.



# Why Data Quality Beats Size: MMLU vs Chatbot Performance

Large-scale finetuning studies often default to “more data is better.” QLoRA's experiments (dettmers et al.) flip that assumption on its head: dataset suitability and quality can be far more important than raw dataset size when you care about downstream behavior. The paper ran over 1,000 QLoRA finetuning runs across model sizes (7B–65B), model families (LLaMA, T5), and eight instruction-following datasets, and the results make the trade-offs crystal clear: a smaller, high-quality instruction dataset tuned for conversational behavior produced better chatbots than much larger, more general instruction corpora—while those larger corpora sometimes produced stronger performance on benchmarks like MMLU.

This post translates the core findings and practical takeaways for researchers and engineers deciding what to finetune on.

## The empirical pattern: MMLU ≠ Chatbot quality

Two consistent observations from the QLoRA study:

- Models finetuned on datasets similar to MMLU-like tasks (e.g., FLAN v2) generally get better MMLU scores, but they do not necessarily become better chatbots on human/GPT-4 judged conversational benchmarks (Vicuna, OpenAssistant).
- Conversely, finetuning on smaller, high-quality conversational datasets (notably OASST1, ~9k chosen examples in the paper’s setup) produced better chatbot behavior, even though those datasets were much smaller than alternatives like FLAN v2 (subsampled to 450k in the experiments).

Concrete examples reported:
- FLAN v2 finetuning improves MMLU performance but performed poorly on the Vicuna chatbot benchmark relative to some conversational datasets.
- OASST1, despite being much smaller, yielded top-performing Guanaco models on Vicuna and Elo-based chatbot evaluations; Guanaco 65B reached near-ChatGPT performance on Vicuna (99.3% relative to ChatGPT in that automated metric) and strong Elo rankings in pairwise comparison tournaments.

The takeaway: benchmark alignment matters. Large quantity of data that is misaligned to the target behavior will not substitute for well-aligned, high-quality examples.

## Why dataset suitability matters (mechanisms)

There are several overlapping reasons why a smaller, well-suited dataset can beat a larger, mismatched one:

- Task distribution shift. MMLU measures multi-task academic knowledge via few-shot multiple-choice queries. Chatbot quality is about turn-taking, instruction interpretation, safety/refusal behavior, conversational style, and pragmatic responses. A dataset that mirrors the target distribution produces models better-calibrated for that use case.
- Signal-to-noise ratio. Large scraped or aggregated corpora can contain inconsistent labeling, mixed objectives, or noisy instruction-response pairs. High-quality curated conversational data has clearer supervision for the chat objective.
- Format and interaction structure. Chat datasets often include multi-turn context, role annotations, and paired utterances that teach the model how to maintain context and follow dialogue moves—things generic instruction datasets may omit.
- Behavioral objectives not captured by simple loss. Chat behavior includes refusal, safety, and turn-management; these behaviors are emergent and require explicit examples to shape them. Cross-entropy over generative text only learns whatever is present in the finetuning distribution.

The QLoRA paper emphasizes that dataset suitability is “a determining factor” — strong MMLU does not imply strong chatbot performance and vice versa.

## What this means for choosing finetuning data

If your goal is a high-quality chatbot, prioritize dataset choices as follows:

1. Match the interaction format. Use multi-turn conversational data when you want multi-turn conversational behavior. Include role labels and representative context lengths.
2. Prioritize quality over raw size. Curated, human-reviewed examples that reflect target user intents and desired safety/clarity norms beat massive heterogeneous instruction corpora.
3. Include explicit examples of desired behaviors. If you want polite refusals, secret-keeping, or step-by-step reasoning, include such examples in the finetuning data rather than hoping the model will generalize from generic instruction pairs.
4. Evaluate with aligned benchmarks. Use evaluation data and procedures that reflect the intended behavior (pairwise comparisons, human raters, Elo-style aggregation) rather than relying on orthogonal academic benchmarks alone.
5. Be cautious about cross-task proxying. High performance on a widely used academic benchmark (e.g., MMLU) is a useful signal for certain capabilities, but it should not be taken as a catch-all proxy for production chatbot quality.

## Practical checklist when preparing instruction data

- Does the dataset contain multi-turn exchanges (if you need multi-turn behavior)?
- Are role labels and conversational cues present and consistent?
- Are safety and refusal cases represented explicitly?
- Are there high-quality, diverse examples representative of your target user base?
- Is the dataset noisy or heavily auto-generated (distilled) without human vetting? If so, consider manual curation or filtering.
- Does the evaluation protocol reflect real use (human pairwise comparisons or well-calibrated model-based judges)?

QLoRA’s experiments showed that a focused dataset (OASST1 in the paper) produced a state-of-the-art open chatbot (Guanaco family) that outperformed larger but less suitable datasets when judged for conversational quality.

## Evaluation implications: pick the right metric

The paper also flags limitations of common benchmarks. MMLU and Vicuna are measuring different axes. The authors recommend:

- Using pairwise, tournament-style evaluations (Elo) aggregated over many head-to-head comparisons to reduce scale grounding problems and subjective score variance.
- Complementing automated model-based evaluation (e.g., GPT-4 comparisons) with human raters; model judges are cheaper and correlate moderately, but they introduce biases (e.g., order effects, self-favoring).
- Being explicit about which facet of model behavior you’re optimizing for—knowledge, reasoning, conversational quality, safety, or task-solving.

## Final thought

QLoRA’s large-scale finetuning experiments make a practical, evidence-backed case: when the goal is a particular behavior (like chat), dataset suitability and quality matter more than the number of examples. For practitioners, that means designing and curating finetuning data with the target distribution in mind, and evaluating with aligned, robust metrics. Bigger isn’t better by default—better is better.



# Who Judges the Judges? Humans vs GPT-4 in model evaluation

Modern chatbot research increasingly relies on comparative evaluations instead of absolute scores. The QLoRA paper uses a tournament-style, Elo-based evaluation with both human annotators and GPT-4 judges, and reports a number of concrete lessons about reliability, bias, and how to get more signal for less cost. This post summarizes that setup, the key empirical findings, and practical recommendations for running cheaper—yet more trustworthy—evaluations.

## Tournament + Elo: the evaluation backbone

Rather than rating responses on a fixed numeric scale, the paper frames evaluation as head-to-head matches:

- For a chosen prompt, two systems each produce a response; a judge (human or GPT-4) picks the better response or declares a tie.
- These pairwise outcomes are aggregated into Elo ratings. Elo converts win/tie/loss frequencies into a single ranking number that reflects expected win probabilities.
- To reduce ordering artifacts, the paper samples many pairings and repeats the whole sampling process 10,000 times with different random seeds (they start Elo at 1000 and use K=32).

Why this is useful: pairwise judgments are simpler for annotators and reduce issues with absolute score anchoring (what does “7/10” mean?), and Elo naturally summarizes relative performance across many comparisons.

## What the paper found: humans vs GPT-4

The authors evaluated a collection of systems (including Guanaco variants, Vicuna, ChatGPT, GPT-4), using both human annotators (AMT) and GPT-4 as judges. Key empirical findings:

- System-level agreement between GPT-4 and humans is moderate. At the system (aggregate) level the rankings correlated with Kendall Tau τ = 0.43 and Spearman r = 0.55. That is, GPT-4 and humans generally agree on who is better, but not perfectly.
- Sample-level agreement is weaker. The agreement between GPT-4 and the human majority vote on individual pairwise comparisons is Fleiss κ = 0.25, showing substantial disagreement at the example level.
- Human inter-annotator agreement is moderate: Fleiss κ ≈ 0.42 for human annotators in this setup (pairwise comparisons / comparisons-to-ChatGPT).
- GPT-4 exhibits measurable biases:
  - Strong ordering effect: GPT-4 tends to give higher scores to the response that appears earlier in the prompt. The authors recommend averaging scores across both possible orders to mitigate this.
  - Self-preference: GPT-4 rated its own outputs noticeably higher than human raters did. In Elo terms, GPT-4’s self-assigned ratings were much higher (Elo ~1348) than human Elo for GPT-4 (Elo ~1176)—roughly a 20% higher expected win probability against opponents.
- Despite these biases and disagreements, GPT-4 can be a cheap, reasonable proxy for humans at the system level. The authors found that GPT-4 and humans largely agree on model ranking, making GPT-4 useful for large-scale model comparisons when human labeling is infeasible.

## What these results mean in practice

- Automated model-based evaluation (GPT-4 judging) is a pragmatic, lower-cost option for broad comparisons, but it is not a drop-in replacement for human judgment. Expect some examples where GPT-4 and humans disagree.
- Absolute scores from single raters (human or model) are noisy. Pairwise comparisons aggregated via Elo are more robust for ranking systems because they focus on relative quality and reduce scale-interpretation issues.
- Ordering effects and modality biases in model judges can distort results unless explicitly controlled.

## Practical tips for better, cheaper evaluations

Based directly on the QLoRA paper’s setup and findings:

1. Use pairwise comparisons and Elo aggregation
   - They reduce anchoring problems and produce interpretable expected win-rate metrics.
   - Repeat sampling across many random matchings and seeds to stabilize Elo estimates.

2. Randomize and average response order
   - GPT-4 showed a significant ordering bias. For every pair, evaluate both orderings (A before B and B before A) or at least randomize order and average across orders.

3. Combine GPT-4 and a small human audit
   - Use GPT-4 for large-scale ranking and reserve human annotators for:
     - Validating top-ranked systems and edge cases.
     - Estimating disagreement rate between GPT-4 and humans for your domain.
   - That gives most of the scale benefits of GPT-4 with a reality check from humans.

4. Report multiple agreement metrics and confidence
   - System-level correlations (Kendall Tau / Spearman) and example-level agreement (Fleiss κ) reveal different failure modes. Report both.
   - Include confidence intervals or bootstrapped Elo distributions (the paper repeats Elo sampling thousands of times).

5. Be cautious about domain mismatch
   - GPT-4 bias and disagreement rates may vary by prompt type (e.g., math, reasoning, creative writing). When possible, stratify comparisons by prompt category and check where model vs human agreement drops.

6. Collect explanations where possible
   - Having GPT-4 provide short rationales for its choices (as the paper did when comparing to ChatGPT) helps diagnose systematic biases or failure modes in the automated judge.

7. Track and disclose biases (e.g., self-preference)
   - If you use GPT-4 as both a judge and a model being evaluated, be aware of potential self-preference effects. Prefer independent judge models or ensure balanced sampling and human checks.

## Bottom line

The QLoRA paper demonstrates that GPT-4 is a useful, low-cost judge for large-scale model evaluation, especially for ranking at the system level. But automated judging is not perfect: it shows ordering effects, partial disagreement with humans, and a tendency to favor its own outputs. The practical middle ground is to use GPT-4 for most comparisons while validating conclusions with targeted human annotation, and to adopt tournament-style pairwise evaluations with Elo aggregation plus precautions like randomized ordering and repeated sampling. Doing so preserves much of the cost advantage of automated judges while keeping evaluations grounded in human preferences where it matters most.



# Hands-on Guide: Fine-tune LLaMA with QLoRA on a single GPU

This guide summarizes practical recommendations from the QLoRA paper so you can finetune LLaMA-style models on a single GPU. It focuses on setup (Transformers + bitsandbytes + QLoRA), dataset choices, recommended LoRA and training hyperparameters, how to handle long sequences and memory spikes, and realistic expectations for time, cost, and memory for 7B–65B models. Everything below is grounded in the QLoRA paper — no extraneous claims.

## Quick summary of what QLoRA gives you
QLoRA enables finetuning a frozen 4-bit quantized pretrained model while training small Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA) parameters in full precision. Key components to use:
- 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) storage for model weights (information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights).
- Double Quantization (quantize the quantization constants) to further reduce memory overhead.
- Paged Optimizers (use unified memory paging) to avoid OOM spikes during gradient checkpointing.
- Computation happens in 16-bit (BFloat16) after dequantizing NF4 storage; only LoRA parameters are updated.

Result: in the authors’ experiments you can finetune a 65B model on a single 48GB GPU and match 16-bit finetuning performance.

## Environment and packages
The paper uses standard Hugging Face Transformers plus custom CUDA kernels and bitsandbytes. To replicate QLoRA-style finetuning, you should have:
- transformers (Hugging Face)
- bitsandbytes (for low-bit kernels; see the QLoRA / bitsandbytes repositories linked in the paper)
- the QLoRA repository (includes CUDA kernels and integration with transformers)
- a CUDA-capable GPU with a compatible driver and enough host RAM for paging when using Paged Optimizers

Install the packages from their official repos (the paper releases code and kernels — follow the readme in the QLoRA and bitsandbytes repos). The QLoRA code integrates into the transformers stack and provides NF4 and double-quantization kernels.

Note: QLoRA uses NF4 storage and dequantizes to BF16 for computation, so ensure your runtime supports BFloat16 or an equivalent 16-bit compute mode.

## Picking datasets (what worked in the paper)
The authors experimented with eight instruction-following datasets. A few takeaways to guide dataset selection:

- High quality beats quantity. A relatively small, high-quality dataset (OASST1, ~9k selected samples in their setting) outperformed much larger noisy collections for chatbot performance.
- Dataset suitability matters. Some datasets (e.g., FLAN v2) help bench tasks like MMLU but perform worse for chatbot-style evaluation (Vicuna/OASST1). Choose data that matches your target behavior (instruction-following chat vs. broad multitask knowledge).
- Datasets used in the paper (examples you can try):
  - OASST1 (OpenAssistant) — excellent for chat/instruction tuning; authors used it for their Guanaco models.
  - Alpaca — distilled instruction-following data.
  - FLAN v2 — good for MMLU-style tasks (massive multitask), but not necessarily best for chat quality.
  - HH-RLHF, Chip2, Longform, UnnaturalInstructions, Self-Instruct — other sources used in ablations.

When a dataset contains multi-turn conversations (OASST1, HH-RLHF), the authors finetuned on the selected conversation trees (they used the top response paths). For datasets with single instruction/response pairs, finetune on the response (with the instruction in the prompt as appropriate).

## LoRA placement and capacity
A crucial practical insight from the paper: default LoRA setups (only query/value matrices) did not match full finetuning performance. To match 16-bit results you should:

- Place LoRA adapters on all linear layers in transformer blocks (i.e., everywhere in the model where linear projections occur), not just attention Q/K/V.
- The projection rank r (the internal LoRA dimension) is less sensitive; performance is more tied to covering all linear layers than to exact r values in their experiments.
- Since LoRA parameters are small compared to activations, enabling adapters in all layers is affordable memory-wise when using 4-bit base weights.

This strategy was key to recovering full finetuning accuracy with QLoRA.

## Recommended hyperparameter ranges and adjustments
The paper provides practical hyperparameter guidance based on their searches:

- Learning rate: search across 1e-6 to 5e-5 for 7B-scale models. For larger models (33B, 65B) they found good results by halving the learning rate compared to 7B settings.
- Batch size: try a range (authors searched 8 to 128 for baselines). For larger models they doubled batch size compared to 7B experiments.
- Epochs: the authors used the same number of epochs across model scales for most settings; the number of epochs they settled on at 7B generalized across sizes (subject to LR and batch size adjustments).
- LoRA rank r: less critical; pick a modest r that fits memory (common choices in literature are r=8..64). The emphasis is on applying LoRA across all layers rather than squeezing r.
- Optimization: use paged optimizers for optimizer states so GPU memory peaks are avoided (see next section).
- Precision: use NF4 storage with double quantization, dequantize to BF16 for computation, and keep LoRA parameters in BF16 for updates.

If you want a starting point: tune a 7B model first with LR in 1e-6..5e-5 and a modest batch size; for 33B and 65B halve LR and increase batch size as needed.

## Handling long sequences and memory spikes
Two practical mechanisms from the paper are essential when working on a single GPU:

- Gradient checkpointing: reduces activation memory by recomputing intermediate activations at backward pass time. This lowers steady memory usage but can cause optimizer-state spikes on some batches.
- Paged Optimizers: use NVIDIA unified memory (automatic CPU/GPU paging) for optimizer states so occasional spikes don’t cause OOM. The optimizer state is allocated as pageable memory so it can be evicted to host RAM when the GPU runs out-of-memory and paged back for updates.

In practice the authors:
- Used gradient checkpointing to control activation memory.
- Allocated optimizer states in pageable memory (Paged Optimizers). This avoids catastrophic OOMs during long sequence batches or variable-length inputs.
- Observed no training speed penalty for 65B experiments with batch size 16 on 48GB GPUs; page-induced slowdowns can appear depending on host/GPU bandwidth and how often paging happens.

If you expect many long sequences, ensure your machine has sufficient host RAM and a fast PCIe/NVLink link for acceptable paging performance.

## What to expect: memory, time, and compute for 7B–65B
From the paper’s reported experiments and practical notes:

- 7B models:
  - Runtime inference/serving footprint: the finetuned 7B Guanaco fits in ~5 GB memory for deployment.
  - Finetuning: comfortably runs on a single consumer GPU (e.g., 24GB or even smaller depending on batch size) when using QLoRA.
  - The authors note the potential for extreme low-resource cases (even a phone), estimating an iPhone 12 Plus could finetune ~3M tokens per night while charging (paper estimate; illustrates low-resource feasibility).
- 33B models:
  - Trainable on a single 24GB consumer GPU with QLoRA.
  - The authors trained a 33B Guanaco in less than 12 hours on a single 24GB GPU (their reported result).
  - Memory footprint is far lower than full 16-bit finetuning because the base weights are stored in 4-bit NF4; total average memory is orders of magnitude smaller than a full 16-bit baseline.
- 65B models:
  - Trainable on a single 48GB GPU using QLoRA with NF4, double quantization and paged optimizers.
  - The authors report finetuning a 65B model on a single 48GB GPU in ~24 hours to reach a top Vicuna benchmark result (Guanaco 65B).
  - QLoRA reduces the average finetuning memory requirement of 65B from >780 GB (16-bit full finetuning) to <48 GB without degrading task performance.

These are the paper's reproducible results and are useful guides for planning compute and runtime budgets.

## Practical checklist before you run
- Get the QLoRA + bitsandbytes repositories and CUDA kernels from the paper authors' releases.
- Verify your runtime supports BF16 compute or an equivalent 16-bit compute path used by the QLoRA code.
- Ensure ample host RAM (paged optimizers need host RAM for optimizer state eviction when paging occurs).
- Choose dataset(s) aligned with your target capability (chat vs. MMLU-style tasks).
- Apply LoRA adapters to all linear layers in the model, keep adapter rank moderate, and tune learning rate (1e-6..5e-5 baseline).
- Enable gradient checkpointing and paged optimizers to prevent OOM.
- Use NF4 + double quantization for storage with BF16 compute and LoRA BF16 updates.

## How to evaluate (what the paper used)
- For knowledge tasks: MMLU (5-shot) was used to measure multi-domain knowledge performance.
- For chatbot quality: pairwise comparisons aggregated into Elo ratings (human annotators and GPT-4 were both used). The paper found reasonable agreement between GPT-4 and humans at the system level, but not perfect; thus human evaluation remains important for fine-grained preferences.
- If you’re doing chat models, consider collecting a small high-quality validation set (authors saw big gains from high-quality OASST1 examples).

## Final notes and caveats
- QLoRA recovers 16-bit full finetuning performance in the experiments reported (7B–65B scales), but the authors caution that exhaustive studies at some scales remain future work. Follow the released code and recipes for best reproducibility.
- Automated evaluation (GPT-4) is a useful, cheaper proxy, but can show systemic biases (e.g., order effects). Where feasible, validate with human raters for critical comparisons.

# Beyond the Lab: What QLoRA means for open AI, phones, and privacy

QLoRA is a practical systems advance with outsized social consequences. Technically, it shows you can finetune very large pretrained language models while storing the base weights in 4-bit precision and still recover full 16-bit finetuning performance by training small Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) on top. Practically, that means finetuning 33B models on a single consumer GPU and 65B models on a single professional 48GB GPU — all without the massive multi-server memory footprint that previously made such work infeasible. The paper also ships code, CUDA kernels, and dozens of pre-trained adapters and finetuned models (the "Guanaco" family), so these effects are immediately usable. Below I summarize what this enables, the main technical hooks that make it possible, and the key societal trade-offs.

## The capabilities QLoRA unlocks

- Finetuning large models on commodity hardware. QLoRA reduces the average GPU memory required to finetune a 65B model from more than 780 GB down to under 48 GB by: (a) storing the pretrained weights in 4-bit NF4 format, (b) applying a small set of trainable LoRA adapters (the only parameters updated), and (c) using memory-saving techniques such as Double Quantization and Paged Optimizers. That makes research-scale experiments with 33B/65B models possible on a single GPU instead of a rack of servers.

- Fast, high-quality instruction tuning. Using QLoRA the authors trained the Guanaco family of instruction-following/chatbot adapters that approach ChatGPT-level performance on the Vicuna benchmark: Guanaco-65B reached 99.3% of ChatGPT’s score (Vicuna relative metric) in their evaluation. Smaller Guanaco models (7B/33B) are also competitive and much cheaper to finetune.

- Open releases. The paper’s authors released models, adapters, code, and CUDA kernels (including 4-bit training kernels) so others can reproduce and build on their results. They also released many finetuned adapters across model sizes (7/13/33/65B) and datasets.

## The core technical ideas (brief)

You don’t need the full paper to appreciate the engineering that makes the democratization possible:

- NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat): a k-bit data type optimized for (approximately) normally distributed pretrained weights. NF4 maps weight quantiles of a normal distribution into 4-bit bins so the quantization is information-theoretically well-matched to typical weight statistics; empirically NF4 beats FP4 and Int4 for this use.

- Double Quantization: the per-block quantization scales themselves are quantized at a second level (e.g., to FP8) so the overhead of storing many per-block scales (needed for small block sizes) is reduced. This saves roughly 0.37 bits per parameter on average for typical block sizes.

- Paged Optimizers: leverage unified memory (automatic CPU/GPU paging) to avoid out-of-memory spikes caused by long sequences and gradient checkpointing. This lets optimizer state be paged out to host memory when needed.

- LoRA adapters + backprop through dequantized weights: the base model weights remain frozen and stored in NF4; at runtime they are dequantized to a 16-bit compute type (BF16) and gradients flow through them to update only the low-rank adapters. This keeps train-time memory dominated by activation gradients and LoRA inputs rather than full-precision weight updates.

The empirical result: 4-bit QLoRA with NF4 + Double Quantization recovers (within experimental noise) the performance of 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA finetuning on a range of benchmarks and scales, enabling large-scale fine-tuning experiments that were previously impractical.

## What this means for open AI research and startups

Democratizing access. Historically, training or finetuning very large models has required multi-GPU servers and large budgets. QLoRA radically lowers that barrier: teams or individual researchers with a single high-memory GPU can experiment with 33B+ models. This equalizes capability across smaller labs, startups, and independent researchers; it enables faster iteration cycles, broader reproducibility, and more diverse experimentation with instruction data and safety interventions.

Faster iteration and customization. Because adapters are small and training is fast (the paper reports training Guanaco models in hours on a single GPU), groups can experiment with narrowly scoped, domain-specific or privacy-sensitive finetuning tasks without heavy infrastructure. That includes personalizing assistants, domain-adapting models for specialized industries, or iterating safety-oriented fine-tuning strategies.

More open alternatives to proprietary models. The authors demonstrate that a carefully finetuned, openly released model (Guanaco) can approach ChatGPT on popular benchmarks. Combined with the released tooling, this lowers the friction to build competitive, non-proprietary chat systems.

## On-device customization and phones

The paper explicitly discusses and estimates on-device prospects. A few practical takeaways from their analysis:

- Small finetuned models can be very lightweight. The Guanaco 7B model’s runtime footprint is reported as around 5 GB, making it plausible to deploy on modern phones (some 7B models already run on phones; QLoRA points to the possibility of finetuning such models as well).

- On-device finetuning is imaginable. The authors estimate an iPhone 12 Plus could finetune on the order of millions of tokens per night (they suggest ~3 million tokens per night while charging). That does not mean full-scale production training, but it implies useful personalization and privacy-preserving adaptation could be done locally overnight.

Practical caveats: on-device finetuning still faces constraints — battery, thermal throttling, reliability of paging, and the need for optimized kernels and memory management tailored to phones. QLoRA demonstrates feasibility in a research setting, and its open kernels and code lower the engineering burden to explore real device deployments.

## Privacy and dual-use implications

Privacy upside. QLoRA enables local finetuning and smaller hosted models, which in turn enables privacy-preserving workflows: a user can finetune a model on their private data locally (or in a trusted environment) instead of sending sensitive data to cloud APIs. That both reduces exposure of private data and returns more control to users and organizations.

Dual-use and abuse risks. Lowering the cost of finetuning is a double-edged sword. Easier access to high-capability finetuning lets benign researchers and small startups build tailored assistants, but it also makes it easier for malicious actors to produce specialized harmful models (e.g., targeted disinformation, malware-authoring assistants, or automated social-engineering tools). The paper explicitly notes this dual-use nature: democratization can accelerate both beneficial and harmful applications.

Responsibility in release. The authors released code, kernels, and models, and they also provide some initial bias analyses (e.g., measuring reduction in certain bias metrics after OASST1 finetuning). Full safety and bias auditing remains a large open problem: the paper reports promising reductions in some measured biases for Guanaco-65B but acknowledges that further third-party evaluations across more metrics are needed.

## How QLoRA could reshape the LLM ecosystem

- Shift from big-rack training to many small customizations. Instead of everyone relying on a single large provider for model improvements, teams can finetune adapters for specific domains and share adapters or methodologies. That encourages modular ecosystems: shared base models + community-maintained adapters.

- Faster innovation cycles. Lower costs and shorter turnaround times mean more rapid prototyping of both capabilities and safety mitigations. Researchers can iterate on datasets, reward models, or calibration strategies without waiting for cloud credits or large training clusters.

- Emergent diversity of models. With easier finetuning, we'll likely see a proliferation of specialized, smaller-footprint models for niches that big providers won't serve. That can increase competition and resilience but will also fragment the landscape and complicate evaluation and safety oversight.

## What to watch next

- Independent audits and benchmarking. The paper itself highlights shortcomings in current benchmarks (Vicuna vs. OA vs. MMLU) and shows GPT-4 evaluations and humans have only moderate agreement. Expect work on better evaluation protocols and broader audits of released Guanaco models and similar open models.

- On-device systems engineering. Turning the iPhone-scale estimates into usable mobile finetuning pipelines requires careful kernel engineering, thermal/battery considerations, and compact optimizer states. The paper provides CUDA kernels and code as a starting point.

- Safety tooling for low-cost finetuning. Easier finetuning increases the need for robust, lightweight safety filters, alignment tests, and auditing tools that can run at the adapter level or during local finetuning.

## Bottom line

QLoRA is not just a quantization trick — it is a practical lever that lowers the computational and financial bar for experimenting with, customizing, and deploying high-quality LLMs. That brings immediate benefits for openness, privacy-preserving personalization, and innovation by small teams. At the same time, it amplifies the long-standing dual-use tension: the same accessibility that empowers beneficial work can be misused. The authors mitigate these risks partly by open tooling and initial bias analyses, but the broader community must now develop norms, evaluations, and safety infrastructure to steward this new tide of capability responsibly.
