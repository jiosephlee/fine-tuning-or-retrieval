Chapter Title: Hyperparameter Scaling in QLoRA: Adjusting Learning Rate and Batch Size for Larger Models

# QLoRA in Context: Efficient Finetuning with Quantization and Adapters
QLoRA is an approach to finetune large language models by combining low-bit quantization of the pretrained base model with Low-rank Adapters. The central idea is to store the base model weights in 4-bit precision using a specialized data type, dequantize them to a 16-bit computation type for forward and backward passes, and update only the adapter parameters. This design reduces the memory required to finetune very large models, enabling training at scales that would otherwise be infeasible with standard 16-bit methods.

The method introduces several components that work together to preserve performance while reducing memory. First, it uses 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), a data type chosen because it is information-theoretically optimal for zero-centered normally distributed weights when normalized to a fixed range. Second, it applies Double Quantization to significantly reduce the overhead from quantization constants by quantizing those constants themselves. Third, it employs Paged Optimizers to prevent out-of-memory errors due to occasional spikes—particularly those introduced by gradient checkpointing—by relying on NVIDIA unified memory to page optimizer state between CPU and GPU as needed. 

Within this system, finetuning is carried out by backpropagating gradients through dequantized weights in 16-bit precision while only computing and applying updates to the adapter parameters. This allows QLoRA to match 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA performance across tasks and scales, including encoder, encoder-decoder, and decoder-only architectures, and model sizes ranging from hundreds of millions to tens of billions of parameters. The training process is performed with supervised cross-entropy finetuning on diverse instruction-following datasets, and the evaluation covers both structured benchmarks (e.g., MMLU) and generative chatbot assessments.

# Training Setup and the Need to Adjust Hyperparameters at Scale
The QLoRA training setup is designed to isolate the effects of finetuning and avoid confounders across datasets and models. The authors finetune models on instruction-following datasets using cross-entropy loss without reinforcement learning. Where datasets distinguish between instructions and responses, they finetune on responses; for multi-turn datasets, they select best-rated responses across conversation trees and finetune on full selected conversations. Across all experiments, NF4, Double Quantization, and Paged Optimizers are employed consistently.

A key part of the setup is how the authors manage hyperparameters across different model sizes. Hyperparameters that work for smaller models, such as a 7B LLaMA, do not always transfer directly to larger models like 33B and 65B. In their study, they perform small hyperparameter searches for the 13B and 33B models and report that most settings from the 7B case generalize. Notably, the number of epochs can remain unchanged. However, two crucial hyperparameters do not generalize as-is: the learning rate and the batch size. For the largest models (33B and 65B), the authors halve the learning rate and double the batch size relative to the settings used for 7B.

This adjustment is part of making QLoRA training robust when scaling to much larger parameter counts. Because QLoRA enables these scales by reducing memory demands, it also opens up the possibility of using larger batch sizes. The combination of lower learning rate and larger batch size is a deliberate change at the point where the same schedule used for smaller models is not sufficient.

# Memory Efficiency and Batch Size in QLoRA
A central reason QLoRA can even consider doubling batch size for larger models lies in its memory efficiency. In traditional finetuning, the memory footprint is dominated not only by model parameters but also by activation gradients and optimizer states. QLoRA reduces the base model parameter footprint dramatically by storing the frozen pretrained weights in 4-bit NF4 and dequantizing them to 16-bit for computation only when needed. This allows a 65B parameter model to fit under $48$GB of GPU memory, a dramatic reduction from the $>780$GB required for full 16-bit finetuning.

Within this reduced memory scenario, LoRA’s trainable parameters remain small compared to activation gradients, even when many adapters are used. For example, in a 7B setting, commonly-used adapters (around $0.2\%$ of original parameters) can amount to approximately tens of megabytes, while input gradients are far larger unless gradient checkpointing is applied. With gradient checkpointing, the average memory for input gradients drops substantially, but they can still be more memory intensive than all LoRA weights combined. Importantly, the 4-bit base model itself consumes only a few gigabytes.

This breakdown matters when adjusting batch size $B$. Because QLoRA reduces the base model footprint and uses paging for optimizer states, it is more feasible to increase $B$ without hitting memory ceilings. The unified memory of Paged Optimizers mitigates occasional spikes that would otherwise cause out-of-memory conditions. Given these constraints and tools, doubling the batch size at larger scales becomes a practical and controlled choice.

# Learning Rate Adjustments for Larger Models
Alongside batch size, the learning rate $\eta$ is a critical hyperparameter in finetuning. In the QLoRA setup described by the authors, a direct carryover of $\eta$ from smaller models does not suffice for larger models. They therefore halve the learning rate for 33B and 65B models. This adjustment complements the increase in batch size and is made in tandem with the goal of maintaining performance parity with 16-bit baselines at larger scales.

In their broader baseline construction, the authors also emphasize that default hyperparameters for fully finetuned baselines are undertuned, and they conduct searches over learning rates in the range from $1\times 10^{-6}$ to $5\times 10^{-5}$ and batch sizes from $8$ to $128$ to establish robust baselines. This underscores that the learning rate is a sensitive parameter even in standard settings, and that proper tuning is required to fairly compare finetuning strategies.

Within QLoRA’s methodology, updates are only applied to LoRA parameters in 16-bit precision. The gradient computations pass through dequantized 4-bit weights to calculate the necessary derivatives. While LoRA’s parameter footprint is small, the learning rate controls how aggressively those parameters are updated. At very large scales, settings that are effective for smaller models may update too quickly or too coarsely; consequently, halving $\eta$ is part of a controlled approach that ensures training remains effective while preserving the parity in performance that QLoRA is designed to achieve.

# How Hyperparameter Changes Fit QLoRA’s Objectives
The authors’ objective is to demonstrate that 4-bit QLoRA can match 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA across tasks and scales, and to push chatbot performance with models as large as 65B parameters. This goal imposes practical constraints on training stability and performance outcomes at scale. Their experimental results show that when these adjustments are made—doubling batch size and halving learning rate for larger models—the approach continues to achieve matching performance in established benchmarks such as GLUE, Super-NaturalInstructions, and MMLU, and supports state-of-the-art chatbot results.

The broader findings in the paper emphasize that data quality matters more than dataset size, that benchmark suitability plays a dominant role in performance on specific tasks, and that careful evaluation (including both human and GPT-4 judgments) is necessary. Within this ecosystem of results, the hyperparameter changes are a local but crucial part of making the large-scale finetuning runs viable and performant. They are an example of the careful engineering that accompanies the core algorithmic advances of NF4 quantization, Double Quantization, and memory management.

# Practical Considerations within QLoRA’s Training Pipeline
To understand how these hyperparameter changes integrate with the rest of QLoRA’s pipeline, it is useful to summarize the computation pathway. The base model weights are stored as $4$-bit NF4, with quantization constants reduced via Double Quantization. For any linear layer used during training, the stored weights are dequantized to $16$-bit before matrix multiplications. The forward pass computes the output as the sum of the base model’s dequantized projection and the LoRA projection with parameters $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$:
\[
\mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \,\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) + \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}^{\text{BF16}}_1\mathbf{L}^{\text{BF16}}_2.
\]
Only the adapter parameters are updated, and all required derivatives are computed in $16$-bit precision after dequantization. Because the storage is $4$-bit while computation is $16$-bit, the method preserves numerical behavior during training where it matters while conserving memory at rest.

Against this computational backdrop, the hyperparameter pair $(\eta, B)$ plays a direct role in how quickly and how noisily the adapter parameters are updated. The authors’ empirical finding—keep epochs the same but halve $\eta$ and double $B$ at 33B and 65B—fits the constraints and behaviors observed in their pipeline. It leverages QLoRA’s memory savings to increase batch size and modulates the update step to remain effective at larger scale.

# Implications for Performance and Evaluation
With these choices, the authors demonstrate that QLoRA matches 16-bit finetuning performance across architectures and datasets and achieves top-tier chatbot performance. Their evaluations span:
- MMLU for multiple-choice language understanding across $57$ tasks, reported as $5$-shot accuracy.
- Generative chatbot benchmarks using both automated judgments (GPT-4) and human judgments on pairwise comparisons, aggregated into Elo ratings.

The results show that the largest QLoRA-tuned models trained on high-quality instruction data perform comparably to leading systems on chatbot evaluations, with Guanaco 65B achieving $99.3\%$ of ChatGPT’s score on the Vicuna benchmark. The paper also emphasizes that while GPT-4-based evaluations offer a practical alternative to human ratings, they come with biases such as ordering effects and self-preference, and agreement is only moderate.

These outcomes underscore that the training regime—including hyperparameter scaling choices—is effective in practice. By preserving performance at large scales and enabling finetuning on single high-memory GPUs for 65B models, QLoRA alters the accessibility of finetuning large LLMs.

# Summary
In scaling QLoRA to very large models, the authors report that most hyperparameters from smaller models generalize directly, but two do not: the learning rate and batch size. For 33B and 65B models, they halve the learning rate and double the batch size. This adjustment is one piece of a broader, carefully constructed training methodology that includes NF4 quantization, Double Quantization, and Paged Optimizers. Together, these choices enable efficient finetuning at unprecedented scales while matching 16-bit baseline performance and achieving state-of-the-art results in chatbot evaluations.
