Chapter: NormalFloat4 (NF4) and Information-Theoretic Optimality for Quantized Finetuning

# Quantization and the Problem of Optimality

Quantization transforms high-precision tensors into lower-precision representations by mapping continuous values to a discrete set. This operation saves memory and computational cost, but it introduces rounding and clipping errors. A foundational design question is how to choose the discrete values—in other words, the quantization bins—so that the encoding captures as much useful information as possible about the underlying tensor. The notion of “information-theoretic optimality” in this context is tied to how the distribution of the input values is matched to the discrete levels. When the input values are drawn from a known distribution, the discrete set can be chosen so that each bin receives, in expectation, the same number of input values. This equal-bin-occupancy condition reduces waste and makes full use of the representational capacity available at a given bit-width.

The paper introduces NormalFloat (NF), and in particular 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), as a quantization data type that is information-theoretically optimal for zero-centered normal distributions. The key implicit assumption is that pretrained neural network weights are drawn from a distribution of the form $N(0,\sigma^2)$ up to a scaling constant. Under this assumption, NF4 is constructed by taking exact quantiles of a standard normal distribution to define the discrete levels, and then normalizing both the codebook and the input weights into a shared range so that the mapping is stable and precise. This yields a data type whose bins each correspond to equal-probability intervals under the assumed normal distribution, establishing information-theoretic optimality for normally distributed weights.

To understand how NF4 arises and why it is optimal under the normality assumption, we first ground the discussion in block-wise $k$-bit quantization and quantile quantization, and then show how the normality assumption simplifies the design of the codebook and the mapping procedure.

# Block-wise k-bit Quantization and the Role of Scaling

Low-bit quantization must contend with scale. If a tensor contains a few large-magnitude outliers, mapping the entire tensor to a small discrete range wastes bins and drives many values toward zero, which incurs large quantization error for typical values. A common remedy is block-wise quantization: the tensor is flattened and split into contiguous blocks of size $B$, each block is normalized independently, and a per-block quantization constant (scale factor) is stored. If $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ is the input, this procedure yields blocks indexed by $i\in\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $n = (b\times h)/B$, each with its own constant $c_i$.

The simplest normalization uses absolute maximum rescaling for a target integer range, as in
\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}} = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{127}{\text{absmax}(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}})} \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\right) = \text{round}(c^{\text{FP32}}\cdot \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}).
\]
Dequantization inverts the mapping:
\[
\text{dequant}(c^{\text{FP32}}, \mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}) = \frac{\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}}{c^{\text{FP32}}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}.
\]
While absolute-maximum scaling ensures that the full range of the data type is used, it does not tailor the discrete levels to the empirical distribution of the values within each block. If the distribution were known and relatively stable, we could do better by setting the codebook levels to align with that distribution’s quantiles.

# Quantile Quantization and Information-Theoretic Optimality

Quantile quantization chooses discrete levels so that, under an assumed distribution of inputs, each quantization bin has equal expected occupancy. This design is information-theoretically optimal in the sense that it allocates representational capacity uniformly across the value distribution, minimizing “empty” bins and reducing the mismatch between the data and the codebook. The construction uses the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the quantile function to partition the support into equal-probability intervals and then places representative points (e.g., mid-quantiles) within each interval.

In practice, directly estimating quantiles for arbitrary input tensors can be expensive, especially when the distribution varies across blocks. Approximate algorithms such as SRAM quantiles can reduce the cost but may incur estimation error, which tends to be most problematic for outliers—the values for which precision often matters most.

The paper addresses this challenge by leveraging a property of pretrained neural network weights: they “usually have a zero-centered normal distribution with standard deviation $\sigma$.” This empirical observation (supported by the paper’s appendix) enables a principled approach to quantile quantization by fixing the distribution up to scale. If the distribution is fixed, its quantiles are fixed and can be computed exactly in advance. With this machinery, quantile quantization becomes computationally feasible and precise.

# NF4: Constructing an Optimal Codebook for Normal Weights

NF4 is a $k$-bit NormalFloat data type that is designed to be optimal for weights drawn from zero-centered normal distributions $N(0,\sigma^2)$ up to a per-block scaling constant. Its construction proceeds in three steps:

1) Compute the $(2^k+1)$ quantiles of the standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$, and set discrete levels to the midpoints of consecutive quantile boundaries. Formally, define
\[
q_i = \frac{1}{2}\left(Q_X\!\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\!\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right),
\]
where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function of $N(0,1)$ and $i$ indexes the bins.

2) Normalize the codebook $q_i$ into the fixed range $[-1,1]$. This step ensures the codebook aligns with a data range that is also normalized into $[-1,1]$ prior to quantization.

3) Quantize the input weights by normalizing each block into $[-1,1]$ via absolute maximum rescaling, then mapping to the nearest codebook level. This normalization is equivalent to rescaling the standard deviation of the input weights so that the distribution matches the standard deviation of the $k$-bit codebook. Up to the per-block scale factor, this aligns the actual distribution with the codebook’s assumed distribution, preserving the equal-bin-occupancy property.

A subtlety arises with symmetric $k$-bit codebooks: they do not necessarily include an exact zero level, which is essential for lossless encoding of zero-valued entries such as padding. To remedy this, NF4 uses an asymmetric construction that estimates quantiles separately for the negative and positive ranges—$2^{k-1}$ levels on the negative side and $2^{k-1}+1$ levels on the positive side—unifies the sets, and removes the duplicated zero so that one discrete level exactly represents $0$. This preserves the optimality condition for the normal distribution while ensuring the data type includes a true zero.

Under the assumption that the pretrained weights are distributed as $N(0,\sigma^2)$ within each block up to scale, the NF4 codebook derived from $N(0,1)$ quantiles and the normalization to $[-1,1]$ yields bins with equal expected occupancy. This is precisely the information-theoretic optimality the paper claims: each of the $2^k$ discrete values is used equally often in expectation, maximizing effective precision at the given bit-width for that distribution.

# Why the Normality Assumption Makes NF4 Practical

Quantile quantization is theoretically attractive but practically challenging because quantile estimation for arbitrary distributions is expensive and error-prone. The normality assumption circumvents this by fixing the distribution of weights up to a scale parameter. With a fixed distribution, quantiles are known exactly and need not be estimated from data, eliminating both computational overhead and approximation errors. The paper emphasizes that approximate quantile estimation methods introduce large errors for outliers, while normality enables precise quantile computation that is robust to such extremes.

Concretely, by treating each block as drawn from $N(0,\sigma^2)$ up to a per-block scaling constant, we obtain a simple, fast, and accurate procedure: compute a fixed codebook from the exact quantiles of $N(0,1)$; normalize both the codebook and the block to $[-1,1]$ via absolute-maximum scaling; and quantize by nearest-level assignment. The scale factor for each block is stored as a quantization constant so that dequantization recovers the correct magnitudes. The result is an efficient and high-fidelity mapping from continuous weights to 4-bit discrete levels.

# NF4 in QLoRA: Precision, Dequantization, and Backpropagation

QLoRA uses NF4 as the storage data type for pretrained model weights and BFloat16 (BF16) as the computation data type. Whenever a weight tensor is needed for computation, QLoRA dequantizes from 4-bit NF4 to BF16 and performs matrix multiplications in 16-bit precision. The dequantization step is straightforward: in the simplest case, it inverts the per-block scaling and maps discrete levels back to their normalized real values; in the presence of double quantization, it first dequantizes the quantized quantization constants and then uses them to dequantize the NF4 weights:
\[
\text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}.
\]
In QLoRA, $\mathbf{W}$ uses NF4 and $c_2$ uses FP8, with a blocksize of $64$ for $\mathbf{W}$ to improve quantization precision and a blocksize of $256$ for $c_2$ to conserve memory. The forward pass thus benefits from the compact NF4 storage and BF16 computation, and the backward pass computes gradients through the dequantized weights to update only the LoRA adapter parameters in 16-bit precision.

This design enables QLoRA to “backpropagate gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model into Low Rank Adapters,” preserving the predictive performance of 16-bit finetuning while drastically reducing memory usage. The choice of NF4 as the storage type is central to preserving fidelity: its information-theoretic optimality under normality ensures that the quantization error is minimized for distributions commonly observed in pretrained weights, and empirical results in the paper show that NF4 outperforms 4-bit Floating Point (FP4) and Int4.

# Double Quantization: Reducing the Overhead of Scale Factors

Block-wise quantization requires storing per-block quantization constants, which add nontrivial memory overhead at small block sizes. Double Quantization addresses this by quantizing the quantization constants themselves. Specifically, if $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ are the per-block constants for the NF4 weights, QLoRA applies a second quantization step to obtain $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ along with a second-level constant $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. Because $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ are positive, the mean is subtracted prior to quantization to center them for symmetric quantization. With a blocksize of $64$ for weights and $256$ for second-level constants, the memory per parameter for constants drops from $32/64=0.5$ bits to $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 0.127$ bits, a reduction of $0.373$ bits per parameter. Importantly, the paper reports that double quantization reduces memory without degrading performance, which is consistent with the overall goal of preserving 16-bit finetuning fidelity under aggressive memory constraints.

In combination with NF4, double quantization allows QLoRA to achieve large-scale finetuning—up to $65\text{B}$ parameters—on a single GPU while matching the accuracy of 16-bit baselines. The dequantization routine composes cleanly: dequantize the second-level constants to recover $c_2$, then dequantize the NF4 weights using $c_2$.

# Empirical Evidence for NF4’s Optimality

The paper evaluates NF4 against FP4 and Int4 across multiple models and tasks, measuring zero-shot accuracy and perplexity under quantization. These results show that NF4 “improves performance significantly over FP4 and Int4” and that double quantization “reduces the memory footprint without degrading performance.” Beyond static quantization tests, QLoRA with NF4 matches both 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA finetuning performance across architectures and scales, including LLaMA models up to $65\text{B}$ parameters on instruction-following tasks and MMLU.

These findings corroborate the core claim: given the implicit assumption that pretrained weights follow a zero-centered normal distribution with variance $\sigma^2$, NF4’s construction by exact normal quantiles yields an information-theoretically optimal 4-bit data type for these weights. The equal-occupancy property translates into reduced quantization error in practice, enabling QLoRA to preserve 16-bit performance while achieving dramatic memory savings.

# Practical Considerations and Limitations

NF4’s optimality hinges on the normality assumption. The paper explicitly notes that “pretrained neural network weights usually have a zero-centered normal distribution with standard deviation $\sigma$,” and it leverages this property to define the NF4 data type. Under this assumption, exact quantile computation is feasible and precise, and normalization into $[-1,1]$ aligns the codebook and the input blocks. When the assumption holds, NF4 delivers both theoretical and empirical advantages.

In practice, distributional deviations may occur, and the paper’s broader system design mitigates their impact. Block-wise quantization with small blocks reduces the effect of outliers by adapting scales locally; absolute-maximum rescaling ensures the full data type range is used per block; and the asymmetric codebook includes a discrete zero for exact encoding of zero entries. These mechanisms, together with double quantization and BF16 computation, contribute to robust performance even when the underlying distributions are not perfectly normal. The empirical results, which demonstrate NF4’s superiority to FP4 and Int4 and show parity with 16-bit finetuning baselines under QLoRA, suggest that the normality assumption is sufficiently accurate for the pretrained weights studied in the paper.

# Summary: NF4’s Role in Efficient, High-Fidelity QLoRA Finetuning

NF4 is a 4-bit data type designed via quantile quantization on a fixed, zero-centered normal distribution. Under the implicit assumption that pretrained weights are distributed as $N(0,\sigma^2)$ up to block-wise scaling, NF4’s discrete levels achieve equal expected occupancy, satisfying an information-theoretic optimality criterion. By normalizing both the codebook and input blocks into $[-1,1]$, and by ensuring a discrete zeropoint through an asymmetric construction, NF4 provides a precise and efficient mapping from continuous weights to 4-bit representations.

In QLoRA, NF4 serves as the storage type, with dequantization to BF16 for computation and gradients. Double quantization further compresses the scale factors with no observed performance loss. Together, these innovations allow QLoRA to finetune very large language models on single GPUs while matching 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA performance across benchmarks. The paper’s empirical results confirm that the theoretical optimality derived from the normality assumption translates into practical gains, making NF4 a central component of efficient finetuning at scale.
