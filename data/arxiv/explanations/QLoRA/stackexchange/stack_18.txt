### How did you correct for ordering bias in GPT-4 evaluations when computing Elo ratings?
Question:
The paper reports strong ordering effects (GPT-4 favors the first response) and recommends averaging scores over both orders. For the pairwise comparisons used to compute Elo, did you always evaluate both orderings and then symmetrize the label, or did Elo ingest individual ordered judgments with random order assignment? If the latter, could residual order bias skew Elo, and how did you estimate confidence intervals on Elo to account for sampling variance across prompts and orderings?
Answer:
Short answer: we evaluated both shown-orderings for comparisons and then used randomized resampling of the labeled matches (including both orderings) to compute Elo distributions and confidence intervals. In practice GPT-4’s ordering bias was handled in two ways: absolute scoring (the score-out-of-$10$ protocol) was averaged across both prompt orders, and pairwise head-to-head labels were collected on all permutations and then Elo was computed by repeatedly sampling from that pool to control for ordering effects ($10{,}000$ random seeds). This is what the paper describes.

Longer explanation / intuition grounded in the paper: the authors observed a clear first-position bias in GPT-4’s absolute scoring and therefore recommend “reporting the mean score over both orders” for any score-based metric (this is stated explicitly in the Evaluation section). For the pairwise comparisons that feed Elo, the paper says they “conduct these head-to-head comparisons on all permutations of pairs of systems on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks,” which means each prompt was used to generate both orderings ($A$ first / $B$ second and $B$ first / $A$ second). Elo itself is computed from the individual pairwise judgments, but those judgments include both orderings.

To avoid residual order-bias contaminating the Elo rankings, the authors do two things. First, by collecting both permutations they remove systematic confounding of a model’s performance with display order: any per-prompt first-position preference in the judge pool is exposed symmetrically for each model. Second, when producing the reported Elo numbers they do not compute a single deterministic Elo from the fixed sequence of matches; instead they “randomly sample from the set of labeled comparisons to compute Elo” and repeat the whole procedure $10{,}000$ times with different random seeds. That repetition serves as an empirical control for sampling variability (including variability due to which ordering instances appear in a sampled tournament and the random sequence in which matches are processed by the Elo update). The distribution of Elo scores across those $10{,}000$ runs is then used to produce uncertainty estimates / confidence intervals. Intuitively, this is a bootstrap-like approach: by resampling many possible tournament sequences from the labeled comparisons (which include both orders), you measure how much Elo fluctuates due to prompt sampling, labeling noise, and ordering.

Could residual order bias still skew Elo? In principle yes if (a) you failed to collect both permutations for many prompts, (b) the judge population has a massive first-position preference that is not symmetric across models (for example if model formatting systematically interacts with position), or (c) sampling/resampling is insufficient. The paper’s protocol addresses these risks by explicitly collecting both orderings for the pairwise comparisons and by using extensive randomized resampling ($10{,}000$ repeats) to measure variance. Because Elo updates are driven by individual match outcomes, any remaining small bias from orderings will be reflected in the empirical spread of Elo across the resampled tournaments; if bias were large it would induce shifting means and wide intervals, which would show up in the reported uncertainty.

Practical takeaway: the authors handled ordering bias by collecting both $A$/$B$ and $B$/$A$ comparisons, symmetrizing absolute-score metrics by averaging over orders, and computing Elo via many randomized resamplings of the measured pairwise labels ($10{,}000$ repetitions) to obtain an empirical Elo distribution and confidence intervals. This combination both neutralizes systematic first-position effects and quantifies the remaining sampling variance.
