### How is the 0.373 bits/parameter saving from double quantization derived?
Question:
The paper claims that with blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for the second-level scales, double quantization reduces per-parameter overhead from \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits (one FP32 scale per block of 64) to \(8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 0.127\) bits, saving 0.373 bits/parameter.

Could you confirm the assumptions behind this arithmetic?
- Are the first-level scales stored as FP32 per block of 64 parameters before DQ, and after DQ are we storing FP8 per block of 64 plus FP32 per block of 256 of those scales?
- Are there any additional metadata costs (e.g., offsets, codebook indices) that should be counted?
- Does this calculation apply uniformly to all weight tensors (including embeddings and layer norms), or only to certain matrices?
Answer:
Short answer: the arithmetic in the paper is consistent given the paper's stated assumptions. They assume one FP32 scale per 64-weight block initially, and after Double Quantization (DQ) they store one FP8 (8-bit float) scale per 64-weight block plus one FP32 second-level scale shared across 256 of those first-level scales. That yields

$32/64 = 0.5$ bits/parameter before DQ,
$8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) = 0.125 + 0.001953125 \approx 0.12695 \approx 0.127$ bits/parameter after DQ,

so the saving is $0.5 - 0.12695 \approx 0.37305$ bits/parameter, which matches the paper’s $0.373$ claim (rounding to three decimals).

Clarifying the assumptions and small caveats so you can see where each term comes from: before DQ the model uses block-wise quantization with blocksize $B = 64$ for the weights; for every block you need to store a 32-bit FP32 quantization constant (scale) $c_2$, so the amortized overhead is $32\ \text{bits} / 64\ \text{weights} = 0.5\ \text{bits/weight}$. After DQ those original per-block FP32 scales $c_2$ are themselves quantized in a second stage: each $c_2$ (one per 64-weight block) is stored as an 8-bit float (FP8) — that gives the $8/64 = 0.125$ bits/weight term — and a second-level FP32 scale (call it $c_1$) is stored once per group of 256 of the $c_2$’s (i.e., shared across 256 first-level scales). That FP32 cost amortizes to $32\ \text{bits}$ divided by the number of weights in that second-level group: $32 / (64\cdot 256) = 32/(16384) \approx 0.001953125\ \text{bits/weight}$. Add the two terms to get $\approx 0.127$ bits/weight.

Other practical metadata and implementation notes

1) What about the mean-subtraction step? The paper says they subtract the mean of the positive $c_2$ values before quantizing $c_2$ to make the distribution centered and use symmetric quantization. That mean must be recoverable at dequant time. In practice that per-group mean can be stored as part of the second-level FP32 constants ($c_1$) or stored as a separate small FP32 per second-level group. The paper’s stated formula already accounts for one FP32 per 256 first-level scales (the $32/(64\cdot 256)$ term), so the expectation is that any per-group FP32 numbers needed for reconstructing $c_2$ are included in that term. If you implemented DQ differently (e.g., storing an extra FP32 mean in addition to $c_1$), you would add that extra FP32-per-256 cost (another $\approx 0.00195$ bits/weight).

2) Are there additional index/metadata costs (offsets, codebook indices, zero-points)? In the QLoRA design as described: the low-bit weights themselves are stored as $k$-bit values (NF4 in the paper, $k = 4$), so the weight storage cost is $k$ bits/parameter (not part of the quoted $0.373$ saving which is only the scale overhead). They use symmetric block-wise quantization (and for NF4 they use a precomputed codebook / table for dequant values), so there is no per-block zero-point to store; the codebook values are global and shared (not per block) so their amortized cost is negligible. Therefore the only per-parameter overheads relevant here are the per-block scale(s). If you chose an asymmetric scheme with per-block zero-points or stored additional per-block metadata you would need to account for it, but the paper’s method avoids that by symmetric quantization and a global NF4 codebook.

3) Which tensors does this arithmetic apply to? The calculation applies to tensors that are block-quantized with the specified block sizes ($B=64$ for weights, groups of 256 scales for the second level). In practice that means the (large) weight matrices that you quantize block-wise — typically the dense projection matrices in attention and MLP layers. Very small tensors and certain parameters are often left in higher precision in implementations: biases, layer-norm parameters, small projection vectors, or embedding layers are sometimes kept at FP16/FP32 to avoid disproportionate overhead or loss. The paper does not give an exhaustive list of exceptions, but its memory-saving claim ($\approx 0.37$ bits/param saved) is averaged over the block-quantized parameters; if you leave some tensors unquantized (e.g., embeddings, layer-norm) then that fraction of parameters would not get the $0.373$ savings and the global average saving across the whole model would be slightly lower. In short: the formula is correct for block-quantized weight tensors; for tensors not quantized or quantized with different block sizes/types you must recompute overheads accordingly.

Summary: yes, the paper’s arithmetic assumes FP32 scales per 64-weight block pre-DQ, and after DQ stores FP8 per 64-block plus FP32 per 256 of those scales (with the mean-centering reconstruction cost folded into that FP32-per-256). The computed numbers $0.5$ and $\approx 0.127$ bits/parameter and the $0.373$ bits saving follow directly from those assumptions. You only need to watch for implementation details (where you store the mean, whether some tensors remain unquantized, any extra per-block metadata) which can add tiny extra amortized costs if handled differently.
