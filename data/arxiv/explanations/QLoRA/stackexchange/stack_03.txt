\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Does absmax scaling in NF4 break the equal-quantile property for normally distributed weights?
Question:
NF4 is motivated by precomputing the \(2^k+1\) quantiles of \(N(0,1)\) and then scaling weights into \([-1,1]\) via absolute-maximum rescaling before quantizing. However, with absmax scaling, the mapping depends on the single maximum magnitude in the block, not on \(\sigma\). For normally distributed data, this makes the effective distribution non-Gaussian after scaling (heavy tails get clipped into the range).

If the equal-quantile codebook was derived assuming inputs are drawn i.i.d. from a centered normal with known \(\sigma\), does using absmax-rescaled blocks preserve the equal probability per bin property? If not, why is absmax preferred over per-block standard deviation scaling? Are there empirical or theoretical reasons (e.g., outlier robustness) for this choice in NF4 specifically?
Answer:
Short answer: No — absmax scaling does not preserve the equal-probability (equal-quantile) property exactly. However, for the block sizes and weight statistics encountered in practice the deviation is small, and absmax is chosen for simplicity, efficiency and favorable empirical robustness in the quantization pipeline used by NF4.

Why it isn’t exact (intuitive + math). The NF4 codebook is built from the quantiles of a standard normal $N(0,1)$. That design assumes the input to the quantizer is a centred Gaussian distribution whose scale (standard deviation $\sigma$) is known or deterministically removed before quantization. If you could divide every block by its true $\sigma$, the distribution of the normalized samples would be exactly $N(0,1)$ and the precomputed equal-quantile codebook would assign roughly equal probability mass to each bin.

Absmax scaling instead divides a block by its maximum absolute value
$$
M = \max_{i\in\text{block}} |x_i|.
$$
For Gaussian i.i.d. $x_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ the conditional distribution of the block values after scaling, $x_i' = x_i / M$, is not Gaussian: conditioning on the observed maximum couples the samples and truncates/warps the tail. Thus the probability that a randomly chosen $x_i'$ falls into one precomputed quantile bin is no longer guaranteed to be equal across bins. Formally, the distribution of $x_i' \mid M$ is the original Gaussian density renormalized and truncated by the event that some sample attains magnitude $M$; that changes bin probabilities.

Why it’s approximately OK in practice (concentration + effective deterministic scaling). The key observation is that for a block of size $B$ the expected maximum scales with $\sigma$:
$$
\mathbb{E}[M] \approx \sigma\cdot a_B,
$$
where $a_B$ is the expected maximum of $|N(0,1)|$ over $B$ draws (a constant depending only on $B$). For moderate block sizes (e.g. 32 or 64, as used in practice) $M/\sigma$ concentrates fairly tightly around $a_B$. So dividing by $M$ is approximately equivalent to dividing by $\sigma$ up to the nearly-constant factor $a_B$:
$$
\frac{x}{M} \approx \frac{x}{\sigma a_B} = \frac{1}{a_B}\cdot\frac{x}{\sigma}.
$$
Because NF4’s codebook is constructed from an $N(0,1)$ quantile grid then scaled into $[-1,1]$, a near-constant multiplicative mismatch (the $1/a_B$ factor) simply rescales the whole distribution and only mildly perturbs the intended equal-quantile mapping. In short: absmax introduces randomness in the normalization, but that randomness is small because of the concentration of extremes at realistic block sizes.

Why choose absmax over per-block standard deviation in NF4 (practical and empirical reasons). First, absmax is extremely cheap and simple to compute (one pass to find max magnitude) and gives a guaranteed endpoint touch: at least one value in the block will map to $\pm 1$, so the dynamic range of the k-bit data type is used. Computing a block standard deviation requires a second moment, is slightly more expensive, and for very small blocks can be numerically less stable (division by a tiny $\hat{\sigma}$ is risky). Second, both absmax and standard deviation are sensitive to outliers, but they behave differently: a single huge outlier inflates $M$ strongly and compresses the rest of the block toward zero (which is what absolute-max chunking was historically used to avoid globally clipping), while $\hat{\sigma}$ would also inflate but in a different (moment-based) way. Third, NF4’s design relies on having a fixed target distribution for which the quantiles are precomputed; absmax yields a single simple scalar per block that maps the block roughly into that target distribution with low overhead. Finally, the paper reports that NF4 (with absmax per-block scaling plus chunking and double quantization) outperforms FP4/Int4 empirically and recovers 16-bit finetuning performance after adapter training — evidence that this engineering choice works well in practice.

Outliers and robustness. You’re right that absmax makes the normalized distribution non-Gaussian when there are heavy tails or strong outliers in a block. This is precisely why quantization is done block-wise: small blocks keep the relationship between $M$ and $\sigma$ tight and limit the damage of any single extreme value. The paper also emphasizes chunking to mitigate the global-outlier problem. In addition, double quantization and designing NF4 around the typical normal shape of pretrained weights (Appendix discussion) are intended to make the scheme robust overall. The authors observed empirically that NF4 with absmax blockwise scaling yields lower quantization error and better downstream performance than other 4-bit formats, which is the strongest practical justification given in the paper.

Summary. Using absmax per block does break the equal-quantile property in a strict probabilistic sense because normalization depends on the realized max rather than the true $\sigma$. But for moderate block sizes the max is closely tied to $\sigma$ (concentration of extremes), so the precomputed NF4 quantiles remain a good fit. The choice of absmax is driven by simplicity, numerical stability, efficiency, and the fact that the entire NF4 + chunking + double-quantization pipeline yields strong empirical performance on pretrained LLM weights, which is the evidence the paper provides for preferring this design.
