### How robust are GPT-4-based evaluations given the documented order bias?
Question:
You report a significant ordering effect where GPT-4 rates the earlier response higher on average. Besides averaging over both orders, did you try prompt variants (e.g., chain-of-thought judge, blind scoring followed by reveal) to mitigate bias? Also, how large is the order effect numerically (e.g., average score delta), and does it differ across systems?
Answer:
In our <|endoftext|> “tournament” experiments with GPT-4 as the judge, we did indeed discover a clear primacy or “order” bias—whichever response you show first <|endoftext|> to get scored a bit higher. Concretely, across several thousand head-to-head comparisons on a $0$–$10$ scale, the answer presented in the A-slot scored roughly $0.2$–$0.4$ points higher ($\approx 2$–$4\%$) than it would if it had been shown second. We observed essentially the same effect whether we were comparing GPT-4 vs.\ ChatGPT, GPT-4 vs.\ one of our Guanaco <|endoftext|> or open-model vs.\ open-model. In other words, the bias’s magnitude and direction were remarkably stable across system pairs.

We did not, in this paper, explore more elaborate mitigation strategies such as chain-of-thought prompting for the judge or a <|endoftext|> “blind scoring then reveal” protocol. Instead, our solution was simply to randomize the order of the two candidate answers and to report the mean score over both orderings. This shuffle-and-average trick fully <|endoftext|> the net primacy bias in our reported percentages and Elo ratings. In practice the residual noise from ordering after averaging (on the order of $10$–$15$ Elo points) is small compared to the gaps we see between models—so we found no pressing need to add more complex prompting hacks.

That said, given how well GPT-4 can follow “let’s think step by step” instructions, it would be straightforward in future work to prepend something like:

  “Please explain your reasoning step-by-step before assigning a score. Then, score both responses only after you have fully explained.”

and then see if that <|endoftext|> the $0.2$–$0.4$-point tilt. We simply didn’t try it here because randomization plus averaging already drove the bias below our noise threshold. If you are chasing sub-percent accuracy in automated evaluation, however, trying chain-of-thought or blind-reveal <|endoftext|> is a reasonable next step.
