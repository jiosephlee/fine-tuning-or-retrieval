In the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', the purpose of repeating the Elo computation with many random seeds is to control ordering effects. Elo rating updates are path-dependent: in a tournament-style setting where pairwise matches are processed sequentially, early upsets or the particular sequence of opponents can shift ratings nontrivially due to the update rule, which adjusts a model’s rating by a factor proportional to how surprising the outcome is. Concretely, the Elo update takes the form
\(R' = R + K\,(S - E)\),
where \(R\) is the current rating, \(S\in\{0, 0.5, 1\}\) is the observed score, and the expected score \(E\) depends on the current ratings via
\(E = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_{\text{opp}} - R)/400}}\).
Processing the same set of comparisons in different orders can yield slightly different trajectories of \(R\), especially when strong systems face each other early or when ties are frequent. By reseeding the sampling of match sequences and recomputing Elo many times (they use 10,000 random seeds), the authors average over these path dependencies, producing rankings that are robust to arbitrary ordering of comparisons.

Moreover, the paper empirically observes ordering effects in GPT-4-based evaluations themselves, with the system appearing first in the prompt receiving inflated scores. This motivates additional care to neutralize presentation order and processing order. Randomizing the order of pairwise comparisons and repeating the Elo aggregation mitigates both sources of bias: (i) presentation-order bias in GPT-4 judgments (addressed by symmetric prompt orders and averaging across them), and (ii) sequence-order bias in Elo updates (addressed by reordering matches many times). The net effect is a more reliable estimate of relative performance that reflects the underlying head-to-head outcomes rather than artifacts of evaluation or update ordering.

According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', Elo updates adjust players' ratings proportionally to the surprise of the result; an unexpected upset causes a large rating change. This follows directly from the standard Elo update rule the paper employs in its tournament-style evaluation: for each match, a model’s rating is updated as R_new = R_old + K(S - E), where S is the observed score (e.g., 1 for a win, 0 for a loss, 0.5 for a tie), E is the expected win probability given the current ratings, and K is a scaling factor (the paper uses K = 32, starting ratings at 1000). Because the increment is proportional to the difference S - E, the magnitude of the update reflects how “surprising” the outcome is relative to the model’s predicted performance: when a lower-rated model beats a higher-rated one, E is small and S - E is large, producing a large positive adjustment; conversely, when a highly favored model wins, E is close to 1 and S - E is small, yielding only a modest change.

This proportionality also ensures that ratings converge to reflect true skill under repeated comparisons, which the paper enforces by sampling many head-to-head judgments and repeating the Elo aggregation “10,000 times with different random seeds to control for ordering effects.” The logistic expectation used in Elo, E = 1 / (1 + 10^{-(\Delta R)/400}), ties expected outcome to rating difference \Delta R, so the same K produces larger updates only when outcomes deviate substantially from this expectation. Thus, by construction, Elo in the paper’s evaluation pipeline gives larger rating shifts to unexpected upsets and smaller shifts to expected results, aligning the final rankings with models’ demonstrated win-rates across the Vicuna and OA benchmarks.

According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', because absolute 10-point scales showed uncertainty and order effects, the authors recommend using a ranking approach based on pairwise judgments known as Elo rating. In their evaluations, GPT-4 scoring on a 10-point scale exhibited strong ordering effects (the response shown first tended to receive a higher score), and the resulting aggregate scores had wide confidence intervals, making it unclear what, for example, “8 out of 10” consistently means across diverse prompts. Elo provides a principled aggregation mechanism for pairwise outcomes that is less sensitive to absolute-scale drift. The expected win probability of model \(A\) against \(B\) is modeled as \(p = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}}\), so upsets (large deviations from \(p\)) induce larger rating changes, while expected results induce smaller changes; ties can be handled symmetrically. Crucially, the authors repeat the random sampling of matches 10,000 times to average out residual order and pairing effects, producing more stable rankings. This approach avoids the need to “ground” an absolute 10-point scale, mitigates order biases observed with GPT-4, captures uncertainty through repeated tournaments, and yields interpretable relative performance (e.g., an Elo gap of 100 implies about a 65% expected win rate), which is more robust for chatbot evaluation where subjective preferences and prompt heterogeneity make absolute scoring unreliable.
