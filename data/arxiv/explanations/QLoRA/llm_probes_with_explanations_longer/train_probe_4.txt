According to the paper 'QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs', the authors implicitly raise a caution about their bias evaluation, namely that it is an incomplete assessment. In the Limitations and Discussion section, they explicitly note that they “only do a limited responsible AI evaluation” of Guanaco and evaluate bias via a single metric (Table~\ref{tbl:crows}), observing a lower average bias score relative to raw pretrained models but acknowledging that “it is unclear if Guanaco does also well when assessed on other types of biases.” This makes clear that their analysis covers a narrow slice of the broader fairness landscape and that generalization across bias categories, populations, and contexts remains untested. By deferring a more comprehensive audit to future work, the authors signal that their current bias results should be interpreted as preliminary rather than definitive.

This caution is further supported by their broader discussion of evaluation limitations and measurement uncertainty throughout the paper. They report moderate and variable agreement between annotators and automated judges (e.g., Fleiss’ $\kappa=0.42$ among human annotators, Kendall’s $\tau=0.43$ and Spearman’s $r=0.55$ between human and GPT-4 system-level rankings), strong order effects in GPT-4 scoring, and evident self-preference biases in GPT-4’s assessments (Elo of $1348$ vs $1176$ under human ratings). These findings underscore that even well-established evaluation pipelines can embed systematic biases and variance that affect conclusions. Combined with the fact that they did not evaluate on several other relevant benchmarks (e.g., BigBench, RAFT, HELM) and that performance is highly sensitive to dataset suitability, it is sensible—and responsible—for the authors to caution that their bias evaluation is incomplete and that a fuller, multi-metric, multi-dataset assessment is needed to substantiate fairness claims.
