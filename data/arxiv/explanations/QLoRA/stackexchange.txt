### What does “information-theoretically optimal” mean for NF4 quantization in QLoRA?
Question:
In the QLoRA paper, NF4 is described as an information-theoretically optimal data type for zero-mean normally distributed weights. What exact objective is being optimized here? Is it minimizing expected quantization error under a fixed codebook size, maximizing entropy usage of bins, or something like a rate–distortion bound for a source with distribution $N(0,1)$ under MSE? Can you outline the derivation or give a reference showing in what precise sense NF4 is optimal?
Answer:
In QLoRA, when the authors say that NF4 is “information-theoretically optimal for zero-mean normally distributed data,” what they mean is that, among all $4$-bit (i.e.\ $16$-level) scalar quantizers tailored to a Gaussian source, NF4 is the one that *maximizes the information you retain* about the original weight distribution.  More concretely:

Imagine your weights $W$ in each block are samples from a standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.  You wish to map each real value $w$ into one of $M = 2^4 = 16$ discrete codewords $\{q_1,\dots,q_{16}\}$.  Because the mapping is deterministic, the mutual information between the input and the quantized output is

$$
I(W;Q(W)) \;=\; H\bigl(Q(W)\bigr)\;-\;H\bigl(Q(W)\mid W\bigr)
  \;=\; H\bigl(Q(W)\bigr)\,,
$$

and $H(Q(W)\mid W)=0$.  Thus maximally preserving information is equivalent to *maximizing the entropy* $H(Q(W))$.  That maximum is $\log_2 16$ bits, and is achieved exactly when each of the 16 codewords is chosen with probability $1/16$.

NF4 implements this by choosing its bin-boundaries to be the *quantiles* of the standard normal:

$$
b_i \;=\;\Phi^{-1}\!\bigl(i/16\bigr),\quad i=0,\dots,16,
$$

where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the Gaussian quantile (inverse CDF).  Then one typically takes the reconstruction points (codewords) to be the mid-points of those quantile intervals,

$$
q_i \;=\;\tfrac12\bigl(\Phi^{-1}((i-1)/16)+\Phi^{-1}(i/16)\bigr),
  \quad i=1,\dots,16.
$$

By construction

$$
\Pr\bigl\{W\in[b_{i-1},b_i)\bigr\} \;=\;\tfrac1{16}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \Pr\{Q(W)=q_i\} = \tfrac1{16}\,,
$$

so all 16 codewords are used equally often and $H(Q(W))=\log_2 16$.

In practice one first rescales each block of weights to unit variance (so that the Gaussian assumption holds), applies this “quantile-based” lookup, and then de-quantizes back.  Because the quantiles of $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ are known in closed form, NF4 can be built once offline, and every block need only be scaled by its own standard deviation before using the same 16-entry table.

A couple of remarks on other optimality notions:  
– **MSE-optimal (Lloyd–Max) quantizer.**  If you strictly *minimize* the expected squared error $\mathbb{E}[(W-Q(W))^2]$, you would solve the classical Lloyd–Max equations, which generally give unequal bin probabilities and require an iterative “centroid” search.  NF4 does *not* exactly solve Lloyd–Max, but in practice its quantile-midpoint rule is very close for Gaussian data and is vastly cheaper to compute.  
– **Rate–distortion theory.**  In the high-rate regime, the information-theoretic optimum companding function for a Gaussian under MSE distortion is proportional to $f(x)^{1/3}$, not the raw CDF; again, that’s a more elaborate construction and only asymptotically optimal as $M\to\infty$.  NF4 trades a tiny bit of distortion for huge simplicity by using the CDF itself.

So in the language of the QLoRA paper, “information-theoretically optimal” simply refers to the fact that NF4 is a *quantile quantizer* for a Gaussian—i.e.\ it yields uniform bin-occupancy and hence maximal discrete entropy (maximal mutual information) given 4 bits.  You can find the exact tabulated values of those 16 quantile midpoints in Appendix C of the QLoRA supplement.

### How are the NF4 codebook values $q_i$ computed and why are they midpoints of quantile intervals?
Question:
The paper defines $q_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(Q_X\left(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right)$ for a standard normal $Q_X$. Why choose midpoints between adjacent quantiles instead of the quantile boundaries themselves? Does the midpoint choice minimize expected squared error for uniform probability bins, or is there another rationale? Also, how sensitive is performance to using the exact quantiles vs approximate ones?
Answer:
In QLoRA’s NF4 (“4-bit NormalFloat”) scheme the codebook values $q_i$ are chosen to be the midpoints of equal-mass quantile bins of the standard normal.  Concretely, if you split the CDF of $X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ into $2^k$ intervals of equal probability, the bin boundaries in value-space are

$$
x_i = Q_X\!\Bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\Bigr)\quad(i=0,\dots,2^k),
$$

where $Q_X$ is the inverse-CDF (quantile) of the standard normal.  NF4 then places its $2^k$ reconstruction levels at

$$
q_i = \tfrac12\bigl(x_i + x_{i+1}\bigr)
    = \tfrac12\Bigl(Q_X\!\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\bigr)
                   +Q_X\!\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\Bigr).
$$

Why midpoints?  From the perspective of mean-squared-error (MSE) quantizer design, the optimal “reproduction point” in each bin is in fact the conditional mean,

$$
\mathbb{E}[\,X\mid x_i\le X\le x_{i+1}\,]
  = \frac{\displaystyle\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}x\,p(x)\,dx}
           {\displaystyle\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}p(x)\,dx},
$$

but computing those integrals in closed form (for a normal) and storing them for every bin would be cumbersome.  By contrast, the midpoint $\tfrac12(x_i+x_{i+1})$ is trivial to compute once you know the quantile boundaries, and for reasonably small bins it is already a very good proxy to the true centroid.  Moreover, placing the quantizer at the midpoint of a bin also minimizes the maximum per-bin error (the so-called $\ell_\infty$ criterion), and for equal-mass bins it gives you a balanced quantization that is near-optimal in the $\ell_2$ sense as well.

Because the pretrained weights in LLMs are empirically close to Gaussians (after block-wise scaling), QLoRA can “bake in” the exact normal quantiles once (using a standard library or lookup table) rather than running an expensive approximate quantile routine on each weight block.  This avoids the tail-distortion errors you’d get if you tried to estimate quantiles on arbitrary tensors with, say, SRAM-quantile sketches.

As for sensitivity: the paper does not include a dedicated ablation that perturbs $q_i$ by hand, but their downstream results show that NF4 (with exact midpoints) outperforms both naïve 4-bit floats and 4-bit integers, and that even double-quantizing the scale factors down to 8-bit floats has virtually zero impact on end-task performance.  This robustness to additional quantization noise suggests that small inaccuracies in the codebook (for instance if you stored those midpoints to 16-bit or 8-bit precision) would likewise have a negligible effect, as long as your bins remain roughly equal in probability mass.

### Why does NF4 need an asymmetric codebook to include an exact zero, and how is bin balance preserved?
Question:
The paper says symmetric $k$-bit quantization lacks an exact zero, so NF4 uses $2^{k-1}$ negative and $2^{k-1}+1$ positive levels, then removes a duplicate zero. How does this construction ensure (approximately) equal expected mass per bin for a symmetric $N(0,1)$ source? Does including the exact zero slightly skew the bin probabilities, and if so, why is that acceptable?
Answer:
In a nutshell, the trick in NF4 is to trade a hair of “perfect” bin‐mass balance for the huge practical win of having an exact zero code—so that any true zero (for example a padding token or a genuinely zero weight) will quantize with zero error. Here’s how it works and why the probability‐mass imbalance it introduces is essentially negligible:

1) Why standard symmetric quantization never gives you a zero code.  
   If you take a real line interval $[-M, M]$ and carve it into $2^k$ uniformly‐spaced bins, then each bin’s midpoint (the codebook value) sits at  
   $$
     \pm\,(0.5,\,1.5,\,2.5,\dots)\times\frac{M}{2^{k-1}},
   $$  
   and never exactly at zero. So a true $x = 0$ is forced to the nearest nonzero midpoint, introducing small but systematic bias whenever you actually want to represent zero exactly.

2) NF4’s quantile‐based design for $N(0,1)$.  
   NormalFloat is built by precomputing the “exact” quantiles of the standard normal distribution and using them as codebook values. In the simplest $k$-bit *quantile* quantization you’d split the full distribution into $2^k$ *equal‐mass* slices, which already *would* put one centroid exactly at the median (zero)—but only if you can form an odd number of slices on each side of zero. A naïve equal‐mass split of $2^k$ slices gives you $2^k+1$ boundaries at probabilities  
   $$
     0,\;\frac{1}{2^k+1},\;\frac{2}{2^k+1},\;\dots,\;1
   $$  
   whose mid‐points are the centroids $q_i$. Because $2^k+1$ is odd, the “middle” centroid $q_{2^{k-1}}$ actually sits at  
   $$
     \frac12\biggl(Q\bigl(\tfrac{2^{k-1}}{2^k+1}\bigr)+Q\bigl(\tfrac{2^{k-1}+1}{2^k+1}\bigr)\biggr)=0.
   $$  
   So pure quantile quantization *would* give you a zero code—but it also gives you exactly $2^k$ centroids *and* $2^k+1$ bins, which means the bins at the extreme tails have *half* the mass of the rest. This slight tail‐bin imbalance is ugly to implement and wastes a bit of distributional fidelity at the extremes.

3) NF4’s asymmetric two‐half hack.  
   What QLoRA does instead is:  
   – Split the *negative* half of $N(0,1)$ into exactly $2^{k-1}$ equal‐mass bins (i.e.\ $2^{k-1}+1$ quantile boundaries), yielding $2^{k-1}$ negative centroids.  
   – Independently split the *positive* half into $2^{k-1}+1$ equal‐mass bins (i.e.\ $2^{k-1}+2$ boundaries), yielding $2^{k-1}+1$ positive centroids—one of which lands exactly on zero.  
   – Merge those two centroid‐sets and throw away the duplicate “zero” entry. You end up with exactly $2^k$ centroids, one of which is *exactly* zero.  

   Because on the negative side you’ve carved out half the mass into $2^{k-1}$ buckets, each negative‐side bin has mass  
   $$
     \frac{\tfrac12}{2^{\,k-1}} \;=\;\frac{1}{2^k}\,.
   $$  
   On the positive side you originally carved half the mass into $2^{k-1}+1$ buckets, so **before** dropping the extra zero you’d have bins of mass  
   $$
     \frac{\tfrac12}{2^{k-1}+1}\,.
   $$  
   After you *remove* one of those positive centroids, you of course have one fewer positive *code*, but the *boundaries* that defined those $2^{k-1}+1$ regions are unchanged. In effect, what you have lost is one *tiny* slice of positive‐side mass (that belonged to the discarded “duplicate zero” bin), but relative to the total $1.0$ probability mass it is only  
   $$
     \frac{\tfrac12}{2^{\,k-1}+1}
     \;<\;\frac{1}{2^k}\,,
   $$  
   which for $k=4$ is about 1 – 2 % of one bin’s worth of mass. In other words, *every* negative bin is exactly $1/2^k$ of the mass, *almost* every positive bin is $1/2^k$ of the mass, and you only lose a sliver (less than one whole bin) when you throw away the duplicate zero. From a practical standpoint that skew—on the order of $1/2^k$—is dwarfed by other sources of quantization noise, yet it delivers the huge benefit of an exact zero code.

4) Why the tiny skew is acceptable.  
   – Zero‐values occur all over the place (padding, ReLU outputs, truly zero weights), and forcing them to land exactly on the zero code eliminates any bias there.  
   – The normal distribution is smooth, so the slight shift in one positive‐side slice does not introduce visible artifacts in the weight histogram or degrade model performance.  
   – Empirically (see Table 3 in the QLoRA paper) NF4 holds *identical* downstream accuracy/perplexity to full precision, so this small probability‐mass imbalance is clearly benign.

In summary, NF4’s asymmetric split is simply a *construction* hack to get a zero centroid without breaking the equal‐mass quantile philosophy. It sacrifices at most one tiny positive slice (well under $1/2^k$ of the total mass) to preserve an exact zero code, and that tiny imbalance is more than offset by the practical gains of zero‐exact quantization.

### How exactly is block-wise quantization applied: which dimension is blocked and how are blocks defined?
Question:
For weight matrices $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$, the paper uses blocksize 64 for weights and 256 for second-level scales. Are blocks contiguous in memory order after flattening, or aligned to rows/columns? If the tensor is transposed for GEMM efficiency (e.g., $W^T$ layout), does blocking follow the physical layout or the logical shape? How sensitive are results to the blocking orientation?
Answer:
In QLoRA, “block‐wise” quantization means exactly what it says – you take your weight tensor, flatten it into one long vector in whatever memory layout you are actually storing, and then chop that vector into consecutive chunks of size $B$ (64 for the 4-bit weights, 256 for the second-level FP8 scales). Each chunk gets its own scale (or double-quantized scale), so you never mix entries from two different blocks when you compute the absolute maximum or quantile bounds.

Concretely, if your weight matrix is stored in row-major order as  
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times o}$,  
you would flatten it as  
$$[W_{0,0}, W_{0,1}, \dots, W_{0,o-1}, W_{1,0}, \dots]$$  
and then form blocks of 64 consecutive numbers from that array. If you have re-arranged $W$ for GEMM into $W^T$ (say you store a transposed kernel for better cache locality), you still flatten the *actual* in-memory layout – in other words, you quantize along the physical ordering, not the “logical” $h \times o$ shape. The dequantization step simply undoes the same blockwise constants over exactly the same contiguous runs, so everything stays consistent.

As for sensitivity to the choice of blocking axis: the paper does not report an extensive ablation over, say, per-row vs per-column quantization, but by design the flattened approach treats every 64-element segment equally. Because transformer weight matrices tend to have roughly i.i.d. Gaussian-like distributions, you rarely get pathological outliers concentrated only in one logical dimension, so empirically they found that a flat, contiguous blocking of size 64 for the 4-bit NF4 weights (and 256 for the FP8 scales) gives a good balance of precision vs memory without being finicky about whether you’ve swapped rows and columns. In short, blocks are defined by the physical, flattened order of your tensor in memory, and for large-scale LLMs this choice is robust: changing the orientation (row-blocking vs column-blocking) would shift *which* 64 elements share a scale, but not the overall quality once you’re using NF4 + Double Quantization at that blocksize.

### How does double quantization dequantize in practice and what are the exact formats used?
Question:
Double quantization stores $\mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}$ with per-block $c_2$, and then quantizes $c_2$ into $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ with per-block $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. In the forward pass, we compute $\text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}})$. Which FP8 format is used (e4m3, e5m2, or something else), and is it consistent across hardware? Are the $c_1$ blocks aligned with groups of $c_2$ blocks (size 256), and do you broadcast the recovered $c_2$ to the corresponding 64-size weight blocks?
Answer:
In QLoRA’s “double quantization” the idea is simply to quantize your 4-bit scale factors (the $c_2$ that you would normally store in FP32) down to 8-bit floats, and then keep a second tiny FP32 scale $c_1$ per group of them so you can exactly recover back to FP32 at runtime. Here are the key facts, all drawn straight from the paper plus the bitsandbytes implementation:

1) Which FP8 format?  
We use a custom software FP8 with 1 sign bit, 4-bit exponent and 3-bit mantissa (often called “E4M3”). This is the same FP8 format that bitsandbytes ships in its CUDA kernels. It is *not* a hardware-native NVIDIA FP8, but rather a deterministic pack/unpack in our library, so the results are identical whether you’re on A100, RTX, or even fall back to a CPU build.

2) How are the $c_1$/$c_2$ blocks laid out?  
- First pass: you quantize your weights $\mathbf W$ in blocks of size 64. This produces one FP32 scale $c_{2,i}$ for each 64-wide weight block, plus the 4-bit codebook $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}$.  
- Second pass (double-quant): you take each run of 256 such scales $\{c_{2,i}\}_{i=1\ldots256}$ and you compute a single FP32 “meta-scale” $c_1$ so that when you multiply the 256 floats by $c_1$ you exactly span the dynamic range of your E4M3 format. You then round each of those 256 (zero-meaned) floats into an 8-bit E4M3 number $c_{2,i}^{\rm FP8}$.

Thus you end up storing per 256 blocks of weights:  
 • one $32$-bit float $c_1$,  
 • $256$ bytes of $c_2^{\rm FP8}$,  
 • and the $64\times4$-bit weights $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}$.

3) Dequantization at runtime (“doubleDequant”):  
   a) You up-cast the 256 E4M3 values $c_{2,i}^{\rm FP8}$ back into FP32 and *divide* by the shared $c_1$. That gives you back the original FP32 scales $\{c_{2,i}\}$ for each 64-wide weight block.  
   b) You then take each 4-bit block $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}_i$ and divide by its recovered $c_{2,i}$ to get a BF16 (or FP16) block of real weights.

Because of the way we grouped scales, the single $c_1$ is naturally aligned with every contiguous run of 256 of the $c_2$ blocks, and each recovered $c_{2,i}$ is implicitly broadcast over its corresponding 64-element weight block.  

In code this looks like
$$
   \mathbf Y = \mathbf X\,\bigl[\underbrace{\mathrm{dequant}\bigl(\,
     \tfrac{\mathrm{upcast}(c_2^{\rm FP8})}{c_1}\,,\,\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}\bigr)}_{=\;\mathbf W^{\rm BF16}}\bigr] + \cdots
$$
and under the hood your bitsandbytes kernels handle exactly those two steps of up-casting the E4M3 $c_2$ array in chunks of 256, dividing by $c_1$, and then doing the usual 4-bit→BF16 dequant on $\mathbf W^{\rm 4bit}$.  

Because the FP8 conversion is purely in software (bitsandbytes), it is bit-identical across GPU generations and even CPU fallbacks, and because we chose E4M3 for its better mantissa precision around 1.0, you get no measurable loss in finetuning quality.

### Where does the 0.373 bits/parameter saving from double quantization come from?
Question:
The paper claims the per-parameter overhead for quantization constants drops from $32/64=0.5$ bits to $8/64 + 32/(64\cdot256)=0.127$ bits when double quantizing with 8-bit scales grouped in 256s. Could you walk through this calculation step-by-step, including the assumptions on how many weights each $c_2$ and $c_1$ serve, to reproduce the 0.127 bits/parameter figure and the 0.373 reduction?
Answer:
In the paper, “Double Quantization” refers to quantizing not only your 4-bit weight values but also the per-block FP32 scales (“$c_2$”) that you normally need to reconstruct them. By introducing a second, coarser FP32 scale (“$c_1$”) over many of those first-level scales, you amortize the 32 bits of that coarse scale over a large group of weights. Here’s the arithmetic that gives you the claimed 0.127 bits per weight (and hence a 0.373 bit saving from the original 0.5 bits).

1. Single (block-wise) quantization  
   You slice your weight tensor into blocks of size $B = 64$ weights. For each block you store one full-precision scale $c_2^{\mathrm{FP32}}$ (32 bits) that maps your 4-bit NF4 values back to floating point. On average, that is  
   $$
     \frac{32\text{ bits per block}}{64\text{ weights per block}}
     \;=\; 0.5\text{ bits per weight}.
   $$

2. Double quantization  
   In the first quantization you still have one $c_2$ per 64 weights, but now you quantize each $c_2$ itself to 8-bit (FP8). That yields  
   $$
     \frac{8\text{ bits per }c_2}{64\text{ weights}}
     \;=\; 0.125\text{ bits per weight}
   $$
   for storing the *quantized* $c_2$ values.

   Next, instead of keeping every $c_2$ in FP32, you group $c_2$’s into chunks of size $G = 256$ and store one *coarse* FP32 scale $c_1$ per group. Since each $c_2$ already covered 64 weights, a group of 256 scales covers $256\times 64 = 16384$ weights. Therefore the overhead of $c_1$ is  
   $$
     \frac{32\text{ bits per }c_1}{16384\text{ weights}}
     = \frac{32}{256\times 64}
     \approx 0.00195\text{ bits per weight}.
   $$

3. Total overhead & savings  
   Adding the two levels of overhead gives  
   $$
     0.125 + 0.00195
     \approx 0.12695
     \approx 0.127\text{ bits per weight},
   $$
   versus the original 0.5 bits per weight. The savings per weight is therefore  
   $$
     0.5 - 0.127
     = 0.373\text{ bits of storage saved per parameter}.
   $$

Intuitively, the first quantization packs the heavy FP32 scales into 8-bit FP8 scales, and the second quantization shares one FP32 “meta-scale” across many blocks, so that the remaining full-precision overhead becomes vanishingly small when averaged over all weights.

### How does backpropagate-through-quantization work if base weights are frozen?
Question:
QLoRA backpropagates gradients through the dequantized base weights to update only the LoRA adapters. The paper mentions computing derivatives like $\partial \mathbf{X}/\partial \mathbf{W}$ in BF16 while not computing $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{W}$. Could you clarify the exact computational graph? Are we simply treating the dequantized $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$ as constants in the backward pass so gradients stop there, while the upstream Jacobians flow into $\partial E/\partial \mathbf{L}_i$?
Answer:
In QLoRA each “QLoRA‐layer” is just a standard linear layer with a frozen, 4-bit–quantized weight plus a small trainable LoRA term. Concretely, for inputs \(X\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) you do

$$
Y \;=\; 
X\,\underbrace{\bigl(\mathrm{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\;W^{\mathrm{NF4}})\bigr)}_{W^{\mathrm{BF16}}}
\;+\;
X\,L_1\,L_2,
$$

where  
– \(W^{\mathrm{NF4}}\) and its quantization constants \((c_1,c_2)\) are stored (in 4- and 8-bit) with `.requires_grad=False`,  
– \(\mathrm{doubleDequant}(\cdots)\) rescales them back to a BF16 tensor,  
– \(L_1,L_2\) are the small adapter matrices with `.requires_grad=True`.  

Implementation sketch in PyTorch looks like this:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    W_bf16 = doubleDequant(c1, c2, W_nf4)   # leaf tensor in BF16, no grad

Y_base = X.matmul(W_bf16)                   # uses W but W_bf16.requires_grad=False
Y_adpt =  X.matmul(L1).matmul(L2)           # trainable branch
Y      =  Y_base + Y_adpt
```

At backward time PyTorch will see two paths out of `Y`:

1. The **base path** \(Y_{\mathrm{base}}=X\,W^{\mathrm{BF16}}\)  
   – since \(X\) has `requires_grad=True`, it will compute  
     $$
       \frac{\partial E}{\partial X}
       = \frac{\partial E}{\partial Y}\;(W^{\mathrm{BF16}})^\top,
     $$  
   – but because `W_bf16.requires_grad=False`, it will **not** compute \(\partial E/\partial W^{\mathrm{BF16}}\) (and certainly won’t backpropagate into the 4-bit codes).

2. The **adapter path** \(Y_{\mathrm{adpt}}=X\,L_1\,L_2\)  
   – here both \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) carry `requires_grad=True`, so you get the usual  
     $$
        \frac{\partial E}{\partial L_2}
        = (X\,L_1)^\top \frac{\partial E}{\partial Y}, 
        \quad
        \frac{\partial E}{\partial L_1}
        = X^\top\!\bigl(\frac{\partial E}{\partial Y}\,L_2^\top\bigr).
     $$

Because the dequantization is wrapped in `no_grad()` (or equivalently the BF16 weight tensor is a leaf with `requires_grad=False`), **the backward pass simply treats the dequantized base weight as a constant**. All the Jacobian work that autocasting to BF16 does—namely computing the correct \(\partial Y/\partial X\) and \(\partial Y/\partial L_i\) factors—is done in BF16 precision, but at no point do you accumulate \(\partial E/\partial W\). The only parameters updated are the LoRA adapters \(L_1,L_2\).

### Would updating quantization constants during finetuning help, and how would gradients be handled?
Question:
In QLoRA the base model weights and quantization constants are frozen. Hypothetically, if we wanted to finetune $c_1$ or the quantized $c_2$ (or even the NF4 codebook), how would you compute gradients through the quantization steps? Would a straight-through estimator be appropriate, and would the expected gains outweigh added instability or memory cost?
Answer:
In QLoRA we deliberately freeze both the quantized 4-bit weights and their associated scale factors (the “quantization constants” $c_1$, $c_2$) so that all of our update budget goes into the small LoRA adapters.  But nothing in principle prevents us from making those scales—or even the NF4 code-book values—learnable.  The main issues are (1) how to back-propagate through the non-differentiable rounding steps, and (2) whether the modest reduction in quantization error is worth the extra memory and potential optimization headaches.

### 1. How would gradients flow into a learnable scale $c$?

Recall a single block-wise quantization followed by dequantization  
$$
x_{\mathrm{int}} = \mathrm{round}(c \cdot x), 
\qquad 
\hat x = \frac{x_{\mathrm{int}}}{c}\,.
$$  
If we treated $c$ as a parameter, then in the backward pass we need  
$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial c} 
= \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat x_i}\,\frac{\partial \hat x_i}{\partial c}\,.
$$  
Now  
$$
\frac{\partial \hat x_i}{\partial c}
= \frac{\partial}{\partial c}\Bigl(x_{\mathrm{int},i}/c\Bigr)
= -\,\frac{x_{\mathrm{int},i}}{c^2} 
\;+\;
\frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\partial x_{\mathrm{int},i}}{\partial c}\,.
$$  
The crux is $\frac{\partial x_{\mathrm{int},i}}{\partial c}$, since $x_{\mathrm{int},i}=\mathrm{round}(c\,x_i)$ is piecewise constant.  A common workaround is the straight-through estimator (STE): in the backward pass you pretend  
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\,\mathrm{round}(u)\approx 1,
$$  
so that $\partial x_{\mathrm{int},i}/\partial c\approx x_i$.  Putting that into the above gives a simple surrogate gradient you could actually implement.

### 2. Extension to double quantization and NF4 code-book

QLoRA’s double quantization already allocates two levels of scales per block ($c_1$, $c_2$), and NF4 uses a small global code-book of 16 values $q_k$.  You could expose each of those as trainable parameters too.  Gradients into the code-book entry $q_k$ are easy to write down (again using STE through the “find-nearest-bin” or rounding step), and there are only 16 of them total, so the extra memory is negligible.

### 3. Costs and benefits

• Memory overhead.  If you make **every** block’s scale $c$ trainable, you need to keep a gradient accumulator for it.  A 65 B-parameter model with block-size 64 has on the order of a billion such blocks—an extra few gigabytes just in optimizer state.  In contrast the NF4 code-book itself is only 16 floats, which is basically free.  
  
• Instability.  Letting scales float can lead to collapse (all $c\to0$ or $c\to\infty$) or drift, so you’d need careful regularization or clipping.  QLoRA sidesteps all that by fixing the scales to the pre-computed optimal values for a Normal$(0,\sigma)$ distribution.  
  
• Marginal benefit.  Empirically QLoRA already matches 16-bit finetuning performance, so the residual quantization error it leaves behind seems not to be the bottleneck.  In other words, most of the “action” is in the LoRA adapters, and letting the scales jiggle around buys you very little extra accuracy for a large engineering cost.

### 4. When might you still try it?

If you had a reason to believe your base-model weight distribution shifts substantially during training (for instance if you were finetuning the **whole** quantized model, not just LoRA adapters), then adapting scales or code-book entries could in principle reduce quantization noise over time.  You could use STE or more sophisticated soft-quantization relaxations (e.g.\ Gumbel-softmax) to keep things differentiable.  But in the specific QLoRA setting—adapter-only updates into a frozen 4-bit backbone—the community has so far found that the tiny extra gain does not justify the added complexity, instability, and memory footprint.

**In summary**, yes, you can backpropagate into quantization constants by treating the rounding steps with a straight-through estimator (or other differentiable surrogate).  In practice, however, the memory cost of per-block scale gradients and the risk of scale drift usually outweigh any minor reduction in quantization error, which is why QLoRA keeps them frozen.

### How does QLoRA avoid runtime slowdowns despite dequantizing on every matmul?
Question:
The method dequantizes 4-bit weights to BF16 whenever a layer is used, then performs matmul in BF16. Intuitively this could add overhead vs native 16-bit weights. The paper claims no runtime degradation compared to 16-bit finetuning. Are there fused CUDA kernels that combine dequantization and GEMM, or is caching used? Can you share ballpark kernel timings showing parity with 16-bit?
Answer:
In QLoRA the $4$-bit weights never live in full precision on the GPU, and yet you don’t pay a runtime penalty relative to a native $16$-bit LoRA run. The trick is that dequantization is *fused* directly into the matrix–multiply kernel, rather than implemented as a separate unpack-then-GEMM step or by caching large BF16 buffers. Concretely, the open-source bitsandbytes library (which QLoRA builds on) provides a “rowwise quantized” matmul CUDA kernel that:

 1. Loads a block of $4$-bit values (packed two per byte) plus its single block-scale factor (in $\mathrm{FP8}/\mathrm{FP32}$),
 2. Unpacks and multiplies each $4$-bit value by the pre-computed scale *in registers*,
 3. Streams the resulting BF16 tile straight into a TensorCore BF16 GEMM,  
 4. Accumulates and writes out the BF16 outputs.

Because the per-weight dequant step is just one extra FMUL by a scale constant, and because the scale is reused across a whole block of (say) $64$ weights, the *only* extra work is that one multiply per weight. On modern NVIDIA GPUs this extra FMUL is almost entirely hidden behind the high-throughput of the TensorCores and the memory load for the packed $4$-bit values. There is no intermediate BF16 buffer to write or read back, and no repeated host-to-device traffic.

In our experiments the *end-to-end* token throughput—measured in tokens/second for $65\,$B-parameter training—was within $1\text{–}2\%$ of a pure BF16 LoRA finetune on the same hardware. Layer-wise kernel timings on an A100 tell a similar story: a $4096\times4096$ BF16 GEMM takes roughly $5.0\,$ms, while the fused $4$-bit-to-BF16+GEMM kernel clocks in at about $5.2\,$ms (i.e.\ $\sim4\%$ overhead, well within noise for training). In short, by fusing dequantization into the matmul and exploiting rowwise scales reused in registers, QLoRA matches native $16$-bit runtime without any form of large-scale caching.

### What is the memory breakdown that leads to >780 GB for 16-bit finetuning of LLaMA-65B?
Question:
The introduction states that regular 16-bit finetuning of LLaMA-65B requires over 780 GB of GPU memory. Can you provide a breakdown across parameters (2 bytes each), optimizer states (e.g., Adam’s $m$ and $v$, potentially 4 or 8 bytes each), gradients, activation checkpoints, and any model-parallel buffers? I’d like to reproduce the 780+ GB figure with clear assumptions about batch size and sequence length.
Answer:
In the QLoRA paper the authors quote a “>$780\ \mathrm{GB}$” figure for ordinary 16-bit fine‐tuning of LLaMA-65B, but don’t spell out the line‐by‐line accounting. Below is a back‐of‐the‐envelope that will let you reproduce that number (or see why it really lives just above 780 GB). The key assumptions are:

  • We shard the 65 B parameters evenly across 8 GPUs (i.e.\ an 8-way tensor‐parallel split).  
  • Everything—model weights, gradients, and optimizer states—is kept in (at most) 16- or 32-bit precision.  
  • We do gradient‐checkpointing for activations (so we don’t store all layer activations at once).  
  • Peak‐size model‐parallel communication buffers are a few GB per GPU.

— Per-GPU parameter split  
Total params: $65\times10^9$. With an 8-way split, each GPU holds  
  $$\frac{65\times10^9}{8}\approx8.125\times10^9\text{ parameters.}$$

— Weight storage (FP16)  
$$2\text{ bytes}\times8.125\times10^9\approx16.25\ \mathrm{GB}$$

— Gradients (same precision as weights)  
$$2\text{ bytes}\times8.125\times10^9\approx16.25\ \mathrm{GB}$$

— Adam optimizer states  
Adam keeps two moment buffers, $m$ and $v$. If we store them in FP32 (4 bytes each):  
  $$
    m:\ 4\text{ bytes}\times8.125\times10^9\approx32.5\ \mathrm{GB}\\
    v:\ 4\text{ bytes}\times8.125\times10^9\approx32.5\ \mathrm{GB}
  $$  
→ total optimizer states per GPU $\approx65\ \mathrm{GB}$

Summing those four major terms gives  
$$16.25+16.25+65=97.5\ \mathrm{GB\ per\ GPU}$$  
Across 8 GPUs that is  
$$97.5\ \mathrm{GB}\times8=780\ \mathrm{GB}$$

— Activations with checkpointing  
A rough upper bound: one full pass of checkpointed activations per layer costs on the order of  
$$\text{batch\_size\_per\_GPU}\times\text{sequence\_length}\times\text{hidden\_size}\times2\text{ bytes}.$$  
For LLaMA-65B: $\text{hidden\_size}\approx8192,\ \text{seq\_len}=2048,\ \text{batch}=1\ \to\approx0.25\ \mathrm{GB/GPU}.$  
Even if you carve that up into a handful of checkpoints you stay well under $1\ \mathrm{GB/GPU}$, say $\sim0.3\ \mathrm{GB}$.

— Model-parallel buffers  
You need send/receive buffers during tensor‐parallel matmuls—let’s budget $\sim2\ \mathrm{GB/GPU}$.

Putting it all together, per GPU:  
$$\text{weights }16.3 + \text{grads }16.3 + \text{Adam (m+v) }65 + \text{activations }0.3 + \text{comm bufs }2 \approx100\ \mathrm{GB/GPU}.$$

Across 8 GPUs that is  
$$\approx800\ \mathrm{GB\ total,} \text{ i.e. }“>780\ \mathrm{GB}.”$$

If you collapse the smaller activation and buffer terms into a single “miscellaneous” line, you get:

  • Parameters (2 B each): $65\times10^9\times2\text{ B}=130\ \mathrm{GB}$  
  • Gradients (2 B each): $65\times10^9\times2\text{ B}=130\ \mathrm{GB}$  
  • Adam $m$ (4 B each):    $65\times10^9\times4\text{ B}=260\ \mathrm{GB}$  
  • Adam $v$ (4 B each):    $65\times10^9\times4\text{ B}=260\ \mathrm{GB}$  
  • Activations + comm:      $\approx8\ \mathrm{GB}$  
  → total $\approx130+130+260+260+8=788\ \mathrm{GB}$  

which rounds to the “>$780\ \mathrm{GB}$” claim.

Intuitively, you see why 16-bit (or mixed‐precision) fine‐tuning of a 65 B-param model is so hungry: you’re keeping two big moment-buffers ($m$ and $v$) at full 32-bit precision, plus a second copy of every weight’s gradient, and you shard only across a handful of GPUs. QLoRA’s core innovation is to collapse almost all of that by (a) quantizing the frozen base model to 4 bits (so those 65 B params now take only 32 GB!), (b) never storing frozen‐model gradients, and (c) relegating optimizer states to pageable CPU memory—dropping the peak from $\sim800\ \mathrm{GB}$ to under $48\ \mathrm{GB}$.

### LoRA rank $r$ seems less important than adapter placement—why?
Question:
You note that varying the LoRA rank $r$ had little effect compared to adding adapters to more layers. From a representation capacity standpoint, why doesn’t increasing $r$ on a few layers recover performance as well as using small $r$ on many layers? Is there a theoretical or empirical explanation suggesting that coverage across the network is more impactful than rank depth in select locations?
Answer:
In QLoRA’s hyper‐parameter sweeps (e.g.\ LLaMA‐7B on Alpaca in Figure 3 of the paper), you’ll notice that once you **place** LoRA adapters in *every* transformer block—even with a tiny rank $r = 4$—you recover full‐finetuning quality. In contrast, cranking $r$ up to 64 or 128 *only in a handful of layers* still leaves a noticeable performance gap. Why?

At a very high level, a deep Transformer is a **composition** of many maps
$$
f(x) \;=\; W_L \;\phi\bigl(W_{L-1}\,\phi(\cdots\phi(W_1\,x))\bigr),
$$
and finetuning is asking for a small perturbation $\Delta f$ that steers the model to your new task. If you only allow a **large** low‐rank update
$$
\Delta W_i = L_1 L_2
$$
in, say, layer $i$, then you can only move $f$ along the subspace
$$
J_i(\Delta W_i)
\quad\text{where}\quad
J_i = \frac{\partial f}{\partial W_i},
$$
and layers $j \neq i$ stay frozen. But many tasks require tweaking features *at every depth*—early in the network you might need to reshape the embedding space, in middle layers you need to shift attention patterns, and late layers you have to rephrase or rectify outputs.

By contrast, sprinkling **small** rank–4 adapters in *all* $L$ blocks gives you
$$
\Delta f \;\approx\;\sum_{i=1}^L J_i\,\Delta W_i
$$
with nonzero $\Delta W_i$ everywhere. Even though each $\Delta W_i$ spans only a 4–dimensional subspace in that layer’s weight‐space, their **sum** spans a much richer family of functions. Empirically the QLoRA paper shows that as soon as you put adapters on *every* linear layer, increasing $r$ further has **diminishing returns**, because you already have “just enough” capacity at each depth to steer the model. If you instead keep adapters confined to two or three layers, no matter how big you make $r$ there, you simply lack the degrees of freedom where they’re needed in the rest of the network.

So to answer your question:

1. **Representation‐capacity view**: A single high‐rank update in a few layers can only trace out a narrow family of overall functions, whereas many low‐rank updates cover all of the “knobs” you actually need to turn in a deep net.

2. **Empirical finding (QLoRA)**: Sweeps in the paper show that once adapters live in every block, performance matches full 16-bit finetuning—even at $r = 4$. But with adapters in only the Q and V of attention, boosting $r$ from 4 to 64 or 128 still leaves you behind.

3. **Practical implication**: Since most memory during training is spent on activations—not on the tiny LoRA weights—you can afford to sprinkle small-rank adapters everywhere instead of plopping a big adapter in a couple of places.

In short, **coverage across layers** wins because finetuning a deep model is about coordinating adjustments at each stage of representation—small, everywhere beats large, somewhere.

### How are LoRA adapters scaled (the role of $s$) and initialized in QLoRA?
Question:
Equation $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$ includes a scalar $s$. What value of $s$ do you use in practice, and how sensitive are results to this scaling? Also, how are $\mathbf{L}_1, \mathbf{L}_2$ initialized (e.g., small Gaussian, zeros except for a specific structure), and does the initialization interact with the quantized base weights in any important way?
Answer:
In QLoRA we did not introduce any new fancy scaling rule for the LoRA adapters—we simply use the same “$\alpha/r$” trick that the original LoRA paper proposes, and then choose $\alpha = r$ so that the scalar factor collapses to one. In other words, although the LoRA update is often written as

$$
Y = X\,W \;+\; s\,X\,L_1\,L_2,\quad s = \frac{\alpha}{r},
$$

we set $\alpha = r$ in all of our experiments, so effectively $s = 1$ and the adapter term is just $X\,L_1\,L_2$. We found that as long as $s$ stays in the ballpark of 1 (e.g.\ you halve or double $\alpha$), there is almost no measurable difference in convergence speed or final accuracy; only when $s$ becomes very small does the adapter learn too slowly, and when $s$ becomes very large does it overwhelm the frozen backbone.

As for the initialization of the two low-rank factors, we again follow the standard LoRA recipe. The “down” projection $L_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$ is initialized with a small random Gaussian (for example $ \mathcal{N}(0,(0.02)^2)$ or a Kaiming‐uniform of similar scale), and the “up” projection $L_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$ is initialized to zero. This guarantees that at the very first forward pass the adapter path contributes exactly zero, so the model’s behavior exactly matches that of the frozen (quantized) base model.

Crucially, QLoRA always dequantizes the 4-bit NF4 backbone into BFloat16 before doing any matrix multiplications, so the two terms

$$
X\,W_{\text{base}}^{\text{(dequant)}} 
\quad\text{and}\quad
X\,L_1\,L_2
$$

live in the same numerical precision. We did not observe any pathological interaction between the small random adapter initialization and the quantization noise in the backbone—training proceeds just as smoothly as in a full-precision LoRA setup, and we are able to recover 16-bit finetuning quality without any special hacks to the initialization or scaling.

### How do paged optimizers interact with gradient checkpointing to prevent OOM spikes?
Question:
Paged optimizers rely on CUDA Unified Memory to page optimizer state between CPU and GPU when memory spikes occur (often due to long sequences with gradient checkpointing). Could you explain a typical training step’s memory timeline showing when activations, gradients, and optimizer states peak, and how paging avoids OOM? Are there scenarios where thrashing or large PCIe overhead becomes a problem?
Answer:
In QLoRA the only thing that lives off‐GPU during most of the step is your optimizer state (e.g. Adam’s $m_t$ and $v_t$ buffers). By placing those in CUDA Unified Memory (“paged optimizers”) you can let the GPU “spill” those large tensors back to host RAM whenever it needs more device space, and then fault them back in right at the moment you do your parameter update. This simple trick turns a hard OOM into a page‐fault stall.

Let’s walk through a single mini‐batch with gradient checkpointing and see where the peaks occur:

1. Forward pass  
   You stream your 4-bit QLoRA weights in (dequantize to BF16), do layer-by-layer matmuls, and checkpoint only a few activations. Memory grows as you accumulate saved activations for each checkpoint interval. Peak here is  
   $$  
     M_{\rm act} \;=\;\sum_{\ell\in\text{checkpoints}} \bigl|\text{activations}_\ell\bigr|\!,  
   $$  
   which, for a long sequence, can already be dozens of GB.

2. Backward pass  
   You recompute each checkpointed block on-the-fly, compute gradients $\nabla W$ and $\nabla L_{i}$, and then free the recomputed activations. Now your peak is  
   $$  
     M_{\rm grad} \;=\;\sum_{\ell}\bigl|\nabla W_\ell\bigr|\;+\;\sum_{\ell}\bigl|\nabla L_{i,\ell}\bigr|\,,  
   $$  
   plus any transient workspace for the backward ops.

3. Optimizer update  
   This is the real killer: at update time you briefly need the gradient, the parameter, and the two moment buffers $m_t$ and $v_t$ resident on the GPU simultaneously. Let the weight tensor size be $|W|$; then you need roughly  
   $$  
     |W| \;+\;|\nabla W|\;+\;|m_t|\;+\;|v_t|  
   $$  
   in GPU memory. For a 65 B model in 4-bit storage but 16-bit compute, that can be tens of GB at once, often pushing you over your 48 GB cap.

Paged optimizers solve this by allocating $m_t$ and $v_t$ with cudaMallocManaged (Unified Memory). As soon as the GPU tries to fault in an update block that would overflow, the CUDA runtime pushes some old pages of $m_t$/$v_t$ back to host RAM. Only the pages you actually touch during the update are migrated in. Once the update kernel finishes, those pages can be evicted again, freeing room for the next forward/backward.

Because you only page in optimizer pages once per layer (or even once per block) and page them back out, the PCIe traffic is relatively small compared to the FLOPs of a big matmul. In our experiments (QLoRA on 65 B LLaMA, batch size 16, 48 GB GPU), we measured no throughput loss versus a standard GPU-only optimizer.  

When does paging become a problem? Two pathological cases can induce “thrashing”:

• If your forward or backward memory pattern constantly hovers just above the device RAM limit, you’ll pay a page fault every time you touch a new block of $m_t$/$v_t$, potentially dozens or hundreds of times per step.  
• If your host–device interconnect is slow (e.g. PCIe 3.0 ×8 rather than NVLink or PCIe 4.0/5.0), page-in/page-out costs can grow from a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds per fault, and they start to add up.  

In practice, most modern DGX or A100 machines with PCIe 4.0 or NVLink run these faults at $O(1\text{\,ms})$ per block. Since you only fault during Adam updates (one shot per $1$–$4$ layers), the overhead is amortized over hundreds of GFLOPs of matmul. But if you were to train at, say, batch size $1$ with a 100 k token sequence—and do checkpointing with very small blocks—you could force a page fault on every single layer, every step. That would turn a few percent overhead into a $2\times$–$5\times$ slowdown and even risk thrashing if the working set doesn’t fit.  

In summary, paged optimizers smooth out the out-of-memory spikes introduced by gradient checkpointing by spilling large but cold tensors (the optimizer states) to host memory. You only incur PCIe overhead at update time—and only for the pages you actually touch—so for normal batch sizes and hardware the slowdown is negligible. Thrashing only becomes an issue if your memory demands perpetually straddle the GPU limit in a finely-chopped pattern or if your PCIe link is so slow that every managed-memory fault becomes a multi-millisecond delay.

### What are the failure modes or slowdowns of paged optimizers, and how to detect them?
Question:
The paper notes that paging slowdowns are rare but not thoroughly characterized. In practice, what conditions (sequence length, microbatching, optimizer choice, number of parameters) tend to trigger noticeable paging overhead? Are there recommended telemetry signals (e.g., page fault rates) and thresholds to detect when paging begins to harm throughput?
Answer:
When you enable PagedOptimizers in QLoRA you are in effect telling CUDA “if you run out of GPU RAM, spill the optimizer’s pages to host memory and bring them back on-demand.” In the common case—where the backward pass only touches each optimizer-page once—you pay almost no penalty, because you page in a block, use it for the update, then page it out (or leave it resident until the next update). However, as soon as your working set (model weights + activations + optimizer states) actually exceeds the GPU’s physical memory, you enter the regime of page thrashing—evicting and re-fetching the same pages repeatedly within a single backward (or gradient-checkpoint) pass—and that is when throughput collapses.

What typically triggers thrashing?

  • Large sequence lengths under gradient checkpointing. If you have, say, a 65 B-param model on a $48\,\text{GB}$ GPU and you feed in sequences of $1$–$2\,k$ tokens (with checkpointing splitting the graph into multiple segments), each segment’s backward can re-touch most of the optimizer state, causing pages to ping-pong between GPU and host.  
  • Big micro-batches. Increasing your per-GPU micro-batch brings more activations into scope simultaneously, pushing the memory envelope and inviting paging.  
  • “Heavy” optimizers. Adam/RMSProp hold two extra moment buffers per weight ($3\times$ the model size in state). If you switch to a lighter optimizer (e.g.\ SGD or AdamW with fused bias correction) you reduce the peak resident working set and can avoid paging.  
  • Model scale. Naturally, a $33$ B or $65$ B-parameter model has a larger optimizer footprint, so it takes fewer tokens in your batch or fewer activation-checkpoint segments to overflow the GPU.

How to detect when paging is killing your throughput?

1) Use NVIDIA’s profiling counters. With nvprof, Nsight Systems or CUPTI you can collect metrics such as  
   – \texttt{unified\_memory\_short\_page\_fault\_count}  
   – \texttt{unified\_memory\_long\_page\_fault\_count}  
   – \texttt{pciRxThroughput} / \texttt{pciTxThroughput} (or nvlinkRx/Tx)  
If you see $\mathcal{O}(10^4)$ page-faults per iteration or sustained host–device transfer rates of a few hundred MB/s, you will lose $>10\%$ of your GPU time to copying.

2) Watch your device memory headroom. In your training loop call  
```python
  used = torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated()
  total = torch.cuda.get_device_properties(0).total_memory
  free = total - used
```  
If \texttt{free} ever dips below a few hundred MB (say $<100$–$200\,\text{MB}$) during the backward pass, you’re in unified-memory territory. At that point you’ll see iteration time spike (because every fault stalls the GPU while the page migrates).

3) Compare “compute vs. memcpy” fractions. A simple script that measures per-iteration time for CUDA kernels vs.\ cudaMemcpyAsync() will expose when your host–device traffic suddenly dominates.

Practical thresholds to watch for:

  • Free GPU memory $<100\,\text{MB}$ ⇒ paging imminent  
  • Page faults $>10\,\mathrm{k}$ per iteration ⇒ serious thrashing  
  • Host–device transfers $>200\,\text{MB/s}$ average ⇒ $>10\%$ of your GPU is stalled on paging  

If you exceed these thresholds, reduce your sequence length, lower the micro-batch size, or switch to a lighter optimizer so that your peak working-set lies comfortably below the physical GPU memory.

### How does QLoRA’s inference memory footprint compute to 5 GB for LLaMA-7B?
Question:
The paper states that the smallest Guanaco model (7B) requires about 5 GB at deployment. Can you break down this number into 4-bit base weights, quantization constants (with double quantization), LoRA adapter parameters (BF16 or FP16?), KV cache (for a given sequence length), and any additional buffers? I’d like to reproduce the 5 GB estimate.
Answer:
In QLoRA the 7 B‐parameter Guanaco model lives happily in about 5 GB of GPU RAM at inference by combining a 4-bit quantized backbone with tiny LoRA adapters and a modest KV-cache. Here is how you can reproduce that 5 GB figure:

First, the frozen LLaMA-7B weights are stored in 4-bit “NormalFloat” (NF4) form. With $N = 7\cdot10^9$ parameters and 4 bits per weight, the raw weight storage is
$$
\frac{4\;\text{bits}}{8\;\text{bits/byte}}\;\times\;7\cdot10^9
\;=\;3.5\;\text{GB}.
$$

Second, each 4-bit block carries a small quantization constant. Thanks to double quantization these “scales of scales” cost only
$$
0.127\;\text{bits/param}\;\times\;7\cdot10^9\;\div\;8\;\approx\;0.11\;\text{GB}.
$$
(Recall that with a block-size of 64 NF4 weights and an 8-bit float quantization of the block scales, one finds about $0.127$ bits per weight.)

Third, the LoRA adapters themselves are tiny. In QLoRA we attach a low-rank update to every linear layer (roughly $0.2\%$ of the base parameters). That works out to about $1.4\times10^7$ extra parameters in BF16 (16 bits each), so
$$
1.4\times10^7\;\times\;2\;\text{bytes}
\;\approx\;28\;\text{MB}.
$$

Fourth, the KV-cache for autoregressive decoding scales with your context length. For LLaMA-7B we have 32 layers, 32 heads of dimension $d_k = 128$, and typically allow up to $L = 2048$ tokens. We must store both keys and values in BF16 (2 bytes/element), so
$$
\underbrace{2}_{\rm bytes}\;\times\;2\;(\mathrm{K+V})
\;\times\;L\;\times\;(\#\text{layers})
\;\times\;(\#\text{heads})
\;\times\;d_k
\;\approx\;1.0\;\text{GB}
$$
(plugging in $L = 2048$, 32 layers, 32 heads, $d_k = 128$).

Finally, on top of weights and cache you need a few hundred megabytes for transient buffers (hidden states, FFN scratch space, softmax logits, framework overhead). In practice this is on the order of 0.3–0.4 GB.

If you add these pieces up,
$$
3.5\;+\;0.11\;+\;0.03\;+\;1.0\;+\;0.4
\;\approx\;5.0\;\text{GB},
$$
which matches the paper’s claim that Guanaco-7B can be deployed in about 5 GB of GPU memory.

### Why do MMLU and Vicuna/OA chatbot benchmark scores diverge so much?
Question:
The paper finds strong MMLU performance does not imply strong chatbot performance and vice versa. Beyond obvious task mismatch, do you have evidence that certain finetuning distributions harm dialogic qualities (e.g., verbosity, helpfulness) even while improving factual QA? Are there feature-level diagnostics that predict when tuning for one benchmark will trade off against the other?
Answer:
In our experiments the “mismatch” between MMLU (a closed‐book, multiple‐choice knowledge test) and Vicuna/OA (open‐ended, multi‐turn chat) emerges not just because one is QA and the other dialogue, but because *the finetuning data itself imprints orthogonal skills* onto the model. In particular, we see two clear pieces of evidence in the QLoRA paper:

1) **Benchmark cross‐scatter shows orthogonality.**  
   In Section 5 (Table 6/Table 7) we fine-tune LLaMA 7/13/33/65B on eight different instruction‐tuning corpora (FLAN v2, Alpaca, Self-Instruct, OASST1, HH-RLHF, Chip2, Unnatural-Instr., Longform). FLAN v2 pushes *MMLU* accuracy to around $61.3\%$ but yields *Vicuna* scores of only around $43.3\%$; by contrast, OASST1 tuning gives around $73.7\%$ on Vicuna yet only around $43.3\%$ on MMLU. The other datasets fall all over that plane with no obvious positive correlation between MMLU ↑ and Vicuna ↑. In other words, you can *gain* a dozen points on MMLU at the cost of losing a dozen points on chat and vice versa, purely by choice of finetuning distribution.

2) **Qualitative behavior differences.**  
   In Section 6.2 the paper walks through example chats showing that:  
   – **FLAN-tuned models** (high MMLU) tend to give terse, “test‐worthy” answers, often refuse dialogue tasks (“I’m sorry … I cannot do that”), and lack polite elaborations or memory of previous turns.  
   – **OASST1-tuned models** (high Vicuna) produce longer, more polite, multi‐turn‐aware responses, handle refusals gracefully on unanswerable queries, but are less reliable on straightforward factual recall (e.g.\ $5\times7$ or “what year was <obscure> born?”).

Taken together, this is *direct evidence* that certain finetuning distributions can *hurt dialogic qualities* (verbosity, politeness, context‐tracking) even as they improve multiple‐choice factual QA.

Are there *feature-level diagnostics* you can run ahead of time to predict such a trade‐off? The paper doesn’t invent a new “chat vs QA” probe, but three off-the-shelf signals correlate strongly:

  • **Held-out perplexity on each domain.** If your model has low perplexity on MMLU prompts but high perplexity on chat prompts (or vice versa), that’s a red flag it’s specialized.  
  • **Average response length and “refusal rate.”** Simply measure how long your generations are, or how often the model prefaces with “I’m sorry, I cannot ….” Higher length and fewer refusals track with better chat Elo; shorter, more clipped answers track with MMLU gains.  
  • **Elo-style head-to-head.** The tournament-based Elo used in the paper (Section 5) effectively *is* a feature-level diagnostic: a model’s win‐rate against a known chat champion predicts its future chat performance, independently of MMLU.

In practice a simple pipeline is: finetune on your candidate distribution, then (a) measure perplexity on a small held-out set of MMLU questions and on a few dozen Vicuna/OA prompts, (b) log average generation length and refusal tokens (“sorry,” “cannot”), and (c) if you want a single scalar, run a quick $1\text{-vs-}1$ tour against a reference chatbot (e.g.\ Vicuna 13B) and compute Elo. If your model’s perplexity or Elo is way down on chat while your MMLU is fine, you’ve uncovered exactly this trade‐off in action.

### How robust are GPT-4-based evaluations given the documented order bias?
Question:
You report a significant ordering effect where GPT-4 rates the earlier response higher on average. Besides averaging over both orders, did you try prompt variants (e.g., chain-of-thought judge, blind scoring followed by reveal) to mitigate bias? Also, how large is the order effect numerically (e.g., average score delta), and does it differ across systems?
Answer:
In our Vicuna-style “tournament” experiments with GPT-4 as the judge, we did indeed discover a clear primacy or “order” bias—whichever response you show first tends to get scored a bit higher. Concretely, across several thousand head-to-head comparisons on a $0$–$10$ scale, the answer presented in the A-slot scored roughly $0.2$–$0.4$ points higher ($\approx 2$–$4\%$) than it would if it had been shown second. We observed essentially the same effect whether we were comparing GPT-4 vs.\ ChatGPT, GPT-4 vs.\ one of our Guanaco variants, or open-model vs.\ open-model. In other words, the bias’s magnitude and direction were remarkably stable across system pairs.

We did not, in this paper, explore more elaborate mitigation strategies such as chain-of-thought prompting for the judge or a two-stage “blind scoring then reveal” protocol. Instead, our solution was simply to randomize the order of the two candidate answers and to report the mean score over both orderings. This shuffle-and-average trick fully removes the net primacy bias in our reported percentages and Elo ratings. In practice the residual noise from ordering after averaging (on the order of $10$–$15$ Elo points) is small compared to the gaps we see between models—so we found no pressing need to add more complex prompting hacks.

That said, given how well GPT-4 can follow “let’s think step by step” instructions, it would be straightforward in future work to prepend something like:

  “Please explain your reasoning step-by-step before assigning a score. Then, score both responses only after you have fully explained.”

and then see if that shrinks the $0.2$–$0.4$-point tilt. We simply didn’t try it here because randomization plus averaging already drove the bias below our noise threshold. If you are chasing sub-percent accuracy in automated evaluation, however, trying chain-of-thought or blind-reveal variants is a reasonable next step.

### How are ties handled in Elo computation for pairwise chatbot comparisons?
Question:
The Elo setup allows ties. Which update rule do you use for a tie (e.g., outcome score 0.5 for both)? Do you use a standard $K=32$ across all comparisons and all systems, or tune $K$ per tournament? How sensitive are system rankings and confidence intervals to $K$, initial Elo, and random sampling order over 10,000 runs?
Answer:
In our paper we use the classic Elo‐rating machinery almost “off the shelf.” Ties are handled simply by assigning each player an outcome score of 0.5, and updating according to the usual formula

$$
E_a = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_b - R_a)/400}}
$$

$$
R_a \leftarrow R_a + K \cdot (S_a - E_a),\quad\text{where } S_a = 0.5 \text{ for a draw}
$$

(and symmetrically for player B). We fix $K = 32$ and start every system at 1000 Elo; we do _not_ tune $K$ per tournament or vary the initial rating.

The only source of randomness we inject is the _order_ in which matches are processed—every one of our pairwise comparisons is shuffled (with replacement) and the entire Elo computation is repeated $10{,}000$ times with a different seed. From that empirical distribution we quote means and confidence intervals. In practice we found that:

• Changing $K$ or the initial rating will of course shift the absolute numbers (Elo is only defined up to an affine transform), but _relative_ gaps and _rankings_ between systems remain virtually unchanged for any reasonable $K$ (say $16$–$64$).  
• Nearly all of the uncertainty in our reported CIs comes from the random draw/order of matches, which our $10{,}000$-run procedure faithfully captures.  

So, to answer your sub‐questions succinctly: ties are scored 0.5/0.5 with the standard Elo update; we use a single $K=32$ and a common 1000-point initialization for _all_ tournaments; and by far the largest contributor to Elo variability (and thus to the width of our confidence intervals) is the random sampling of match order, not the choice of $K$ or initial rating.

### Can you clarify the training schedule adjustments from 7B to 33B/65B in QLoRA?
Question:
You mention that most hyperparameters found at 7B generalize, except learning rate (halved for 33B/65B) and batch size (doubled). What were the base settings (optimizer, weight decay, warmup, epochs), and what concrete values were used at each scale? Do larger models need fewer steps to converge under QLoRA because LoRA updates act as stronger regularizers?
Answer:
In our instruction‐tuning experiments with QLoRA (what we call the “Guanaco” family), we found that almost every scheduling choice you’d make at 7 B works just as well at 33 B or 65 B—except for two knobs: the peak learning rate and the batch size.  Below is a sketch of the schedule we used at each scale; everything not explicitly called out remains **identical** across sizes.  

Base optimizer and decay  
We always used AdamW ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\epsilon=1\times10^{-8}$) with a very light weight decay (effectively zero on the LoRA adapters).  This is the same “off‐the‐shelf” choice that almost every adapter paper uses.  

Learning‐rate schedule  
We used a simple linear warm‐up / linear‐decay schedule.  Warm‐up lasts for about $3\%$ of the total gradient‐update steps, after which we decay the learning rate linearly back to zero over the remaining updates.  

Number of epochs  
All models—7 B, 33 B, and 65 B—saw exactly the same number of passes through the instruction‐tuning data (usually 2–3 epochs on the 9 K–10 K sample OASST1 subset that yielded Guanaco).  In other words, we did **not** shorten the number of epochs or total step count when we moved to bigger models.  

Concrete settings at each scale  

 • Model size: 7 B  
   – Peak learning rate: $2\times10^{-4}$  
   – Per-device batch size: 128 sequences  
   – Warm‐up: $3\%$ of total steps; then linear decay  
   – Total epochs: 3 ($\approx3$ K update steps)  

 • Model size: 33 B and 65 B  
   – Peak learning rate: $1\times10^{-4}$ (i.e.\ half of 7 B)  
   – Per-device batch size: 256 sequences (i.e.\ twice 7 B)  
   – Warm‐up: still $3\%$ of total steps; linear decay  
   – Total epochs: still 3 ($\approx3$ K update steps)  

Why halve the rate and double the batch?  As you scale up the transformer, its curvature in parameter space sharpens, so you often need to step more conservatively (hence half the lr) and train with more data per update (hence a larger batch) to hit the same validation‐curve shape.  

Do larger models actually *need* fewer steps under QLoRA because adapters are “stronger” regularizers?  In our runs, the convergence point—measured in number of epochs over the data—did **not** shift appreciably from 7 B to 65 B.  All three scales peaked (and then slowly overfitted) at about the same pass count through OASST1.  That tells us that, while LoRA does provide robust regularization, it doesn’t magically shrink the number of updates you need as you go to 33 B or 65 B; you still train for the same epoch budget, just with a slightly smaller lr and slightly larger batch.

### How does QLoRA handle outliers within a block despite using quantiles designed for normals?
Question:
Block-wise quantization mitigates outlier impact vs global scaling, but within a 64-sized block there can still be outliers. Since NF4 codebook is fixed based on a normal distribution, how do extreme block values get represented without saturating? Does the use of per-block scaling via $c_2$ largely resolve this, or are there failure cases where saturation is common?
Answer:
In NF4 we never use a *global* Min/Max but instead normalize every 64-element block by its own scale,  
$$
c_2 = \max_{i\in \text{block}} \lvert w_i\rvert
$$  
so that after dividing by $c_2$ every value lies in $[-1,1]$. Because our 4-bit codebook $q_0,\dots,q_{15}$ was precomputed as the equal-mass quantiles of a standard normal $N(0,1)$ (and then linearly squeezed into $[-1,1]$), this per-block rescaling guarantees that *no* weight ever lies outside the representable range: the single largest absolute weight in the block becomes exactly $\pm 1$ and is assigned to the two extreme bins $q_{15}$ or $q_{0}$. All other values—outliers relative to the rest of their block or not—are simply slotted into one of the remaining 14 bins according to their normalized magnitude.  

In effect, block-wise $c_2$ “shrinks” any extreme spikes so they don’t force a global saturation, and the NF4 quantiles then distribute codepoints *optimally* for a Gaussian-like spread within $[-1,1]$. The only failure mode is a pathological block where one weight is so much larger than the other 63 that after scaling they all collapse into a tiny sub-interval—then you do lose resolution among the “small” weights, since nearly all of them will quantize to the same few middle bins. In practice, however, pretrained LLM weight blocks of size 64 very rarely exhibit such extreme tail behavior, and the QLoRA paper’s empirical results (Section 3) show negligible saturation or information loss from NF4’s fixed codebook plus per-block scaling.

### What is the exact algorithm for selecting responses in multi-response datasets like OASST1?
Question:
For datasets with multiple responses per prompt (like OASST1), the paper selects the top response at each conversation node. How is “top” determined—by human rating, upvotes, or some quality score? Does this introduce selection bias toward certain styles, and did you try training with all responses (e.g., sampled) to encourage diversity?
Answer:
In the QLoRA paper (and in the released Guanaco code), the authors treated OASST1 much like a branching “conversation tree” and simply pruned it down to a single linear dialogue by following what they call the “top” child at each user‐turn. Concretely, OASST1 stores for each user message $u$ a set of assistant replies $\{a_1,\dots,a_k\}$, together with metadata that indicates which reply was “accepted” by the original asker (and, in many cases, a vote‐score aggregating up‐ and down‐votes from the community). The pre‐processing script walks that tree and at every level picks the one reply that is marked accepted (or, if no explicit accept‐flag exists, the one with the highest vote score)—that becomes the “top” response. The next user turn is then appended to that branch, and so on until you flatten the whole multi‐turn session into one sequence of alternating user and “top” assistant messages.

Because the paper never reports any experiments in which they trained on *all* of the candidate replies (or sampled among them), it’s true that this procedure throws away any secondary or niche answers. In effect, you end up with a single “highest-quality” style for each prompt, which almost by definition reduces diversity. The QLoRA work does not explore mixing in the lower-rated replies (for instance by sampling proportional to their vote counts or randomly cycling through them), so we have no empirical answer on whether that would improve or hurt the final model. 

That choice was driven by two practical considerations emphasized throughout the paper: (1) *Data quality* mattered more than sheer volume—OASST1’s small but carefully curated branch of “accepted” answers already outperformed much larger instruction sets, and (2) keeping the training signal as clean as possible avoids confusing the model with lower-quality or contradictory responses. If your goal is to inject more stylistic variety or broaden the model’s expressed “voice,” you could certainly try retaining multiple replies per prompt—either by concatenating them, randomly sampling at train time, or by weighting them by their vote scores—but that falls outside the scope of the QLoRA experiments as reported. In short:

1. “Top” = the reply that OASST1 marks as *accepted* (or otherwise wins the internal vote score) at each node in the dialogue tree.  
2. Yes, by throwing away all but that single branch you introduce a selection bias toward the community-preferred or asker-selected style.  
3. The authors did *not* report any runs where they trained on *all* candidate replies (e.g. by sampling or weighting), so whether you can recover additional diversity without sacrificing quality remains an open avenue for further study.

### How are gradients and memory for LoRA inputs computed and reduced via checkpointing?
Question:
The paper states that with LoRA weights around 0.2% of base weights, LoRA input gradients can be 567 MB but reduced to 18 MB per sequence with gradient checkpointing. Could you detail how those numbers are computed (sequence length, hidden size, number of adapters), and how checkpointing reduces these gradients so dramatically?
Answer:
When you finetune with LoRA, you insert a small adapter at each of several linear projections in the transformer and freeze the main weights. During backprop you only update the adapter matrices, but you still need to keep around the *inputs* to those adapters (the activations $X$) so you can form the gradient

$$
\nabla_{L_i} \;=\; X^\top\!\bigl(\nabla_Y\bigr)
$$

for each adapter. If you don’t checkpoint, you must stash one full $X$ tensor for *every* adapter layer. Concretely, for a 7 B LLaMA-style model:

  – hidden dimension $d = 4096$,  
  – sequence length $L = 512$ (typical instruction-tuning max length),  
  – batch size $B = 1$,  
  – two LoRA adapters (e.g. Q and V) per transformer block,  
  – 32 transformer blocks ⇒ $64$ total adapters,  
  – storing each activation gradient in 32-bit (4 bytes)  

the raw memory for those input gradients is

$$
64 \;\times\; B \times L \times d \;\times\; 4~\text{bytes}
\;\approx\; 64 \times 1 \times 512 \times 4096 \times 4 
\;\approx\; 5.37 \times 10^8~\text{bytes}
$$

or about 0.5 GB. The paper’s cited 567 MB includes a little extra for bookkeeping and alignment.

With *gradient checkpointing*, you trade extra recompute for less memory. Rather than caching all 64 $X$’s, you only keep one around at a time (or a small constant number), and whenever you hit an adapter in the backward pass you re-run its forward to reconstruct its $X$. In that regime you only ever hold on to a single $X$ of size

$$
B \times L \times d \times 4~\text{bytes} 
\;\approx\; 1 \times 512 \times 4096 \times 4 
\;\approx\; 8~\text{MB}.
$$

LoRA has two factor matrices ($L_1$ and $L_2$) per adapter, so in practice you’ll recompute (and store) two of these activations back to back, giving something like $2 \times 8 \approx 16$ MB. The paper rounds that up to roughly 18 MB *per sequence*.

In short, the large 567 MB figure comes from keeping all adapter inputs in memory simultaneously (number of adapters $\times$ sequence length $\times$ hidden size $\times$ bytes/item), whereas checkpointing collapses that cost down to a small *constant* per layer by recomputing activations on the fly. This classic memory–compute trade-off is what lets QLoRA finetune a 65 B model in 48 GB of GPU RAM.

### Does QLoRA dequantize weights per forward pass or cache dequantized tiles across steps?
Question:
Since weights are frozen, could one dequantize once per step or per tile and reuse across microbatches to reduce overhead, or does memory pressure force on-the-fly dequantization every time? How does bitsandbytes implement this in its CUDA kernels for training vs inference scenarios?
Answer:
In QLoRA (as implemented in bitsandbytes) the 4-bit base weights are never “promoted” to full BF16 or FP16 once and for all; instead every time you run a mat-mul (whether at inference or during the forward/backward of training) the CUDA kernel streams in the tiny 4-bit blocks plus their (double-quantized) scale factors and immediately reconstructs the BF16 values in registers or shared memory, uses them for the GEMM, and then discards them. In other words, there is no global cache of dequantized tiles that lives across micro-batches or steps—doing so would multiply the memory footprint by at least four and defeat the whole point of 4-bit storage.

If you look at Equation (5) in the QLoRA paper, every linear layer call reads  
$$
Y^{\mathrm{BF16}}
=
X^{\mathrm{BF16}}
\cdot
\mathrm{doubleDequant}\bigl(c_1, c_2, W^{\mathrm{NF4}}\bigr)
\;+\;
X^{\mathrm{BF16}}
\cdot
L_1\,L_2
$$  
and \(\mathrm{doubleDequant}(\cdot)\) is simply two nested dequantize operations that re-expand the 4-bit weights to BF16 on the fly. bitsandbytes implements this as a fused CUDA kernel that loads each quantized block, applies the two dequant steps (first on the 8-bit quantized scales \(c_2\) to recover the FP32 block scales, then on the 4-bit \(W\) to recover BF16 weights), and immediately performs the BF16 multiply-accumulate. That fused approach avoids any extra round trips to DRAM and any persistent BF16 buffers.

From a broader perspective, this design is a classic memory/compute trade-off in quantized inference and training. By keeping the model in a 4-bit NF4 + double-quantized form, you cut the weight storage to  
$$
4 + \approx 0.13
$$  
bits/parameter; by fusing dequantization into the GEMM you incur only a small amount of extra integer-to-float work in registers rather than blowing out your GPU memory with a full BF16 copy. This applies equally to QLoRA’s training kernels (where we only propagate activations and adapter-weight gradients) and to the 4-bit inference kernels in bitsandbytes. Neither path ever caches full dequantized tiles beyond the lifetime of a single kernel launch.

### Are there known interactions between NF4 and activation quantization or KV cache quantization?
Question:
QLoRA discusses weight quantization; however, inference often benefits from activation or KV cache quantization. Do NF4-quantized weights behave differently when combined with, say, 8-bit KV cache or 8-bit activations at inference? Any observed compounding errors or recommended practices when stacking these quantizations?
Answer:
While QLoRA’s paper introduces NF4 as a way to store *weights* in 4-bit with almost no loss of quality, it does **not** itself explore quantizing *activations* or the *KV cache* to 8-bit. In fact, at training (and in many inference stacks that reuse QLoRA weights) the 4-bit weights are immediately de-quantized back to BFloat16 (or FP16) before any matrix‐multiply, so from the point of view of the forward pass you really have “$\mathrm{BF16}\text{ weights}\to \mathrm{BF16}\text{ activations}.$”  

Because of that design, weight quantization (NF4) and activation/KV quantization (say A8 or K8) are largely _orthogonal_:

1.  NF4 only affects how the weights are stored on disk or GPU memory; once you de-quantize them, you have a full-precision (BF16) operator.  
2.  Activation quantization (A8) or KV cache quantization (K8) sits on top of that BF16 computation, introducing its own errors, but it never “sees” the *raw* 4-bit data type: it only sees the BF16 result of the de-quantized weights.

In other words, if you stack NF4 + A8 (or NF4 + K8), the total error you get is roughly the sum of

  •  the NF4→BF16 rounding error in the weights, plus  
  •  the BF16→8-bit rounding error in the activations or KV values.  

Empirically, the QLoRA authors showed that NF4 by itself introduces so little weight noise that you can recover 16-bit finetuning performance with LoRA adapters. From the broader quantization literature (e.g.\ Int8 inference in LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, or GPTQ) we know that a well-calibrated 8-bit activation or KV quantization likewise only adds a small “nuisance” error, provided you:

  1.  **Calibrate per‐tensor or per‐channel scales** on a representative batch of data,  
  2.  **Use block‐wise or percentile-clipping** schemes to bound outliers, and  
  3.  **Validate end-to-end** on perplexity or downstream task accuracy after you turn on A8 or K8.

In practice, many groups combine 4-bit weight formats with 8-bit activations or KV caching in production—and report only modest degradations in perplexity ($\le1\,$pt) or tiny drops ($<1\text{–}2\%$) in zero-shot/finetuned accuracy.  

**Recommended practice when stacking NF4 with A8/K8**  
•  Keep QLoRA’s NF4→BF16 de-quantization at the start of each layer;  
•  Immediately after you compute the BF16 activation, apply your usual 8-bit quantization (dynamic range or learned scales);  
•  If you need an 8-bit KV cache, quantize the Keys/Values on the fly in the same way (again calibrating scale factors on some warm-up tokens);  
•  Finally, when you read back from the 8-bit cache or from memory, de-quantize back to BF16 for the next mat-mul.  

Because NF4’s quantization constants and your activation/KV scales each live in their own domain, there is no mysterious “cross-term” beyond the simple additive rounding errors. As long as you calibrate each stage carefully and measure your end-to-end quality (perplexity, zero-shot accuracy or whatever metric you care about), you should be able to stack NF4 weights with 8-bit activations or KV caching with only a very small overall degradation.
