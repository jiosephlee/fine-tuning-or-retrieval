\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 11: From QLoRA to Guanaco: Training State-of-the-Art Chatbots

# Instruction datasets: OASST1, HH-RLHF, Alpaca, FLAN v2, Self-Instruct, Unnatural Instructions, Chip2, Longform

A key contribution enabled by QLoRA is the ability to experiment broadly across many instruction-following datasets at scales (up to 65B parameters) that would otherwise be prohibitively expensive. The study assembled eight recent and heterogeneous instruction datasets that cover different collection methods, sizes, and intended uses. Understanding the provenance and characteristics of these datasets is essential for interpreting model behavior.

OASST1 (Open Assistant) is a crowd-sourced, multi-turn dialog collection. It was designed with the goal of supporting training of assistant-style chatbots and—importantly for this work—its collection guidelines explicitly forbid generation from proprietary models (e.g., GPT family). As a relatively small but high-quality dataset (the paper cites an effective size near 9k examples in the analysis), OASST1 emphasizes human-curated conversational quality.

HH-RLHF (Anthropic's Helpful-Honest-Preferences dataset) is another dataset that stems from human preference data and is typically associated with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) work. In this study HH-RLHF was used in a supervised finetuning regime (no RLHF was applied during training), and when multiple candidate responses existed in the dataset the authors selected the top responses for training (see training recipe).

Alpaca and Self-Instruct are distillation-style datasets: they were generated by using an instruction-tuned model to produce input–output pairs that mimic human-like assistant responses. These data are generally larger but reflect the characteristics and biases of the model that produced them.

FLAN v2 is a large aggregation of instruction-style tasks and prompts that has been widely used for instruction tuning. It is broad and diverse in task coverage; the paper contrasts its performance profile with other datasets and finds that its suitability depends on the evaluation target (it helps MMLU performance more than chatbot-style conversation quality).

Unnatural Instructions is another synthetic instruction collection designed to broaden instruction types, often focusing on unusual or adversarial instructions that can improve generalization.

Chip2 (OIG/Chip2) and Longform are hybrid and long-form instruction datasets, respectively. Chip2 aggregates different corpora and includes a mixture of styles and length; Longform focuses on longer, multi-paragraph responses. The mix of dataset types in the experiments allows the authors to compare the effect of dataset origin (crowd-sourced human dialogs, model distillation, aggregated corpora, and hybrid sources) on downstream chatbot and task performance.

The paper’s design used this variety to probe two central questions: (1) Is dataset size or dataset quality more important for chatbot performance? and (2) How does dataset suitability to the evaluation task affect results? The answers, supported by experimental evidence, emphasize quality and suitability over mere size.

# Training recipe: supervised cross-entropy only; no RLHF

A deliberate and important decision in this work was to finetune all models using supervised cross-entropy training alone—no reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or other preference-based optimization was applied. This simplifies comparisons between datasets because all models were trained under the same objective and removes RLHF as a confounding factor when interpreting the contribution of dataset choice and model scale.

For datasets that present a clear instruction/response split, the training targets were the assistant responses (i.e., supervise the model to generate the response given the instruction prompt). For multi-response datasets (notably OASST1 and HH-RLHF) the authors used a selection strategy: when multiple responses existed at nodes in a conversation tree, they selected the top response at each level and then trained on the entire constructed conversation (including instruction turns). This approach keeps multi-turn conversational structure in the training examples rather than training on isolated single-turn Q→A pairs. The choice to include preceding turns in the prompt reflects the aim of training models that behave as chatbots in a multi-turn dialogue setting.

All finetuning used the QLoRA machinery: the base model weights were quantized (4-bit NormalFloat, NF4) with Double Quantization applied to the quantization constants, and Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) were inserted and trained while the base model remained frozen. During forward and backward passes, quantized weights were dequantized to a 16-bit computation type (BFloat16) for accurate computation; only the LoRA parameters were updated in 16-bit. Additionally, Paged Optimizers were used to manage memory spikes during gradient checkpointing by leveraging unified memory to page optimizer state between GPU and CPU when needed.

For generation at evaluation time, the authors used nucleus (top-p) sampling with $p=0.9$ and temperature $0.7$ consistently across systems to produce responses for human and model-based evaluations.

# Hyperparameter scaling with model size (LR halving, batch size doubling)

Hyperparameters were tuned carefully but pragmatically across model scales. The authors performed small hyperparameter searches for the 13B and 33B LLaMA models and found that the hyperparameter configurations discovered at 7B generally transferred across larger scales with two important exceptions: learning rate and batch size. Concretely, when moving from 13B to 33B and 65B models they adjusted these hyperparameters as follows:

- Reduce (halve) the learning rate when scaling to 33B and 65B relative to smaller models. This reflects the common intuition that larger models are more sensitive to step size and can diverge if the same learning rates used for smaller models are applied naively.
- Increase (double) the batch size for the larger models. Larger batch sizes stabilize training dynamics for larger parameter counts and help utilize available compute efficiently.

These adjustments enabled stable QLoRA finetuning across scales from 7B up to 65B parameters. The broader LoRA configuration was also important: rather than only applying LoRA to query and value projection matrices (a common practice), the authors found that applying LoRA adapters to all linear layers within transformer blocks was critical to recover full finetuning performance when using adapter-based PEFT. This demonstrates that model-scale hyperparameter and architectural decisions in adapter placement meaningfully affect downstream performance and that simple defaults do not always generalize.

# Results: Guanaco 65B at 99.3% of ChatGPT on Vicuna; 7B footprint ~5 GB

By applying QLoRA across many finetuning runs and dataset choices, the authors trained the Guanaco family of models. The headline results show that Guanaco models achieve state-of-the-art performance among openly released models on standard chatbot evaluation benchmarks.

On the Vicuna benchmark (80 curated prompts), Guanaco 65B achieves an automated score equating to 99.3% of ChatGPT’s performance on the same benchmark, as measured by the GPT-4 relative scoring procedure used in the study. This 65B Guanaco model was produced with QLoRA finetuning and, in the reported best run, required about a single professional GPU and roughly 24 hours of finetuning. A smaller Guanaco model (33B) achieves a strong performance that is competitive with or better than previous open-source distilled models and can be finetuned on a single 24 GB consumer GPU in less than 12 hours. The smallest Guanaco (7B) is particularly notable for deployment: it requires only about 5 GB of memory at inference time and still outperforms a 13B Alpaca model by roughly 20 percentage points on Vicuna. These results demonstrate that QLoRA enables state-of-the-art finetuning across a wide range of model sizes and that smaller, more efficient models can be highly competitive when trained on suitable data with the right finetuning recipe.

The paper also reports wide confidence intervals around some of these relative scores, an observation that motivates the use of pairwise Elo-style evaluations rather than sole reliance on absolute score percentages.

# Comparisons with Vicuna, OpenAssistant; GPT-4 and ChatGPT baselines

The evaluation framework included both human and automated comparisons with a broad set of baselines: open research models such as Vicuna (a 13B LLaMA finetuned on ShareGPT data) and Open Assistant (a 33B LLaMA finetuned with RLHF on OASST1), and commercial systems including ChatGPT (GPT-3.5-turbo) and GPT-4. Two complementary testbeds were used: the Vicuna prompts (80 prompts across many categories) and a larger Open Assistant (OA) benchmark constructed from 953 user queries in OASST1 validation (including previous turns where applicable).

Automated evaluation used GPT-4 in two modes. First, a relative rating mode where GPT-4 scored both ChatGPT’s response and a model’s response out of ten; performance was reported as a percentage of ChatGPT’s score. This approach directly gauges how model responses compare to a high-performing reference. The authors identified a strong ordering bias in GPT-4 scoring (the response shown earlier in the prompt tended to receive higher scores), so they controlled for this by averaging scores across both prompt orderings.

Second, the authors used GPT-4 as a head-to-head arbiter that selects the better of two responses (or declares a tie), producing pairwise comparisons across all system permutations. These pairwise outcomes were then distilled into Elo ratings. Elo rating was computed starting from an initial rating of $1000$ with a constant $K=32$, and the randomized tournament procedure was repeated $10{,}000$ times with different seeds to control ordering effects. The Elo aggregation yields a robust relative ranking where an Elo difference corresponds to an expected win probability between systems.

Human evaluation was carried out on Amazon Mechanical Turk. Two annotators provided ratings when comparing systems to ChatGPT and three annotators were used for pairwise comparisons. Human pairwise judgments were also aggregated via Elo in the same fashion. The authors compared human-based Elo rankings to GPT-4-based Elo rankings: system-level agreement was moderate (Kendall’s $\tau = 0.43$, Spearman $r = 0.55$), while example-level agreement between GPT-4 and human annotators was weaker (Fleiss $\kappa = 0.25$). The authors conclude that GPT-4 evaluations are a reasonable and cheap proxy for human evaluation in many cases, but that they have biases and do not perfectly mirror human judgments.

In this multi-evaluator setting, Guanaco 33B and 65B rank near the top among open models and approach ChatGPT’s performance. Guanaco 65B is the highest-performing open-source model in these tests and—according to human pairwise comparisons—has an expected win probability of about 30% against GPT-4, which is a strong showing given the gap to GPT-4 across many tasks.

# Dataset suitability insights: FLAN v2 vs chatbot benchmarks

One of the most important empirical lessons from the study is that dataset suitability and quality matter more for chatbot performance than raw dataset size. The paper provides several concrete observations illustrating this point.

First, smaller, high-quality conversational datasets like OASST1 (around 9k selected high-quality samples in the analysis) led to better chatbot performance (Vicuna/OA benchmarks) than much larger but less conversational or differently-structured datasets. For example, the authors sub-sampled FLAN v2 to comparable sizes and found that, despite FLAN v2 being large and diverse, it underperformed OASST1 when finetuning models for chatbot-like behavior.

Second, the match between finetuning data distribution and evaluation tasks heavily influences results. FLAN v2 finetuning produced strong gains on MMLU (Massively Multitask Language Understanding), consistent with FLAN’s emphasis on multi-task instruction data and tasks resembling MMLU items. However, that same FLAN v2 finetuning performed poorly on chatbot benchmarks like Vicuna and OA, where conversational fluency, turn-taking, and response style are critical. This points to partial orthogonality between benchmarks: doing well on MMLU does not guarantee good chatbot-style performance, and vice versa. Consequently, dataset choice should be guided by the intended downstream use case—aggregating many tasks is not a one-size-fits-all solution for conversational agents.

Third, the paper highlights that among the top-performing models evaluated, Guanaco is unique in being trained only on non-proprietary data (OASST1 preprocessing and selection), and it substantially outperforms other open-source models that did not use the same high-quality conversational data. The Anthropic HH-RLHF model (trained with their own data) scored substantially lower than Guanaco on Vicuna under the study’s evaluations, emphasizing that openness of data and careful selection can lead to very strong open-source models.

Finally, the authors stress that raw dataset size can be misleading. A very large dataset that is poorly matched to dialogue tasks can be less valuable than a smaller set with high-quality multi-turn conversational examples. The recommendation is to prioritize dataset quality and task alignment when building instruction-tuning corpora for chatbots.

# Practical considerations, evaluation caveats, and takeaways

Beyond headline scores, the paper documents several practical and scientific considerations that are important when interpreting results and for anyone looking to reproduce or extend this work.

First, evaluation mechanics matter. The authors observed an ordering bias in GPT-4 scoring (the first-presented response tended to be favored), and they recommend averaging scores over both prompt orders to mitigate this artifact. They also found that GPT-4’s own outputs received higher Elo ratings from GPT-4 than human annotators gave—GPT-4 assigned itself an Elo of about $1348$ while humans assigned GPT-4 around $1176$, indicating potential self-favoring or distributional differences in GPT-4’s evaluation behavior. This means automated evaluators are useful and cost-effective but must be used carefully and validated against human judgments.

Second, inter-annotator agreement was moderate. Human annotators agreed only moderately on pairwise preferences (Fleiss $\kappa \approx 0.42$ among humans) and agreement between humans and GPT-4 was weaker at the example level ($\kappa \approx 0.25$). These numbers indicate that chatbot preference is often subjective; as models approach parity, subtle subjective preferences dominate, which widens confidence intervals in comparative metrics. The authors therefore favor pairwise Elo-style evaluations as they are less sensitive to absolute scoring scales.

Third, QLoRA’s practical benefits are emphasized: it allows many more experiments by lowering memory and compute barriers. Guanaco 33B can be finetuned on a single 24 GB consumer GPU in under 12 hours; Guanaco 65B was trained in the top run on a single professional GPU in about 24 hours. The smallest Guanaco (7B) fits in roughly 5 GB of memory for inference, enabling potential on-device deployment and personalization.

Fourth, the authors note open questions and limitations. While QLoRA recovers 16-bit finetuning performance across many tasks and scales, full verification for 33B/65B matching of 16-bit full finetuning was not exhaustively established (resource constraints limit exhaustive comparisons). Also, the study did not explore alternative PEFT methods beyond LoRA or more aggressive quantization (e.g., 3-bit), leaving these as directions for future work.

Final takeaways: QLoRA makes high-quality finetuning of very large models feasible on limited hardware; dataset quality and alignment to downstream tasks are critical for chatbot performance; supervised cross-entropy finetuning on high-quality conversational data (e.g., OASST1 with top-response selection and multi-turn context) can produce open-source chatbots that rival proprietary systems on standard benchmarks; and careful, multi-faceted evaluation (human + model-based, pairwise + Elo aggregation) is necessary to obtain robust conclusions about relative model quality.
