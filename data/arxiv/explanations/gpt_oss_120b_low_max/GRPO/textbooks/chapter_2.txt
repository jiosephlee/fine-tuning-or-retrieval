\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: **Building a Large‑Scale Math Pre‑Training Corpus**

The mathematical abilities of a language model are fundamentally tied to the quality and breadth of the data on which it is trained.  In the DeepSeekMath project, a dedicated corpus—**the DeepSeekMath Corpus**—was created by mining 120 billion tokens of mathematics‑related text from the public Common Crawl (CC) archive.  This chapter explains, in detail, how that corpus was assembled, emphasizing the intuition behind each design choice and the concrete engineering steps that turned raw web pages into a high‑quality, multilingual resource suitable for large‑scale pre‑training.

---

### Seed Data: OpenWebMath and Its Role

The construction process began with a **seed corpus** that would guide all subsequent data mining.  The authors selected **OpenWebMath**, a curated collection of math‑related web texts, for two reasons. First, OpenWebMath already contains verified examples of high‑quality mathematical content drawn from the Common Crawl, providing a reliable “positive” signal. Second, its size is modest enough to permit efficient training of a classifier while still being representative of the diverse styles (explanatory articles, problem statements, proofs) that appear on the web.

Using OpenWebMath as the seed serves two purposes: (1) it supplies concrete examples for a supervised classifier to learn what constitutes “math‑related” text, and (2) it anchors the iterative pipeline so that each new round of collection can be evaluated against an established benchmark of relevance.

---

### FastText Classifier Design and Training Procedure

To sift through billions of web pages, the authors employed **fastText**, a lightweight linear model that learns word‑level n‑gram embeddings. The simplicity of fastText makes it feasible to train on millions of examples and to evaluate tens of billions of documents quickly.

The training set was constructed as follows:

| Category | Source | Number of Examples |
|----------|--------|---------------------|
| Positive | Randomly sampled from OpenWebMath | 500 000 |
| Negative | Randomly sampled from Common Crawl (non‑math pages) | 500 000 |

Key hyper‑parameters were:

- Embedding dimension = 256  
- Learning rate = 0.1  
- Maximum n‑gram length = 3 (captures short phrases such as “solve for x”)  
- Minimum word occurrence = 3 (filters extremely rare tokens)  
- Training epochs = 3  

The resulting classifier outputs a **score** for each web page indicating its likelihood of containing mathematical content.  By ranking pages according to this score, the pipeline can retain only those pages that are most reminiscent of the seed data.

---

### Iterative Data‑Collection Pipeline (Four Iterations)

A single pass of the classifier does not retrieve all relevant material because the initial seed lacks diversity.  Consequently, the authors designed an **iterative refinement loop**:

1. **First Pass** – Apply the classifier to a deduplicated subset of Common Crawl (≈40 B HTML pages after URL deduplication). Keep only the highest‑scoring pages until a pre‑chosen token budget is reached (initially 40 B tokens).  
2. **Analysis & Expansion** – Examine which domains contributed many retrieved pages and which still had low coverage.  
3. **Domain Enrichment** – Identify domains where *≥10 %* of their pages were already classified as math‑related; manually label additional URLs within those domains that likely contain math (e.g., `mathoverflow.net/questions`). These newly labeled pages are added to the seed set.  
4. **Retraining** – Re‑train fastText on the enlarged positive set and repeat steps 1–3.

After four such iterations, the process converged: the third iteration had already captured ~98 % of what would later be obtained in the fourth, indicating diminishing returns.  The final output consisted of **35.5 million** distinct web pages amounting to **120 billion tokens**.

---

### Domain‑Level Heuristics and Manual URL Annotation

To accelerate coverage beyond what automatic classification alone could achieve, the authors introduced a **domain‑level heuristic**:

- The entire Common Crawl corpus was partitioned into *domains* defined by a shared base URL (e.g., all pages under `math.stackexchange.com`).
- For each domain, they computed the proportion of pages flagged as mathematical in the first iteration.
- Domains with a proportion >10 % were flagged as *potentially math‑rich*.
- Within these domains, human annotators examined URL patterns (such as `/questions/` or `/problems/`) to pinpoint sections most likely to host math content.
- URLs thus identified were crawled for additional pages that had not been retrieved automatically; these new pages became part of the positive training set for subsequent classifier retraining.

This combination of coarse statistical filtering followed by fine‑grained manual labeling ensured both breadth (capturing whole sites) and depth (collecting lower‑level pages that may have been missed).

---

### Deduplication, Near‑Deduplication, and Token Budgeting

Processing hundreds of billions of raw HTML documents would be infeasible without aggressive duplication removal.  The pipeline applied two complementary strategies:

1. **URL‑Based Deduplication** – Exact duplicate URLs were discarded immediately.
2. **Near‑Deduplication** – Documents with highly similar textual content (e.g., mirrored tutorials across different subdomains) were identified using hashing techniques and removed, preserving only one representative copy.

These steps reduced the raw Common Crawl dump to **40 B unique HTML pages**, dramatically lowering both storage requirements and downstream computational cost.

Because pre‑training resources are finite, the authors experimented with several token budgets—40 B, 80 B, 120 B, and 160 B—to determine diminishing returns. Empirically, models trained on up to 120 B tokens continued to improve on downstream benchmarks, while additional tokens yielded marginal gains; thus 120 B was adopted as the final corpus size.

---

### Benchmark De‑Contamination Strategy

A crucial concern is **benchmark leakage**: if any training document contains verbatim excerpts from evaluation datasets (e.g., GSM8K or MATH), model performance could be artificially inflated.

To guard against this, DeepSeekMath applied an exact‑match filter inspired by prior work:

- Any 10‑gram sequence appearing in any benchmark test set was searched across the collected corpus.
- If a page contained such a sequence, that segment was excised.
- For shorter benchmark excerpts (3–9 grams), exact matching was also performed.
- This procedure was applied both to English benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, AGIEval).

By removing any overlapping n‑grams before pre‑training, the authors ensured that improvements measured on those benchmarks stem from genuine reasoning ability rather than memorization.

---

### Multilingual Coverage and Its Impact

Unlike many existing math corpora that are overwhelmingly English (\>85 % arXiv papers), the DeepSeekMath Corpus deliberately retained **multilingual content**, with English and Chinese comprising the majority of tokens. This multilinguality emerged naturally because:

- The domain heuristics captured entire sites that host bilingual educational material (e.g., Chinese university lecture notes alongside English translations).
- Manual URL annotation did not restrict collection to English domains.

Empirical evaluation showed that models pre‑trained on this corpus achieved superior scores on both English and Chinese mathematical benchmarks compared with models trained on monolingual corpora such as MathPile or OpenWebMath. The multilingual exposure also yielded modest improvements on general language understanding tasks (MMLU) and coding benchmarks, suggesting cross-lingual transfer benefits.

---

### Summary

The DeepSeekMath Corpus demonstrates how systematic mining of publicly available web data can produce a massive, high-quality mathematical dataset without relying on proprietary sources.  Starting from a modest seed (OpenWebMath), training an inexpensive fastText classifier, iteratively expanding positive examples via domain statistics and human annotation, rigorously deduplicating content, enforcing strict de‑contamination against benchmark leaks, and preserving multilingual material together resulted in **120 billion tokens** spanning **35.5 million** web pages.  

These engineering decisions directly translated into measurable gains: when used for continued pre‑training of a 7B parameter model, this corpus enabled performance comparable to much larger closed‑source systems on challenging reasoning benchmarks such as MATH and GSM8K. The methodology outlined here provides a reproducible template for building domain-specific corpora at scale from open web archives.
