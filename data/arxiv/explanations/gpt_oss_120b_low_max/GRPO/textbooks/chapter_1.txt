\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models  

Mathematical reasoning is a distinctive form of intelligence that demands precise manipulation of symbols, strict logical flow, and often the construction of multi‑step arguments.  When we consider large language models (LLMs) – neural networks trained on massive text corpora to predict the next token – it is natural to ask how well such models can emulate the disciplined thought processes that underlie mathematics.  This chapter explains why mathematical reasoning poses a special challenge for LLMs, surveys the historical trajectory of research in this area, and then presents **DeepSeekMath**, a recent open‑source system that pushes the limits of what can be achieved with a 7 billion‑parameter model.  Throughout we draw exclusively on the findings reported in the DeepSeekMath paper, emphasizing intuition and concrete experimental evidence.

---

## Why Mathematical Reasoning Is Hard for Language Models  

### Structured Symbolic Knowledge vs. Statistical Prediction  

At their core, LLMs are probabilistic sequence models: given a context of tokens they assign a probability distribution over the next token.  This works remarkably well for natural‑language tasks where meaning is often inferred from statistical regularities (e.g., “the cat sat on …” → “the”).  Mathematics, however, relies on **exact** relationships among symbols.  An arithmetic expression such as  

\[
\frac{(3+5)\times2}{4}=4
\]

cannot be tolerated to an off‑by‑one error; any deviation yields a completely invalid result.  The model must therefore learn to treat certain token sequences (operators, numbers, variable names) as **syntactic entities** with rigid semantics, rather than as loosely associated words.

### Long‑Range Dependencies and Multi‑Step Planning  

A typical competition‑level problem from the MATH benchmark contains a stem, several intermediate lemmas, and a final answer.  Solving it often requires **chain‑of‑thought** reasoning: generating an explicit sequence of intermediate statements before reaching the conclusion.  Each intermediate step may depend on calculations performed many tokens earlier, creating long‑range dependencies that exceed the usual context windows (4 K tokens in DeepSeekMath).  A naïve language model may lose track of earlier deductions, leading to contradictory or incomplete solutions.

### Requirement for External Knowledge and Tools  

Many mathematical problems become tractable only when auxiliary tools—symbolic algebra systems (e.g., *sympy*) or numerical libraries—are invoked.  A purely autoregressive model must either **internalize** these tool behaviours or explicitly generate code that calls them (the **program‑of‑thought** paradigm).  This hybrid reasoning—combining natural language explanation with executable code—adds another layer of difficulty: the model must learn both natural linguistic patterns and programming conventions.

### Sparse Supervision in Benchmarks  

Unlike next‑token prediction where every token provides a learning signal, most mathematical benchmarks provide only a **single correctness label** per problem (right or wrong answer).  Consequently, standard supervised training yields weak gradients for reasoning ability; the model rarely receives explicit feedback on whether each intermediate step was valid.

These obstacles collectively explain why early LLMs performed modestly on mathematics despite their impressive language abilities.

## Historical Progress: From Early LLMs to GPT‑4, Gemini‑Ultra, and Open‑Source Efforts  

### Early Attempts and Quantitative Reasoning Benchmarks  

The first systematic evaluations of LLMs on mathematics appeared with datasets such as **GSM8K** (grade‑school math) and later **MATH**, which contains competition‑level problems requiring multi‑step solutions.  Baseline transformer models trained solely on general text achieved accuracies in the low twenties on GSM8K and struggled dramatically on MATH (< 10 %).  

Researchers quickly discovered that **domain‑specific pre‑training** could raise performance substantially.  By feeding models large amounts of math–rich web text (e.g., StackExchange posts), papers like *Minerva* demonstrated that a 540 B parameter model pretrained on heavily curated mathematics data could reach roughly 45 % accuracy on MATH—still far below closed‑source giants but dramatically better than generic pretrained models.

### The Rise of Chain‑of‑Thought and Program‑of‑Thought Prompting  

A breakthrough came with **Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)** prompting: simply asking a powerful model to “think step by step” caused it to generate explicit intermediate reasoning, boosting GSM8K scores by ~10 % points across many architectures.  Extending this idea, **Program‑of‑Thought (PoT)** prompted models to write executable Python snippets using libraries such as *math* or *sympy*; execution then produced a final answer.  PoT improved performance on problems where precise arithmetic or symbolic manipulation is essential.

### Closed‑Source Leaders: GPT‑4 and Gemini‑Ultra  

The most recent closed models—**GPT‑4** and **Gemini‑Ultra**—combine massive parameter counts (hundreds of billions), extensive instruction fine‑tuning, tool integration (code interpreter), and aggressive reinforcement learning from human feedback.  Their reported MATH scores exceed 60 % (GPT‑4) and approach 70 % (Gemini Ultra), establishing a new performance ceiling that open research strives to approach.

### Open‐Source Momentum  

Open‐source communities have responded with several notable projects:

* **Mistral 7B**, **Llemma 34B**, and **InternLM2-Math 20B**, which augment base language models with math datasets but remain considerably behind closed models.
* **WizardMath**, **MetaMath**, and **ToRA**, which incorporate CoT/PoT instruction tuning and sometimes PPO‐style reinforcement learning.
* Larger multilingual efforts such as **OpenWebMath** (13.6 B tokens) and **MathPile** (8.9 B tokens) that collect web pages containing mathematical content.

Despite these advances, none of the publicly available models have yet achieved > 50 % accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark—a gap that motivates the work presented in DeepSeekMath.

## Overview of DeepSeekMath: Goals and High-Level Architecture  

DeepSeekMath is designed as an end‑to‑end pipeline that systematically addresses each barrier identified above:

1. **Massive High-Quality Math Pretraining Corpus** – The authors construct the *DeepSeekMath Corpus* comprising ~120 B tokens drawn from Common Crawl webpages filtered for mathematical relevance using an iterative fastText classifier pipeline.  
2. **Foundation Model Initialized from Code-Optimized Pretraining** – The base model begins from *DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5* (7 B parameters).  Starting from a code‐oriented checkpoint gives the model strong familiarity with programming constructs, which later benefits PoT reasoning.
3. **Continual Pretraining Across Diverse Domains** – After initializing from the coder base, DeepSeekMath continues training on a mixture of math web text (56 %), algebraic code repositories (4 %), arXiv papers (10 %), GitHub code (20 %), and natural language data (10 %).  The total exposure is roughly 500 B tokens.
4. **Mathematical Instruction Tuning** – Using a curated dataset of ~776 K instruction examples covering both English and Chinese problems, each paired with CoT, PoT, or tool‐integrated solutions.
5. **Reinforcement Learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – A lightweight variant of PPO that eliminates the need for an explicit value network by estimating baselines from groups of sampled completions; this reduces memory usage while preserving performance gains.
6. **Evaluation Across Multilingual Benchmarks** – Performance is measured not only on English datasets (GSM8K, MATH) but also Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, Gaokao variants) and formal theorem proving tasks (miniF2F).

The overall architecture can be visualized as three stages:

```
[Common Crawl → FastText Selector] → DeepSeekMath Corpus
          │
          ▼
[DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5] --(continual pretraining)→ DeepSeekMath-Base
          │
          ▼
[Math Instruction Data] --(supervised finetuning)→ DeepSeekMath-Instruct
          │
          ▼
[GRPO RL] ------------------------------------→ DeepSeekMath-RL
```

Each stage builds upon the previous one: higher-quality data yields richer representations; instruction tuning teaches explicit reasoning patterns; RL refines policy towards higher reward according to a learned comparator.

## Key Contributions of the DeepSeekMath Paper  

The authors delineate two major thematic contributions: (**I**) scalable mathematical pretraining; (**II**) an analysis and novel algorithm for reinforcement learning.

### I. Scalable Math Pretraining  

1. **Demonstration of Public Web Data Value** – By carefully filtering Common Crawl using an iterative fastText classifier trained first on OpenWebMath seeds then enriched through domain analysis, they assemble a corpus that is *seven times* larger than Minerva’s math webpages and *nine times* larger than OpenWebMath.  
2. **Empirical Validation Against Existing Corpora** – Training a 1.3 B parameter model for 150 B tokens on each candidate corpus shows that the DeepSeekMath Corpus yields superior downstream performance across eight benchmarks, especially in multilingual settings where English-only corpora fail to improve Chinese tasks.  
3. **Evidence That Parameter Count Is Not Sole Determinant** – The resulting *DeepSeekMath-Base* (7 B) attains performance comparable to Minerva’s 540 B model on GSM8K (64.2 %) and MATH (36.2 %). This indicates that high-quality domain data can compensate for smaller model capacity in mathematical reasoning contexts.
4. **Insights into Code Pretraining Benefits** – Starting from a code-specialized checkpoint markedly improves both program-aided reasoning (PoT) and pure CoT abilities compared with initializing from generic language models—a partial answer to whether “code training improves reasoning”.  
5. **Negative Result Regarding ArXiv Papers** – Contrary to prevailing practice, adding large volumes of arXiv LaTeX text does not yield measurable gains on any evaluated benchmark; in some cases it slightly harms performance.

### II. Exploration and Analysis of Reinforcement Learning  

1. **Introduction of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – GRPO removes the traditional critic/value network required by PPO by computing baselines from *group-relative* reward averages across multiple sampled completions per question. The objective retains PPO’s clipped ratio term but adds an explicit KL penalty between current policy and reference model rather than embedding it into per-token rewards.
2. **Empirical Gains Using Only Instruction Data** – Applying GRPO solely on English instruction data boosts GSM8K accuracy from 82.9 % → 88.2 % and MATH from 46.8 % → 51.7 %, surpassing all open-source counterparts in the same parameter range.
3. **Unified Paradigm for SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO** – The paper formalizes all these methods under a single gradient equation involving three components: data source, reward function, algorithmic gradient coefficient. This abstraction enables systematic comparisons such as online versus offline sampling or outcome versus process supervision.
4. **Extensive Ablation Studies** – Experiments dissecting online vs offline data generation, outcome vs process reward signals, single-turn vs iterative RL illuminate why GRPO outperforms alternatives: group-relative advantage estimation provides finer-grained reinforcement while avoiding noisy value function training.
5. **Analysis of Why RL Helps** – Results show RL improves “Maj@K” accuracy (the proportion of correct answers among top-K generations) without substantially increasing “Pass@K”. This suggests RL reshapes the output distribution so that correct solutions become more likely among sampled candidates rather than fundamentally increasing per-sample competence—a subtle but valuable effect for downstream sampling strategies.

Collectively these contributions establish DeepSeekMath as both a high-performance open-source math LLM and a methodological reference point for future work in domain-specific pretraining and efficient reinforcement learning for large language models.

--- 

In summary, mathematical reasoning remains one of the most demanding tasks for language models due to its reliance on exact symbol manipulation, long-range logical dependencies, sparse supervision, and occasional need for external computational tools.  Over time researchers have progressed from naïve pretraining to sophisticated prompting techniques, massive closed-source systems now achieve near-human performance on competitive benchmarks, while open-source efforts continue catching up through better data pipelines and algorithmic innovations such as GRPO.  

DeepSeekMath exemplifies how targeted collection of high-quality web mathematics data—combined with code-aware initialization, instruction tuning spanning multiple languages, and memory-efficient reinforcement learning—allows a modestly sized 7 B model to surpass all existing open-source baselines and approach closed-source capabilities on challenging benchmarks like MATH.   The next chapters will delve deeper into each component described here: the fastText‐based data selection pipeline, the specifics of continual pretraining schedules, chain-of-thought versus program-of-thought instruction formats, and finally the mathematical derivation and practical implementation details of Group Relative Policy Optimization.*
