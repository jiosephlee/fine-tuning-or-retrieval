\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does the fastText classifier retrieve math pages from Common Crawl in the DeepSeekMath data pipeline?
Question:
The paper describes an iterative pipeline where a fastText model is trained on 500k positive (OpenWebMath) and 500k negative pages, then used to recall more math pages. I'm unclear about:
1. What features does fastText use for this classification (subword n‑grams, bag‑of‑words, etc.)?
2. How is the scoring threshold chosen for selecting the "top‑ranking" pages?
3. After each iteration, how are newly collected pages incorporated into the training set for the next fastText model? A step‑by‑step description would help.
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DeepSeekMath pipeline the fastText classifier is only a *data‑selection* tool – it is never used to label individual math tokens, but to decide which HTML pages harvested from Common Crawl are worth keeping for the pre‑training corpus.  
The paper gives enough details to reconstruct what the classifier sees, how a decision threshold is applied, and how the newly‑found pages are fed back into the next iteration.

---

### 1. What features does fastText use?

The authors train fastText exactly as described in the original Joulin et al. (2016) implementation:

* **Bag‑of‑words + sub‑word n‑grams** – each page (after cleaning the HTML) is tokenised into words; for every word the model also extracts all character n‑grams up to length 3 (the “maximum length of word n‑gram” in their configuration).  
* The model builds a **linear classifier** on top of the average of all word vectors and their character‑n‑gram vectors.  
* No additional hand‑crafted features (e.g., HTML tags, DOM depth, equation counts) are mentioned; the classifier therefore relies purely on lexical information.

The training hyper‑parameters reported in the paper (vector dimension = 256, learning rate = 0.1, min word count = 3, epochs = 3) are exactly those of the standard fastText “supervised” mode, confirming that the feature set is the default bag‑of‑words + sub‑word n‑gram representation.

---

### 2. How is the scoring threshold chosen?

FastText outputs a **raw confidence score** (the logit before applying the softmax) for each class (math vs. non‑math). The authors do not fix an absolute probability value; instead they **rank all pages by this score** and keep a *budget* of tokens that fits their pre‑training schedule.

Concretely:

1. After a model has been trained on $500\,\text{k}$ positive and $500\,\text{k}$ negative examples, it is run over the deduplicated Common Crawl dump (≈ $40\,\text{B}$ HTML pages).  
2. Every page receives a score; pages are sorted from highest to lowest.  
3. The authors then *experimentally* evaluate several token caps ($40\,\text{B}$, $80\,\text{B}$, $120\,\text{B}$, $160\,\text{B}$) by training a short pre‑training run on each subset and measuring downstream math performance.  
4. The **first iteration** adopts the **top‑ranking pages that contain roughly $40\,\text{B}$ tokens** – this number is chosen because it gave the best trade‑off between quality and size in their pilot experiments.

Thus there is no fixed probability threshold; “top‑ranking” simply means *the highest‑scoring pages up to the desired token budget*. In later iterations the same ranking procedure is repeated with an updated classifier, but the token budget remains $40\,\text{B}$ until the fourth iteration (after which > $98\%$ of new math pages have already been collected).

---

### 3. How are newly collected pages incorporated into the next fastText model?  

The pipeline is deliberately iterative so that each round expands the seed set and improves classifier diversity. The steps can be described as follows:

1. **Initial seed** – OpenWebMath (≈ $13.6\,\text{B}$ tokens) is taken as positive examples; a random $500\,\text{k}$ subset of these forms the *positive training set*. An equal‑size random sample from all deduplicated Common Crawl forms the *negative set*.  
2. **Train fastText** on this balanced $1M$‑example dataset with the hyper‑parameters above.  
3. **Score & rank** every page in the deduplicated crawl; keep the highest‐scoring pages until a predetermined token budget (e.g., $40\,\text{B}$) is reached – these become *collected math pages* for this iteration.  
4. **Domain analysis** – The collected pages are grouped by domain (same base URL). Domains where > $10\%$ of their pages were selected are labelled “math‐related”. Human annotators then inspect those domains and tag additional URLs that clearly contain math content (for example, specific sub‑paths on `mathoverflow.net`).  
5. **Seed expansion** – All *uncollected* pages that belong to those freshly annotated URLs are added to the positive pool. In practice this means appending thousands of previously unseen high‑quality math pages to the original OpenWebMath seed.  
6. **Rebalance** – For the next iteration a new balanced training set is built: again $500\,\text{k}$ positive examples are randomly drawn from *the enlarged positive pool*, and another $500\,\text{k}$ negatives are sampled from the remaining crawl pages that were not selected in step 3.  
7. **Retrain fastText** with exactly the same configuration as before but now on a more diverse positive set; because more terminology and styles appear in the expanded seed, the classifier’s decision boundary becomes broader and recalls more math pages in step 3 of the next round.  
8. Steps 3–7 are repeated four times total; after iteration 4 only ≈ $2\%$ of remaining pages add any new math content, so data collection stops.

In short, each iteration **(i)** uses the current classifier to retrieve high‑scoring pages, **(ii)** enriches the positive training pool by hand‑annotating URLs inside domains that show strong math density, and **(iii)** retrains a fresh fastText model on a fresh balanced sample from this larger pool. The loop continues until marginal returns vanish.

---

### Putting it together

- The fastText model works with standard bag‑of‑words plus character n‑grams (up to trigrams), nothing exotic.
- A page’s inclusion is decided by its rank among all scores rather than an absolute probability; a token budget (first $40\,\text{B}$ tokens) determines where to cut off.
- After each recall step, newly identified math URLs are manually labelled and their pages are merged into the positive set; a new balanced training sample is drawn and a fresh fastText model is trained for the next iteration.

This simple yet effective iterative scheme lets DeepSeekMath harvest ≈ $35$ million high‑quality math webpages (≈ $120\,\text{B}$ tokens) from Common Crawl while keeping annotation effort minimal—exactly what enabled their large‐scale math pre‑training corpus.
