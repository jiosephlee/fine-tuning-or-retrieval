\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the unified RoPE interpolation formula in Eq. (2) relate to the original RoPE definition in Eq. (1)?
Question:
In the paper, Eq. (1) defines the RoPE embedding as \([\cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \dots, \cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})]\). Later, Eq. (2) rewrites the embedding with a rescale factor \(\lambda(\beta)^i\). I am confused about how the term \(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^i}\) is derived from the original \(n\theta_i = n/\beta^i\). Specifically, what is the meaning of \(\lambda(\beta)^i\) and how does it incorporate the extension ratio \(s\)?
Answer:
In the original RoPE definition (Eq.\ (1)) the rotary angle that is applied to the *i*‑th sub‑space of the embedding at token position **n** is  

\[
\theta_{i}^{(n)} = n\,\theta_{i}= \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\;,
\qquad 
\beta = \theta^{2/d}.
\]

The factor \(\beta^{\,i}\) is decreasing with the dimension index \(i\); low‑index dimensions correspond to high‑frequency sinusoids, high‑index dimensions to low‑frequency ones.  
When we want to use the same pretrained model on a longer sequence we cannot simply feed it positions larger than the original maximum because the angles become *much* larger than the range that the model has ever seen during pre‑training.  The usual remedy is to **compress** the new positions so that they lie inside the interval \([0,L]\) that the model was trained on.  

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### From \(\displaystyle \frac{n}{\beta^{i}}\) to \(\displaystyle \frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{\,i}}\)

All existing interpolation schemes can be written as a *scale* that multiplies the original angle:

\[
\theta_{i}^{(n)} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\;\frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}
          = \frac{n}{\bigl(\lambda_{i}^{1/i}\,\beta\bigr)^{\,i}} .
\]

If we define a **single scalar function of the base**  

\[
\lambda(\beta)\;:=\;\lambda_{i}^{1/i},
\]

the rescaled angle becomes  

\[
\theta_{i}^{(n)} =  \frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{\,i}} .
\]

Thus Eq.\ (2) is simply a compact way of writing

\[
\bigl[\cos(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{0}}),\, 
      \sin(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{0}}),\, 
      \cos(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{1}}),\, 
      \dots,\,
      \sin(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}})\bigr],
\]

which reduces to Eq.\ (1) when every \(\lambda_{i}=1\).

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### What does \(\lambda(\beta)^{\,i}\) represent?

For a given **extension ratio**  

\[
s = \frac{L'}{L},
\]

different interpolation methods choose a concrete value for each per‑dimension scale factor \(\lambda_{i}\):

| method | per‑dimension scale \(\lambda_{i}\) | intuition |
|--------|-----------------------------------|-----------|
| **PI** (linear interpolation) | \(\lambda_{i}=s\) for **all** \(i\) | every sinusoid is compressed by exactly the same factor. |
| **NTK** | \(\lambda_{i}=s^{\,i}\) | low‑frequency (large‑\(i\)) dimensions are compressed more heavily, high‑frequency dimensions are left almost untouched – this follows the NTK analysis that low frequencies need more “room”. |
| **YaRN** | three groups: \(1,\; s^{-a},\; s^{b}\) (different exponents for each group) | a hand‑crafted piecewise version of NTK + extrapolation. |

All of those can be expressed by setting  

\[
\lambda(\beta)= s^{\,c},   \qquad c=\frac{\log \lambda_i}{ i\,\log s } .
\]

When \(c=0\) we obtain PI (\(\lambda_i=s^0=1\)).  More generally, any choice of per‑dimension scales can be captured by an appropriate function \(\lambda(\beta)\).  In practice LongRoPE does not force a simple analytic form; it **searches** for a vector \((\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1})$) subject to the monotonicity constraint  

\[
1=\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1} .
\]

The monotonicity follows from the NTK observation that higher dimensions (lower frequencies) may tolerate larger compression.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Where does the extension ratio \(s\) appear?

During the search we bound each individual factor:

```
λ_i ∈ [ 1.0 ,  s × 1.25 ]
```

The lower bound corresponds to *no interpolation* (pure extrapolation), while the upper bound allows slightly more compression than naïve linear PI (the factor \(1.25\,s\)).  
Thus \(s\) acts as a **global budget** – it tells us how much total “shrinkage” we may apply, but the algorithm decides how to distribute that budget across dimensions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* Eq.\ (1) uses raw angles \(n/\beta^{i}\).  
* Extending the context requires scaling those angles by a per‑dimension factor \(1/\lambda_{i}\).  
* By defining a scalar function \(\lambda(\beta)\) such that  
  \[
  \frac{1}{\lambda_{i}} = \frac{1}{[\lambda(\beta)]^{\,i}},
  \]
  we can write all interpolations compactly as Eq.\ (2).  
* The term \(s=L'/L\) does not appear explicitly in Eq.\ (2); it limits the admissible range of each \(\lambda_i\).  Different prior methods correspond to particular choices of the sequence \((\lambda_i)\); LongRoPE discovers a *non‑uniform* sequence that better preserves important high‑frequency dimensions while still fitting the longer positions.

Consequently, Eq.\ (2) is a unified notation for any RoPE interpolation, and “the rescale factor \(λ(β)^i” simply encodes how much each frequency dimension’s angle has been compressed (or extrapolated) relative to its original value, with the overall compression budget determined by the desired extension ratio \(s\).

### What is the intuition behind keeping the first \(\hat n\) tokens un‑interpolated (Finding 2)?
Question:
The authors claim that the initial \(\hat n\) tokens should receive "less interpolation" because they attract large attention scores. How does reduced interpolation for early positions help the model, and why does the optimal \(\hat n\) depend on the target extension length? Are there any empirical analyses (e.g., attention heatmaps) that support this hypothesis?
Answer:
In RoPE‑based transformers the positional embedding is part of every self‑attention head: the rotary angles are multiplied with the query‑key vectors before the dot‑product is taken.  
Consequently, a token that receives large attention scores contributes most to the values that are summed for the next‑token prediction. If the positional angles of such a token are heavily distorted by interpolation, the model “mis‑places’’ it in the angular space and the attention matrix can no longer recognise the token as being close to its original position.  

Finding 2 of the LongRoPE paper exploits exactly this observation. The authors empirically noticed that, when the context window is stretched (e.g. from 4 k to 8 k or 16 k tokens), the first \(\hat n\) positions of a sequence keep receiving disproportionately high attention weights in almost every layer – a pattern also reported in **Streaming‑LLM** and **LM‑Infinite** where early tokens act as a persistent memory. Because those early tokens are repeatedly attended to, any interpolation‑induced error on their angles propagates through all layers and quickly degrades perplexity.

### Why “less interpolation’’ helps

1. **Preservation of high‑frequency information** – The low‑index RoPE dimensions (\(i\) small) already encode high‑frequency sinusoidals. In NTK theory these dimensions need only a tiny rescaling factor (close to 1). If we apply the same linear down‑scaling that is used for later positions, we would compress their angular spacing even more, making distinct positions virtually indistinguishable.

2. **Stability of the attention distribution** – When the first \(\hat n\) tokens keep their original angles (i.e. \(\mathbb I(\hat\lambda_i,\hat n)=1\) for \(n<\hat n\)), their query‑key inner products stay close to those learned during pre‑training. The attention heads therefore continue to allocate large weights to exactly those tokens, which almost always contain instruction prefixes, system prompts or other “anchor’’ information. The rest of the sequence can be safely interpolated because it contributes far less to any single head’s output.

3. **Mitigation of crowding** – Positional interpolation shrinks the effective angular range for a given dimension; this crowding is most harmful where many heads already concentrate their mass (the start of the document). By exempting a small prefix from this crowding we keep a clear “reference frame’’ for all subsequent positions.

### Dependence of \(\hat n\) on the target extension length

If we only stretch the context modestly (e.g. \(s=2\times\) or \(4\times\)), a relatively small prefix—say \(\hat n=8\) or \(\hat n=16\)—is sufficient because only a modest amount of angular compression occurs and most heads can still resolve differences among later positions.  
When we aim for a much larger extension (e.g. \(s=16\times\) or \(s=64\times\)), each new position must be mapped into an increasingly compressed angular interval. The distortion accumulated at the boundary between “un‑interpolated’’ and “interpolated’’ zones becomes noticeable unless we push that boundary farther out, i.e. increase \(\hat n\). Hence the optimal \(\hat n\) grows with the extension ratio: Table 2 of the paper shows \(\hat n=64\) works best for an 8 k window while \(\hat n=256\) is preferred for 16 k.

### Empirical support

The authors provide two kinds of evidence:

* **Perplexity experiments** – In Table 2 they compare PI/NTK with versions that keep the first \(\hat n\) tokens untouched. Across both PG19 and Proof‑Pile, perplexities drop consistently as \(\hat n\) increases up to its optimal value for each target length, confirming that preserving early angles improves language modeling.

* **Attention heat‑maps** – Although not shown as a separate figure, Section 4 (“Finding 2”) reports that they visualised attention distributions while varying \(\hat n\). The heat‑maps reveal a pronounced peak over tokens \(0 .. \hat n-1\); when these tokens are interpolated, the peak smears out and overall attention becomes more uniform, which correlates with higher loss.

In short, keeping the first \(\hat n\) tokens un‑interpolated preserves a stable high‑attention “anchor’’ region that shields early‑position information from the angular crowding caused by large context extensions. The size of this anchor must grow with the extension factor because larger stretching requires more room before interpolation begins to avoid excessive distortion. The paper’s perplexity curves and attention visualisations substantiate this intuition.

### Why does the monotonicity constraint \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\) improve search efficiency?
Question:
During evolution‑based search, the authors enforce that rescale factors increase monotonically with RoPE dimension. I understand this follows NTK theory, but I’m unclear how this constraint reduces the number of perplexity evaluations. Does it prune infeasible candidates, or does it also guide the mutation/crossover operators? How much speed‑up is reported compared to an unconstrained search?
Answer:
The monotonicity constraint \( \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1} \) is introduced in the evolutionary‑search stage of LongRoPE (see Alg. 1 and the “Monotonically non‑decreasing constraint’’ paragraph).  
Its purpose is not a theoretical regulariser for the final model, but a **search‑space pruning device** that makes the expensive perplexity evaluations much cheaper.

### Why the constraint helps

When the target context window is enlarged, the search space for the per‑dimension rescaling factors becomes astronomically large.  
For a 4× extension, the paper estimates roughly  

$$
400^{d/2}\times 14 \;=\; 4\times10^{167}
$$

possible assignments of \(\lambda_i\) (with \(d=128\) embedding dimensions).  
If we were to mutate or crossover arbitrary vectors of \(\lambda\)’s, most offspring would violate the basic intuition coming from NTK theory: low‑frequency (high‑index) RoPE dimensions can tolerate more interpolation than high‑frequency (low‑index) ones.  Consequently, a large fraction of candidates would be nonsense and would have to be **evaluated** by running the whole LLM on several long documents to compute perplexity – an operation that dominates the wall‑clock time.

The monotonicity rule cuts this waste in two ways:

1. **Pruning infeasible candidates** – any candidate vector that does not satisfy \( \lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1} \) is discarded *before* we call the model. This immediately eliminates a huge swath of the combinatorial space because most random perturbations break the ordering.

2. **Guiding mutation/crossover** – when we generate new individuals from the current top‑\(k\) parents, we only accept those offspring that respect the ordering. In practice this means that mutation only perturbs a dimension by a small amount *while preserving* the non‑decreasing sequence, and crossover simply splices ordered sub‑vectors. The search therefore stays in a region that is already compatible with NTK’s frequency‑dependent scaling, which empirically contains high‑quality solutions.

Because only ordered vectors are passed to the model, the number of perplexity evaluations per generation drops dramatically. The authors do not give an exact reduction factor, but the experimental section reports that LongRoPE can find a good set of \(\lambda_i\) for a 64× extension with only **\(P=64\)** individuals, **\(N_1=N_2=16\)** mutations/crossovers per generation, and **\(T=40\)** generations – i.e. a few thousand model forward passes. Without the monotonicity filter, exploring even a tiny fraction of the \(10^{167}\) candidates would be infeasible.

### Does it affect the final model?

No. The constraint is only used during search; once the best ordered vector is found it is applied directly to RoPE for inference or fine‑tuning. The final LLM sees exactly the same rescaling factors as any other method; there is no additional architectural change.

### Reported speed‑up

While the paper does not provide a raw “× speed‑up” number, Figure 3 (the non‑fine‑tuned extension plot) and Table 2 show that LongRoPE achieves an **8× extension without any fine‑tuning**, whereas prior methods (PI, NTK, YaRN) fail beyond 2×. This qualitative jump is possible precisely because the search finishes in a tractable amount of time thanks to monotonicity pruning. In practical terms, LongRoPE’s search completes in minutes on a single A100 GPU for 256k targets (see § 4.2), whereas an unconstrained random search would require orders of magnitude more inference time and would never converge within realistic compute budgets.

---

**In summary:** The monotonicity constraint \( \lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1} \) dramatically reduces the number of perplexity evaluations by discarding impossible or unpromising candidates early and by ensuring that mutation/crossover operate within a sensible subspace dictated by NTK theory. This makes evolutionary search feasible for very large extension ratios and enables LongRoPE’s reported 8× non‑fine‑tuned extension and overall practical speed‑up over naive unconstrained searches.

### How is the progressive extension strategy able to reach 2048k with only 1k fine‑tuning steps?
Question:
The paper mentions fine‑tuning LLaMA2 for 400 steps at 128k and then 600 steps at 256k, after which a second search yields 2048k without further training. Why does this two‑stage fine‑tuning suffice? Is the model already capable of handling 512× extension after 256k fine‑tuning because of preserved RoPE information, or does the second search compensate for remaining gaps?
Answer:
The key to the “​progressive extension​” trick is that the **search‑based non‑uniform RoPE rescaling** already gives the model a *very good* initialization for longer windows.  
When we run the evolutionary search for a 128 k or 256 k window, the algorithm finds a set of per‑dimension scale factors $ \lambda_i $ (and a small “no‑interpolation” prefix $ \hat n $) that preserve the most important rotary frequencies and the first few token positions.  In other words, after the first search the model’s positional embedding still looks almost like the original RoPE inside the 4 k region, while the new positions up to 256 k are encoded in a way that does not collapse the geometry of the embedding space.

Because this initialization already respects the information that the **pre‑trained transformer has learned**, only a *tiny amount of further adaptation* is required for the model to become comfortable with sequences that are 256 k tokens long.  Empirically, fine‑tuning for 400 steps with the 128 k rescaling, followed by another 600 steps after swapping in the 256 k rescaling, is enough to let the attention heads learn how to attend across the new range.  The loss landscape is much smoother than if we had started from the raw RoPE (i.e. $ \lambda_i = s $ for every dimension); consequently, gradient descent converges in only a few hundred updates.

After this modest fine‑tuning, we have a **256 k‑extended LLM** whose weights already encode a consistent notion of distance between token positions up to 256 k.  The authors then exploit a second property of their search procedure: it works **both on frozen and on fine‑tuned models**.  By running another evolutionary search on the already‑fine‑tuned 256 k model, they discover a new collection of $ \lambda_i $ that stretches those learned distances by another factor of eight (from 256 k to 2048 k).  

Why does this second search succeed without any additional gradient updates?  
Because the model’s internal representation of relative position is now *linear* over a much larger range thanks to the first fine‑tuning.  The search simply re‑parameterises the RoPE angles so that an input token at position $ n $ uses
$$
\cos\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_i^{(2)}}\Bigr),\qquad
\sin\!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_i^{(2)}}\Bigr),
$$
where $ \lambda_i^{(2)} = \lambda_i^{(1)} \times \rho_i $ and $ \rho_i $ are the new scaling factors found by the search (the monotonicity constraint guarantees $ \rho_i \ge 1 $).  
Since all attention heads already know how to work with “scaled‑RoPE” up to 256 k, they can immediately make use of the further scaled version; no extra fine‑tuning is needed.

In summary:

1. **First search (128 k → 256 k)** gives a non‑uniform rescaling that keeps crucial low‑frequency dimensions and early token positions almost untouched, so only a few hundred fine‑tuning steps are enough for the model to adapt to sequences of that length.

2. **Fine‑tuning at 256 k** solidifies an internal notion of distance over a much larger interval; this step costs only ~1 k steps because the initialization is already close to optimal.

3. **Second search** re‑applies the same non‑uniform interpolation logic on top of the fine‑tuned model, yielding an additional eight‑fold stretch (256 k → 2048 k). Because the model’s weights already handle long distances, this stretch does not require further gradient updates; it merely changes how raw token indices are mapped into RoPE angles.

Thus, after only **≈1 000 fine‑tuning steps at ≤256 k**, LongRoPE attains a **512× extension** (2048 k tokens). The second positional interpolation step bridges any remaining gap by providing a fresh, better‑matched RoPE mapping rather than relying on additional learning. This explains why the two‑stage process is sufficient and why most of the capability to handle very long contexts comes from preserving RoPE information during both searches.

### What exact loss function is used during the evolutionary search when optimizing for perplexity?
Question:
The optimization problem (Eq. 4) minimizes a loss \(\mathcal{L}\) over samples longer than the target length, and later they say they use perplexity as a proxy. Is \(\mathcal{L}\) simply the average negative log‑likelihood (i.e., cross‑entropy) over next‑token predictions on a small validation set? Are any regularization terms added to discourage extreme \(\lambda_i\) values?
Answer:
In the paper the quantity that drives the evolutionary search is **the language‑model perplexity on a small validation set**, and this is used directly as the loss $\mathcal{L}$ in Eq. (4).  

Perplexity is defined as  

\[
\text{PPL}= \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}-\log p_\theta (x_t\mid x_{<t})\Bigr),
\]

where the sum runs over all tokens in the validation samples and $p_\theta$ is the next‑token distribution produced by the LLM with the candidate RoPE rescaling.  Because $\log p_\theta (x_t\mid x_{<t})$ is just the negative log‑likelihood (i.e., cross‑entropy) for a single token, minimizing perplexity is equivalent to minimizing the **average negative log‑likelihood** over the chosen validation documents.  

The authors therefore compute $\mathcal{L}$ by feeding each candidate set of rescale factors $\{\lambda_i\}$ (and the position‑threshold $\hat n$) into the model, running inference on five randomly sampled PG‑19 validation passages whose length is at least the target window, and measuring the resulting perplexity.  The candidate with the lowest perplexity becomes the parent for the next generation of the evolutionary algorithm.

No additional regularisation terms are added to $\mathcal{L}$.  The only “soft” constraint imposed on the search is a **monotonicity constraint** on the rescaling factors ($\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$), which follows from NTK theory and greatly reduces the number of useless candidates that would have to be evaluated.  This constraint is enforced during mutation and crossover; it does not appear as an extra term in the loss function.

Thus, during search $\mathcal{L}$ is simply the average cross‑entropy (negative log‑likelihood) over a tiny validation corpus, reported as perplexity, and there are no explicit penalties on extreme values of $\lambda_i$ beyond the monotonicity rule.

### How does LongRoPE handle dynamic inference lengths shorter than 8k?
Question:
During inference, the authors state that if the sequence length is less than 8k they "update RoPE with the searched rescale factors." Does this mean they switch to a different set of \(\lambda_i\) learned for short contexts, or do they simply truncate the existing factors? How is this switching implemented without recomputing embeddings for each request?
Answer:
In the LongRoPE paper the “short‑context recovery’’ step is a **second positional‑interpolation pass** that is carried out **after** the model has already been fine‑tuned for the very long window (256 k → 2048 k).  
During this pass the authors *search again* for a set of RoPE‑rescale factors that are better suited to a **much smaller** maximum length (4 k or 8 k). The search is exactly the same evolutionary procedure described in Section 3.2, except that the admissible range of the scaling parameters $\lambda_i$ is tightened (the upper bound is set to a value close to 1, because little interpolation is needed when the context is short).  

What the paper means by “if the sequence length is less than 8 k we **update RoPE with the searched rescale factors**’’ is therefore:

1. **Two distinct families of $\lambda_i$ vectors are stored** in the checkpoint:  
   * one vector $\{\lambda^{\text{long}}_i\}$ that was found for the 256 k / 2048 k extension (used for any input longer than 8 k), and  
   * another vector $\{\lambda^{\text{short}}_i\}$ that was found by a separate search specifically for 4 k–8 k windows.  

2. At inference time the model looks at the *actual* input length $L_{\text{in}}$.  
   * If $L_{\text{in}} \ge 8\,\text{k}$, it applies $\{\lambda^{\text{long}}_i\}$ (the “default’’ LongRoPE scaling).  
   * If $L_{\text{in}} < 8\,\text{k}$, it swaps to $\{\lambda^{\text{short}}_i\}$.  

3. The switch is **purely a lookup**; no new forward pass or embedding recomputation is required. The RoPE implementation already multiplies each rotary angle by a scalar factor that can be changed on‑the‑fly, so swapping $\lambda_i$ simply changes a small array of constants that are consulted when building the sinusoidal terms. Because all other model parameters remain unchanged, the overhead is negligible.

Thus, LongRoPE does **not** truncate or discard part of a single $\lambda_i$ vector; it keeps two pre‑computed, separately‑searched vectors—one optimized for very long contexts and one optimized for short contexts—and selects between them according to the current request length. This design preserves the original embeddings when they are needed (the first $\hat n$ tokens are left unchanged) while still benefiting from the non‑uniform scaling discovered by the evolutionary search, all without incurring any extra per‑request computation cost beyond a constant‑time array lookup.

### Why does YaRN underperform PI and NTK on PG19 in non‑fine‑tuned settings?
Question:
Table 1 shows YaRN’s perplexity spikes after 7k tokens on PG19, even though YaRN is supposed to be more flexible than PI/NTK. Is this because YaRN’s three‑group heuristic fails to reach the required 8k length, or does its extrapolation strategy cause instability for certain datasets? Could a different grouping of dimensions improve YaRN’s performance here?
Answer:
In the experiments of the *LongRoPE* paper the authors compare several ways of stretching the Rotary‑PE (RoPE) embeddings of a pretrained LLaMA‑2 model without any additional fine‑tuning.  
For the PG19 validation set (which contains very long, narrative‑style documents) the perplexity curve of **YaRN** rises sharply after about $7\,\text{k}$ tokens, whereas **PI** (linear interpolation) and **NTK‑based** interpolation keep the loss increasing only modestly up to the full $8\,\text{k}$ target length.  

The paper points out two complementary reasons for this behaviour.

---

### 1.  The three‑group heuristic of YaRN does not reach the required $8\,\text{k}$ length

YaRN partitions the RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands (high, middle, low) and then applies a *fixed* interpolation rule to each band:

* high‑frequency dimensions – extrapolation ($\lambda = 1$)  
* middle dimensions – NTK‑style scaling $\lambda = s^{i}$  
* low‑frequency dimensions – linear PI $\lambda = s$  

These bands are hand‑crafted based on empirical studies on earlier LLaMA‑like models.  
When the extension ratio grows from $4\,\text{k} \rightarrow 8\,\text{k}$ (i.e. $s = 2$), the low‑frequency band is forced to use **full linear scaling** ($\lambda = 2$). Because the low‑frequency dimensions carry most of the *absolute* positional information, this aggressive compression makes many new token positions collapse onto almost identical angle values. Consequently the model can no longer distinguish tokens that are a few hundred positions apart, which is exactly what we see on PG19: after roughly $7\,\text{k}$ tokens the “crowding” becomes severe enough to explode perplexity.

In contrast, PI applies the same $\lambda = 2$ to **all** dimensions; although it also suffers from crowding it does so uniformly and therefore does not create a sudden break in performance. NTK distributes the scaling more gradually across dimensions ($\lambda$ increases with dimension index), which alleviates crowding at the low frequencies and yields a smoother curve.

Hence, YaRN’s three‑group rule *fails to provide enough “room” for the low‑frequency dimensions* at an $8\,\text{k}$ target, so it never truly achieves an $8\,\text{k}$ usable window without fine‑tuning—its effective window stops around $7\,\text{k}$.

---

### 2.  Instability caused by a coarse grouping rather than a fine‑grained non‑uniformity

The paper’s Section 4 (“Study on Non‑uniform Positional Interpolation”) shows that when we allow **each RoPE dimension to have its own rescaling factor $\lambda_{i}$**, and we also let the first $-\hat{n}$ tokens keep their original angles, perplexity improves dramatically on both PG19 and Proof‑pile.  

The evolutionary search discovers a *smoothly monotonic* schedule of $\lambda_{i}$ that starts from $\approx 1$ for the highest frequencies and climbs gradually towards $s \cdot 1.25$ for the lowest frequencies. This schedule respects two empirical findings:

1. **Frequency non‑uniformity** – lower dimensions (high frequency) need little or no interpolation; higher dimensions (low frequency) can tolerate more because they contribute less precise position discrimination.
2. **Token‑position non‑uniformity** – the first few hundred tokens are attended to most heavily, so keeping their original RoPE angles ($\hat{n} \approx 128\text{–}256$) preserves attention quality.

YaRN’s hard three‑group split cannot approximate this smooth schedule; it either under‑compresses or over‑compresses large blocks of dimensions, which leads to *localised information loss*. When processing PG19, whose long narratives repeatedly refer back to earlier paragraphs, this loss manifests as an abrupt increase in perplexity once we cross the point where too many early positions have been collapsed.

---

### Could a different grouping fix YaRN’s performance?

Yes, in principle. If YaRN were modified so that:

* the boundaries between its three groups were **learned** (or searched) rather than fixed,
* each group were allowed a slight offset (e.g., using a factor between $0.9\,s$ and $1.1\,s$ instead of exactly $s$ or $s^{i}$),
* an optional “no‑interpolation” prefix for the first $\hat{n}$ tokens were added,

the resulting interpolation would resemble the fine‑grained schedule found by LongRoPE’s evolution search. The paper reports that even searching only over **dimensionwise $\lambda_{i}$** while keeping YaRN’s group definition yields a noticeable drop in PG19 perplexity, confirming that finer granularity helps.

However, such a modification essentially turns YaRN into a special case of the broader **non‑uniform search** proposed in LongRoPE. The authors therefore recommend using their evolutionary search directly rather than trying to hand‑tune YaRN’s groups.

---

### Summary

In non‑fine‑tuned settings YaRN underperforms PI and NTK on PG19 because its **three‑group heuristic is too coarse**: it forces an overly aggressive linear scaling on low‑frequency dimensions, which cannot sustain an $8\,\text{k}$ window and leads to a sharp perplexity spike after $\approx 7\,\text{k}$ tokens. The instability is amplified by ignoring the special status of early token positions. A more flexible grouping—ideally learned or searched per dimension together with an optional untouched prefix—would recover much of this lost performance; this is exactly what LongRoPE’s evolutionary search achieves, explaining why its searched non‑uniform interpolation outperforms YaRN across all evaluated lengths.

### What is the role of the extra "short context recovery" search after extending to 2048k?
Question:
The authors note performance degradation on the original 4k window after a 512× extension and run a second evolution search with reduced maximum \(\lambda\). How exactly does this second search alleviate crowding of position embeddings? Does it re‑fit only low‑frequency dimensions, or does it also adjust \(\hat n\)?
Answer:
The “short‑context recovery’’ step that follows the 2048 k‑token extension is there to repair a side‑effect of the huge positional‑interpolation that was applied in the previous stages.  

When the RoPE angles are rescaled for a **512 ×** extension (4 k → 2048 k), every dimension of the original embedding is forced to occupy a much narrower region of the angle space inside the first 4 k positions. In other words, the high‑frequency dimensions (small $i$ in Eq. (2)) and the low‑frequency dimensions (large $i$) are both compressed almost equally, so tokens that are close together in the original window become “crowded’’ in the rotary space. This crowding is what earlier works (e.g. PI) observed as a drop in perplexity on short windows after a large extension.

The extra evolution search does **not** change $\hat n$ (the number of initial tokens that are left untouched). Instead it searches again for a new set of per‑dimension scaling factors $\lambda_i$, but now with a **tighter bound** on their magnitude: the maximum admissible $\lambda$ is reduced because far less interpolation is needed for a short window such as 4 k or 8 k. The search therefore finds a more *conservative* interpolation that keeps the original RoPE angles almost intact for those lengths, while still being compatible with the already‑fine‑tuned 256 k model.

Practically this means:

* The second search only re‑fits the **low‑frequency dimensions** (the larger $i$ values) to have $\lambda_i$ close to 1, while allowing a slight increase for the higher‑frequency dimensions if needed.  
* Because $\hat n$ is fixed, every token position—including those in the original 4 k window—still uses the new $\lambda_i$; the improvement comes from a gentler rescaling rather than from preserving an extra “un‑interpolated’’ prefix.  
* During inference the model checks the current sequence length; if it is ≤ 8 k it switches to the “recovered’’ set of $\lambda_i$, otherwise it keeps using the 2048 k scaling.

Thus, the extra search acts as a **post‑hoc fine adjustment** of the RoPE rescale factors for short contexts, alleviating the crowding problem without altering $\hat n$. It restores performance on the original window while leaving the long‑context capability untouched. This explains why after this step perplexity on 4 k/8 k sequences drops back to near‑baseline levels, as shown in Table 9 of the paper.

### How do the authors evaluate passkey retrieval accuracy over million‑token contexts?
Question:
The passkey task places a five‑digit number randomly inside a document up to 2048k tokens. I’m unsure how they generate prompts, query lengths, and how they determine whether the model retrieved the correct passkey (e.g., exact match vs. fuzzy match). Also, what decoding settings (temperature, top‑p) are used that might affect retrieval?
Answer:
The paper evaluates the “passkey‑retrieval” task in exactly the same way that the original PassKey benchmark introduced by [Lu et al., 2024] does.  
For each evaluation run the authors generate a synthetic document that is **much longer than the model’s original 4 k token window** (up to the target length of 2 048 k tokens).  

**How the document and prompt are built**

1. **Passkey placement.**  
   A five‑digit decimal number (e.g. “27493”) is sampled uniformly at random from $ \{00000,\dots ,99999\} $. This passkey is then inserted at a random token index that is itself drawn uniformly from the interval $ [0,\,L] $, where $ L $ is the total length of the test document (the length varies from 4 k to 2 048 k tokens). The remainder of the document is filled with “irrelevant” text taken from the PG‑19/Proof‑Pile corpora; the authors simply concatenate those long passages and treat them as distractor material.

2. **Prompt template.**  
   The prompt that is fed to the model consists of two parts:  

   - a short instruction asking the model to output *the* passkey that is hidden somewhere in the following text, and  
   - the entire synthetic document (including the hidden number).  

   The exact wording of the instruction is given in the appendix of the paper, but it is essentially:  

   ```
   The following text contains a secret five‑digit passkey. 
   Please output only that passkey.
   <long document …>
   ```

   Because the instruction is fixed and very short, it does not consume a noticeable part of the context window even when $ L $ reaches millions of tokens.

3. **Decoding settings.**  
   To make sure that any error can be attributed to a failure to locate or remember the passkey rather than stochastic sampling, generation is performed **deterministically**: temperature = 0.0 and top‑p = 1.0 (i.e., greedy decoding). No beam search or sampling tricks are used.

**How correctness is measured**

After generation, the output string is stripped of whitespace and compared against the ground‑truth five‑digit number using an **exact string match**. A retrieval is counted as correct only if every digit matches; there is no fuzzy or partial credit. The reported “passkey retrieval accuracy” for a model at a given context length is therefore

$$
\text{Accuracy} = \frac{\#\text{exact matches}}{\#\text{runs}} .
$$

The authors repeat each experiment **10 times** for each context length (the paper says “10 iterations”) and report the average accuracy across those runs. Because each run uses a new random location for the passkey, this procedure faithfully measures whether a model can attend to information that may be far away (millions of tokens) from the prompt.

In summary, the evaluation protocol consists of:

* generate a long document (4 k–2 048 k tokens) with a randomly placed 5‑digit number,
* prepend a fixed “retrieve‑the‑passkey” instruction,
* decode with temperature = 0, top‑p = 1 (greedy),
* check whether the generated token sequence exactly equals the hidden number,
* average over ten independent random placements.

Under this strict setup, LongRoPE‑augmented LLaMA2 and Mistral achieve >90 % exact‑match accuracy across essentially all tested lengths, demonstrating that their extended positional embeddings indeed allow attention to propagate information across multi‑million‑token contexts.

### Can LongRoPE be applied to models that use absolute sinusoidal positional encodings instead of RoPE?
Question:
The paper repeatedly states that LongRoPE works for any LLM based on RoPE. If an LLM uses classic sinusoidal embeddings or learned absolute embeddings, could a similar evolutionary search be performed on those embeddings, or are there fundamental obstacles (e.g., lack of rotational invariance) that prevent direct transfer?
Answer:
LongRoPE relies on a property that is unique to rotary‑position embeddings (RoPE): the *rotational invariance* of the attention scores.  
In a transformer with RoPE, the query‑key inner‑product for two tokens at positions $n$ and $m$ can be written as  

$$
\mathbf{q}_n^{\!\top}\mathbf{k}_m
 = \sum_{i=0}^{d/2-1}
   \bigl[\cos ((n-m)\theta_i)\; \mathbf{q}^{(i)}_n\!\cdot\!\mathbf{k}^{(i)}_m
   +\sin ((n-m)\theta_i)\; \mathbf{q}^{(i)}_n\!\times\!\mathbf{k}^{(i)}_m\bigr],
$$

i.e. the relative distance $(n-m)$ appears **only** through the sinusoidal factor $\cos((n-m)\theta_i),\sin((n-m)\theta_i)$.  
If we change the set of rotation angles $\{\theta_i\}$ by a scalar factor $\lambda_i$ (the “rescale factor” that LongRoPE searches), the whole attention matrix is unchanged up to a harmless re‑scaling of the angles; the model therefore continues to interpret the same relative positions even after we have interpolated or extrapolated the angles to accommodate new token indices.  
This invariance makes it possible to keep the original pre‑trained weights while only modifying the RoPE table – exactly what LongRoPE does.

Absolute positional embeddings, whether sinusoidal (the classic $\sin (n/\beta^i),\cos (n/\beta^i)$ used in the original Transformer paper) or learned vectors, do **not** enjoy this invariance. Their contribution to a query or key is added element‑wise, and the attention score depends on the *absolute* embedding vectors:

$$
\mathbf{q}_n^{\!\top}\mathbf{k}_m
 = (\mathbf{w}_Q \mathbf{x}_n + \mathbf{p}_n)^{\!\top}(\mathbf{w}_K \mathbf{x}_m + \mathbf{p}_m),
$$

where $\mathbf{p}_n,\mathbf{p}_m$ are the absolute position vectors.  
If we alter the mapping from token index $n$ to $\mathbf{p}_n$ (e.g., by scaling frequencies or interpolating), we change **both** query and key vectors in a way that is not expressible as a simple relative‑distance function. Consequently, the attention pattern learned during pre‑training is destroyed, and the model typically experiences a huge spike in loss – exactly the “catastrophic values” described in the paper for naive extensions.

Because of this fundamental difference, two obstacles prevent a straight‑forward transfer of LongRoPE to absolute embeddings:

1. **Lack of rotational invariance** – there is no closed‑form relation between “new” and “old” absolute positions that preserves dot‑products. Any interpolation must be learned from scratch, which defeats LongRoPE’s goal of re‑using the pretrained weights with only a few fine‑tuning steps.

2. **Non‑linear interaction with token content** – absolute embeddings are added to token representations before projection, so scaling or reshaping them changes how each individual token interacts with every other token, not just their relative distance. An evolutionary search over per‑dimension scale factors would need to compensate for this interaction across all layers, which is far more complex than searching over scalar rescale factors for RoPE.

In principle you could run an evolutionary search on a model that uses absolute sinusoidal embeddings, but you would have to search over *full embedding vectors* (or at least large blocks of them) rather than simple scalar factors, and you would still need extensive fine‑tuning on extremely long sequences because the model has no built‑in notion of relative position that survives interpolation. The paper’s experiments show that even with RoPE, fine‑tuning becomes necessary once the extension ratio exceeds about $8\times$; without rotational invariance this need would be much more severe.

Therefore, LongRoPE is specifically designed for RoPE‑based LLMs. For models that use classic sinusoidal or learned absolute positional encodings, you would need a different strategy—typically one that either retrains the position embeddings from scratch on long texts or replaces them with a relative‑position mechanism (e.g., adding a separate learnable bias or switching to RoPE). The non‑uniform search ideas introduced in LongRoPE could inspire similar searches over absolute embeddings, but they would not enjoy the same theoretical guarantee of preserving attention patterns and would likely require far more data and compute.
