\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does YaRN underperform PI and NTK on PG19 in non‑fine‑tuned settings?
Question:
Table 1 shows YaRN’s perplexity spikes after 7k tokens on PG19, even though YaRN is supposed to be more flexible than PI/NTK. Is this because YaRN’s three‑group heuristic fails to reach the required 8k length, or does its extrapolation strategy cause instability for certain datasets? Could a different grouping of dimensions improve YaRN’s performance here?
Answer:
In the experiments of the *LongRoPE* paper the authors compare several ways of stretching the Rotary‑PE (RoPE) embeddings of a pretrained LLaMA‑2 model without any additional fine‑tuning.  
For the PG19 validation set (which contains very long, narrative‑style documents) the perplexity curve of **YaRN** rises sharply after about $7\,\text{k}$ tokens, whereas **PI** (linear interpolation) and **NTK‑based** interpolation keep the loss increasing only modestly up to the full $8\,\text{k}$ target length.  

The paper points out two complementary reasons for this behaviour.

---

### 1.  The three‑group heuristic of YaRN does not reach the required $8\,\text{k}$ length

YaRN partitions the RoPE dimensions into three frequency bands (high, middle, low) and then applies a *fixed* interpolation rule to each band:

* high‑frequency dimensions – extrapolation ($\lambda = 1$)  
* middle dimensions – NTK‑style scaling $\lambda = s^{i}$  
* low‑frequency dimensions – linear PI $\lambda = s$  

These bands are hand‑crafted based on empirical studies on earlier LLaMA‑like models.  
When the extension ratio grows from $4\,\text{k} \rightarrow 8\,\text{k}$ (i.e. $s = 2$), the low‑frequency band is forced to use **full linear scaling** ($\lambda = 2$). Because the low‑frequency dimensions carry most of the *absolute* positional information, this aggressive compression makes many new token positions collapse onto almost identical angle values. Consequently the model can no longer distinguish tokens that are a few hundred positions apart, which is exactly what we see on PG19: after roughly $7\,\text{k}$ tokens the “crowding” becomes severe enough to explode perplexity.

In contrast, PI applies the same $\lambda = 2$ to **all** dimensions; although it also suffers from crowding it does so uniformly and therefore does not create a sudden break in performance. NTK distributes the scaling more gradually across dimensions ($\lambda$ increases with dimension index), which alleviates crowding at the low frequencies and yields a smoother curve.

Hence, YaRN’s three‑group rule *fails to provide enough “room” for the low‑frequency dimensions* at an $8\,\text{k}$ target, so it never truly achieves an $8\,\text{k}$ usable window without fine‑tuning—its effective window stops around $7\,\text{k}$.

---

### 2.  Instability caused by a coarse grouping rather than a fine‑grained non‑uniformity

The paper’s Section 4 (“Study on Non‑uniform Positional Interpolation”) shows that when we allow **each RoPE dimension to have its own rescaling factor $\lambda_{i}$**, and we also let the first $-\hat{n}$ tokens keep their original angles, perplexity improves dramatically on both PG19 and Proof‑pile.  

The evolutionary search discovers a *smoothly monotonic* schedule of $\lambda_{i}$ that starts from $\approx 1$ for the highest frequencies and climbs gradually towards $s \cdot 1.25$ for the lowest frequencies. This schedule respects two empirical findings:

1. **Frequency non‑uniformity** – lower dimensions (high frequency) need little or no interpolation; higher dimensions (low frequency) can tolerate more because they contribute less precise position discrimination.
2. **Token‑position non‑uniformity** – the first few hundred tokens are attended to most heavily, so keeping their original RoPE angles ($\hat{n} \approx 128\text{–}256$) preserves attention quality.

YaRN’s hard three‑group split cannot approximate this smooth schedule; it either under‑compresses or over‑compresses large blocks of dimensions, which leads to *localised information loss*. When processing PG19, whose long narratives repeatedly refer back to earlier paragraphs, this loss manifests as an abrupt increase in perplexity once we cross the point where too many early positions have been collapsed.

---

### Could a different grouping fix YaRN’s performance?

Yes, in principle. If YaRN were modified so that:

* the boundaries between its three groups were **learned** (or searched) rather than fixed,
* each group were allowed a slight offset (e.g., using a factor between $0.9\,s$ and $1.1\,s$ instead of exactly $s$ or $s^{i}$),
* an optional “no‑interpolation” prefix for the first $\hat{n}$ tokens were added,

the resulting interpolation would resemble the fine‑grained schedule found by LongRoPE’s evolution search. The paper reports that even searching only over **dimensionwise $\lambda_{i}$** while keeping YaRN’s group definition yields a noticeable drop in PG19 perplexity, confirming that finer granularity helps.

However, such a modification essentially turns YaRN into a special case of the broader **non‑uniform search** proposed in LongRoPE. The authors therefore recommend using their evolutionary search directly rather than trying to hand‑tune YaRN’s groups.

---

### Summary

In non‑fine‑tuned settings YaRN underperforms PI and NTK on PG19 because its **three‑group heuristic is too coarse**: it forces an overly aggressive linear scaling on low‑frequency dimensions, which cannot sustain an $8\,\text{k}$ window and leads to a sharp perplexity spike after $\approx 7\,\text{k}$ tokens. The instability is amplified by ignoring the special status of early token positions. A more flexible grouping—ideally learned or searched per dimension together with an optional untouched prefix—would recover much of this lost performance; this is exactly what LongRoPE’s evolutionary search achieves, explaining why its searched non‑uniform interpolation outperforms YaRN across all evaluated lengths.
