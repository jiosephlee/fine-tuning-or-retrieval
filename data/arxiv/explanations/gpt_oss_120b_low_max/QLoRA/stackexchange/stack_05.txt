\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why do default LoRA hyperparameters (e.g., adapters only on Q/K projections) fail to match full‑model finetuning performance on large models?
Question:
Figure 1 in the paper shows that adding LoRA adapters to *all* linear layers, not just query/value projections, is crucial for matching full finetuning on LLaMA‑7B. Is there an intuitive explanation for why limiting adapters harms performance? Does this relate to which parts of the transformer contribute most to instruction following, or is it simply a matter of capacity (more trainable parameters)?
Answer:
In the LoRA paper the usual recipe is to insert low‑rank adapters only into the **query** and **value** projections of the self‑attention block.  
That works well for models whose size is modest (≤ 1 B – 3 B) because most of the “knowledge” that has to be tweaked for an instruction‑following task can be expressed by a relatively small number of additional parameters placed on those two matrices.  

When we move to **large decoder‑only LLMs (7 B and above)** the situation changes in two related ways.

---

### 1.  The parts of the transformer that carry most of the learnt knowledge are not limited to Q/K/V

A transformer layer consists of two sub‑components:

* **Self‑attention** (Q, K, V, O) – mainly responsible for mixing information across positions.
* **Feed‑forward network (FFN)** – a two‑layer MLP that expands the hidden dimension, applies a non‑linearity and projects back.

Empirically, for instruction‑following finetuning the FFN contributes a *large* share of the representational capacity that needs to be altered.  
The FFN has about **four times more parameters** than the four attention projection matrices together, and its hidden‑to‑output projection is where most of the model’s “knowledge” about facts, reasoning patterns and style resides. If we freeze all those weights and only adapt Q/K/V, the gradients have to push the limited changes in attention to compensate for a massive part of the network that remains untouched. For a 7 B model this mismatch becomes visible as a several‑point drop in downstream metrics (see Fig. 1 of QLoRA).

---

### 2.  Capacity of the low‑rank adapters

A LoRA adapter adds a rank‑$r$ matrix $\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{L}_{2}$ to a given weight $\mathbf{W}$.  
The number of trainable parameters contributed by an adapter on a layer with input size $h$ and output size $o$ is $(h+o)r$.  
If we place adapters only on Q and V (two projections per layer) we obtain roughly

$$
2\;(h+o)\,r \quad\text{trainable parameters per layer}.
$$

When we also add adapters to **K**, **O**, **the two FFN matrices**, and possibly even the embedding layer, the count grows to

$$
(4 + 2)\;(h+o)\,r = 6\;(h+o)\,r,
$$

i.e. three times more trainable weights per layer.  
Because the memory occupied by LoRA gradients dominates over the static LoRA tensors (the paper notes that even for a 7 B model LoRA gradients take ≈ 0.6 GB while the adapters themselves are only a few tens of MB), this extra capacity comes essentially *for free* in terms of GPU memory.

The extra capacity lets the optimizer find a direction that actually modifies the FFN hidden activations as well as attention routing, which is precisely what is needed to recover the performance gap seen with “default” LoRA.

---

### 3.  Interaction with quantisation (QLoRA)

QLoRA keeps the base model frozen in 4‑bit NF4 format and only updates the LoRA weights in full precision.  
If only a tiny slice (Q/K) receives updates while the bulk of the model stays quantised and immutable, any error introduced by quantisation in deeper layers cannot be corrected later on. By spreading adapters across **all linear layers** we give every quantised block a dedicated low‑rank “correction term”. The paper shows that this restores full‑precision performance even after aggressive 4‑bit quantisation.

---

### Putting it together

*Limiting adapters to Q/K/V reduces both where information can be changed (no FFN updates) and how many low‑rank parameters are available.*  
For small models this restriction is not fatal because their FFNs are tiny and their knowledge can be shifted through attention alone. For larger models, which have large FFNs containing most of the stored knowledge, we need either to fine‑tune those weights directly or provide enough trainable capacity to compensate for them – which is precisely what adding LoRA adapters **to every linear layer** does.

Thus the observation in Figure 1 (QLoRA paper) is not an accident; it reflects a fundamental scaling property: as model size grows, instruction tuning increasingly relies on adapting FFN parameters, and because LoRA parameters are cheap relative to activation memory, expanding them to all layers restores full‑model finetuning performance without any additional memory burden.
