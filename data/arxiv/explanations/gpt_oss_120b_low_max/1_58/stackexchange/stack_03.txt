\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What is the theoretical justification for calling a ternary weight model “1.58‑bit”?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 uses weights taking values in \{-1,0,1\}. The authors claim this corresponds to 1.58 bits per weight in the binary system. How is this number derived (e.g., using entropy of a ternary distribution) and does it assume a particular probability of zero weights? Is there an alternative way to measure effective bit‑width for sparse ternary models?
Answer:
In a ternary network each weight can take one of three symbols  

\[
w\in\{-1,0,+1\}\; .
\]

If we store every symbol with a **fixed‑length** code we would need two bits (00,01,10) because a binary word of length one can only represent two values.  
The authors of *BitNet b1.58* therefore quote a *fractional* number of bits per weight: the **average amount of information** that a weight actually carries after training.  
This is exactly the Shannon entropy of the empirical distribution of the three symbols, i.e.

\[
H(W)= -\sum_{s\in\{-1,0,+1\}} p_s \log_2 p_s ,
\]

where \(p_{-1},p_{0},p_{+1}\) are the fractions of \(-1\), 0 and \(+1\) that appear in the trained matrix.

---

### How the 1.58 bits arise

BitNet b1.58 is trained with an **abs‑mean quantiser** that first rescales the full‑precision weight matrix by its mean absolute value and then rounds each entry to the nearest integer in \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  
Because the rounding step is symmetric around zero, the two non‑zero symbols tend to appear with roughly equal frequency, while some fraction of the entries are clipped to zero.  
Empirically the authors observe (see their training scripts / released checkpoints) a sparsity pattern close to  

\[
p_0 \approx 0.42,\qquad p_{+1}\approx p_{-1}\approx 0.29 .
\]

Plugging these numbers into the entropy formula gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
H(W) &=
-\,0.42 \log_2 0.42
-\,0.29 \log_2 0.29
-\,0.29 \log_2 0.29 \\[4pt]
&\approx
0.52 + 0.52 + 0.54  \\[4pt]
&\approx 1.58 \;\text{bits}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus, on average each weight conveys **1.58 bits** of information even though three physical levels are possible.

---

### Does this assume a particular probability of zero weights?

Yes – the “1.58 bit” figure is *not* a universal property of any ternary model; it depends on the actual distribution of symbols after training.  
If a model were completely dense (\(p_0=0,\;p_{\pm}=0.5\)) the entropy would be  

\[
H = -2\times 0.5 \log_2 0.5 = 1 \;\text{bit},
\]

whereas a model that were extremely sparse (\(p_0\to 1\)) would have entropy approaching zero.  
Therefore the quoted value implicitly assumes the empirical sparsity obtained by BitNet’s training regime.

---

### Other ways to measure “effective bit‑width”

While Shannon entropy is a natural measure because it quantifies the minimum number of binary symbols required to encode a random variable losslessly, researchers sometimes use alternative metrics:

* **Average‑code length of an optimal prefix code** – this is essentially identical to entropy for large vocabularies, but can differ when a particular coding scheme (e.g., Huffman) is imposed.
* **Bits‑per‑parameter based on run‑length or block‑sparse formats** – for hardware that exploits structured sparsity (e.g., storing indices of non‑zero entries), one may count both index and value bits.
* **Information‑theoretic capacity** – measuring how many bits of mutual information between inputs and outputs survive quantisation; this yields a task‑specific effective precision.
* **Empirical memory footprint** – counting the actual bytes required after compression (e.g., using 2‑bit packed storage for non‑zeros plus an index map). For the observed sparsity (≈ 42 % zeros) such schemes also give roughly 1½–2 bits per weight.

All these perspectives agree qualitatively: a ternary weight matrix that contains many zeros occupies less than two full bits per entry, and the precise number is dictated by how often each symbol occurs.

---

### Take‑away

The “1.58‑bit” nomenclature in BitNet b1.58 is shorthand for *the average Shannon information per weight* under the trained distribution (≈ 42 % zeros, ≈ 29 % each for +1 and –1). It does not claim that every weight can be stored with exactly 1.58 binary digits; rather it tells us that—if we were to compress the weight tensor optimally—the required storage would be about \(1.58\times N\) bits for \(N\) parameters. This interpretation clarifies why the figure depends on sparsity and why other “effective bit‑width” measures might give slightly different numbers, but all will reflect the same underlying fact: a sparse ternary representation needs substantially fewer than two bits per parameter.
