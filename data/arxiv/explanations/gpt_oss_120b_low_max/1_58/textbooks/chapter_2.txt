\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 2: # Fundamentals of Low‑Bit Quantization  

Low‑bit quantization is the cornerstone of modern techniques that strive to shrink the memory footprint and computational cost of large neural networks, especially large language models (LLMs).  The paper *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits* demonstrates that a carefully designed quantization pipeline can reduce every weight of a Transformer to a ternary set \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) while preserving the performance of full‑precision (FP16/BF16) models.  This chapter develops the mathematical background required to understand such pipelines.  We proceed from the most abstract formulation—mapping a continuous real‑valued parameter space onto a finite codebook—to concrete rounding operators used in practice, and finally to error metrics that quantify the trade‑off between compression and accuracy.  Throughout we illustrate the concepts with the specific choices made in BitNet b1.58, showing how each design decision follows from the underlying theory.

---

## Quantization Theory: Mapping Real Numbers to Discrete Codebooks  

Quantization can be seen as a two‑step mapping:

1. **Codebook definition** – a finite set \(\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}\) (the *quantization levels*) that we will allow each real number to occupy after quantization.  
2. **Quantizer function** – a deterministic or stochastic rule \(Q:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}\) that assigns every input value \(x\) to one element of \(\mathcal{C}\).

Mathematically,
\[
\widetilde{x}=Q(x), \qquad \widetilde{x}\in\mathcal{C}.
\]
When applied elementwise to a weight matrix \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\) we obtain its quantized counterpart \(\widetilde{W}=Q(W)\).  The goal is to keep the *distortion* between \(W\) and \(\widetilde{W}\) as low as possible while dramatically reducing the number of bits required to store each entry.

The **effective bit‑width** of a quantizer is
\[
b = \log_2 |\mathcal{C}|,
\]
where \(|\mathcal{C}|\) denotes cardinality of the codebook.  In BitNet b1.58 the codebook is ternary,
\[
\mathcal{C}_{\text{b1.58}}=\{-1,0,+1\},
\]
so \(b=\log_2 3 \approx 1.58\) bits per weight, hence the name “1.58‑bit LLM”.  

A practical quantizer often incorporates a **scale factor** \(\gamma>0\) so that the codebook can represent values with magnitude comparable to the original distribution.  In a *scalar* symmetric quantizer,
\[
Q_{\text{sym}}(x;\gamma)=\gamma\, q\!\left(\frac{x}{\gamma}\right), \qquad q(y)\in\{-1,0,+1\},
\]
where \(q(\cdot)\) is an integer rounding operation described in detail later.

## Uniform Quantization, Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Intervals  

Uniform quantization partitions the real line into equally spaced intervals of width \(\Delta>0\).  For an unsigned symmetric codebook with \(M=2^b-1\) non‑zero levels (e.g., binary or ternary), the interval boundaries are
\[
B_k = k\,\Delta,\qquad k=-K,\dots,K,
\]
with \(K=\frac{M-1}{2}\).  Any input falling inside interval \([B_{k-1},B_k)\) is mapped to quantization level \(k\,\Delta\).  

Two important variants exist:

### Symmetric Uniform Quantization  
The intervals are centered at zero; the codebook is symmetric (\(-c_i = c_{-i}\)). This is natural for weight matrices whose distribution is roughly zero‑mean (as observed in trained Transformers).  
For BitNet b1.58, symmetry simplifies hardware because multiplying by \(-1,0,+1\) reduces to sign flips or drops, eliminating any need for explicit multiplication.

### Asymmetric Uniform Quantization  
When activations or embeddings have a non‑zero mean (e.g., after ReLU or after adding bias), an offset \(z_\text{zero}\) is introduced:
\[
Q_{\text{asym}}(x;\gamma,z_\text{zero}) = \gamma\, q\!\bigl(\frac{x - z_\text{zero}}{\gamma}\bigr)+z_\text{zero}.
\]
The offset restores representational power at the cost of additional bookkeeping (the *zero point*).  

In BitNet b1.58 activations are **centered on zero** by scaling each token’s activation vector to lie in \([-\!Q_b,\!Q_b]\); therefore no zero‑point term is required and the quantizer stays symmetric even for activations.

## Rounding Functions: Stochastic, Deterministic, and Round‑Clip  

The heart of any uniform quantizer lies in how we decide which integer level a scaled value should occupy.  Three families are commonly used:

### Deterministic Rounding  
The classic “nearest integer” map:
\[
\operatorname{round}(x)= \lfloor x+0.5 \rfloor.
\]
This yields minimal **absolute** rounding error for each scalar but can introduce systematic bias when many values cluster near a boundary.

### Stochastic Rounding  
To mitigate bias, stochastic rounding makes the output random:
\[
Q_{\text{stoch}}(x)= 
    \begin{cases}
        \lfloor x \rfloor & \text{with probability } \lceil x\rceil - x,\\[4pt]
        \lceil x\rceil   & \text{with probability } x - \lfloor x \rfloor .
    \end{cases}
\]
Its expected value equals \(x\), preserving first‑order statistics over many samples—useful during training when gradients flow through quantized weights.

### Round‑Clip (Used in BitNet b1.58)  
BitNet b1.58 employs a deterministic *round‑clip* operator defined as
\[
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=
    \max(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))).
\tag{1}
\]
The arguments \(a=-1,b=+1\) enforce that after rounding any out‑of‑range value is clamped to the nearest admissible codebook entry.  This simple operator guarantees that all weights lie exactly in \(\{-1,0,+1\}\), which is essential for hardware that only supports three discrete states.

#### Absmean Scaling – The Full Weight Quantizer  

BitNet b1.58 first computes a global scale factor equal to the **average absolute magnitude** of all entries in weight matrix \(W=[W_{ij}]\):
\[
\gamma = 
   \frac{1}{nm}
   \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} |W_{ij}|.
   \tag{2}
\]
Adding a tiny constant $\epsilon$ prevents division by zero:
\(W' = W/(\gamma+\epsilon)\).  
Finally,
\[
\widetilde{W}= 
   \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(
     W',\, -1,\,+1
   \right).
   \tag{3}
\]
Equation (3) implements exactly the ternary mapping used throughout BitNet b1.58.

## Statistical Measures for Quantization Error  

Quantifying how much information is lost when replacing real numbers by discrete levels is vital for both theoretical analysis and practical engineering.

### Mean Squared Error (MSE)  

For a single scalar,
\[
e(x)= (x-Q(x))^{2},
\]
and for an entire tensor \(X=\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}\),
the average MSE becomes
\[
{\rm MSE}= 
   \frac{1}{N}
   \sum_{i=1}^{N} e(x_i)
   = 
   \frac{1}{N}
   \|X-Q(X)\|_{2}^{2}.
   \tag{4}
\]
When $Q$ is uniform with step size $\Delta$, assuming $X$ has uniform density over each interval, MSE per dimension simplifies to $\Delta^{2}/12$—the classic result for high‑resolution uniform quantizers.

### Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio (SNR)

A more interpretable metric relates error power to signal power:
```
SNR = 10·log₁₀( Var(X) / MSE )  dB .
```
Higher SNR indicates less distortion; for ternary quantization with properly chosen $\gamma$, SNR can remain competitive with FP16 because $\gamma$ adapts to the signal’s magnitude.

### Impact on Matrix Multiplication  

LLMs consist largely of matrix products $Y = XW^{T}$ where $X$ denotes activations (often 8‑bit in BitNet b1.58) and $W$ are ternary weights.  The product can be written as
```
Y = X·(γ·Q_absmean(W/γ))^T
    = γ·(X·Q̂)^T ,
```
where $Q̂$ contains only −1, 0, + values.  
Because multiplying by −1 or +1 reduces to sign flipping and addition/subtraction, and multiplying by 0 discards contributions entirely, *the arithmetic cost collapses from FP16 multiply–add pairs to pure integer additions*.  

If we denote by $k$ the fraction of zero entries after quantization, then only $(1-k)$ % of terms survive; this sparsity further reduces effective FLOPs:
```
Effective FLOPs ≈ (1−k)·(2·|X|·|W|).
```
Thus even though MSE might increase slightly compared with higher‐bit schemes, overall runtime decreases dramatically—a central observation of BitNet b1.58.

## Putting Theory into Practice: Lessons from BitNet b1.58  

- **Scale selection via average absolute value** ensures that most weights lie inside $[-γ,γ]$, minimizing clipping while keeping $\gamma$ small enough that most scaled weights sit near −1, 0 or + 1 after rounding.
- **Round‑Clip guarantees exact ternarity**, which eliminates any need for floating‑point multipliers in hardware and enables direct implementation on integer adders.
- **Zero‑centered activation scaling** removes asymmetric zero points; all activation values reside within $[-Q_b,Q_b]$, simplifying both software kernels and potential ASIC designs.
- **Error analysis shows acceptable MSE** because language model performance depends more on preserving relative sign patterns than on tiny magnitude differences—a property exploited by the inclusion of the “0” state which acts as an explicit feature filter.
- **Energy models** based on per‑operation costs confirm that INT8 addition consumes orders of magnitude less energy than FP16 multiplication; combined with reduced memory traffic stemming from ternary weights, this yields up to 71× energy savings for matrix multiplication on advanced process nodes (e.g., 7 nm).

Through these design choices, BitNet b1.58 demonstrates how low‑bit quantization theory translates into concrete gains: matching full precision perplexity at model sizes ≥ 3 B parameters while achieving multi‑fold improvements in latency, memory usage and energy consumption.

--- 

In summary, low‑bit quantization rests on three pillars: a well‑chosen codebook (often symmetric and uniform), an appropriate scaling strategy (such as average absolute value), and a robust rounding/clipping rule (e.g., round‑clip).  By rigorously analyzing distortion via MSE and SNR and understanding how these distortions propagate through matrix multiplication, researchers can design LLMs like BitNet b1.58 that retain accuracy despite operating with merely 1.58 bits per weight—ushering in an era where massive language models become both performant and environmentally sustainable.
