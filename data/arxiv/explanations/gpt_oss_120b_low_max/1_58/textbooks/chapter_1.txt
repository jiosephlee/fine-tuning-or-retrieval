\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Large Language Models and the Need for Quantization

Large language models (LLMs) have transformed natural‑language processing by scaling model size and data to unprecedented levels.  Modern LLMs such as LLaMA, GPT‑3, and their open‑source descendants contain billions of parameters and are trained on trillions of tokens.  Their performance on a wide array of benchmarks—ranging from reading comprehension to commonsense reasoning—has improved dramatically, often surpassing human baselines on specific tasks.  Yet this rapid progress comes with substantial computational, memory, and energy demands that hinder both research scalability and practical deployment.  Quantization, the process of representing model weights and activations with fewer bits than the conventional 16‑bit floating‑point formats (FP16 or BF16), offers a principled way to mitigate these costs while preserving model quality.

In this chapter we first trace the evolution of LLMs from early recurrent architectures to today's massive transformer families.  We then examine the hardware bottlenecks that arise when such models are trained and served.  A brief survey of model‑compression strategies situates quantization among other techniques such as pruning and distillation.  Finally, we contrast post‑training quantization with training‑time quantization and motivate the field’s current push toward extreme low‑bit regimes—specifically the 1‑bit (ternary) representations exemplified by BitNet b1.58.

## Evolution of LLMs: from early models to today’s billions‑parameter transformers

The journey of language modeling began with statistical n‑gram methods, which captured limited local context using count‑based probability tables.  The introduction of neural networks—first simple feed‑forward language models and then recurrent neural networks (RNNs)—enabled the modeling of longer dependencies through learned continuous representations.  However, RNNs suffered from vanishing gradients and limited parallelism, constraining their scalability.

The breakthrough came with the transformer architecture, which replaces recurrence with self‑attention mechanisms.  Self‑attention computes pairwise interactions between all tokens in a sequence using matrix multiplications:

\[
\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V,
\]

where \(Q,K,V\) are linear projections of the input embeddings and \(d_k\) is the dimensionality of the key vectors.  Because these operations are highly parallelizable on modern GPUs and TPUs, transformers could be scaled to ever larger depths and widths.

Early transformer language models such as the original GPT family contained tens or hundreds of millions of parameters.  Successive generations—GPT‑2 (1.5 B), GPT‑3 (175 B), PaLM (540 B), and open‐source variants like LLaMA (7 B–70 B)—demonstrated that increasing parameter count together with data volume yields systematic improvements across virtually all language tasks.  The “scaling law” phenomenon observed in these works suggests that performance (e.g., perplexity) improves predictably as a function of model size \(N\) and token count \(T\), typically following a power law:

\[
\text{Perplexity} \approx \alpha N^{-\beta} + \gamma T^{-\delta},
\]

with empirically fitted exponents \(\beta,\delta >0\).  Consequently, research has focused on building ever larger models, culminating in the current era where multi‑billion‑parameter transformers are standard.

While these massive models achieve state‑of‑the‑art results, each parameter is traditionally stored in a 16‑bit floating point format (FP16 or BF16).  For a model with \(N=10^9\) parameters this translates to roughly \(2\,\text{GB}\) of weight memory before any additional overhead from activations, optimizer states, or gradient buffers.  The cost multiplies quickly for larger models, making training and inference expensive both financially and environmentally.

## Hardware bottlenecks: memory bandwidth, compute energy, and latency

Two intertwined hardware constraints dominate the deployment of LLMs: **memory bandwidth** and **compute energy**.

1. **Memory bandwidth**: During inference each token generation requires loading all weight matrices for linear layers (the bulk of a transformer).  The dominant operation is matrix multiplication \(XW^{\top}\), where \(X\) is an activation matrix (\(b \times d_{\text{model}}\)) and \(W\) is a weight matrix (\(d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{model}}\)).  Even if compute units can perform FLOPs at petaflop rates, the speed at which weight data can be shuttled from DRAM to on‑chip SRAM becomes the limiting factor.  Quantizing weights reduces their size dramatically; moving from FP16 (16 bits per weight) to a ternary representation \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) (effectively 1.58 bits per weight) cuts bandwidth requirements by more than an order of magnitude.

2. **Compute energy**: Modern silicon chips are constrained not merely by raw FLOP capacity but by power budgets.  Floating‑point multiply–accumulate (MAC) operations consume considerably more energy than integer additions.  In an FP16 linear layer each output element requires both a multiplication and an addition:
   \[
   y_i = \sum_{j} x_j \cdot w_{ij} .
   \]
   By contrast, BitNet’s “BitLinear” replaces multiplications with sign checks because ternary weights can be represented as \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  The operation reduces to:
   \[
   y_i = \sum_{j:\;w_{ij}=+1} x_j - \sum_{j:\;w_{ij}=-1} x_j ,
   \]
   i.e., solely integer additions/subtractions over INT8 activations.  Energy models for advanced process nodes (e.g., 7 nm) estimate that an INT8 addition consumes roughly \(0.03\,\text{pJ}\) whereas an FP16 multiplication costs around \(2\,\text{pJ}\).  Consequently, BitNet achieves more than a **70×** reduction in arithmetic‐operation energy for matrix multiplication—a dominant component of total inference power.

3. **Latency**: Latency per generated token depends both on how many operations can be pipelined and how quickly data can be accessed.  Because BitLinear eliminates multiplications entirely, it enables specialized hardware kernels—such as the 2‑bit kernels from Ladder—that exploit SIMD addition pipelines efficiently, yielding speedups that grow with model size (e.g., a reported **4.1×** faster inference for a 70 B BitNet b1.58 versus its FP16 counterpart).

These bottlenecks collectively motivate research into ultra‑low‑bit representations where memory footprints shrink dramatically while compute kernels become simpler, faster, and more energy‐efficient.

## Overview of model compression techniques (pruning, distillation, quantization)

Before focusing on quantization it is useful to place it within the broader family of model compression methods:

- **Pruning** removes redundant parameters by zeroing out weights based on magnitude or sensitivity criteria.  Structured pruning deletes entire rows/columns or attention heads to enable dense matrix kernels after fine‐tuning; unstructured pruning leads to sparse matrices that require specialized sparse linear algebra libraries.

- **Distillation** trains a smaller “student” model to mimic the logits or embeddings produced by a larger “teacher” model.  This approach preserves much of the teacher’s knowledge while drastically reducing parameter count; however it still typically relies on conventional FP16 arithmetic during inference.

- **Quantization** reduces numeric precision of weights and/or activations after (post‑training quantization) or during training (training‑time quantization).  Quantization can be uniform (linear scaling across a range) or nonuniform (learned codebooks).  The lowest practical bitwidth historically for large language models has been around **4 bits**, achieved through techniques such as GPTQ or AWQ that carefully calibrate rounding errors per layer.

Each technique trades off between *accuracy loss* and *resource savings*.   Pruning often requires sophisticated sparse kernels; distillation introduces an additional training stage; quantization directly addresses memory bandwidth without needing algorithmic redesigns but may suffer from larger approximation error when bitwidth becomes extreme.

## Post‑training quantization vs. training‑time quantization

**Post‑training quantization (PTQ)** takes a fully trained FP16 model and compresses its weights after training completes.  
Typical PTQ pipelines involve:
1. Collecting calibration data to estimate activation ranges.
2. Applying a uniform affine transform \(x_q = \text{round}(x / s) + z\), where \(s\) is scale and \(z\) is zero point.
3. Optionally fine-tuning small subsets of layers (“quantization-aware fine tuning”) to recover lost accuracy.

While PTQ is attractive for rapid deployment—no retraining needed—it is inherently *suboptimal* because the original optimization never considered low precision during gradient updates. Empirically PTQ works well down to about **4 bits**, but further reductions cause significant degradation in perplexity or downstream task performance.

In contrast, **training‑time quantization** integrates low‐bit constraints into the forward pass during every optimization step.  
The BitNet family exemplifies this approach: weights are forced into ternary values throughout training using an *absmean* quantizer:

\[
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\,+1\right),
\]
with
\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,
\qquad
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\min(b,\operatorname{round}(x))).
\]

Here \(\gamma\) normalizes each weight matrix by its average absolute magnitude before rounding, ensuring that after quantization the distribution remains balanced around zero while preserving sparsity via the possible zero value.  
During backpropagation gradients flow through a straight‑through estimator: although \(\widetilde{W}\) is discrete for forward computation, its underlying full­precision copy \(W\) continues to receive gradient updates.

Training with such quantizers enables models like **BitNet b1.58**—where every parameter takes values in \(\{-1,0,+1\}\)—to achieve *parity* with full precision baselines on perplexity and zero‑shot downstream tasks when trained from scratch on standard token counts (e.g., 100 B tokens).  

Thus training‐time quantization avoids the accuracy gap typical of PTQ at extreme bitwidths while still delivering all benefits of reduced memory footprint during inference.

## Motivation for moving beyond 4‑bit and toward 1‑bit representations

Quantizing down to four bits already offers substantial hardware savings—a factor of four reduction in weight storage compared with FP16—and has become commonplace in industry pipelines (e.g., smoothquant or GPTQ). However several compelling reasons drive research toward even lower bitwidths:

1. **Energy proportionality**: Energy consumption scales superlinearly with bit precision because each extra bit doubles transistor switching activity in arithmetic units. Dropping from four to one effective bits yields up to an order–of–magnitude reduction in dynamic power for matrix multiplications—critical for large deployments where total power can exceed megawatts.

2. **Memory bandwidth ceiling**: Even if compute pipelines are fast enough, feeding weights across DRAM remains bounded by bus width and latency. A ternary weight set occupies only about **6%** of FP16 memory; thus streaming entire transformer layers becomes feasible without resorting to multi‑chip interconnects or aggressive compression schemes.

3. **Algorithmic simplicity**: When weights are confined to \(\{-1,0,+1\}\), matrix multiplication reduces mathematically to signed additions only—a kernel amenable to aggressive software optimizations (e.g., using SIMD population count tricks) and dedicated ASIC designs that omit floating point units entirely.

4. **Scaling laws shift**: Empirical results from BitNet b1.58 reveal an emerging *new scaling law*: for comparable computational budgets,
   - A **13 B** ternary model outperforms a **3 B** FP16 model,
   - A **30 B** ternary model surpasses a **7 B** FP16 counterpart,
   - A **70 B** ternary model exceeds what a **13 B** FP16 model can achieve.
   
   Hence performance gains obtained solely by increasing precision can instead be realized by enlarging parameter counts within a low‐bit regime—a more cost‐effective path given fixed hardware constraints.

5. **Enabling new deployment scenarios**: Edge devices and mobile platforms have strict power envelopes (<10 W). Ternary LLMs dramatically cut both memory use (allowing entire models to reside in SRAM) and arithmetic energy; this opens avenues for on-device inference that were previously limited to tiny distilled models.

In summary, moving beyond traditional four‐bit quantization toward truly binary/ternary representations not only addresses fundamental hardware bottlenecks but also reshapes how researchers think about scaling LLMs—favoring larger low‐precision models over smaller high‐precision ones without sacrificing task performance.
