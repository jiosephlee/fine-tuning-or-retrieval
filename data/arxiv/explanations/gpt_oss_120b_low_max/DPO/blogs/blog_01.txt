# Why Your Language Model is Actually a Hidden Reward Model  

*An accessible walkthrough of the core insight behind Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): how the policy itself encodes the reward function, eliminating the need for a separate reward model.*

---

## 1. The usual RLHF picture – two separate pieces  

When we train a large language model (LLM) to follow human preferences, the standard pipeline looks like this:

1. **Supervised fine‑tuning (SFT).**  
   We start from a massive, unsupervised LM and fine‑tune it on high‑quality instruction data.

2. **Reward modelling.**  
   Human annotators compare two model outputs, marking one as “better”.  
   A neural network learns to predict these binary preferences; this network is called the *reward model* \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\).

3. **Reinforcement learning (RL).**  
   Using the reward model as a proxy for human judgment, we run a policy‑optimisation algorithm (usually PPO) that nudges the LM toward higher‑reward responses while staying close to the original SFT model (the KL‑constraint).

The three steps feel natural, but they also introduce a lot of engineering overhead: we need a separate reward network, we must sample from the LM during RL, and hyper‑parameter tuning becomes fiddly.

---

## 2. What if the LM *already* contains the reward?  

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) asks a bold question: *Do we really need an explicit reward network at all?*  

The answer is “no”. By looking at the mathematics of the RLHF objective, we can rewrite it so that **the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) itself encodes the same information that a reward model would**. In other words, your language model is secretly a reward model.

### 2.1 The KL‑constrained reward maximisation objective  

The classic RLHF objective (Eq. 3 in the paper) is  

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\!\big[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\big]
\;-\; \beta \, D_{\text{KL}}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big).
\]

* \(\pi_{\theta}\): the policy we are trying to learn.  
* \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): the reference SFT policy (the “old” model).  
* \(r_{\phi}\): the learned reward function.  
* \(\beta\) controls how far we are allowed to drift from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

Because language generation is discrete, we normally solve this with REINFORCE‑style RL.

### 2.2 The optimal policy has a closed form  

If we treat \(r\) as any function of \((x,y)\), solving the above optimisation under a KL constraint yields an analytical solution:

\[
\boxed{\;
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big)
\;}
\tag{1}
\]

where \(Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\) normalises the distribution.

*Think of Eq. (1) as “re‑weighting” the reference policy: answers that receive higher reward get boosted exponentially, while low‑reward answers get suppressed.*

### 2.3 Flip it: express reward in terms of the policy  

From Eq. (1) we can isolate \(r(x,y)\):

1. Take logs:  
   \(\log \pi_{r}(y|x)=\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)+\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)-\log Z(x).\)

2. Rearrange:  

\[
\boxed{\;
r(x,y)=\beta\,\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\,\log Z(x)
\;}
\tag{2}
\]

Notice that **the only term that depends on \(y\) is the log‑ratio of policies**; the partition function \(\log Z(x)\) depends solely on the prompt \(x\). 

Now imagine we replace the *unknown* optimal policy \(\pi_{r}\) with our *learnable* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). Equation (2) tells us exactly what reward function would correspond to any candidate policy: it is simply a scaled log‑ratio between that policy and the reference.

### 2.4 Preferences depend only on *differences* of rewards  

Human preference data is modelled with a Bradley–Terry (BT) probability:

\[
p(y_1 \succ y_2|x)=
\sigma\!\big(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\big),
\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]

Plugging Eq. (2) into the difference \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\) cancels out \(\log Z(x)\):

\[
r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)=
\beta\,\Bigl[\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
-
\log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}\Bigr].
\]

Therefore **the human preference likelihood can be written directly in terms of policies**, without ever mentioning an explicit reward network:

\[
p(y_w \succ y_l | x)=
\sigma
\Bigl(
\beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

---

## 3. From math to a single loss function  

Equation (3) looks exactly like a binary classification probability! We can therefore maximise its likelihood by minimising a standard cross‑entropy loss:

```math
L_{DPO}(\theta)
= -\, \mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim D}
    \Big[ 
      \log\sigma
      \big(
        \beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)}
        - 
        \beta\, \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}
      \big)
    \Big].
```

That’s **all** we need! The loss:
* pushes up the probability of preferred completions (\(y_w\)),
* pushes down the probability of dispreferred completions (\(y_l\)),
* weights each example by how badly the current policy’s implicit “reward” disagrees with human judgment (the sigmoid term).

No separate reward model, no Monte‑Carlo rollouts, no extra value‑function estimator—just a tidy binary cross entropy over *policy ratios*. This is Direct Preference Optimization.

---

## 4. Visual intuition – “re‑weighting” versus “scoring”  

Imagine you have two jars of marbles:

- **Jar A** contains marbles drawn according to the reference LM \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  
- **Jar B** contains marbles drawn according to your current LM \(\pi_{\theta}\).

Each marble is labelled with its text output \(y_i.\)

In classic RLHF we first attach a **score** (the reward \(r_\phi(y_i)\)) to every marble, then use an RL algorithm to shift probability mass toward high‑scoring marbles while keeping overall distribution similar to Jar A.

In DPO we skip scoring entirely. Instead we directly **compare** how often each marble appears in Jar B relative to Jar A:
- If marble \(y_w\) appears *more often* in Jar B than in Jar A, its log‑ratio is positive → DPO increases its weight.
- If marble \(y_l\) appears *less often*, its log‑ratio is negative → DPO decreases its weight.

The preference loss simply says “the ratio for preferred marbles should be larger than for dispreferred ones.” The learning dynamics automatically adjust Jar B so that these ratios align with human judgments.

---

## 5. Why does this work? Theoretical guarantees  

A few key facts back up this simple reparameterisation:

1. **Equivalence class of rewards.**  
   Two reward functions differing only by an additive term that depends solely on \(x\) induce exactly the same BT preferences (Lemma 1). Equation (2) picks one member from each class—specifically, the one whose additive term makes Eq. (1)’s partition function equal to one.

2. **No loss of expressivity.**  
   Theorem 1 in the paper proves that *any* reward function compatible with BT can be written as   
   \(r(x,y)=\beta\, \log (\pi(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)) + f(x)\).  
   Hence using this form does not restrict what rewards we could have learned.

3. **Exact optimality under KL constraint.**  
   Because Eq. (1) is derived from solving the KL‑constrained optimisation analytically, any policy that maximises Eq. (3)’s likelihood is *exactly* the optimal solution to the original RLHF objective—no approximation error from sampling or value‑function bias.

These results explain why DPO achieves comparable or better performance than PPO while being far simpler.

## 6. Practical takeaways  

- **Implementation:** Replace PPO’s two‑step loop (reward training → policy RL) with one pass over your preference dataset using standard cross‑entropy code (e.g., `torch.nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss`). Compute logits as `beta * (log_pi_theta - log_pi_ref)` for both preferred and dispreferred completions.
- **Reference policy:** Usually set `π_ref = π_SFT` because that is what generated your preference pairs. If you only have raw preference data without an SFT checkpoint, you can initialise `π_ref` by maximum likelihood on all preferred completions.
- **Hyperparameter β:** Controls how aggressively you trade off preference satisfaction vs staying close to `π_ref`. Small β = strong KL penalty (conservative), large β = more freedom.
- **Stability:** The weighting term `σ(·)` in front of gradients automatically downweights examples where your current implicit reward already agrees with human preference—preventing catastrophic over‑confidence that plain unlikelihood training suffers from.

---

## 7. Closing thoughts  

Direct Preference Optimization shows that *the distinction between “policy” and “reward” can be blurred* when preferences are expressed as pairwise comparisons. By re‑expressing the Bradley–Terry likelihood in terms of log‑probability ratios, we obtain a clean loss that trains **the same network to be both generator and scorer**.

So next time you fine‑tune an LLM on human preferences, remember: you’re not adding another head on top of your model—you’re simply reshaping its own output distribution until it behaves like an implicit reward model that already knows what you like.
