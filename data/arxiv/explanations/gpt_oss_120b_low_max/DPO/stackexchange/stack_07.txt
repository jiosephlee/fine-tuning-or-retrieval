\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What are the theoretical implications of Lemma 2 (same optimal policy for equivalent rewards)?
Question:
Lemma 2 states that reward functions differing by a function of $x$ only produce identical optimal policies under the KL‑constrained objective. How does this fact justify discarding absolute reward scaling during training? Does it imply that any additive constant to $r(x,y)$ is irrelevant for learning, and why does this matter for DPO’s reparameterisation?
Answer:
**Answer**

Lemma 2 in the DPO paper says that if two reward functions differ only by a term that depends on the prompt \(x\) (i.e.  
\(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)\) for some arbitrary function \(f\)), then they lead to exactly the same optimal policy when we solve the KL‑constrained RLHF objective

\[
\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]-
\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr].
\]

The reason is simple: the extra term \(f(x)\) does not depend on the action \(y\), so it factors out of the expectation over \(y\) and from the KL‑penalty, which only involves the *ratio* \(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Concretely,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[r'(x,y)]
&= \mathbb{E}_{y}[r(x,y)+f(x)]\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{y}[r(x,y)] + f(x),
\end{aligned}
\]

and the KL term is unchanged because it does not contain \(r'\). Adding a constant that is identical for every possible completion under a given prompt therefore shifts the objective by the same amount for **all** policies, leaving the arg‑max unchanged. Hence every reward in the same equivalence class yields the same optimal policy.

---

### Why this lets us ignore absolute reward scaling

During training we never have access to the true “absolute” reward; we only observe *preferences* between two completions. A preference model such as Bradley–Terry (or Plackett–Luce) depends on **differences** of rewards:
\[
p(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l})\bigr).
\]
If we add a constant \(c(x)\) to *both* rewards, the difference is unchanged and so is the probability of observing a particular preference. Consequently any estimator that maximises the likelihood of the observed preferences is free to pick any member of the equivalence class—there is no statistical signal that tells it which particular additive term to use.

Lemma 2 tells us that, **regardless of which member we pick**, the induced optimal policy will be identical. Therefore we can arbitrarily normalise the reward (e.g. force its mean to be zero for each prompt, or divide by its standard deviation) without affecting the final policy. In practice this normalisation removes an otherwise arbitrary degree of freedom that would otherwise make optimisation ill‑conditioned: without it, identical updates to two policies could produce wildly different scalar reward values merely because of a different offset, leading to unstable gradients.

---

### Connection to DPO’s re‑parameterisation

DPO rewrites the optimal policy in closed form:

\[
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
   \qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
   \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\]

If we replace \(r(x,y)\) by an equivalent reward \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{\beta} r'(x,y)\Bigr)
&=\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr)\,
  \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{\beta} f(x)\Bigr),\\
Z'(x)&=\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac1{\beta} f(x)\Bigr) Z(x),
\end{aligned}
\]

and these extra exponential factors cancel in the fraction defining \(\pi_{r}\). Hence the policy expression already *absorbs* any additive term into its normalising constant, making it invisible to the policy itself. This cancellation is exactly what Lemma 2 formalises.

When we invert (take logs of) this relationship to express a reward in terms of a policy,

\[
r(x,y)= \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x),
\]

the term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) plays precisely the role of that arbitrary function of \(x\). DPO **chooses** the representative reward in each class by setting that term to zero—i.e. it uses the *unnormalised* log‑ratio as its implicit reward:

\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)= \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
\]

Because any other member of the equivalence class differs only by a function of \(x\), using this particular representative does not sacrifice expressiveness: every admissible reward can be represented after suitable re‑normalisation, and its optimal policy is exactly \(\pi_{\theta}\).

---

### Summary

* **Lemma 2** shows that adding an \(x\)-only term to a reward does not change either (i) the preference probabilities (which depend on reward differences) or (ii) the optimal KL‑constrained policy (the extra term cancels out after normalisation).
* Consequently, during training we may **discard absolute scaling** or any additive constant attached to rewards; doing so merely fixes an otherwise arbitrary degree of freedom and makes optimisation numerically stable.
* DPO exploits this fact by re‑parameterising every admissible reward as a log‑ratio between the current policy and a reference policy. The additive “partition‑function” term is set to zero, yielding a clean expression for an implicit reward that can be learned directly with a binary‑cross‑entropy loss—no separate reward model or RL loop is required.

Thus Lemma 2 justifies why DPO can work with *only* relative preferences and still recover the exact optimal policy despite never learning an explicit absolute reward function.
