\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Can DPO be combined with off‑policy preference data (e.g., collected from older models)?
Question:
The paper mentions that preference datasets are typically collected from a fixed SFT model and that DPO uses that model as $
abla_{ref}$. If I have a mixture of preferences generated by several historic policies, how should I choose or construct $
abla_{ref}$? Is there a principled way to weight different reference policies within DPO?
Answer:
In the DPO formulation the **reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$** plays the same role as it does in the usual RL‑HF pipeline: it is the distribution that the KL‑penalty keeps the fine‑tuned model close to.  
All of the theory (Eq. (5)–(9) and Theorem 1) assumes that a *single* reference distribution is fixed during training; the loss

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})
=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\log\sigma\Bigl(\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}-
                \beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\Bigr)
$$

is derived under that assumption. Consequently, if the preference dataset $\mathcal{D}$ contains examples generated by several historic policies, you do **not** need to keep each of those policies separate in the loss; you only need a single $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that approximates the *overall* behaviour of the data‑collection policies.

### How to build $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from a mixture of historic policies

1. **Start from a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model, if one exists.**  
   The paper recommends $\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ whenever the preference pairs were sampled from an SFT model, because then the KL‑penalty exactly reflects the distribution on which the human labeler saw the candidates.

2. **If no SFT model is available (e.g., preferences come from many older checkpoints), learn a “proxy” reference by maximum‑likelihood on the *preferred* completions.**  
   Concretely,

$$
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi}
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w|x)] .
$$

   This optimisation collapses all historic policies into a single language model that places high probability on the responses humans actually liked. The paper’s Section 4 (“DPO outline”) shows that this step “mitigates the distribution shift between the true reference distribution which is unavailable, and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ used by DPO.”

3. **Optionally, use importance weighting when fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.**  
   If you know for each pair which historic checkpoint produced it (e.g., checkpoint $k$ with weight $w_k$), you can maximise a weighted likelihood,

$$
\sum_{k} w_k\;
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}_k}[\log \pi(y_w|x)] ,
$$

   where $\mathcal{D}_k$ is the subset generated by policy $k$. This “averages’’ the old policies according to their contribution to the dataset, but it does not change any part of the DPO loss itself—the loss still uses a single reference distribution.

### Why you do not need an explicit mixture inside the DPO loss

The KL term in Eq. (2) already expresses *how far* the current policy deviates from whatever reference you supply.  If you defined a mixture

$$
\tilde{\pi}_{\text{ref}}(y|x)=\sum_{k}\alpha_k\,\pi^{(k)}(y|x),
$$

with non‑negative weights $\alpha_k$ that sum to one, you could plug this mixture directly into Eq. (9).  However:

* The mixture is just another valid language‑model distribution; DPO makes no distinction between a mixture and a single checkpoint.
* All theoretical guarantees (Theorem 1, Lemma 2) rely only on *the* reference distribution being fixed, not on how it was obtained.
* Empirically, fitting a single proxy model (step 2 above) works as well as any hand‑crafted mixture while being far simpler to implement.

### Practical recipe

1. **Collect all preference triples** $(x,y_w,y_l)$.  
2. **Train a reference LM** on only the $y_w$ side (or on all generations if you prefer). Use standard cross‑entropy; any transformer architecture compatible with your final policy works.  
3. **Run DPO** with this trained LM as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  Choose your KL‑strength $\beta$ as usual (the same values reported in the paper work well).  
4. **Optional sanity check:** after training, compute average per‑token KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  If it is dramatically larger than what you anticipate, you may want to increase $\beta$ or re‑train a more expressive reference (e.g., larger hidden size).

In short, DPO does not require an explicit weighting scheme over multiple historic policies; you collapse them into a single reference model—either an existing SFT checkpoint or a maximum‑likelihood model trained on the preferred answers—and then use that model unchanged in Eq. (9).  This approach preserves all of DPO’s theoretical properties while remaining simple and computationally cheap.
