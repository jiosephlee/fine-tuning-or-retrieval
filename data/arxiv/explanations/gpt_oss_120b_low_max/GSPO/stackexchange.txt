\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GRPO's token‑level importance weight violate the principle of importance sampling?
Question:
The paper argues that GRPO applies the importance weight \( w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})} \) at each token, but importance sampling requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution. Could someone explain in detail why using a single‑sample weight per token is mathematically incorrect and how this leads to high‑variance gradients?
Answer:
In GRPO the “importance weight’’ that appears in the loss is  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

and this factor is multiplied with the (group‑wise) advantage and summed over all tokens of all sampled responses.  
At first sight this looks like the usual importance‑sampling ratio that one uses in PPO, but the paper points out that it is **not** a valid importance‑sampling estimator. The reason can be understood by recalling what importance sampling actually estimates.

---

### What importance sampling does

Suppose we want the expectation of some function $f(z)$ under a *target* distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] .
\]
If we can only draw samples $z^{(1)},\dots ,z^{(N)}$ from a *behavior* (or proposal) distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, we rewrite the expectation as  

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
   = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim\pi_{\text{beh}}}
     \Bigl[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\Bigr].
\]

Crucially, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ is **the same for every sample** drawn from $\pi_{\text{beh}}$. The estimator therefore averages many *independent* terms
$\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z^{(k)})}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z^{(k)})}f(z^{(k)})$; as $N\to\infty$ the law of large numbers guarantees convergence to the true expectation, and the variance diminishes roughly as $1/N$.

---

### What GRPO does instead

In language generation each token is drawn from a *different* conditional distribution:
\[
y_{i,t}\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,|x,y_{i,<t}).
\]
GRPO treats each token as a separate “sample’’ and plugs in a *single‑sample* importance weight  
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
                       {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

Two problems arise:

1. **The denominator is a single draw, not an average.**  
   For a fixed context $(x,y_{i,<t})$ the behavior distribution is $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x,y_{i,<t})$. The correct importance‑sampling estimator would require an expectation over many draws from this *same* distribution, i.e. many alternative next‑tokens for that exact prefix. GRPO uses only the one token that actually appeared in the rollout. Consequently, the ratio is a random variable with huge variance; there is nothing to “smooth it out’’ by averaging.

2. **The target quantity being estimated does not match the unit of weighting.**  
   The reward (and therefore the advantage) belongs to an entire **sequence** $y_i$. The true off‑policy correction should therefore involve the ratio of full‑sequence probabilities,
   \[
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
     = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}
            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})},
   \]
   which is exactly what GSPO uses (with length‑normalisation). By breaking this product into token‑wise factors and treating each factor as an independent importance weight, GRPO discards the fact that they belong to a single joint likelihood. The variance of the product grows exponentially with length, while the variance of each factor remains uncontrolled because it is evaluated on a single sample.

Because GRPO’s loss multiplies each token’s gradient by its own noisy weight, errors accrue additively over the whole sequence. For long responses this accumulation can dominate the update direction, causing wildly fluctuating gradients and—after repeated updates—catastrophic collapse of the policy.

---

### How this explains high‑variance gradients

Consider a simple two‑token example. The unbiased estimator of the sequence‑level expectation would be  

\[
\bigl[w_1 w_2\bigr]\,A
  = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_1|x)\,\pi_\theta(y_2|x,y_1)}
         {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_1|x)\,\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_2|x,y_1)}\,A,
\]
where $A$ denotes the (group‑wise) advantage.

GRPO replaces this by  

\[
w_1 A + w_2 A,
\]

which has expectation $A\,(\mathbb{E}[w_1] + \mathbb{E}[w_2]) = 2A$, i.e. it is **biased** (it over‑counts the advantage) and its variance contains two independent terms instead of one combined term. When many tokens are present, the bias grows linearly with length and the variance scales roughly with the number of tokens, precisely what the paper observes empirically.

---

### Summary

* Importance sampling requires averaging a weight that corrects *the entire target distribution*; using a **single** next‑token draw to compute that weight breaks the estimator’s unbiasedness and variance reduction properties.
* GRPO applies token‑level ratios as if each token were an independent off‑policy sample, even though they are correlated components of one sequence whose reward is assigned at the sequence level.
* This mismatch injects large, uncontrolled noise into every gradient step; as sequence length grows, the noise accumulates, leading to unstable updates and eventual model collapse.

GSPO resolves the issue by moving the importance weight to the **sequence level**, i.e.

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
          = \exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_t \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr),
\]

and applying clipping/advantage scaling to this single scalar per response. This obeys the fundamental principle of importance sampling, dramatically reduces variance and restores stable learning.

### How is the sequence‑level importance ratio \( s_i(\theta) \) derived and why is length normalization needed?
Question:
GSPO defines \( s_i(\theta)=\left( \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{1/|y_i|}=\exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})}\right) \). I understand the exponentiation is a length‑normalization, but I'm unclear on two points: (1) why taking the \(1/|y_i|\) power yields a more stable importance ratio, and (2) what would happen if we omitted this normalization – e.g., how would the clipping range need to change?
Answer:
In GSPO the importance ratio for a sampled response  

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
       =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr)
\]

is obtained from the **sequence‑level likelihood ratio** \(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\) by raising it to the power \(1/|y_i|\).  
The paper calls this operation *length‑normalisation* and gives two practical reasons for it.

### 1. Why the \(1/|y_i|\) power makes the ratio more stable  

The raw sequence likelihood ratio is a product of token‑wise ratios:

\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
   = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

When a response is long, even modest changes in a few token probabilities can cause the product to explode (if the ratios are > 1) or collapse (if they are < 1). Consequently the raw importance weight varies over many orders of magnitude across responses of different lengths. This high variance is exactly what caused GRPO to become unstable: token‑level weights are multiplied many times, and the clipping mechanism amplifies the resulting noise.

Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum; dividing by \(|y_i|\) computes the **average log‑ratio per token**, and exponentiating brings us back to a scalar that reflects the *geometric mean* of the token‑wise ratios:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\exp\Bigl(\underbrace{\frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t}\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid …)}%
                           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid …)}}_{\text{average log‑ratio}}\Bigr).
\]

Because we are averaging, a single outlier token can no longer dominate the whole importance weight; its effect is diluted across the entire sequence. In statistical terms the variance of an average scales as \(1/|y_i|\), so longer sequences actually have *lower* variance after normalisation. This is why the paper states that length normalisation “reduces the variance and controls \(s_i(\theta)\) within a unified numerical range”.

### 2. What would happen if we omitted the normalisation  

If we used the raw sequence ratio  

\[
w_i^{\text{raw}}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
\]

the magnitude of \(w_i^{\text{raw}}\) would be tightly coupled to response length:

* For a short response (\(|y|\approx 5\)) a modest per‑token change (e.g. a factor of 1.2) results in a total weight around \(1.2^5 \approx 2.5\).
* For a long response (\(|y|\approx 200\)) the same per‑token change blows up to \(1.2^{200}\approx 10^{12}\).

Hence a single clipping interval \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\) would be far too tight for short sequences and far too loose for long ones. To keep most weights inside the clipping window one would have to **scale \(\varepsilon\) with the sequence length**, e.g.

\[
\varepsilon(|y|)\approx
   \exp(C/|y|)-1
\]

for some constant \(C\). In practice this means different queries would need different clipping thresholds, which defeats one of PPO’s design goals (a simple, global hyper‑parameter). Moreover, extremely large or tiny raw weights would cause gradient explosion or vanishing, respectively, leading again to unstable training and, as observed in GRPO, to catastrophic collapse.

Thus omitting length normalisation would either (i) force us to use a very large \(\varepsilon\) so that long responses are not clipped—making the clipping ineffective for short responses, or (ii) use a tiny \(\varepsilon\) that clips almost every long response—drastically reducing sample efficiency. Both regimes destroy the balance that GSPO achieves: a single, modest clipping range (e.g., \(3\times10^{-4}\) – \(4\times10^{-4}\)) works uniformly across all response lengths because \(s_i(\theta)\) stays near 1.

### Summary  

*The power \(1/|y_i|\) converts a product of many token‑wise likelihood ratios into their geometric mean.*  
This geometric mean has two crucial properties:

1. **Variance reduction** – averaging over tokens makes the importance weight less sensitive to any single token’s probability shift, especially for long sequences.
2. **Length‑invariant scale** – all responses, regardless of length, produce importance ratios that lie in roughly the same neighbourhood of 1, allowing a single global clipping range.

If we drop this normalisation we would have to redesign the clipping mechanism for every possible sentence length, and training would again suffer from high‑variance importance weights—the very problem GSPO was created to solve.

### What exactly is the "group‑based advantage" used in GSPO and how does it differ from token‑wise advantages in PPO?
Question:
The paper computes \( \widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\}}{\text{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}} \) across a group of responses. In PPO we normally have a value model that gives a token‑level advantage \( \widehat{A}_t \). Why is it valid to share the same advantage for all tokens of a response, and does this affect bias or variance of the gradient estimator?
Answer:
In PPO the advantage that multiplies the importance ratio is a **token‑level** estimate  

\[
\widehat A_t \approx V_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x,\;y_{<t})-r_t ,
\]

which is produced by a separate value network.  The rationale is that the reward signal is assumed to be decomposable (or at least well‑approximated) into per‑token returns, so each token can be given its own learning signal.  In practice this works only when the value model is accurate; otherwise the gradient may be dominated by the noise of the value estimate.

GSPO (and its predecessor GRPO) avoid a value model altogether.  Instead they exploit the fact that the **reward is defined on whole responses**: a verifier \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) assigns a single scalar to an entire sequence \(y\).  When the reward cannot be meaningfully split across tokens, any token‑wise advantage is an artificial construct.  The authors therefore propose a **group‑based advantage**

\[
\widehat A_i \;=\;
\frac{ r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j\in\mathcal G}\!\bigl[r(x,y_j)\bigr] }
     { \operatorname{std}_{j\in\mathcal G}\!\bigl[r(x,y_j)\bigr] },
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\mathcal G=\{y_1,\dots ,y_G\}\) is the set of responses generated for the same query \(x\).  All tokens belonging to response \(y_i\) share this same scalar \(\widehat A_i\).

### Why it is valid to reuse the same advantage for every token

1. **Reward‑to‑objective alignment** – The RL objective ultimately integrates over the whole distribution of responses:
   \[
   \mathbb E_{x\sim \mathcal D,\;y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)} 
   \bigl[ r(x,y)\bigr].
   \]
   When we write this expectation as a policy‑gradient estimator we obtain a term
   \[
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}\,r(x,y),
   \]
   i.e. a *sequence‑level* importance weight multiplied by the *sequence reward*.  There is no natural place for a token‑wise factor unless we artificially decompose \(r(x,y)\).  Using (1) respects this fundamental unit mismatch: the advantage is defined on exactly the same object (the response) that receives the reward.

2. **Unbiasedness of the Monte‑Carlo estimator** – In importance sampling we have  
   \[
   \mathbb E_{y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \bigl[ s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\bigr]
      = \mathbb E_{y\sim \pi_\theta}\bigl[ r(x,y)-c(x)\bigr],
   \]
   where \(s_i(\theta)=\bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}\) and \(c(x)=\operatorname{mean}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]\) is a constant that disappears after centering.  Because the same scalar multiplies *all* log‑probability terms of that response (see Eq. (9) in the paper), taking it outside the token sum does not alter the expectation; it merely re‑weights an unbiased estimator of the gradient.

3. **Variance reduction through grouping** – The denominator in (1) normalises across a batch of responses with identical query.  This *group normalisation* converts raw rewards into z‑scores, guaranteeing zero mean and unit variance across the group.  Consequently, even if absolute rewards vary widely, their centred and scaled versions have bounded magnitude, which keeps gradient magnitudes well behaved.  Sharing this normalised scalar across tokens avoids injecting per‑token noise from an unreliable value model.

### Effect on bias and variance

* **Bias** – The group‑based advantage does **not introduce bias** with respect to the true policy gradient as long as we use the *sequence* importance ratio \(s_i(\theta)\).  The derivation in §4.1 shows that the expectation of the GSPO gradient (ignoring clipping) equals  

  \[
  \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigl[ s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)\Bigr],
  \]

  which is exactly the REINFORCE estimator with a baseline \(c(x)=\operatorname{mean}_j r(x,y_j)\).  Because every token in \(y_i\) contributes via its log‑probability inside \(\log \pi_\theta(y_i|x)=\sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\), pulling a constant factor out does not change its expectation.

* **Variance** – There are two opposing forces:

  1. **Reduction** from using a *single* scalar per response instead of many tokenwise advantages.  
     In GRPO each token’s advantage equals \(\widehat A_i\), but it is multiplied by a *different* token‑level importance weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Those weights can vary wildly because they depend on single-token likelihood ratios, especially for long sequences or MoE models where routing changes cause abrupt likelihood shifts. This multiplicative noise inflates variance dramatically (the authors observe orders‑of‑magnitude more clipped tokens in GRPO).

  2. **Potential increase** from discarding any finer‑grained signal that might exist within a response.  
     If one could obtain an accurate per‑token value estimate, tokenwise advantages could, in principle, provide lower variance because they would subtract out more of the baseline locally. However, in large‑scale LLM RL there is no reliable value model, and any such estimate would itself be noisy and costly.

  Empirically (Figure 4 in the paper) GSPO’s sequence‑level clipping results in far fewer “effective” gradient samples being discarded yet still yields higher training efficiency, indicating that variance from tokenwise weighting dominates over any benefit from finer granularity.

### Intuition

Think of each generated response as a single “arm” in a multi‑armed bandit: you pull an arm (sample a response), you obtain one payoff \(r(x,y)\), and you decide how much to push up or down its probability. The natural policy gradient weight for that arm is precisely its likelihood ratio; there is no need to split the payoff among intermediate steps along that arm’s trajectory. The group normalisation simply makes sure that when you compare several arms drawn for the same query, you are rewarding relative superiority rather than absolute magnitude—exactly what PPO’s clipping intends but now at the correct granularity.

### Summary

- The “group‑based advantage” in GSPO is a z‑score computed over all responses for one query; it serves as a baseline that removes query‑level bias.
- Sharing this scalar across all tokens of a response matches the granularity of both reward and importance weight (both are defined at sequence level), preserving unbiasedness.
- By avoiding per‑token importance ratios, GSPO eliminates a major source of high variance that plagued GRPO, especially for long sequences and MoE models.
- Consequently, GSPO achieves more stable gradients without needing an auxiliary value network, and any remaining variance comes only from sampling different responses—not from noisy tokenwise weighting.

Thus, using one advantage per response is theoretically sound and practically advantageous: it keeps the estimator unbiased while considerably lowering its variance compared with token‑wise advantages in PPO/GRPO.

### Can anyone clarify the gradient derivation for GSPO (Eq. 13) and why the importance ratio appears outside the log‑gradient term?
Question:
In Eq. 13 the gradient is written as
\[ \nabla_\theta J_{GSPO}= \mathbb{E}[ s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i \nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)] .\]
Given that \( s_i(\theta) = (\pi_θ(y_i|x)/
π_{old}(y_i|x))^{1/|y_i|} \), how do we expand \( \nabla_θ \log s_i(θ) \) to obtain the sum over token log‑gradients shown in Eq. 14? A step‑by‑step derivation would help.
Answer:
The key to understanding Eq. 13 is the way the authors treat the **sequence‑level importance ratio**  

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log
          \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
\]

Because the objective (ignoring the clipping term) is simply the expectation of $s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i$,

\[
J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i \Bigr],
\]

the gradient follows the usual “log‑derivative” (a.k.a. REINFORCE) trick:

\[
\nabla_{\theta} J_{\text{GSPO}}
   = \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G 
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i \;\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)\Bigr].
\tag{13}
\]

Notice that **$s_i(\theta)$ appears outside the log‑gradient**. This is exactly what the log‑derivative identity tells us:

\[
\nabla_{\theta} s_i(\theta)=s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta).
\]

If we had written the gradient as $\mathbb{E}[\,\widehat A_i\nabla_{\theta} s_i(\theta)]$, we would obtain the same expression, but factoring out $s_i(\theta)$ makes the relationship with importance sampling explicit: the weight $s_i(\theta)$ multiplies the advantage $\widehat A_i$ *before* the log‑gradient is taken.

---

### Expanding $\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)$

Starting from the definition of $s_i(\theta)$,

\[
\log s_i(\theta)=
   \frac{1}{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                {\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

The old policy does not depend on $\theta$; therefore its derivative vanishes. Differentiating term‑by‑term gives

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\log s_i(\theta)
   = \frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\tag{14}
\]

Plugging this back into Eq. 13 yields exactly the gradient that appears later in the paper (Eq. 15):

\[
\nabla_{\theta}J_{\text{GSPO}}
   = \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\Bigl[\frac1G
        \sum_{i=1}^{G}
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i
        \frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
           \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
      \Bigr].
\]

Thus each token contributes an **identical weight** $s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i/|y_i|$ to its log‑likelihood gradient, in contrast with GRPO where each token is multiplied by its own token‑level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. The derivation relies only on elementary calculus and the log‑derivative trick; no additional approximations are introduced.

---

### Intuition

* **Why does $s_i(\theta)$ sit outside?**  
  Think of importance sampling: we want an unbiased estimator of an expectation under the *target* distribution $\pi_\theta(y|x)$. We draw samples from the *behavior* distribution $\pi_{\text{old}}(y|x)$ and re‑weight them by the likelihood ratio $\pi_\theta(y)/\pi_{\text{old}}(y)$. When we differentiate that estimator, the weight remains attached to the whole term, giving exactly $s_i(\theta)\,\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)$.

* **Why a sum over token log‑gradients?**  
  The sequence likelihood factorises autoregressively, so its log is a sum over token logs. Differentiating a sum yields a sum of derivatives, which is why we obtain Eq. 14. The length‑normalisation (division by $|y_i|$) simply averages over tokens; it does not alter the mathematics, it only reduces variance.

* **Connection to broader concepts**  
  This derivation mirrors the classic policy‑gradient formula  

  \[
  \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\text{old}}
      [\,w(a;\theta)A(a)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a)\,],
  \]

  where here a “action’’ is an entire response sequence rather than a single token. By moving from token‑wise ratios to a single sequence ratio, GSPO aligns the unit of importance weighting with the unit of reward, eliminating the high‑variance noise that plagued GRPO.

In summary, Eq. 13 follows directly from applying the log‑derivative trick to a weighted expectation, and expanding $\nabla_\theta\log s_i(\theta)$ uses the autoregressive factorisation of the sequence likelihood, giving the token‑wise sum shown in Eq. 14. The placement of $s_i(\theta)$ outside the log‑gradient is therefore not mysterious—it is simply a consequence of differentiating a product of a weight and a function via $\nabla(w f)= w\,\nabla f + f\,w\,\nabla\log w$; because we treat $f=\widehat A_i$ as constant w.r.t. $\theta$, only the first term remains, leading to Eq. 13.

### Why does GSPO clip a much larger fraction of tokens yet achieve higher training efficiency than GRPO?
Question:
Figure 3 in the paper shows that GSPO discards two orders of magnitude more tokens due to clipping, but still learns faster. Is this because the remaining (unclipped) tokens have lower variance gradients, or does sequence‑level clipping provide a better signal? How can we quantitatively relate the fraction of clipped samples to gradient variance in both algorithms?
Answer:
Both GRPO and GSPO are built on the PPO‑style surrogate loss, but they differ in **where** the importance ratio is inserted and, consequently, in **how much stochastic noise** is injected into the gradient estimator.  
The empirical observation in Figure 4 of the paper – that GSPO discards roughly two orders of magnitude more tokens by clipping yet learns faster – follows directly from this difference.

### 1.  What the clipping “fraction” actually measures  

In GRPO the clipping operator works on the *token‑level* importance weight  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$

so every token that falls outside the interval $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$ is discarded from the gradient.  Because a typical response contains hundreds of tokens, even a modest token‑level clipping probability (e.g. a few percent) translates into a *large absolute number* of clipped tokens.

GSPO, on the other hand, clips the **sequence‑level** weight  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|},
$$

and either **keeps or discards an entire response**.  The clipping interval for $s_i(\theta)$ is very tight (e.g. $[3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}]$) because after the length‑normalisation the ratios are already close to one.  
Consequently a single response that is clipped eliminates *all* its tokens at once; when the batch contains many short responses this yields a much higher *fraction of clipped tokens* even though the *fraction of clipped responses* is comparable to—or even smaller than—that of GRPO.

### 2.  Gradient variance under token‑ vs. sequence‑level weighting  

The stochastic gradient of each algorithm can be written in the form  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal J = \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\, \widehat A\, \underbrace{c(\cdot)}_{\text{clipping mask}}
     \; \underbrace{w(\cdot)}_{\text{importance weight}}
     \; \underbrace{\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi}_{\text{log‑likelihood term}}\Bigr],
$$

where $c(\cdot)\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether a sample survives clipping.

*For GRPO* the weight inside the expectation is token‑specific:

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

Because each token is drawn from a different next‑token distribution, these ratios have **high variance** across tokens even for a fixed sequence.  The variance of the gradient estimator therefore contains a term proportional to  

$$
\operatorname{Var}_{t}\!\bigl[w_{i,t}(\theta)\bigr] .
$$

When a response is long, these variances accumulate (the sum over $t$ in Eq. (9) of the paper), inflating the overall gradient noise.  The clipping operation can only reduce variance by discarding extreme values of $w_{i,t}$, but it does **not** change the fact that surviving tokens still carry heterogeneous weights.

*For GSPO* all tokens in a response share exactly the same importance factor

$$
s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr),
$$

which is simply the geometric mean of the token ratios.  By construction

$$
\operatorname{Var}_{t}\!\bigl[\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\bigr] \;\longrightarrow\; \operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\log s_i(\theta)\bigr] = \frac{1}{|y_i|^2}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\operatorname{Var}\!\bigl[\log w_{i,t}(\theta)\bigr],
$$

so the variance **shrinks roughly as $1/|y_i|$**.  In other words, after length‑normalisation the sequence weight has far lower dispersion than any individual token weight.  When clipping discards an out‑of‑range response, *all* its tokens are removed together, eliminating an entire high‑variance contribution at once.

Because GSPO’s surviving samples are weighted uniformly within each response, its gradient estimator can be written as  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}} = 
   \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\, \widehat A_i\, c(s_i)\,
        s_i(\theta)\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}) \Bigr],
$$

which contains **only one random scalar factor** per sequence (the clipped $s_i(\theta)$).  Hence its variance is essentially  

$$
\operatorname{Var}_{i}[\,c(s_i)s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\,] ,
$$

a quantity that is typically an order of magnitude smaller than  

$$
\operatorname{Var}_{i,t}[\,c(w_{i,t})w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i\,] 
$$

appearing in GRPO.

### 3.  Why more clipped tokens can still improve efficiency  

Clipping removes samples whose importance ratios lie far outside the trust region; those samples would otherwise contribute gradients with extremely large magnitude and direction, blowing up variance and destabilising learning.  
In GRPO many *individual* tokens are clipped, yet many “borderline” tokens with moderately large $w_{i,t}$ remain and still inject substantial noise because their weights differ from one another.  
 GSPO’s tighter clipping interval means that when a response is deemed too off‑policy it is completely excluded; consequently **the remaining minibatch consists almost exclusively of low‑variance, well‑behaved samples**.  

Even though this leaves fewer total tokens for back‑propagation (the paper reports a two‑order‑of‑magnitude increase in clipped‑token fraction), each surviving token carries a *cleaner* learning signal, and each gradient step makes more efficient use of those signals.  Empirically this manifests as higher reward per unit compute and faster convergence, exactly what Figure 3 shows.

### 4.  Quantitative relationship (rough guideline)

If we denote by $p_\text{clip}^{(G)}$ and $p_\text{clip}^{(S)}$ the fractions of **responses** kept after clipping for GRPO and GSPO respectively (typically both around $0.8-0.9$), then the expected number of usable tokens is

$$
N^{(G)} = B\,G\,p_\text{clip}^{(G)}\, \mathbb{E}[|y|] ,
    \qquad
N^{(S)} = B\,p_\text{clip}^{(S)}\, \mathbb{E}[|y|],
$$

where $B$ is the number of queries per batch and $G$ is the group size.
Because GSPO discards whole responses, its effective token count can be much smaller,
yet its per‑token variance satisfies roughly

$$
\sigma^2_{\text{GSPO}} \;\approx\; 
    \frac{\sigma^2_{\log w}}{|y|},
    \qquad
\sigma^2_{\text{GRPO}} \;\approx\; 
    \sigma^2_{\log w},
$$

so that the **signal‑to‑noise ratio per token** improves by a factor of about $|y|$.  
When responses are on average $100–200$ tokens long (as in most RLHF setups), this explains how GSPO can afford to lose an order–of–magnitude more raw tokens while still achieving lower overall gradient variance and higher training efficiency.

### 5.  Takeaway  

The key reason why GSPO clips many more tokens yet learns faster is not that “more data are better”, but that *the data it keeps* are far less noisy because:

1. The importance weight is computed at the sequence level, turning many independent noisy token ratios into a single low‑variance scalar.
2. Length normalisation further damps fluctuations.
3. Sequence‑level clipping removes outlier responses entirely, preventing any high‑variance contribution from entering the gradient estimator.

Thus, the fraction of clipped samples should be interpreted together with **how they are weighted**: a high clipping fraction under a well‑behaved sequence weighting scheme can lead to lower gradient variance and higher sample efficiency – exactly what GSPO demonstrates compared with GRPO.

### How does expert‑activation volatility in MoE models break GRPO’s token‑level importance weights?
Question:
The authors state that after each update about 10 % of experts activated for a token change, causing large fluctuations in \( w_{i,t}(θ) \). Could someone explain mechanistically why a change in routing leads to an unstable importance ratio, and why GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio is immune to this effect?
Answer:
In a mixture‑of‑experts (MoE) language model each token is processed by a *routing* function that selects a small subset of experts (the “activated experts”) from a large pool.  
During RL training the policy is updated after every minibatch, i.e. we replace the old parameters $ \theta_{\text{old}} $ by new parameters $ \theta $. The routing function depends on the current parameters (it uses the same hidden‑state that drives the token‑level logits), so after an update the set of experts that would fire for a given token can change.

### Why a change in routing makes $ w_{i,t}(\theta) $ volatile

The token‑level importance weight used by GRPO is  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

Both the numerator and denominator are **conditional probabilities of a single token** given the same context, but they are computed by *different* expert subnetworks because the routing decisions differ between $ \theta_{\text{old}} $ and $ \theta $.  

When the routing changes, the forward‑pass that yields $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ may now involve completely different weight matrices and activation patterns. Even if the logits change only slightly, the *ratio* of two probabilities that are produced by distinct expert mixtures can swing dramatically: a token that was previously handled by a strong expert may now be handed to a weaker one, making the denominator large and the numerator small, or vice‑versa.  

Empirically the paper observes that about 10 % of the experts used for a token differ after each update; this is enough to cause **large fluctuations** in the per‑token ratios $ w_{i,t}(\theta) $. Since GRPO multiplies each token’s log‑gradient by its own $ w_{i,t}(\theta) $, these noisy, sometimes very large, scalars amplify variance in the gradient estimate. The effect compounds over long sequences because the variance accumulates across all token positions, which ultimately destabilises training and can lead to catastrophic collapse.

### Why GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio is immune

GSPO replaces the token‑wise weight with a **sequence‑level importance ratio**

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
= \exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
$$

Two facts make this ratio robust to routing volatility:

1. **Averaging over tokens** – The logarithms of the per‑token ratios are summed and then divided by the sequence length. Even if a few tokens experience abrupt routing changes, their contribution is diluted by the many tokens whose routing remains unchanged or changes only mildly. The law of large numbers therefore reduces variance: the sequence‑level weight is effectively an average of many noisy token ratios.

2. **Length normalisation** – By raising the product of probabilities to the power $1/|y_i|$ (or equivalently averaging log‑probabilities), GSPO keeps $s_i(\theta)$ in a narrow numerical band irrespective of how long the response is. This prevents long sequences from exploding the importance weight, something that would happen if one multiplied raw token ratios without normalisation.

Because GSPO’s objective only needs **the overall likelihood of the whole response**, it does not require matching which experts were active for each individual token. The sequence likelihood is computed by summing log‑probabilities across tokens; even if some tokens pass through different expert subsets after an update, their log‑probabilities still contribute additively to the same scalar. Consequently, a 10 % change in expert activation does not translate into a large swing in $s_i(\theta)$; the ratio remains relatively stable and can be clipped reliably.

### In short

* In GRPO each token carries its own importance weight; when routing changes, those weights fluctuate because they compare two *different* expert mixtures.
* In GSPO we first aggregate all token probabilities into a single sequence probability, then normalise by length. The aggregation averages out the sporadic routing differences, so the resulting importance ratio is much less sensitive to which experts fired for any single token.

This explains mechanistically why expert‑activation volatility breaks GRPO’s token‑level importance weights while GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio remains stable.

### What is the ‘Routing Replay’ strategy and why is it unnecessary for GSPO?
Question:
In GRPO training of MoE models, Routing Replay caches old routing decisions and reuses them when computing \( w_{i,t}(θ) \). How exactly is this implemented (e.g., which tensors are stored, how are they combined with the new policy), and why does GSPO not need such replay buffers? Is it simply because GSPO never looks at token‑level likelihoods?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the GRPO algorithm the importance weight that appears in the loss is a *token‑level* ratio  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\; .
\]

For a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) transformer each token is processed by a different subset of experts, selected by the routing network.  
When we compute $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ we must evaluate two forward passes:

1. **old‑policy pass** – the rollout was generated with $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. During this pass the routing network decides which experts are active for every token; we cache these decisions (the *routing mask*).
2. **new‑policy pass** – to obtain the numerator we run the same token through the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$. If we let the routing network choose its experts again, the set of active experts will typically be different (the paper reports ≈10 % change per update). Because the denominator and numerator are then computed by *different* sub‑networks, the ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ becomes noisy and can explode, breaking the clipping mechanism and leading to divergence.

### What is “Routing Replay”?

Routing Replay is a simple engineering trick that forces the two forward passes to share **exactly the same expert activation pattern**:

* **What is stored?**  
  For every token in every sampled response we store the *routing mask* produced by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. The mask is usually a binary (or top‑$k$) indicator vector of shape $[\#\text{experts}]$ that tells which experts contributed to that token.

* **How is it used?**  
  When we later compute $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})$ we feed the token through the current model **with the stored mask attached**. In practice this means:
  - The routing logits of the current model are ignored (or overwritten) and replaced by the cached mask.
  - The expert layers are executed only on those cached experts, exactly as they were during the old‑policy pass.
  - The resulting likelihood is therefore computed by *the same sub‑network* that produced the denominator.

* **Combining with the new policy**  
  After masking, we obtain a likelihood value $\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\cdot)$ that uses the updated weights of the already‑selected experts. The importance weight becomes  

  \[
  w^{\text{replay}}_{i,t}(\theta)=
  \frac{\tilde{\pi}_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
       {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
  \]

  which now varies only because of parameter updates, not because of a different routing decision.

The replay buffer therefore consists of a per‑token tensor of shape $[\text{batch},\,G,\,
\max|y|,\,\#\text{experts}]$ (or a compressed sparse representation). It is kept in GPU memory for the duration of one “mini‑batch” of gradient updates; after all updates on that batch are finished it can be discarded.

### Why does GSPO not need Routing Replay?

GSPO replaces $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ with a **sequence‑level** importance ratio

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}
                     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
         =\exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}
               \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
               \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               \Bigr].
\]

Crucially:

1. **Only whole‑sequence likelihoods are required.**  
   To compute $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ we run each response once through the current model, using *its own* routing decisions. The product (or sum of log‑probs) automatically aggregates whatever expert assignments occur token by token. No per‑token ratio needs to be formed, so there is no requirement that the old and new policies share an identical routing pattern.

2. **The variance introduced by routing changes is strongly dampened.**  
   Because we take a geometric mean across tokens (the $1/|y_i|$ exponent), a change in a single token’s expert set contributes only additively inside a log and then is averaged over all tokens. Even if 10 % of tokens switch experts, their effect on $s_i(\theta)$ is modest compared with directly dividing two *different* token probabilities as in GRPO.

3. **No extra memory or communication overhead.**  
   Since we never store masks, there is nothing to replay. The algorithm needs only one forward pass through $\pi_{\theta}$ per sampled response (plus an optional recomputation of $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, which can be performed on an inference engine because only sequence‑level scores are required).

Therefore GSPO sidesteps the root cause that made Routing Replay necessary: it does not rely on *stable token‑level importance weights*. By aligning the importance weight with the unit on which rewards are defined (the full response), GSPO eliminates sensitivity to expert‑activation volatility, removes the need for cached routing masks, and consequently simplifies both memory usage and implementation complexity.

In short, Routing Replay is a workaround that forces token‑wise likelihoods to be comparable by reusing old routing decisions; GSPO’s sequence‑level formulation makes such a workaround redundant because it never asks for those token‑wise ratios in the first place.

### How can inference‑engine likelihoods be used directly in GSPO without recomputation?
Question:
The paper claims that sequence‑level likelihoods are tolerant to precision differences between training and inference engines, allowing us to skip recomputing \( π_{old}(y|x) \). What are the practical steps to retrieve these likelihoods from an inference engine like vLLM, and what precision issues might still affect the importance ratio?
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper’s claim that *sequence‑level* likelihoods are “tolerant to precision differences between training and inference engines” rests on two observations made in the GSPO derivation:

1. **GSPO only needs the scalar $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)$ for each sampled response** (the product of token probabilities, or equivalently the sum of log‑probabilities).  
2. **The importance ratio that GSPO uses is length‑normalised**  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
   =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                                            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr).
$$

Because the ratio is taken **after averaging over the whole sequence**, a small per‑token numerical error (e.g. a few bits of floating‑point difference) is diluted by the division by $|y_i|$. Consequently the final scalar $s_i(\theta)$ is much less sensitive to the exact 16‑bit or 32‑bit representation used in the inference engine than the token‑level weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ that GRPO employs.

Below is a concrete recipe for extracting $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)$ from a typical inference service such as **vLLM** and an outline of the residual precision concerns that may still affect the importance ratio.

---

### 1. How to retrieve sequence‑level likelihoods from vLLM

vLLM (and similar serving stacks such as SGLang) expose two useful APIs:

| API | What it returns | When to use |
|-----|----------------|-------------|
| `logprobs=True` (or `return_logprobs`) | For a given prompt and generated token list, returns a list of per‑token log‑probabilities under the *current* model parameters. | When you need the *old* policy’s log‑likelihood for a rollout that was generated with exactly those parameters. |
| `loglikelihood(prompt, continuation)` | Directly returns $\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y\mid x)$ for an arbitrary continuation `y`. Some forks expose this as a separate endpoint; otherwise you can simulate it by feeding the full `(x, y)` pair and masking the prompt part. | Useful when you have stored rollouts (prompt + generated response) and want to recompute $\log$‑likelihood later, possibly after a checkpoint reload. |

The practical steps are:

1. **Keep a copy of the old checkpoint** (`θ_old`) that was used to generate each batch of rollouts.  
   - In a distributed RL loop you typically snapshot `θ_old` once per PPO epoch; store its identifier together with each mini‑batch.

2. **Load `θ_old` into the inference engine** (vLLM can hot‑swap model weights without restarting).  
   - vLLM’s `engine.reload_model(checkpoint_path)` is sufficient; it reinitialises the KV cache but leaves serving infrastructure untouched.

3. **Request log‑probabilities for each rollout**:  

   ```python
   # pseudo‑code
   engine = VLLMEngine(model_path=checkpoint_path)
   # x is the query string, y is the sampled response tokens
   request = {
       "prompt": x,
       "generated_text": y,
       "logprobs": True,           # ask for per‑token logprob list
       "max_tokens": 0             # we already have all tokens
   }
   resp = engine.generate(request)
   logprobs = resp["logprobs"]    # list of float32 values
   seq_logprob = sum(logprobs)    # this equals log π_old(y|x)
   ```

   - The server returns **float32** values regardless of its internal tensor precision (vLLM may run in FP16/BF16 for speed). The conversion to float32 happens on the CPU side before sending over RPC, so you obtain a consistent numerical format.

4. **Store `seq_logprob` together with the sample** (or compute $s_i(\theta)$ on‑the‑fly after loading the *new* policy).  
   - In GSPO you also need $|y|$, which is just `len(y)`.

5. **During training**, when you have both `θ_old` (loglikelihood already cached) and `θ_new` (the current policy being updated), compute  

   $$
   s_i(\theta)=\exp\bigl((\log \pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)-\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x))/|y_i|\bigr).
   $$

   The log‑likelihood under `θ_new` can be obtained in exactly the same way as step 3, but now using the *current* checkpoint (which typically lives in your training process anyway).

**Why this avoids recomputation?**  
If you keep `seq_logprob` from step 3, you never need to feed each token through the *training* forward pass again merely to obtain $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y)$. The only forward passes required during an update are those needed for $\pi_{\theta}(y)$, which you already compute for gradient estimation anyway.

---

### 2. Precision issues that may still matter

Even though GSPO’s importance ratio damps per‑token noise, a few practical sources of discrepancy remain:

1. **Log‑prob rounding when converting from FP16/BF16 to float32**  
   - vLLM typically computes logits in FP16/BF16, applies softmax, then takes a log. The conversion from reduced precision to float32 introduces at most a few units in the last place (ULP). Because we sum many token log‑probs, these errors accumulate linearly with sequence length, but after division by $|y|$ they become roughly constant magnitude ($\approx$ ULP of a single token). In practice this error is < $10^{-5}$, far below typical clipping thresholds ($3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$) used in GSPO.

2. **Numerical stability of softmax in low precision**  
   - When logits have very large magnitude differences, FP16 softmax may underflow/overflow for very low/high probability tokens, yielding exact 0 or 1 probabilities and thus $-\infty$ or 0 log‑probs. vLLM mitigates this with log‑softmax tricks (subtracting max logit), but pathological prompts can still expose slight bias. The effect shows up as an occasional NaN or $-\infty$; you should filter out such samples or replace their likelihood with a large negative constant before exponentiation.

3. **Different tokenizers or token IDs across engines**  
   - The paper assumes identical tokenisation between training and inference; mismatched vocabularies would produce completely different likelihood values. Ensure that both engines use exactly the same tokenizer version and special tokens handling.

4. **Batch‑wise scaling factors**  
   - Some serving stacks apply *temperature* or *top‑k/p* sampling at generation time, which changes the effective distribution from which samples are drawn but does not affect logged probabilities if you request raw logits (`temperature=1`). If temperature ≠ 1 is applied during generation *and* kept when querying log‑probs, then $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ no longer matches the true behaviour distribution used for sampling, violating the importance sampling assumption. The safe practice is: generate with temperature = 1 (or record temperature), then query log‑probs with temperature = 1.

5. **Sequence truncation / padding mismatch**  
   - When storing rollouts you may truncate long responses for memory reasons but later request log‑probs on a padded version; make sure that the exact token sequence fed to vLLM matches what was originally sampled (including eos tokens).

Overall, these residual issues are *much smaller* than those encountered by GRPO because they affect only an aggregated scalar rather than every token weight individually. Empirically, as shown in Figure 5 of the paper, using inference‐engine likelihoods directly does not degrade performance and considerably simplifies infrastructure.

---

### 3. Summary checklist

1. **Snapshot `θ_old`, load it into vLLM, request per‑token log‑probs (`logprobs=True`).**  
2. **Sum them to obtain $\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)$; store together with each rollout record (`seq_logprob`, length).**  
3. During each GSPO update, compute new log‑likelihoods under `θ_new` (already needed for gradient).  
4. Form $s_i(\theta)=\exp((\log \pi_{new} - \log \pi_{old})/|y|)$ and plug into Eq.(4).  
5. Verify that:  
   - Both engines use identical tokenizer & vocab.  
   - Generation and log‑prob queries use temperature = 1 (or consistent).  
   - No NaNs appear; filter pathological samples if necessary.

By following these steps you can take full advantage of GSPO’s tolerance to precision differences and skip any costly re‑evaluation of old logits on the training engine, freeing GPU memory and pipeline bandwidth especially in large MoE or multi‑turn RL setups.

### When would one prefer GSPO-token over plain GSPO?
Question:
GSPO-token introduces per‑token advantages while keeping the same sequence‑level importance ratio. In what scenarios (e.g., multi‑turn dialogue, fine‑grained reward shaping) does this flexibility provide a measurable benefit, and does it reintroduce any of GRPO’s instability?
Answer:
**Answer**

`GSPO‑token` is simply a token‑wise variant of the basic `GSPO` objective.  
Both algorithms share the same *sequence‑level* importance ratio  

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|} ,
\]

so that the clipping, the reward normalisation and the overall gradient direction are identical when every token in a response receives the same advantage.  
The only extra degree of freedom that `GSPO‑token` supplies is the possibility to assign **different advantages $\widehat A_{i,t}$ to individual tokens** while still using the same sequence‑level importance weight (the term `sg[·]` detaches the weight from the gradient).

Consequently, you would reach for `GSPO‑token` instead of plain `GSPO` in any situation where a **fine‑grained, token‑level shaping of the learning signal** is useful or even required, but you still want to keep the stability guarantees of sequence‑level clipping. The paper mentions two concrete families of scenarios:

1. **Multi‑turn dialogue or interactive RL** –  
   In a turn‑based conversation the reward is often obtained *after* several utterances, yet you may wish to credit (or penalise) particular turns more heavily than others. By supplying a per‑turn (or per‑token) advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$ you can express “the answer was good overall, but this specific phrase was misleading”. The underlying importance ratio remains sequence based, so the update does not suffer from the high‑variance token‑level weights that made GRPO unstable.

2. **Fine‑grained reward shaping** –  
   When training on tasks such as code generation, math reasoning, or safety alignment, it is common to augment the scalar reward with auxiliary signals (e.g., correctness of individual steps, adherence to style guidelines). These signals naturally decompose into token‑wise advantages. `GSPO‑token` lets you inject them directly into the objective without breaking the theoretically sound importance sampling that `GSPO` provides.

#### Does `GSPO‑token` re‑introduce GRPO’s instability?

No. The instability of GRPO originated from **weighting each token by its own token‑level importance ratio**  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

which fluctuates wildly for long sequences and for MoE models where expert routing changes between policies.  

In `GSPO‑token` the weight applied to every token is **identical**:

\[
s_{i,t}(\theta)=\operatorname{sg}[s_i(\theta)]\,
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\operatorname{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})]}
= s_i(\theta),
\]

the `sg[·]` (stop‑gradient) operation forces the numerical value of the ratio to be 1, leaving only the *sequence* importance factor $s_i(\theta)$.  
Thus all tokens in a response receive exactly the same clipping decision and scaling factor; any per‑token variation comes exclusively from the user‑supplied advantage $\widehat A_{i,t}$. Because we have removed the noisy token‑level ratios, the variance that caused GRPO’s catastrophic collapse does not reappear.

#### Summary

- Use **plain GSPO** when a single scalar advantage per response suffices – e.g., standard RLHF where each sampled answer receives one reward.
- Switch to **GSPO‑token** when you need *token‑ or turn‑level* advantage modulation (multi‑turn dialogue, stepwise correctness rewards, style penalties) but still want to retain the stable, sequence‑level importance weighting.
- The algorithmic change does **not** bring back GRPO’s instability because it never reintroduces token‐level likelihood ratios; all variance reduction benefits of GSPO are preserved.

### Is there a theoretical connection between GSPO and standard REINFORCE with baseline subtraction?
Question:
GSPO’s objective resembles a weighted REINFORCE estimator where the weight is the clipped sequence importance ratio. Can we view GSPO as REINFORCE with an adaptive baseline equal to the group mean reward? If so, does this interpretation suggest any alternative variance‑reduction techniques?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Group‑Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) objective can indeed be regarded as a *REINFORCE* estimator that uses a *group‑wise* baseline, i.e. the mean reward of the responses that belong to the same query.  The correspondence becomes clear once we rewrite the GSPO loss in the canonical REINFORCE form

$$
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y_i\sim \pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\,\cdot\,|x)}\bigl[
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i \bigr],
\qquad 
\widehat A_i=\frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline r(x)}{\operatorname{std}_G(x)} ,
$$

where  

* $\,s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i|x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}$ is the **sequence‑level importance ratio**, and  

* $\,\overline r(x)=\frac1G\sum_{j=1}^G r(x,y_j)$ is the **group mean reward** (the baseline).

If we ignore the clipping operator for a moment, the gradient of this objective is

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigl[\, s_i(\theta)\, \widehat A_i\,\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta) \Bigr].
$$

Because $\,s_i(\theta)=\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac1{|y_i|}\sum_t \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\bigr)$,  

$$
\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
=
\frac1{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t}),
$$

so that

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}
\Bigl[
s_i(\theta)\,
\widehat A_i
\,\frac1{|y_i|}
\sum_{t}\nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
\Bigr].
$$

Now compare this expression with the classic REINFORCE gradient

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathbb E_{y\sim \pi_\theta}[r(x,y)] 
= 
\mathbb E_{y\sim \pi_\theta}[ (r(x,y)-b(x))\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x)],
$$

where $b(x)$ is any baseline that does not depend on the sampled trajectory.  Setting  

* $b(x)=\overline r(x)$ (the group mean),  

* using **off‑policy samples** drawn from the old policy and correcting them with the importance weight $s_i(\theta)$,

turns GSPO precisely into a REINFORCE estimator with an adaptive, per‑query baseline.  The only extra ingredient relative to vanilla REINFORCE is the **clipping** of the importance weight, which limits how far an off‑policy sample can influence the gradient – a standard safety device in PPO‑type algorithms.

---

### Why the group mean works as a baseline

The advantage term  

$$
\widehat A_i = 
\dfrac{ r(x,y_i)- \overline r(x)}{\operatorname{std}_G(x)}
$$

has two properties that make it an effective variance reducer:

1. **Zero mean**: by construction  
   $$\displaystyle \mathbb E_{i}[\,r(x,y_i)-\overline r(x)\,]=0.$$
   Consequently $\mathbb E_{i}[\,\widehat A_i]=0,$ which eliminates the part of the gradient that does not depend on the policy (the “baseline” term).

2. **Scale normalisation**: dividing by the within‑group standard deviation puts all advantages on roughly the same scale, preventing outlier rewards from dominating updates.

These are exactly the conditions under which a baseline is known to reduce variance without biasing the estimator.

---

### Implications for alternative variance‑reduction tricks

Viewing GSPO through this REINFORCE‑with‑baseline lens suggests a handful of additional techniques that could be combined with GSPO:

| Technique | How it would fit into GSPO |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| **Control variates / state‑dependent baselines** | Instead of using only the empirical group mean, one could train a lightweight value head $V_\phi(x)$ to predict the expected reward for a query.  The baseline would then be $b_\phi(x)=V_\phi(x)$. Because GSPO already removes the need for a token‑level value model, such a *query‑level* value function adds very little overhead while potentially further lowering variance. |
| **Generalised Advantage Estimation (GAE)** | GAE blends multi‑step returns with an exponential decay factor to trade bias and variance.  In GSPO one could compute a *sequence‑level* GAE by averaging rewards over multiple sampled continuations of the same query, weighting earlier tokens more heavily.  This would refine $\widehat A_i$ without breaking the sequence‑level importance weighting. |
| **Self‑normalised importance sampling** | The current sequence weight $s_i(\theta)$ is multiplied directly into the gradient.  Normalising across the group, i.e. using  
  $$ 
  \tilde s_i = \frac{s_i}{\sum_j s_j},
  $$  
  preserves unbiasedness while reducing variance due to occasional extremely large ratios (especially when some responses become very unlikely under the new policy). |
| **Trust‑region or KL penalties on groups** | GSPO already clips $s_i(\theta)$.  An alternative is to augment the loss with a KL penalty term  
  $$ 
  -c\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot|x)\;||\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\bigr),
  $$  
  computed at sequence level (or approximated by averaging tokenwise KL).  This provides an additional smooth constraint that can further curb high‑variance updates. |
| **Batch‑level control variates** | Since GSPO processes many groups per minibatch, one can subtract the minibatch average of $s_i\,\widehat A_i$ from each term (a “centering” trick). This does not affect expectation but reduces variance analogously to baselines used in policy gradient estimators for continuous control. |

All these ideas keep the core principle of **matching the unit of optimisation (a whole response) with the unit of reward**, which is what gives GSPO its stability advantage over token‑wise methods such as GRPO.

---

### Summary

*GSPO* ≈ *off‑policy REINFORCE* + *group‑wise baseline* + *sequence‑level importance weighting* + *clipping*.  
The group mean reward plays exactly the role of an adaptive baseline: it makes the advantage zero‑mean and therefore removes an unnecessary source of variance. Recognising this connection opens up standard variance‑reduction extensions—query‑level value baselines, normalised importance weights, GAE, KL penalties, and batch centering—that can be blended with GSPO without violating its theoretical foundation. These extensions may further improve sample efficiency, especially when training ever larger language models or when working with limited compute budgets.
