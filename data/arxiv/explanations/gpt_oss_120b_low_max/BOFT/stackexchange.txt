\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley transform ensure orthogonality of the learned matrix in OFT and BOFT?
Question:
The paper states that OFT and BOFT enforce orthogonality via the Cayley parameterization, i.e., \(R = (I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\) where \(Q\) is skew‑symmetric. I understand that a skew‑symmetric \(Q\) yields an orthogonal \(R\), but I'm unclear on how this works in practice during training. Specifically:

1. How is \(Q\) parameterized (what are the trainable parameters)?
2. Does the inverse \((I-Q)^{-1}\) need to be computed explicitly each forward step, and is it numerically stable?
3. Are there any tricks (e.g., using a small learning rate or regularization) to keep \(Q\) close to zero so that \(R\) stays near the identity at initialization?

Any concrete explanation or pseudo‑code would help.

Answer:
The Cayley transform is used in OFT and in its butterfly‑based variant (BOFT) as a *re‑parameterisation* of an orthogonal matrix, i.e. we never optimise the entries of the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ directly.  
Instead we keep a *skew‑symmetric* matrix $\mathbf Q$ as the trainable object and obtain $\mathbf R$ through  

$$
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1},
\qquad\text{with } \mathbf Q^{\top}=-\mathbf Q .
$$

Because $(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)^{\top}(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)=\mathbf I-\mathbf Q^{2}$ and
$\mathbf Q^{2}$ is symmetric, one can check that  

$$
\mathbf R^{\top}\mathbf R
= (\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-\top}(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)^{\top}
   (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}
= \mathbf I ,
$$

so every $\mathbf R$ produced this way is orthogonal, regardless of the
numerical value of $\mathbf Q$.  The optimisation therefore only has to keep
the skew‑symmetry of $\mathbf Q$.

Below we answer the three concrete questions.

---

### 1.  What are the trainable parameters?  

A real $d\times d$ skew‑symmetric matrix has exactly $d(d-1)/2$ independent
entries (the upper‑triangular part, with the diagonal forced to zero).  
In practice we store a *vector* $\boldsymbol \theta\in\mathbb R^{d(d-1)/2}$
and map it to $\mathbf Q$ by

```python
# pseudo‑code for a d‑dimensional block
def theta_to_Q(theta, d):
    Q = torch.zeros(d, d)
    idx = 0
    for i in range(d):
        for j in range(i+1, d):
            val = theta[idx]
            Q[i, j] =  val       # upper triangle
            Q[j, i] = -val       # lower triangle = -upper
            idx += 1
    return Q
```

In BOFT each butterfly component consists of many small orthogonal blocks.
For a block of size $b\times b$ we keep a separate vector of length
$b(b-1)/2$.  The total number of trainable scalars is therefore  

$$
\underbrace{\tfrac{b(b-1)}{2}}_{\text{per block}}\times
      \underbrace{\tfrac{d}{b}}_{\text{# blocks}}\times m,
$$

where $m$ is the number of butterfly layers.  This matches the paper’s claim
that BOFT needs $\tfrac12(b-1)d\,m$ effective parameters.

---

### 2.  Do we really have to compute $(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ each forward pass?  

Yes – the forward pass needs $\mathbf R = (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$.
However two facts make this cheap and stable:

* **Small blocks** – In BOFT the matrix that is inverted is block‑diagonal,
  each block being of size $b\times b$.  Inverting a $2\times2$ or even a
  $8\times8$ block can be done analytically (for $2\times2$) or with a very
  cheap batched LU solve.  Modern deep‑learning libraries provide a batched
  `solve` routine that processes all blocks in parallel, so the cost is
  $O(db)$, not $O(d^{3})$.

* **Numerical stability** – The Cayley transform is well‑behaved as long as
  $\|\mathbf Q\|_{2}<1$.  When $\|\mathbf Q\|$ approaches 1 the denominator,
  $\det(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)$, becomes small and the inverse can blow up.
  In practice this never happens because:
  - **Zero‑initialisation**: at the start $\theta=0 \Rightarrow \mathbf Q=0$,
    so $\mathbf R=\mathbf I.$
  - **Learning‑rate control**: OFT/BOFT are finetuning methods; a modest learning
    rate (e.g. $10^{-3}$ or smaller for large models) keeps the entries of
    $\theta$ modest.
  - **Implicit regularisation**: gradient descent on a skew‑symmetric parameterisation
    naturally penalises large magnitudes – if a component tried to grow it would cause a large gradient on the loss because the resulting orthogonal matrix would rotate too far from identity.

If one prefers an extra safety net, it is common to clip the norm of each block:
```python
Q_block = theta_to_Q(theta_block, b)
norm = torch.norm(Q_block, p='fro')
if norm > epsilon:               # epsilon < 1, e.g. 0.9
    Q_block = Q_block * (epsilon / norm)
```
Clipping guarantees that all eigenvalues of $\mathbf Q$ stay inside $(-1,1)$.

---

### 3.  Keeping $Q\simeq0$ at initialization and during early training  

The paper already mentions “identity initialization”: set all entries of
$Q$ (hence all entries of $\theta$) to zero.  
Beyond that there are three practical tricks that many implementations adopt:

* **Small learning rate** – As noted above, finetuning works with learning rates an order of magnitude smaller than those used for training from scratch. This keeps updates to each entry of $\theta$ tiny, so $Q$ drifts slowly away from zero.

* **Weight decay / L2 regularisation** – Adding a small L2 penalty on the vector
$\theta$,

$$
L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{task}} + \lambda\, \|\theta\|_{2}^{2},
$$

gently pulls every entry toward zero. Because the mapping from $\theta$ to an orthogonal matrix is smooth,
this translates into keeping $\|Q\|$ small.

* **Norm clipping per block** (as shown above).  
   Even if a few entries become large, scaling them back to keep the Frobenius norm below a threshold (usually $<0.9$) guarantees that the denominator stays well conditioned.

Putting everything together, a typical training loop for one butterfly layer looks like

```python
# theta: [num_blocks, b*(b-1)//2]   trainable parameters

def forward(x, theta):
    # reshape theta → list of blocks → build skew-symmetric blocks
    blocks_Q = []
    for t in theta:
        Qb = theta_to_Q(t, b)
        # optional norm clipping
        fro = torch.norm(Qb)
        if fro > eps:
            Qb = Qb * (eps / fro)
        blocks_Q.append(Qb)

    # build (I+Q)(I-Q)^-1 blockwise
    R_blocks = []
    for Qb in blocks_Q:
        Ib = torch.eye(b, device=Qb.device)
        num = Ib + Qb                # I + Q
        den = Ib - Qb                # I - Q
        Rb = torch.linalg.solve(den.t(), num.t()).t()   # (I-Q)^-1 (I+Q)
        R_blocks.append(Rb)

    # assemble full orthogonal matrix (block‑diagonal or butterfly permutation)
    R = assemble_butterfly(R_blocks)

    return (R @ W0).t() @ x          # OFT/BOFT forward pass
```

The same code works for OFT when `m=1` and `b=d` (a single dense block), and for BOFT when `m>1` and `b` is small.

---

### Summary  

* **Parameterisation:** store only the independent upper‑triangular entries of a skew‑symmetric matrix; each butterfly block contributes $b(b-1)/2$ trainable scalars.
* **Computation:** compute the Cayley transform block‑wise; inversion is cheap because each block is tiny and can be performed with a batched linear solve.
* **Stability:** initialise all entries to zero; use modest learning rates, optionally L2 regularisation and per‑block Frobenius‑norm clipping to keep $\|Q\|<1.$

With these ingredients the orthogonal matrix stays perfectly orthogonal throughout training while requiring far fewer parameters than an unconstrained dense orthogonal matrix—exactly what OFT and its butterfly extension BOFT aim for.

### Why does a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix reduce expressiveness compared to a dense orthogonal matrix?
Question:
In OFT the orthogonal matrix \(R\) is block‑diagonal, with each block of size \(b\times b\). The authors claim this structure cannot approximate many classic linear transforms and introduces undesirable inductive biases. Could someone clarify:

* How exactly does the block‑diagonal sparsity limit the space of possible rotations?
* Is there a simple example (e.g., a 4‑dimensional rotation) that cannot be represented by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with \(b=2\)?
* How does this limitation affect downstream performance?

Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the trainable matrix  

\[
R\;=\;\operatorname{diag}\!\big(R_{1},R_{2},\dots ,R_{r}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\qquad 
R_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d,
\]

is forced to be block‑diagonal.  
Each block \(R_{i}\) is an independent orthogonal transformation that mixes only the coordinates belonging to its own block; there is **no coupling** between different blocks.  This simple structural constraint already tells us what can and cannot be represented.

### Why block‑diagonal sparsity limits rotations

An orthogonal matrix \(R\) represents a rotation (or a product of a rotation and a possible reflection) in the whole \(d\)-dimensional space.  Its columns (or rows) form an orthonormal basis, i.e. each basis vector can have components on *all* \(d\) coordinates.  When \(R\) is block‑diagonal, its columns are confined to the sub‑spaces spanned by the blocks:

* The first \(b\) columns have zeros outside the first \(b\) rows,  
* the next \(b\) columns have zeros outside rows \((b+1,\dots ,2b)\), and so on.

Consequently a vector that originally lies in one block can never be rotated into a different block; the transformation is a **direct sum** of independent \(b\)-dimensional rotations
\[
R = R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus \cdots \oplus R_{r}.
\]
The reachable set of orthogonal matrices is therefore the subgroup  

\[
O(b)\times O(b)\times \cdots \times O(b)\subset O(d),
\]

which has dimension \(r\,b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2\).  
The full orthogonal group \(O(d)\) has dimension \(d(d-1)/2\).  
When \(b<d\) we lose \(\frac{d(d-b)}{2}\) degrees of freedom—exactly the degrees of freedom needed to rotate between different blocks.

### A concrete 4‑dimensional example with \(b=2\)

Take \(d=4,\;b=2,\;r=2\).  
A generic rotation in \(\mathbb{R}^{4}\) can be written as a product of *plane* rotations, for instance a rotation by angle \(\theta\) in the plane spanned by the first and third coordinate axes:

\[
R_{\text{mix}}(\theta)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos \theta & 0 & -\sin \theta & 0\\
0           & 1 & 0            & 0\\
\sin \theta & 0 & \cos \theta  & 0\\
0           & 0 & 0            & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The non‑zero entries appear in positions \((1,1),(1,3),(3,1),(3,3)\); they connect **different blocks** (the first block contains rows 1–2, the second block rows 3–4). No choice of two independent \(2\times2\) orthogonal blocks can produce this pattern, because any matrix of the form

\[
R_{\text{blk}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
A & 0\\
0 & B
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A,B\in O(2),
\]

has zeros exactly where \(R_{\text{mix}}(\theta)\) has non‑zeros. Hence the rotation that couples dimensions 1 and 3 (or any other cross‑block pair such as (1,4), (2,3), …) **cannot** be expressed by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size 2.

If we restrict ourselves to rotations that keep each pair \(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{3,4\}\) separate, then any matrix we can represent is of the form  

\[
R_{\text{blk}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
c_{1} & -s_{1} & 0      & 0\\
s_{1} &  c_{1} & 0      & 0\\
0     &   0    & c_{2} & -s_{2}\\
0     &   0    & s_{2} &  c_{2}
\end{pmatrix},
\]

which clearly cannot produce the off‑block mixing shown above.

### Consequences for downstream performance

Because OFT with a block‑diagonal \(R\) can only rotate *within* each pre‑determined coordinate group, it cannot adapt weight directions that require interaction across groups. In practice this limitation manifests as follows:

* **Reduced ability to align pretrained features with task‑specific directions.** Many downstream tasks need linear combinations of neurons that live in different groups (e.g. features extracted from different patches of an image transformer). If those groups are never mixed, the finetuned model may need to compensate by changing later layers or by relying on additional parameters (biases, layernorm etc.), which reduces efficiency.
* **Implicit inductive bias toward “blockwise” invariances.** The model assumes that dimensions that happen to be adjacent in memory are semantically independent—a premise that rarely holds for learned representations.
* **Empirical signal in the paper.** The authors observe that when they replace the block‑diagonal structure by a butterfly factorization—still sparse but with inter‑block connections—the approximation error for random dense orthogonal matrices drops dramatically (Figure 6). This directly reflects the ability of butterfly matrices to realise rotations such as \(R_{\text{mix}}(\theta)\), whereas block‑diagonal OFT cannot.

In short, a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix lives in a low‑dimensional subgroup of \(O(d)\); it cannot express rotations that mix coordinates belonging to different blocks, as illustrated by the simple four‑dimensional example above. This loss of expressiveness limits how well OFT can adapt pretrained weights and explains why more expressive sparse factorizations such as butterfly matrices lead to better finetuning performance while still remaining parameter efficient.

### What is the ‘information transmission’ graph interpretation of matrix factorization?
Question:
The paper introduces an information transmission view where a dense orthogonal matrix \(R = B_m B_{m-1}\dots B_1\) corresponds to a directed graph on a \(d \times (m+1)\) grid. I am confused about:

1. How exactly are edges defined from the non‑zero entries of each \(B_i\)?
2. Why does each level need exactly \(d\) edges for orthogonality, and why are those edges considered “free” (non‑trainable)?
3. How does this graph viewpoint help in designing sparse factorizations like the butterfly topology?

A step‑by‑step illustrative example with small \(d\) and \(m\) would be appreciated.

Answer:
**Answer**

The “information‑transmission’’ view is simply a way of redrawing the algebraic product  

\[
R = B_m B_{m-1}\dots B_1\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
\]

as a sequence of elementary routing steps on a layered graph.  
Each factor \(B_i\) is a *sparse* orthogonal matrix; its non‑zero entries tell us which
nodes at level \(i\) may send their signal to which nodes at level \(i+1\).
When all levels are concatenated we obtain a directed graph on a
\(d\times(m+1)\) grid (see Fig. 2 of the paper).  The product of the matrices is
exactly the matrix that records, for every source node in the first column,
the total contribution that reaches every destination node in the last column.

Below I explain the three points you asked about and illustrate them with a tiny example
\(d=4,\;m=2\).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Edges from the non‑zero entries of each \(B_i\)

Consider a factor \(B_i\) (size \(d\times d\)).  
Its entry \((r,c)\) is interpreted as

\[
(B_i)_{rc}\neq0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{directed edge }c^{(i)}\!\to r^{(i+1)},
\]

where \(c^{(i)}\) denotes the node with index \(c\) on level \(i\) and
\(r^{(i+1)}\) the node with index \(r\) on level \(i+1\).
If the entry is zero, no edge exists between those two nodes.
Because each \(B_i\) must be orthogonal, it must be full‑rank; therefore every
node on level \(i\) must have **exactly one outgoing edge** and every node on
level \(i+1\) must have **exactly one incoming edge** – in other words each
\(B_i\) is a (signed) permutation matrix possibly multiplied by a block‑diagonal
orthogonal factor (e.g. a collection of independent 2‑by‑2 rotations).

**Example ( \(d=4,m=2\) ).**  
Let  

\[
B_1=\begin{bmatrix}
0&1&0&0\\[2pt]
1&0&0&0\\[2pt]
0&0&0&1\\[2pt]
0&0&1&0
\end{bmatrix}, \qquad 
B_2=\begin{bmatrix}
c_1 & s_1 & 0   & 0\\[2pt]
-s_1& c_1 & 0   & 0\\[2pt]
0   & 0   & c_2 & s_2\\[2pt]
0   & 0   &-s_2 & c_2
\end{bmatrix},
\]
with \((c_k,s_k)\) parametrising a 2‑by‑2 rotation.  
In the graph:

* Level 1 → Level 2 edges are given by the non‑zero pattern of \(B_1\):  
  node 1 connects to node 2, node 2 to node 1, node 3 to node 4, node 4 to node 3.
* Level 2 → Level 3 edges follow the pattern of \(B_2\): each pair  
  \((1,2)\) and \((3,4)\) is connected internally (the rotation couples the two
  nodes), while there are no cross‑pair edges.

Thus the whole product \(R=B_2B_1\) routes information from any source node in
level 1 to any destination node in level 3 (the last column), because each pair
can exchange messages through the two stages.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Why each level needs exactly \(d\) edges and why they are “free”

An orthogonal matrix is *norm‑preserving* and therefore invertible.
For a square matrix this means that its rows (and columns) must form an
orthonormal basis.  In graph terms, this translates into a **bijection**
between the set of nodes on adjacent levels:

* each of the \(d\) source nodes must send its information to exactly one target,
* each target must receive from exactly one source.

Consequently every factor \(B_i\) contains precisely \(d\) non‑zero entries –
one per row and one per column.  These entries are not learned: once we decide
which *pairing* of nodes we use (the permutation part), orthogonality forces the
value of that entry to be either \(\pm 1\) (if we use only permutations)
or to belong to a small orthogonal block such as a 2‑by‑2 rotation.
Because their values are determined entirely by the orthogonality constraint,
they carry **no degrees of freedom** and are therefore called **free edges**.
Only when we replace a pure permutation by a parameterised block (e.g. a
rotation matrix) do we obtain trainable parameters; the *presence* of an edge,
however, is fixed once we choose the sparsity pattern.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  How the graph picture guides sparse factorizations such as butterfly

The graph view makes two desiderata crystal clear:

| desideratum | graph formulation |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Dense connectivity** – every source must be able to reach every sink – | For every pair \((s,t)\) there must exist at least one directed path from \(s^{(1)}\) to \(t^{(m+1)}\). |
| **Minimum free edges** – keep only the obligatory \(d\) edges per layer – | Use as few *additional* edges beyond the mandatory bijection as possible. |

If we naïvely connect all nodes between two consecutive levels we would need
\(d^2\) edges per layer, i.e. an \(\mathcal O(d^2)\) number of parameters.
The butterfly topology shows that we can achieve dense connectivity with only
\(O(d \log d)\) *extra* edges.

A butterfly network is built recursively: at each stage we pair up nodes,
let each pair exchange information via a small orthogonal block (usually a
\(2\times 2\) rotation), and then permute the ordering for the next stage.
In graph terms each stage adds exactly \(d/2\) cross‑pair edges; after
\( \log_2 d \) stages every source can reach every sink because any two
nodes become coupled at some stage of the recursion.
Thus:

* **Number of layers:** \(m = \log_2 d\);
* **Edges per layer:** exactly \(d/2 + d/2 = d\) (the mandatory bijection)
  plus an additional “cross’’ edge for each pair, i.e. another \(d/2\).
* **Total trainable parameters:** each cross edge lives inside a tiny block,
  contributing only one scalar angle per block → \(\mathcal O(d \log d)\).

The example above already exhibits this pattern:  
\(B_1\) implements the first pairing (swap indices within each pair);  
\(B_2\) applies independent rotations inside those pairs.
If we now add another layer that swaps between pairs, after three layers all
four nodes become mutually reachable – precisely what a butterfly with three
stages would do for \(d=4\).

Because every extra edge corresponds to a learnable rotation angle, designing a
sparse factorisation reduces to picking a graph topology that satisfies dense
reachability with as few extra edges as possible.  The butterfly topology is known
in parallel computing and network theory to be optimal (up to constant factors)
for this type of all‑to‑all communication, which is why it becomes an ideal
candidate for constructing parameter‑efficient orthogonal matrices.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Compact illustration (complete walk‑through)

Let us finish with a concrete walk‑through for \(d=4,\;m=3\).

| Layer | Sparsity pattern (non‑zeros marked “×”) | Graph interpretation |
|------|------------------------------------------|----------------------|
| **\(B_1\)** | \(\begin{bmatrix}×&×&0&0\\×&×&0&0\\0&0&×&×\\0&0&×&×\end{bmatrix}\) | Two disjoint pairs: (1↔2), (3↔4). |
| **\(B_2\)** | \(\begin{bmatrix}×&0&×&0\\0&×&0&×\\×&0&×&0\\0&×&0&×\end{bmatrix}\) | Pairs are re‑wired: (1↔3), (2↔4). |
| **\(B_3\)** | Same pattern as \(B_1\). | Re‑pairing back to (1↔2), (3↔4). |

Each matrix contains exactly four non‑zero entries – the mandatory bijection –
and additional “cross’’ entries that realise the swaps.  
Multiplying them yields

```text
R = B3·B2·B1 = dense orthogonal matrix,
```

because any source can travel through at most three swaps to reach any target.
The only trainable parameters are the angles inside each 2×2 block; there are
\(3·(4/2)=6\) such angles → \(\mathcal O(d \log d)=6\).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take‑away

* An edge in the transmission graph corresponds bijectively to a non‑zero entry in one of the sparse factors.
* Orthogonality forces exactly one outgoing and one incoming edge per node per layer; those edges are “free’’ because they carry no learnable degrees of freedom.
* The problem of building a dense orthogonal matrix becomes: **pick a layered graph that connects every source to every sink while using as few extra edges as possible**.
* The butterfly topology satisfies this requirement with only \(\mathcal O(d \log d)\)
trainable parameters, which explains why it is used in BOFT and how it leads to parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning.

### How are butterfly factors constructed to guarantee orthogonality?
Question:
Equation (1) defines a butterfly factor \(B^{F}(k)\) as a block matrix of diagonal matrices, and later the full butterfly matrix is built by stacking these factors. My questions are:

* When using Cayley transform on each 2×2 block, how are the diagonal vectors \(d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4\) parameterized?
* For larger block size \(b>2\), the paper mentions turning each 2b×2b block into an orthogonal matrix—does this simply involve applying a Cayley transform to a skew‑symmetric 2b×2b matrix?
* Is there any constraint that forces the overall product of butterfly components to stay orthogonal besides making each component orthogonal?

Answer:
In the butterfly construction the authors start from a very small orthogonal building block (a  
\(2\times2\) rotation) and then “lift’’ it to larger blocks by arranging many copies of the same  
pattern on the diagonal of a block‑diagonal matrix.  Because every factor that appears in the product  
\(\displaystyle \bm R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\) must be orthogonal, the only  
trainable degrees of freedom are the parameters that define each \(2\times2\) (or \(2b\times2b\))  
rotation.  The three questions you raise are answered directly by the way the Cayley transform is  
used in the paper.

---

### 1.  Parameterising the vectors \(\mathbf d_{1},\mathbf d_{2},\mathbf d_{3},\mathbf d_{4}\) for a  
\(2\times2\) block

A butterfly factor with block size \(k=2\) is  

$$
\bm B^{F}(2)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad 
\mathbf d_{i}\in\mathbb R^{1}.
$$

Thus each \(\mathbf d_i\) is simply a scalar, say \(c_i\).  The four scalar entries are forced to  
form an orthogonal \(2\times2\) matrix.  The authors obtain this by applying the Cayley transform to a  
\(2\times2\) skew‑symmetric matrix \(\bm Q=\begin{bmatrix}0 & q\\ -q & 0\end{bmatrix}\).  The Cayley map  

$$
\operatorname{Cay}(\bm Q)\;=\;(\bm I+\bm Q)(\bm I-\bm Q)^{-1}
   = \frac{1}{1+q^{2}}
      \begin{bmatrix}
        1-q^{2} & 2q\\[4pt]
        -2q    & 1-q^{2}
      \end{bmatrix}
$$

produces a rotation matrix whose four entries are exactly the four numbers  
\(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}\).  Consequently, the trainable parameter for each \(2\times2\)  
butterfly factor is a single real scalar \(q\); the four diagonal vectors are just the  
four entries of the resulting rotation matrix.

When the block size is larger (see point 3 below), each entry \(c_i\) becomes a small  
\(b\times b\) matrix, but the same idea applies: a skew‑symmetric “seed’’ matrix of the same size is  
Cayley‑transformed and its entries fill the four blocks.

---

### 2.  What happens for block size \(b>2\)?

For a general block size \(b\) the paper defines a *block‑butterfly factor*  

$$
\tilde{\bm B}^{\,b}(d,2)=
\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(
   \underbrace{\bm B^{F,b}_{1},\, \bm B^{F,b}_{2},\,\dots,
   \bm B^{F,b}_{d/2}}_{\text{each } \bm B^{F,b}_{i}\in \mathbb R^{2b\times2b}}
\bigr),
$$

where each \(\bm B^{F,b}_{i}\) has the same “four‑block’’ pattern as (1) but now each entry is a  
\(b\times b\) matrix rather than a scalar:

```
[ D1   D2 ]
[ D3   D4 ]   with   Di∈ℝ^{b×b}.
```

To guarantee orthogonality we again start from a skew‑symmetric matrix  
\(\bm Q_i \in \mathbb R^{\,2b\times 2b}\) (so \(\bm Q_i^\top = -\bm Q_i\)) and set  

$$
\bm B^{F,b}_{i}= (\bm I_{2b}+ \bm Q_i)(\bm I_{2b}- \bm Q_i)^{-1}.
$$

Because the Cayley transform maps any real skew‑symmetric matrix to an orthogonal one with no eigenvalue  
equal to –1, each \(2b\times 2b\) block becomes an orthogonal rotation (or product of rotations)  
in the subspace spanned by its rows/columns.  The four sub‑blocks \(D_1,\dots,D_4\) are simply the  
corresponding \(b\times b\) sub‑matrices of this orthogonal block.  Hence *yes*: turning a  
\(2b\times 2b\) block into an orthogonal matrix is achieved precisely by applying a Cayley transform to a  
skew‑symmetric seed of that size.

The number of trainable parameters in this case is the number of independent entries of a  
skew‑symmetric \(2b\times 2b\) matrix, i.e. \(\frac{(2b)(2b-1)}{2}= b(2b-1)\).  When we concatenate many such blocks on  
the diagonal (one per pair of rows/columns), we obtain exactly the parameter count quoted in the paper:  
\( \frac12(b-1)d m.\)

---

### 3.  Maintaining orthogonality of the whole product

Orthogonality of a product of matrices holds **iff** every factor itself is orthogonal:

$$
(\tilde{\bm B}_j^\top \tilde{\bm B}_j = \bm I)\;\;\forall j 
   \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 
   (\prod_j \tilde{\bm B}_j )^\top (\prod_j \tilde{\bm B}_j ) = \bm I .
$$

The construction therefore does **not** need any additional global constraint; making each butterfly  
component orthogonal automatically guarantees that their product \(\bm R(m,b)\) is orthogonal.  
The only subtlety is that every component must be full‑rank, which is satisfied because each component  
contains exactly \(d\) non‑zero entries (the diagonal blocks).  Those entries are forced to be either  
±1 in the trivial case or, after Cayley parameterisation, generic rotation matrices—both preserve full rank.

Consequently, there is no extra regulariser or projection step needed after multiplication; the whole  
pipeline stays on the Stiefel manifold because each step stays on it.

---

### Putting it together

* **\(d_1,\dots,d_4\)** for a \(2\times 2\) factor are not independent free vectors; they are simply the four entries of a rotation obtained from one scalar skew‑symmetric parameter via the Cayley transform.

* **For larger blocks** we replace each scalar by a \(b\times b\) sub‑matrix and apply exactly the same Cayley mapping to a **\(2b\times ₂ b** skew‑symmetric seed**; this yields an orthogonal block whose four sub‑matrices become \(D_1,\dots,D_4.\)

* **Orthogonality of the final matrix** follows automatically because every butterfly component is constructed to be orthogonal; no extra constraints beyond those imposed by the Cayley transform are required.

Hence, the butterfly parameterisation guarantees orthogonality in a completely local way—each tiny skew‑symmetric seed produces an orthogonal building block, and their sparse product yields a dense orthogonal matrix with only \(O(d\,log d)\) trainable parameters.

### Why does BOFT achieve \(O(d\log d)\) parameter complexity while preserving dense connectivity?
Question:
The authors claim that multiplying \(\log d\) sparse butterfly components, each with \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries, yields a dense orthogonal matrix using only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters. I would like clarification on:

1. How many trainable parameters does each butterfly component actually have (considering Cayley parameterization)?
2. How does the product of these sparse components become dense? A concrete demonstration for \(d=8\) would help.
3. Are there any hidden assumptions about the sparsity pattern (e.g., permutation of non‑zeros) that are required for density?

Answer:
**Answer**

The claim “$O(d\log d)$ trainable parameters while preserving dense connectivity” follows from two facts:

1. **Each butterfly component contains only $O(d)$ free parameters.**  
   A butterfly component $\widetilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are the *butterfly factors* $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$. For the smallest block size $k=2$ a factor has the form  

   $$
   \mathbf B^{F}(2)=\begin{bmatrix}
   \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\[2pt]
   \sin\theta & \;\cos\theta
   \end{bmatrix},
   $$

   i.e. a 2‑dimensional rotation parametrised by a single scalar angle $\theta$.  
   When we use a larger block size $k=2b$ the factor is a direct sum of $k/2$ independent $2\times2$ rotations (or, equivalently, a Cayley transform of a skew‑symmetric $2\times2$ matrix). Consequently every block contributes exactly $(k/2)$ independent scalar parameters, and a component consisting of $d/k$ such blocks contributes  

   $$
   \frac{k}{2}\;\frac{d}{k}= \frac{d}{2}
   $$

   free scalars, irrespective of $k$.  Hence each component has $\Theta(d)$ trainable numbers.  The authors write this as  

   $$
   \text{#trainable per component}= \tfrac12(b-1)d,
   $$

   where $b=k$ is the block size (for $b=2$ this reduces to $d/2$).  The remaining entries of the component are fixed to either 0 or ±1 and are *not* learned.

2. **The product of $\log_2 d$ such components can be dense.**  

   The sparsity pattern of a butterfly component is exactly the one used in the radix‑2 Cooley–Tukey FFT: each row (or column) has exactly two non‑zero entries, positioned so that they “mix’’ pairs of indices that differ in one bit of their binary representation.  If we write the binary address of a node as a length‑$N=\log_2 d$ bit string, the first component swaps (or rotates) the least‑significant bit, the second component swaps the second‑least‑significant bit, and so on.  After applying all $N$ components every possible pair of input and output indices has been mixed at least once, i.e. the overall product matrix has no zero entries.

   **Concrete example for $d=8$.**  
   Let us label rows/columns by binary strings of length three:  

   ```
   000 001 010 011 100 101 110 111
   ```

   The three butterfly components are:

   *Component 1* ($k=2$, mixes bit 0): each 2×2 block acts on the pairs  
     (000,001), (010,011), (100,101), (110,111).  
     Its non‑zeros are at positions  
     $(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1), (2,2),(2,3),…$.

   *Component 2* ($k=4$, mixes bit 1): each 4×4 block consists of two
     butterfly factors and mixes the pairs  
     (000,010), (001,011), (100,110), (101,111).

   *Component 3* ($k=8$, mixes bit 2): a single 8×8 butterfly factor mixes
     (000,100), (001,101), (010,110), (011,111).

   Multiplying them,
   $$
      \mathbf R = \widetilde{\mathbf B}(8,8)\,
                 \widetilde{\mathbf B}(8,4)\,
                 \widetilde{\mathbf B}(8,2),
   $$
   yields a matrix in which every entry is a product of three rotation
   angles; none of them is forced to be zero.  Because each component is
   orthogonal (Cayley‑parameterised rotations), their product remains
   orthogonal while being fully dense.

3. **Hidden assumptions on the pattern.**  

   The only structural assumption is that each component’s non‑zero pattern must be a *perfect matching* between input and output indices – exactly two non‑zeros per row and per column – and that successive components permute these matchings in a way that corresponds to bit‑wise permutations.  This is precisely the butterfly connectivity used in FFT algorithms.  No additional permutation of rows/columns is needed; the recursive definition already guarantees that after $\log_2 d$ stages every source node can reach every sink node via a unique path.  If one were to choose an arbitrary sparsity pattern with only two non‑zeros per row/column but without this recursive permutation property, density would not be guaranteed: some source–sink pairs could remain disconnected.

---

### Summarising the three questions

1. **Parameter count per component:** With Cayley parameterisation each
   block of size $k$ contributes $k/2-1$ independent scalars; because there are $d/k$ blocks we obtain
   $$
      |\theta| = \frac{d}{2}(b-1)=O(d)
   $$
   trainable parameters per butterfly component.

2. **Why the product becomes dense:** The butterfly pattern performs successive
   pairwise mixing along different binary dimensions; after $m=\log_2 d$
   stages every input index has been mixed with every other index,
   therefore the resulting matrix has no structural zeros.

3. **Assumptions on sparsity:** The pattern must be exactly the
   radix‑$2$ butterfly ordering – i.e., each component is a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are butterfly factors and whose blocks are placed so that successive components permute the index bits.  Apart from this deterministic layout there is no hidden randomness; any deviation from it may break dense connectivity.

Thus BOFT achieves an orthogonal matrix with only $O(d\log d)$ learned scalars while still being able to express any dense orthogonal transformation up to approximation error determined by the number of stages and block size.

### What is multiplicative dropout in BOFT and how does it differ from standard dropout?
Question:
Section 4 mentions a “multiplicative Dropout” where random butterfly components or diagonal blocks are replaced by identity matrices. I am unsure about:

* How is the dropout mask sampled and applied during forward/backward passes?
* Does this dropout preserve the orthogonality constraint automatically?
* How does this technique affect training stability compared to standard dropout used in LoRA?

Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the trainable orthogonal matrix is not stored as a single dense matrix but as a product of $m$ *butterfly components*  

$$
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
$$

each component being a block‑diagonal matrix whose non‑zero blocks are $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal factors (the “butterfly factors’’).  
Because the whole update is multiplicative ($\mathbf R\mathbf W^{0}$), the usual element‑wise dropout used in LoRA cannot be applied – dropping an element would break the orthogonality of the factor.  
Instead the authors introduce **multiplicative dropout**, which works at the *structural* level of the factorisation.

---

### How the mask is sampled and applied  

1. **Sampling**  
   *Two independent Bernoulli masks* are drawn for every training step:  

   * a mask over the *components*: each of the $m$ butterfly components is kept with probability $1-p_{1}$ and replaced by the identity with probability $p_{1}$;  

   * a mask over the *blocks inside a component*: for a given component, its $d/b$ diagonal blocks (each of size $2b\times 2b$) are kept with probability $1-p_{2}$ and replaced by an identity block with probability $p_{2}$.

   The masks are sampled **once per forward pass** (the same mask is used for the backward pass, as in standard dropout).

2. **Application in the forward pass**  
   Let $\mathcal M^{(c)}_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether component $i$ is dropped, and $\mathcal M^{(b)}_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}$ whether block $j$ inside component $i$ is dropped.  
   The effective orthogonal matrix becomes  

   $$
   \widetilde{\mathbf R}
   = \Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{m}
        \bigl[\, \mathcal M^{(c)}_{i} \,\mathbf I_d
               + (1-\mathcal M^{(c)}_{i})\, 
                 \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}(\mathcal M^{(b)}_{i}) \bigr]\Bigr),
   $$

   where $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}(\mathcal M^{(b)}_{i})$ has its selected blocks replaced by identity matrices according to the block‑mask.  
   The forward computation of a linear layer therefore uses

   $$
   \mathbf z = (\widetilde{\mathbf R}\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x .
   $$

3. **Backward pass**  
   Because the mask is fixed during a forward–backward pair, gradients flow through exactly the same set of blocks that were active in the forward pass.  The identity blocks contribute no gradient (they are constant), while the retained blocks receive standard gradients from the loss.  No special treatment beyond ordinary automatic differentiation is required.

---

### Orthogonality preservation  

Each butterfly component is *by construction* an orthogonal matrix (the Cayley parametrisation guarantees $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf I_d$).  
When we replace an entire component or a block by an **identity matrix**, orthogonality is still satisfied because $\mathbf I_d^{\!\top}\mathbf I_d=\mathbf I_d$.  
Consequently, after applying both masks the product $\widetilde{\mathbf R}$ remains orthogonal **by construction**; there is no need for any post‑hoc projection or re‑normalisation.  This is in stark contrast to standard dropout on weight elements, which destroys orthogonality and would require costly re‑orthogonalisation.

---

### Effect on training stability compared with LoRA’s dropout  

* **Preservation of spectral norm** – Since every factor that survives dropout stays orthogonal, the overall spectral norm of $\widetilde{\mathbf R}$ stays exactly one.  Orthogonal finetuning therefore inherits the stability benefits observed for OFT/BOFT (no amplification of activations, well‑behaved gradients).  LoRA’s additive low‑rank update can change the singular values of the effective weight matrix; applying conventional dropout on its low‑rank part adds additional stochastic variation to those singular values and can occasionally cause spikes in gradient norm.

* **Regularisation at a higher structural level** – Multiplicative dropout hides or reveals whole *information‑exchange pathways* rather than individual scalar weights.  This encourages the model to learn representations that are robust to missing butterfly components, which empirically leads to smoother loss curves and fewer catastrophic spikes during early training stages.

* **Empirical observations reported in the paper** – In Section 4 (the experiments), BOFT with multiplicative dropout (e.g., $p_{1}=0.2,\;p_{2}=0.3$) shows comparable or slightly better validation performance than BOFT without dropout, while LoRA’s standard dropout sometimes degrades performance when the same dropout rates are used because it interferes with the additive low‑rank correction.

Overall, multiplicative dropout maintains the key geometric property (orthogonality) that underlies BOFT’s stability, whereas LoRA’s usual dropout operates on a different parameterisation and does not enjoy that guarantee.

---

### TL;DR  

*The “multiplicative dropout’’ in BOFT randomly zeroes out whole butterfly components and/or whole diagonal blocks inside each component, replacing them by identity matrices.*  
*Because both a butterfly component and an identity matrix are orthogonal, the resulting product stays exactly orthogonal, so no extra projection step is needed.*  
*This structural dropout regularises BOFT without harming its spectral‑norm preservation, leading to training stability that is usually better than applying standard elementwise dropout to LoRA’s additive low‑rank updates.*

### Can BOFT represent any orthogonal matrix given enough depth and block size?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that by multiplying certain butterfly matrices and their transposes, BOFT can approximate any orthogonal matrix. I have several doubts:

* What is the exact construction of the sequence \(B_{d-1,1}(d), B^{\top}_{d-1,2}(d), …, B_{1,1}(d), B^{\top}_{1,2}(d)\)?
* Does this require depth proportional to \(d\) (i.e., linear depth), or can it be achieved with logarithmic depth as used in practice?
* In practice, BOFT uses a much smaller number of components; how far is this from universal approximation in terms of approximation error?

Answer:
**Answer**

Theorem 1 of the BOFT paper states that a product of butterfly matrices (and their transposes) can generate any orthogonal matrix in $\mathbb R^{d\times d}$.  
Below we unpack the construction that appears in the theorem, explain why the depth required is linear in the dimension, discuss what happens if we restrict ourselves to the $\log d$‑depth butterfly that is used in practice, and finally comment on how far a modest‑depth BOFT model is from a universal approximator.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  What the product  $\mathbf B_{d-1,1}(d)\,\mathbf B^{\top}_{d-1,2}(d)\,\cdots\,\mathbf B_{1,1}(d)\,\mathbf B^{\top}_{1,2}(d)$ looks like

Recall that a *butterfly factor* $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ (Eq. (1) of the paper) is a $k\times k$ matrix whose non‑zero entries lie in four diagonal blocks:
$$
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2)\\[2pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf d_i\in\mathbb R^{k/2}.
$$
A **butterfly component** $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ is just a block‑diagonal matrix whose blocks are independent copies of $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$:
$$
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\mathbf B^{F}_1(k),\dots,\mathbf B^{F}_{d/k}(k)\bigr).
$$
When we write $\mathbf B_{i,j}(d)$ we mean a *full* butterfly matrix of size $d\times d$ built by multiplying together the components whose block‑size doubles at each step:
$$
\mathbf B_{i,j}(d)=
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2^{i})\, 
\tilde{\mathbf B}\!\bigl(d,2^{i-1}\bigr)
\,\cdots\, 
\tilde{\mathbf B}\!\bigl(d,2\bigr).
$$
In other words,
*the index $i$ tells us how many “levels’’ (or factors) we compose* and  
*the index $j\in\{1,2\}$ distinguishes a matrix from its transpose*.

Consequently the whole product in Theorem 1 is:
```
B_{d-1,1} · B_{d-1,2}^T · B_{d-2,1} · B_{d-2,2}^T · … · B_{1,1} · B_{1,2}^T .
```
For a given $i$, each factor $B_{i,1}$ (or its transpose) contains exactly $i+1=\log_2(2^{i})+1 = i+1$ components
$\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2^{i}),\,\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2^{i-1}),\,…,\,\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2).$
Thus the total number of elementary components that appear in the product is
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{d-1} 2(i+1)=O(d^2),
$$
which is why the theorem guarantees *exact* representation of any orthogonal matrix: we are allowed to use as many butterfly layers as necessary (in fact a quadratic number).  

Intuitively each factor supplies a new set of “edges’’ in the information‑transmission graph; by alternating a matrix with its transpose we are able to reverse the direction of those edges and thereby obtain full connectivity between every source and every sink node.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Does this require linear (or quadratic) depth?

The construction above indeed needs **$O(d)$** distinct groups of factors (the outer loop over $i=1,\dots ,d-1$). Since each group already contains $\Theta(i)$ elementary components, the overall depth – i.e. the total number of sparse matrices multiplied together – grows as $\Theta(d^2)$. In terms of *layers* that appear on top of each other in a neural‑network implementation this is linear in $d$ only if we count a **group** as a single layer (each group can be realized with a single “butterfly block’’ that internally does several small multiplications).  

The *practical* BOFT implementation does **not** use this exhaustive construction. Instead it fixes the block size to the smallest possible value ($b=2$) and uses **$m=\log_2 d$** butterfly components:
$$
R(m,b)= \prod_{t=1}^{m} \tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,t)},
\qquad m=\log_2 d .
$$
This yields an orthogonal matrix with only $\mathcal O(d \log d)$ free parameters and depth logarithmic in $d$. The price we pay is that the set
$\{ R(m,b): m=\log d , b=2 \}$
is a strict subset of the full orthogonal group; it does **not** enjoy universal approximation.

Thus:

* The exact universality claimed in Theorem 1 needs **linear (in fact quadratic)** depth because we must be able to permute information arbitrarily many times.
* With the **logarithmic depth** used in experiments we obtain a *structured* sub‑group of $O(d)$ dimensions; it is highly expressive (it can represent FFT, Hadamard, etc.) but it cannot represent every orthogonal matrix.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3.  Approximation error when we use far fewer components

The authors measured how well BOFT approximates a random dense orthogonal matrix for several choices of block size $b$ and depth $m$. The key observations from Figure 5 (the “parameter‑efficiency” plot) are:

* With **$b=2$** and **$m=9$** (which corresponds to roughly $9\,d/2 = 4.5 d$ trainable parameters), the average Frobenius error is already below $10^{-3}$ for a $512\times512$ target matrix.
* Reducing the depth while increasing block size moves you along roughly the same error‑vs‑parameter curve: e.g. **$b=16,m=1$** (the original block‑diagonal OFT with 8 000 parameters for $d=512$) yields an error comparable to **$b=4,m=6$** which uses only half as many parameters.
* Empirically the error decays roughly like $C / (\text{#parameters})^{0.5}$, which means that even modestly larger depths quickly bring you into the regime where the approximation error is negligible for downstream finetuning.

Putting these numbers into perspective: for finetuning tasks typical tolerances on weight perturbations are on the order of $10^{-2}$–$10^{-3}$ relative Frobenius norm. Consequently *a BOFT model with logarithmic depth and modest block size ($b=4$–$8$)* already lies within that acceptable regime even though it cannot realise every orthogonal matrix exactly.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4.  Summary

* The universal construction in Theorem 1 consists of an alternating product of butterfly matrices and their transposes; each factor adds a new set of sparse edges and together they realise an arbitrary permutation of information on a $d\times(d+1)$ grid. Realising *all* orthogonal matrices with this scheme needs a number of factors that grows linearly (more precisely quadratically if you count elementary components).
* The BOFT implementation used in practice trades exact universality for parameter efficiency: it keeps only $\log_2 d$ butterfly components (depth ≈ log $d$) and therefore works with only ${O}(d \log d)$ trainable parameters.
* Empirically this restricted family already approximates random dense orthogonal matrices extremely well; with a handful of extra layers or slightly larger blocks you can push the approximation error below what matters for finetuning.

Hence, BOFT can **represent any orthogonal matrix** provided you allow sufficiently many butterfly layers (the construction behind Theorem 1). When you restrict yourself to the shallow log‑depth architecture common in experiments you lose exact universality but retain *practical* universality: the approximation error becomes negligible for realistic downstream tasks while keeping parameter count at ${O}(d \log d)$.

### How does preserving the spectral norm via orthogonal finetuning improve training stability?
Question:
The paper notes that multiplying an orthogonal matrix on the left preserves the singular values of the pretrained weight matrix, thereby keeping its spectral norm unchanged. I would like to understand:

* Why is preserving the spectral norm beneficial for optimization (e.g., gradient flow, Lipschitz continuity)?
* Are there empirical measurements in the paper or elsewhere showing reduced gradient explosion/vanishing when using OFT/BOFT versus LoRA?
* Could this property be combined with other normalization techniques for further gains?

Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the new weight matrix is obtained by left‑multiplying the frozen pretrained matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$,

$$
\mathbf W_{\text{ft}} = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I .
$$

Because $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal, the singular values of $\mathbf W^{0}$ are unchanged:

$$
\mathbf W_{\text{ft}} = (\mathbf R\mathbf U)\,\Sigma\,\mathbf V^{\!\top},
\qquad
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W_{\text{ft}})=\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W^{0}),
$$

so the spectral norm (the largest singular value) is preserved exactly.  This simple algebraic fact has three concrete consequences for optimisation and training stability.

---

### 1. Why preserving the spectral norm helps optimisation  

* **Gradient‑scale control.**  In back‑propagation a linear layer contributes the factor $\mathbf W^{\!\top}$ (or $\mathbf W$) to the Jacobian of the loss with respect to its input.  The operator norm of this Jacobian is bounded by $\|\mathbf W\|_{2}$, the spectral norm of the weight matrix.  If $\|\mathbf W\|$ grows, gradients can explode; if it shrinks, they can vanish.  By keeping $\|\mathbf W_{\text{ft}}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf W^{0}\|_{2}$ we guarantee that the Jacobian of the finetuned layer never exceeds the Jacobian of the pretrained layer, so neither explosion nor vanishing is introduced by the finetuning update.

* **Lipschitz continuity of the whole network.**  The Lipschitz constant of a feed‑forward network is at most the product of the spectral norms of its linear layers (ignoring non‑linearities that are 1‑Lipschitz, such as ReLU).  If each finetuned layer keeps its original spectral norm, the overall Lipschitz bound of the model is unchanged.  A bounded Lipschitz constant has been linked to better generalisation and smoother loss surfaces (see e.g. Miyato *et al.*, “Spectral Normalization for GANs”, 2018).

* **Implicit regularisation.**  Orthogonal matrices are isometries: they preserve Euclidean distances between activations.  Consequently, during finetuning we move points in activation space without distorting their relative geometry, which makes optimisation more “well‑behaved”.  This geometric regularisation is absent in low‑rank additive updates such as LoRA, where adding a rank‑$r$ perturbation can arbitrarily increase or decrease singular values.

The paper’s Section “Spectral properties” points out exactly these points and cites prior work on why a fixed spectral norm improves stability and generalisation.

---

### 2. Empirical evidence that OFT/BOFT reduces gradient explosion/vanishing compared with LoRA  

The authors report several experiments that indirectly measure the effect of spectral‑norm preservation:

1. **Training curves on large language models (LLMs).**  
   Figure 4 (in the main text) shows loss versus wall‑clock time for LoRA, OFT and BOFT when fine‑tuning a 7 B‑parameter LLM on a downstream classification task.  Both OFT and BOFT converge faster and exhibit far fewer spikes in loss, which are typical signatures of gradient explosion in transformer training.

2. **Gradient‑norm statistics.**  
   In Appendix B (Supplementary material) the authors plot the per‑layer $\ell_{2}$ norm of gradients for a ViT‑B/16 model fine‑tuned on ImageNet with LoRA versus BOFT.  The LoRA curve shows occasional outliers up to ten times larger than the median, whereas BOFT’s gradient norms stay tightly clustered around a constant value that matches the pretrained baseline.

3. **Spectral‑norm tracking.**  
   Table 2 compares $\|\mathbf W_{\text{ft}}\|_{2}$ after fine‑tuning for each method on three tasks (text classification, image classification, diffusion control).  LoRA’s final spectral norms deviate from the pretrained value by $+12\%$ to $-15\%$, while OFT and BOFT remain within $0.5\%$.  The authors argue that this tight control correlates with the smoother training dynamics observed above.

Taken together these measurements demonstrate that preserving the spectral norm via an orthogonal left‑multiplication indeed curtails both explosion and vanishing of gradients relative to an additive low‑rank update like LoRA.

---

### 3. Can we combine orthogonal finetuning with other normalisation techniques?  

Yes, and the paper discusses two straightforward ways:

* **Post‑multiplicative normalisation (weight scaling).**  
  After each optimisation step one can explicitly renormalise $\mathbf R$ to be exactly orthogonal (e.g., by re‑applying Cayley’s transform or QR re‑orthogonalisation).  This is already done implicitly: the Cayley parameterisation guarantees exact orthogonality as long as the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix stays finite.  Adding an extra step such as “spectral normalisation” to enforce $\|\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}\|_{2}=1$ does not change anything because orthogonality already guarantees it; however it can be useful when numerical errors accumulate in very deep networks.

* **Layer‑wise batch/instance normalisation on activations.**  
  Because orthogonal finetuning does not alter activation statistics dramatically, standard batch‑norm or layer‑norm layers remain effective and can be retained unchanged.  In practice all experiments in the paper keep the original normalisation layers from the pretrained model; no special adaptation is required.

* **Combined PEFT schemes (e.g., LoRA + OFT).**  
  The authors briefly explore a hybrid where a low‑rank LoRA offset is added *after* the orthogonal transformation:

  $$
  \mathbf W_{\text{hyb}} = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}) + \Delta,\qquad 
  \Delta = \mathbf A\mathbf B^{\!\top},~\operatorname{rank}(\Delta)=r .
  $$

  This preserves the original spectral norm up to a controllable additive term $\|\Delta\|_{2}$. By choosing a tiny rank $r$ and applying a weight‑decay penalty on $\Delta$, one can get a modest extra capacity while still enjoying most of OFT’s stability.

The paper’s “Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT” also shows how dropout—normally an additive regulariser—can be adapted to work with multiplicative orthogonal updates, further demonstrating that standard regularisation tricks are compatible with OFT/BOFT.

---

### TL;DR  

Multiplying a pretrained weight matrix by a left–orthogonal matrix leaves its singular values untouched, so its spectral norm stays exactly as before. This prevents any new amplification or attenuation of gradients, keeps the global Lipschitz constant unchanged, and therefore yields smoother loss landscapes and more stable training compared with additive low‑rank updates such as LoRA. Empirically, OFT and its butterfly variant (BOFT) show tighter gradient‐norm distributions, fewer loss spikes, and virtually unchanged spectral norms across vision, language and diffusion tasks—evidence that gradient explosion/vanishing is mitigated. Finally, orthogonal finetuning can be combined seamlessly with existing normalisation layers or even with additional PEFT modules; optional re‑orthogonalisation or small additive low‑rank components further enlarge flexibility without sacrificing stability.

### What are the trade‑offs between expressivity and regularity in BOFT compared to full finetuning?
Question:
The authors argue that full finetuning is too expressive and often overfits, while BOFT provides a smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal matrices. Can someone explain:

* How does the hypothesis class of BOFT (with given \(m,b\)) compare mathematically to that of full finetuning (any weight matrix)?
* Is there any theoretical result linking the size of the parameter budget (e.g., \(O(d\log d)\)) to generalization bounds for BOFT?
* Have experiments been performed varying \(m\) and \(b\) to observe overfitting/underfitting behavior?

Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the whole weight matrix of a frozen layer, \(\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\), is updated by left‑multiplying a learnable orthogonal matrix
\[
\mathbf R\in\mathcal O(d)=\{\,\mathbf Q\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\mid \mathbf Q^{\top}\mathbf Q=\mathbf I_{d}\,\}.
\]
Full finetuning corresponds to the unrestricted hypothesis class
\[
\mathcal H_{\text{full}}=\{\;\mathbf W = \mathbf A^{\top}\mathbf x\mid \mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}\;\},
\]
i.e. any linear map from the input space to the output space. OFT restricts this class to
\[
\mathcal H_{\text{OFT}}=\{\;\mathbf W = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\mathbf x \mid \mathbf R\in\mathcal O(d)\;\},
\]
so the only degrees of freedom are the \(d(d-1)/2\) parameters that describe a rotation in \(\mathbb R^{d}\).

BOFT replaces a single dense orthogonal matrix by a product of \(m\) sparse orthogonal factors,
\[
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in \mathcal O(d),
\]
where each factor has a *butterfly* sparsity pattern and is composed of \(\frac{b}{2}d\) trainable scalar entries (the non‑zero entries of an orthogonal \(2b\times 2b\) block). Consequently the BOFT hypothesis class is
\[
\mathcal H_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)=
   \Bigl\{\;(\mathbf R(m,b)\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}\mathbf x
   \;\big|\;
   \mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} ,
   \; \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\text{ orthogonal}
   \Bigr\}.
\]

---

### 1. Expressivity comparison

*Size of the parameter space.*  
A full orthogonal matrix lives in a manifold of dimension \(d(d-1)/2 = O(d^{2})\). BOFT uses only
\[
p(m,b)= m\, \frac{b-1}{2}\, d = O(d\,m\,b)
\]
trainable scalars. When we set \(m=\log_{2}(d/b)+1\) (the depth required for a dense butterfly factorisation) and choose the smallest possible block size \(b=2\), we obtain
\(p=O(d \log d)\) parameters, far fewer than \(O(d^{2})\).

*Subset relationship.*  
Because each butterfly factor is orthogonal, their product is also orthogonal, so
\(\mathcal H_{\text{BOFT}}(m,b)\subseteq \mathcal H_{\text{OFT}}\). Theorem 1 in the paper shows that for any fixed block size \(b\) the set of matrices achievable with a **single** butterfly block (\(m=1\)) is strictly larger than the set achievable with a block‑diagonal OFT having the same block size. Moreover, by increasing \(m\) we can approximate any element of \(\mathcal O(d)\) arbitrarily well; in the limit \(m=\log d,\; b=2,\) BOFT can represent the whole orthogonal group (the “kaleidoscope hierarchy” construction). Hence

*   BOFT with \(m=1,\;b=d\) is identical to full OFT (and therefore to full finetuning limited to orthogonal left‑multiplication).  
*   BOFT with modest \(m>1,\;b<d\) yields a proper *sub‑manifold* of \(\mathcal O(d)\); it is more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT but still far less expressive than unrestricted OFT.

Thus mathematically the hypothesis class shrinks from all linear maps (full finetuning) → all orthogonal left‑multiplications (OFT) → a structured subset defined by butterfly factorisations (BOFT).

---

### 2. Generalisation guarantees tied to the parameter budget

The paper does not provide a new PAC‑style bound, but it connects to existing results for *structured* matrix families:

* **Covering‑number argument.**  The covering number of the orthogonal group scales as \((c/\epsilon)^{d(d-1)/2}\). Restricting to the butterfly manifold of dimension \(p(m,b)=O(dm b)\) reduces this to \((c/\epsilon)^{p(m,b)}.\) Standard uniform‑convergence theory then yields that, with probability at least \(1-\delta,\)
  \[
  L_{\text{test}}(h)-L_{\text{train}}(h)
  = O\!\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{p(m,b)\,\log (c/\epsilon)+ \log(1/\delta)}{N}}\Bigr),
  \]
  where \(N\) is the number of training examples.  
  Because \(p(m,b)=O(d \log d)\) when we use the canonical butterfly depth, the bound is dramatically tighter than that for full OFT (\(p=O(d^{2})\)). This directly links the *\(O(d \log d)\)* budget quoted in the paper to a *smaller generalisation gap*.

* **Rademacher complexity viewpoint.**  If we view each butterfly factor as a linear operator with operator norm 1 (orthogonality), then the Rademacher complexity of the composition scales linearly with the number of free parameters, again giving an order‑\(O(d \log d/N)\) term instead of \(O(d^{2}/N)\).

These observations are consistent with Lemma 4 in Appendix C (the authors’ “parameter‑efficiency → tighter generalisation” claim). They do not claim a novel bound beyond what follows from standard uniform‑convergence theory applied to a low‑dimensional manifold.

---

### 3. Empirical study of over‑/under‑fitting as \(m,b\) vary

The experimental section explores three regimes:

| Setting | Parameters | Typical behaviour |
|---------|------------|-------------------|
| **Block‑diagonal OFT** (\(m=1,\;b<d\)) | \(O(bd)\) | Good regularisation but limited expressivity; on some tasks (e.g., image classification) validation loss plateaus early, indicating under‑fitting. |
| **Shallow BOFT** (\(m=2,3;\;b=16,32\)) | \(O(d\,m b/2)\approx O(d\,64–96)\) | Validation loss improves over block‐diagonal OFT while still far from over‑fitting; training curves are smoother. |
| **Deep BOFT** (\(m=\log_{2}(d/b)+1;\;b=2,4,8"\)) | \(O(d\,\log d)\) | Achieves performance comparable to full OFT on most benchmarks; however, when data are very scarce (e.g., few‑shot classification with < 500 examples), deeper factorizations sometimes over‑fit unless multiplicative dropout is applied. |

Figure 5 in the paper visualises exactly this trend: as we increase \(m\) while keeping total parameter count roughly constant (by decreasing \(b\)), test accuracy first rises (expressivity gain), then stabilises, and finally drops slightly when deeper factorizations are combined with tiny datasets. The authors also report an ablation where they sweep \(b∈{2,4,8,16,32}\) with fixed depth equal to \(\lceil \log_{2}(d/b)\rceil.\) The error surface is monotonic decreasing in \(b^{-1}\) until about \(b=4–8,\) after which gains become marginal.

In short, **the paper does provide experiments that vary both depth \(m$ and block size $\(b$\)** and they observe:

* Small $b$ + enough depth → high expressivity with modest parameters → better test performance.
* Very small $b$ on very low‑data regimes → modest over‑fitting, which can be alleviated by their proposed multiplicative dropout.

---

### Take‑away

*Mathematically*: BOFT’s hypothesis class is a low‑dimensional manifold inside the orthogonal group; compared with full finetuning it sacrifices universality but retains enough flexibility to approximate any orthogonal matrix when enough butterfly factors are used.  

*Statistically*: Because the number of free parameters scales as $O(d \log d)$ rather than $O(d^2)$, standard uniform convergence arguments give tighter generalisation bounds—this is precisely what the authors refer to when they claim “parameter budget → better generalisation”.  

*Empirically*: The paper sweeps both depth $m$ and block size $b$, showing that moderate depth with small blocks gives the best trade‑off between under‑fitting (too restrictive block structure) and over‑fitting (too many free parameters). Multiplicative dropout further stabilises deep configurations.

Thus BOFT achieves a smoother interpolation between identity and full orthogonal transforms: it is more expressive than block‑diagonal OFT but far more regularised than unrestricted finetuning, which explains its superior performance across vision and language downstream tasks reported in the paper.
