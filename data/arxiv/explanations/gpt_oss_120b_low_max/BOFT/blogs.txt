# Why Orthogonal Finetuning Matters: Preserving Knowledge in Big Models

## Introduction  

Foundation models such as GPT‑4, Stable Diffusion, or large vision transformers have shown astonishing abilities across many tasks. The catch? Training them from scratch costs billions of dollars and months of GPU time. Practitioners therefore rely on **parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT)** – methods that adapt a frozen pretrained model to a downstream task while touching only a tiny fraction of the weights.

Among the PEFT toolbox we have **adapters**, **prompt tuning**, and **low‑rank updates** (the most popular being LoRA). A newer, theoretically elegant approach is **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**. In OFT we multiply the original weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix. This seemingly simple operation has deep geometric consequences: it keeps the pairwise angles between neurons unchanged, which means the relational knowledge encoded during pretraining stays intact.

Below we break down why orthogonality matters, how OFT works in practice, and how it stacks up against other PEFT strategies.

---

## Geometry of a Linear Layer  

Consider a linear layer with pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\). For an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) the output is  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

Each column of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) corresponds to a **neuron** (or feature direction). During pretraining the model discovers a geometry: the angles between these columns capture semantic relations (e.g., “cat” and “dog” may be close). Preserving those angles while finetuning is desirable because it protects the knowledge that the massive pretraining effort has already built.

An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\). Multiplying \(\mathbf{W}^0\) on the left yields a new weight matrix  

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0,
\]

and the forward pass becomes  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\widetilde{\mathbf{W}})^{\top}\mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{x}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal, it acts as a **rotation (or reflection)** in the input space: distances and inner products are preserved. Consequently, the pairwise angles between any two columns of \(\mathbf{W}^0\) remain exactly the same after multiplication. In other words, OFT *re‑orients* the whole layer without distorting its internal geometry.

---

## Why Preserve Geometry?  

1. **Knowledge Retention** – Pretraining aligns neurons with semantic concepts. If those relationships are broken, finetuning may overwrite useful representations and require more data to recover them.

2. **Training Stability** – Orthogonal transformations keep the spectral norm of the weight matrix unchanged (the largest singular value stays fixed). Stable spectra are known to improve optimization dynamics and prevent exploding/vanishing gradients.

3. **Generalization** – Theoretical work shows that preserving angles reduces hyperspherical energy, which correlates with better generalization on unseen data.

In practice, researchers observed that OFT converges more reliably than low‑rank additive methods like LoRA, especially on tasks that require subtle style or content control (e.g., text‑to‑image diffusion).

---

## OFT vs. Other PEFT Techniques  

| Method | Parameter budget | How it updates weights | Geometric effect |
|--------|------------------|-----------------------|------------------|
| **LoRA** | Adds two low‑rank matrices (\(r \ll d\)) → \(2dr\) extra params | Additive: \(\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}^0 + \Delta_{\text{low-rank}}\) | Changes singular values; can distort neuron angles |
| **Adapters** | Inserts small bottleneck MLPs inside each layer | Additive non‑linear modules | Alters activations locally; no explicit geometric guarantee |
| **Prompt Tuning** | Only learns extra prefix tokens | No weight change in backbone | Geometry unchanged but expressive power limited to input side |
| **OFT** | Learns an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) (dense → \(d^2\) params, or structured variants → fewer) | Multiplicative: \(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0\) | Exactly preserves all pairwise neuron angles and spectral norm |

The main drawback of vanilla OFT is that a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix needs \(O(d^2)\) trainable parameters—often too many for large models. The original OFT paper mitigates this by employing a block‑diagonal orthogonal structure, which reduces parameters at the cost of expressivity (each block acts independently on a subset of dimensions).

---

## Towards Parameter Efficiency: Butterfly Orthogonal Finetuning  

A recent advance—**Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)**—replaces the block‑diagonal sparsity with *butterfly factorization*. Inspired by the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform, a butterfly matrix can be expressed as a product of \(\log d\) sparse orthogonal factors, each with only \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries. The total trainable count drops to \(O(d\log d)\), dramatically smaller than \(O(d^2)\) but still capable of representing dense rotations.

Key properties:

- **Dense connectivity:** Even though each factor is sparse, their product yields a fully dense orthogonal matrix, guaranteeing angle preservation.
- **Structured inductive bias:** Butterfly structures can exactly encode classic transforms (FFT, Hadamard), providing useful priors that improve generalization.
- **Flexibility:** By varying the number of factors (\(m\)) and block size (\(b\)), BOFT interpolates smoothly between block‑diagonal OFT (few parameters, limited expressivity) and full OFT (maximal expressivity).

Empirically, BOFT matches or outperforms LoRA and adapters across vision, language, and diffusion tasks while using far fewer trainable parameters than naïve OFT.

---

## Practical Takeaways  

1. **When to choose OFT/BOFT:**  
   - You care about *preserving* pretrained relational knowledge (e.g., style transfer, controlled generation).  
   - You have moderate compute budget for finetuning but want zero inference overhead (no extra adapters at run time).  
   - Your downstream dataset is small enough that over‑parameterized updates risk overfitting.

2. **Implementation hints:**  
   - Initialize the orthogonal matrix as identity (via Cayley transform with zero skew‑symmetric part).  
   - For BOFT, use a small number of butterfly factors (e.g., \(m=\log_2 d,\ b=2\)) to keep parameters low while still achieving dense rotations.  
   - Consider *multiplicative dropout*—randomly replace selected butterfly components with identity matrices during training—to avoid over‑fitting similar to LoRA’s dropout.

3. **Limitations:**  
   - Training BOFT incurs modest extra time because multiple sparse matrices must be multiplied each step.  
   - The butterfly topology is not provably optimal for all dimensions; alternative network topologies might yield even better parameter/expressivity trade‑offs.

---

## Conclusion  

Orthogonal finetuning offers a principled way to adapt massive foundation models while keeping their internal geometry untouched. By viewing finetuning through the lens of angle preservation and spectral stability, we understand why OFT often trains more smoothly than additive low‑rank methods like LoRA. Recent butterfly‑based parameterizations make OFT practical for today’s trillion‑parameter beasts, delivering strong performance with only a modest parameter budget.

If you’re looking for a PEFT method that respects what your model has already learned—and does so with elegant linear algebra—give Orthogonal Finetuning (or its butterfly variant) a try. Your downstream task may just benefit from keeping those pretrained neuron relationships intact.



# From Block‑Diagonal to Butterfly: Making Orthogonal Finetuning Parameter‑Efficient  

Large foundation models (think GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or Vision Transformers) are powerful, but training them from scratch is a huge financial and environmental burden.  In practice we **finetune** a pretrained model on a downstream task, trying to keep the original knowledge while adapting to new data.  One recent finetuning paradigm—**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**—does exactly this by left‑multiplying each linear layer with a learnable orthogonal matrix.  Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neurons, the pretrained representation stays largely intact, leading to better generalization and training stability.

### Why OFT Still Feels Heavy  

The idea is elegant, but an orthogonal matrix of size \(d\times d\) needs \(\Theta(d^{2})\) free parameters.  In modern vision or language models \(d\) can be several thousand, so the extra memory and compute quickly become impractical.  The original OFT paper tackled this by imposing a **block‑diagonal** structure:

\[
\mathbf{R}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}),\qquad
\mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d .
\]

Now we only store \(r\) small orthogonal blocks, reducing parameters to \(\mathcal{O}(bd)\).  Unfortunately the price is a strong inductive bias: dimensions that happen to fall in different blocks never interact, limiting expressivity.  Moreover, there is no principled way to pick the block size or pattern.

### Rethinking the Problem as Information Transmission  

Imagine the dense orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) as a **communication network**: each row (output neuron) receives information from every column (input neuron).  If we factor \(\mathbf{R}\) into a product of sparse matrices,

\[
\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{B}_{m}\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\dots \mathbf{B}_{1},
\]

the factorization can be visualized as a layered graph with \(d\) nodes per layer and edges defined by non‑zero entries of each \(\mathbf{B}_{i}\).  Information flows from the first layer to the last through these edges.  To reconstruct a *dense* transformation we only need **dense connectivity** (every source can reach every sink) while using as few edges—as few trainable parameters—as possible.

A naïve dense connection would require \(d^{2}\) edges per layer.  However, clever graph topologies can achieve full connectivity with far fewer edges.  This is exactly what the **Cooley‑Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT)** does: its “butterfly” graph shuffles information efficiently, using only \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) edges.

### Enter the Butterfly Factorization  

The butterfly factor comes from the radix‑2 FFT recursion.  At each stage we pair up nodes, apply small orthogonal “mixing” blocks, and permute the results before the next stage.  Formally for a power‑of‑two size \(k\),

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_{4})
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf{d}_{i}\in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}.
\]

Stacking such factors recursively yields a **butterfly matrix** \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) that is the product of \(\log_{2} d\) sparse components:

```
Stage 1:   B̃(d, 2)
Stage 2:   B̃(d, 4)
…
Stage log d: B̃(d, d)
```

Each component \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is block‑diagonal with blocks of size \(k\), and every block follows the same sparse pattern as \(\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)\).  

Crucially, if each component is **orthogonal**, then their product remains orthogonal.  Enforcing orthogonality is simple: each \(2\times2\) block can be parameterized with a Cayley transform (or an explicit rotation angle), exactly as in OFT’s original block‑diagonal design.  By replacing scalar entries with small \(b\times b\) sub‑matrices we obtain a **block‑butterfly** that works for any hidden dimension.

### Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)

Putting it together gives us **BOFT**:

1. **Initialize** every butterfly component as identity (Cayley’s skew‑symmetric matrix set to zero), guaranteeing zero deviation from the pretrained model at start.
2. **Forward pass** replaces the OFT update  
   \[
   \mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x}
   \]  
   with  
   \[
   \mathbf{z}= (\underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,m)}\dots \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,1)}}_{\displaystyle \mathbf{R}(m,b)}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x},
   \]  
   where each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}\) is an orthogonal butterfly component.
3. **Parameter count:** each component contributes roughly \((b-1)d/2\) trainable values (the independent elements of its skew‑symmetric blocks). With \(m=\log_{2}(d/b)\) components we obtain  
   \[
   \text{\#params} = \mathcal O(d\,\log d),
   \]  
   far below OFT’s original \(\mathcal O(d^{2})\) and even more efficient than block‑diagonal OFT when a dense transform is needed.
4. **Multiplicative dropout:** during training we randomly replace a fraction of butterfly components—or individual blocks inside them—with identities, acting like dropout for multiplicative updates.

### Why Butterfly Beats Block‑Diagonal  

| Property | Block‑Diagonal OFT | BOFT (Butterfly) |
|----------|-------------------|------------------|
| **Expressivity** | Only intra‑block mixing; no cross‑block interaction | Dense connectivity after ≤ log d stages; can approximate any orthogonal matrix (theorem in paper). |
| **Parameters** | \(\mathcal O(bd)\); dense matrix needs \(b=d\). | \(\mathcal O(d\,\log d)\) for a fully dense orthogonal transform regardless of \(b\). |
| **Inductive bias** | Hard sparsity pattern; may miss classic transforms (FFT, Hadamard). | Structured sparsity mirrors FFT/DCT patterns → beneficial bias for natural signals. |
| **Training stability** | Good (preserves angles), but limited flexibility. | Same stability (orthogonal product), plus richer hypothesis space without extra cost. |

A simulation in the paper shows that with only a few hundred thousand parameters BOFT can approximate a random \(512\times512\) orthogonal matrix much better than any block‑diagonal configuration of comparable size.

### From Theory to Practice  

The authors tested BOFT on three kinds of foundation models:

- **Vision Transformers** on image classification and detection,
- **Large Language Models** on text generation and question answering,
- **Stable Diffusion**–style text‑to‑image diffusion models for controllable generation.

Across all benchmarks BOFT outperformed LoRA (low‑rank PEFT), traditional block‑diagonal OFT, and many recent adapters—sometimes by a large margin—while keeping the number of trainable parameters tiny.  After finetuning, the learned butterfly matrix can be folded into the original weight matrix, incurring *no* extra inference latency.

### Takeaways  

1. **Sparse products can be dense:** By chaining several carefully designed sparse orthogonal matrices we obtain a full dense orthogonal transform with only \(\mathcal O(d\,\log d)\) learnable numbers.
2. **Butterfly structures are more than FFT tricks:** Their graph topology guarantees dense information flow using minimal edges, making them perfect for parameter‑efficient PEFT.
3. **Orthogonal finetuning gets a boost:** BOFT keeps the nice spectral properties of OFT (spectral norm preservation → stable training) while dramatically shrinking its footprint.
4. **Inductive bias matters:** The butterfly’s built‑in resemblance to classic linear transforms seems to act as an implicit regularizer that improves generalization.

### Where We Still Have Work  

- BOFT introduces extra matrix multiplications during training, so its runtime overhead is modestly higher than plain OFT.
- The butterfly isn’t provably optimal for information transmission; future work could explore other network topologies from computer networking or graph theory.

Nevertheless, Orthogonal Butterfly offers a compelling recipe: replace bulky block‐diagonal orthogonal updates with a cascade of tiny butterfly layers, and you get an *expressive*, *stable*, and *parameter‑efficient* finetuning method that works across vision and language domains.

---  

*Ready to try BOFT on your own model? The authors provide code that plugs into standard PyTorch/Transformers pipelines—just swap your LoRA or adapter module for `BOFT(m,b)` and watch the parameter budget shrink without sacrificing performance.*



# The Information Transmission View: How Graph Theory Helps Design Better Finetuning Layers

Finetuning massive foundation models—whether they are vision transformers, large language models, or text‑to‑image diffusion systems—has become a daily routine for researchers.  The challenge is not just *how* to adapt a pretrained model, but *how* to do it while touching as few parameters as possible.  

A recent line of work called **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** tackles this by multiplying each pretrained linear layer with a learned orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \).  Orthogonal transforms preserve pairwise neuron angles and keep the spectral norm of the original weights unchanged, which often translates into better stability and generalization.  The downside?  An orthogonal matrix of size \( d \times d \) normally needs \( \mathcal{O}(d^2) \) trainable entries—far too many for truly parameter‑efficient finetuning.

Enter the **Information Transmission View**.  By reinterpreting the factorisation of an orthogonal matrix as a problem of routing information on a grid‑structured graph, we obtain clean design criteria that guide us toward much more compact parameterizations.  This perspective also reveals why **butterfly graphs**, the backbone of the fast Fourier transform, are ideally suited for building dense orthogonal matrices with only \( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \) trainable parameters.

Below we walk through this graph‑theoretic lens, explain the notions of “edges” and “paths,” and show how they culminate in the **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** finetuning method.

---

## 1. From Orthogonal Matrices to Information Flow

Consider a single linear layer in a pretrained model with weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n} \).  OFT replaces the forward pass

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x}
\]

by

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0)^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{x},
\qquad \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\; \mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\!\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_d.
\]

If we write \( \mathbf{R} \) as a product of \( m \) square factors,

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_m}_{\text{layer }m}
               \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m-1}}_{\text{layer }m-1}
               \dots
               \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_1}_{\text{layer }1},
\]

each factor can be thought of as a **step** that shuffles and mixes information across neurons.

### The Grid Graph Interpretation

Create a grid with \( d \) nodes on each level and \( m+1 \) levels total (one level for the input, one for each factor, and one for the output).  An entry \( (\mathbf{B}_i)_{jk}\neq0 \) corresponds to a directed edge from node \(k\) on level \(i-1\) to node \(j\) on level \(i\).  

Multiplying all factors together is then exactly the same as *traversing* from the first level to the last along **paths** composed of these edges.  The resulting dense matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) has a non‑zero entry \( R_{pq} \) if and only if there exists at least one path that connects input node \(q\) (level 0) to output node \(p\) (level \(m+1\)).

Thus, constructing a **dense orthogonal matrix** translates into designing a layered graph where **every source node can reach every sink node**, using as few edges as possible.

---

## 2. Two Design Desiderata

From the graph viewpoint two natural criteria emerge:

| Criterion | Graph‑theoretic meaning | Why it matters |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Dense connectivity** | Every node on level 0 must have at least one directed path to every node on level \(m+1\). | Guarantees that the final matrix \( \mathbf{R} \) is dense (no zero entries), preserving full expressive power. |
| **Minimum free edges** | The total number of *trainable* edges (i.e., non‑zero entries that are not forced by orthogonality) should be as low as possible. | Directly reduces the number of learnable parameters, giving us parameter efficiency. |

A subtlety: orthogonal matrices impose extra constraints on edge patterns. For an orthogonal factor \( \mathbf{B}_i \), each row must be an orthonormal vector; consequently each level must contain exactly \( d \) edges forming a bijection between its nodes and those of the next level. Those “mandatory” edges are not learnable (they are essentially fixed signs), so we only count *free* edges that we actually get to optimise.

The goal becomes: **connect all sources to all sinks with the smallest possible set of free edges while respecting orthogonality**.

---

## 3. Why Block‑Diagonal Sparsity Falls Short

The original OFT paper used block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbf{R}_1,\dots,\mathbf{R}_r\big),
\qquad
\mathbf{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d.
\]

In graph terms this corresponds to splitting the node set into \( r \) independent islands—edges never cross between blocks. Consequently, many source–sink pairs have *no* path at all, violating dense connectivity unless we set \( b=d \), which defeats the purpose of parameter saving.

Thus, block‑diagonal sparsity trades expressivity for fewer parameters but creates an undesirable inductive bias: it forces neurons in different blocks never to interact during finetuning.

---

## 4. Butterfly Graphs: The Optimal Trade‑off

The **butterfly graph**—famously used in Cooley‑Tukey’s fast Fourier transform—provides precisely what we need:

* It consists of \( \log_2 d \) layers.
* Each layer connects every node to two nodes in the next layer via a fixed pattern reminiscent of “butterfly wings.”
* The total number of free edges is only \( \mathcal{O}(d\log d) \).

Visually, each step interleaves pairs of nodes, swaps halves, and repeats; after \( \log_2 d \) steps every source can reach every sink through a unique combination of those sparse connections.

### Parameter Count

Each butterfly factor is mostly diagonal blocks; each block contributes only a small constant number of trainable parameters (e.g., a single rotation angle for a \(2\times2\) orthogonal block). With \( m = \log_2 d \) factors we obtain

\[
\#\text{params} = O(d\,\log d),
\]

a dramatic reduction from \( O(d^{2}) \). Yet because paths exist between all source–sink pairs, the resulting product matrix is dense.

---

## 5. From Theory to Practice: Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

Armed with butterfly graphs, we can now define a concrete finetuning layer:

1. **Factorisation** – Represent the learnable orthogonal matrix as  
   \[
   \mathbf{R}(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
   \]
   where each factor \( \tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} \) is an orthogonal butterfly component with block size \(b\).  
   - If \(b=2\), each component is built from many independent \(2\times2\) rotations (the classic butterfly).  
   - Larger blocks allow more flexibility at modest extra cost.

2. **Orthogonality enforcement** – Each component is parametrised via a Cayley transform or directly as a small rotation matrix; thus every factor stays perfectly orthogonal throughout training.

3. **Zero‑initialisation** – Set all rotation angles to zero so that every component starts as an identity matrix; consequently the whole product starts as identity and the finetuned model initially coincides exactly with the pretrained one.

4. **Multiplicative Dropout** – Randomly replace selected butterfly components or interior blocks by identities during training, preventing over‑fitting without breaking multiplicative structure.

Because we multiply all components **before** applying them to the frozen weight matrix (\( (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^0 )^{\!\top}\)), BOFT incurs **no extra inference cost**: after training we simply fold the learned orthogonal matrix into the original weights.

---

## 6. Why Butterflies Shine: Expressivity vs Regularity

One might ask whether restricting ourselves to butterfly‑structured orthogonal matrices hurts expressivity compared to an unrestricted dense orthogonal matrix. Empirical studies show:

* With only a few dozen thousand parameters (for \(d=1024\)), BOFT can approximate random dense orthogonal matrices much better than block‑diagonal OFT at comparable parameter budgets.
* Theoretical analysis (Theorem 1 in the paper) proves that composing a modest number of butterfly matrices can generate *any* orthogonal matrix when we allow alternating transposes—so BOFT sits in a sweet spot between full freedom and strong structural priors.

The structured sparsity acts like an inductive bias similar to how low‑rank adapters bias solutions toward smoother changes; here the bias leans toward transforms familiar from signal processing (FFT, Hadamard), which appear to regularize finetuning and improve downstream generalization.

---

## 7. Takeaways

1. **Graph theory offers a clean abstraction for designing compact orthogonal transforms**—the problem becomes “connect every source to every sink using few edges while respecting orthogonality.”
2. **Butterfly graphs satisfy both desiderata**: dense connectivity with only \( O(d\log d)\) trainable edges.
3. **BOFT turns this insight into a practical finetuning recipe**, bringing down OFT’s parameter count from quadratic to near‑linear without sacrificing stability or performance.
4. **The approach is generic**—it works for vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models alike, demonstrating that orthogonal finetuning is not limited to any single modality.

If you’re building PEFT pipelines and care about preserving pretrained knowledge while keeping your parameter budget tight, give BOFT a try.  Its elegant blend of information theory, graph design, and classic signal‑processing structures might just become your new go‑to tool for efficient model adaptation.



# BOFT vs. LoRA: Spectral Norms, Stability, and Generalization Explained  

In the past few years the “fine‑tuning” stage of large foundation models has become a research arena of its own. Two families of methods dominate the conversation: **low‑rank updates** (the most popular being **LoRA**) and **orthogonal transformations** (the newest entrant is **Orthogonal Butterfly**, or **BOFT**). Both strive to adapt a massive pretrained model using only a tiny fraction of new parameters, yet they take very different routes. In this post we unpack why BOFT’s focus on preserving the **spectral norm** of the original weights leads to more stable training, how its **structured inductive bias** helps generalization, and what the practical trade‑off looks like between **expressivity** and **regularity** compared with LoRA.

---  

## 1. The core ideas behind LoRA and BOFT  

### LoRA – low‑rank additive updates  
LoRA rewrites a pretrained linear layer  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}} = \mathbf{W}^{0} + \Delta\mathbf{W}, \qquad 
\Delta\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B},
\]

where \(\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times n}\) are learned, with rank \(r \ll \min(d,n)\). The original weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) stays frozen; only the small low‑rank factors are optimized. This additive scheme is cheap (only \(r(d+n)\) trainable parameters) and works well for many downstream tasks.

### BOFT – multiplicative orthogonal updates via butterfly factorization  
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) proposes a *multiplicative* update:

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top},
\]

with \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf I\)). The orthogonal matrix rotates the *neuron space* while preserving all pairwise angles. A naive orthogonal matrix would require \(O(d^{2})\) parameters—clearly not parameter‑efficient.  

**BOFT** solves this by expressing \(\mathbf R\) as a product of a handful of *sparse* orthogonal matrices organized in a **butterfly** pattern (the same structure that powers the Cooley–Tukey FFT). With \(m = \log_{2} d\) butterfly components each having only \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries, BOFT needs only \(O(d\log d)\) trainable parameters—a massive reduction while still yielding a dense orthogonal transformation.

---  

## 2. Why preserving the spectral norm matters  

The spectral norm \(\|\mathbf W^{0}\|_{2}\) is the largest singular value of a weight matrix; it governs how much the layer can amplify an input vector.  

* **LoRA:** Adding a low‑rank term changes the singular value spectrum in an uncontrolled way. Even if \(\|\Delta\mathbf W\|_{2}\) is small, its effect can be amplified when combined with the existing singular vectors of \(\mathbf W^{0}\). This can inadvertently increase the overall spectral norm, sometimes causing exploding gradients or instability during fine‑tuning.  

* **BOFT:** Because an orthogonal matrix has singular values all equal to 1, the product \(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}\) retains exactly the same singular values as \(\mathbf W^{0}\). In other words,

  \[
  \sigma_{\max}\!\bigl(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}\bigr)=
  \sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W^{0}),
  \]

  where \(\sigma_{\max}\) denotes the largest singular value. The spectral norm never grows, guaranteeing that gradient magnitudes stay in a familiar range throughout training. Empirically this translates into smoother loss curves and fewer “spikes” that would otherwise demand aggressive learning‑rate tuning.

Preserving the spectral norm thus provides an **implicit regularization**: the fine‑tuned model cannot drift far from the geometry of the pretrained representation, which is crucial when adapting gigantic models with limited data.

---  

## 3. Structured inductive bias from butterfly factorization  

### What is an inductive bias?  
Any learning algorithm makes assumptions about which functions are “likely”. In deep learning these assumptions are often encoded in architecture (convolutions assume locality, attention assumes pairwise interactions). A stronger bias can improve data efficiency but may limit expressivity.

### Butterfly‐induced bias  
The butterfly pattern forces information to flow through a hierarchy of **pairwise mixing stages**:

1. At level 1, each element swaps with one partner (a \(2\times2\) rotation).  
2. At level 2, partners are regrouped across larger blocks, mixing again, and so on until level \(\log_{2} d\).

This schedule mirrors classic fast linear transforms—Fourier, Hadamard, cosine—so BOFT’s hypothesis space naturally contains those transforms as special cases. Consequently:

* **Implicit smoothness:** Transformations that respect global frequency structure are favored over completely arbitrary orthogonal matrices.
* **Better generalization:** When downstream data are scarce or noisy, this smoothness prevents over‑fitting to idiosyncratic patterns that would be possible with an unrestricted orthogonal matrix or with an unconstrained low‑rank addition.

Experimental results in the original BOFT paper show consistent gains over LoRA across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models—evidence that this bias pays off in practice.

---  

## 4. Expressivity vs. regularity: where does each method sit?  

| Method | Parameter count (per layer) | Expressivity | Regularity (bias) |
|--------|----------------------------|--------------|-------------------|
| Full fine‑tuning | \(d\times n\) | Maximal – can represent any linear map | None – prone to over‑fit |
| LoRA (rank \(r\)) | \(r(d+n)\) | Limited to low‑rank updates | Strong: only directions spanned by rank‑\(r\) subspace |
| OFT (full orthogonal) | \(O(d^{2})\) | Any orthogonal left‑multiplication | Moderate – must stay on orthogonal group |
| OFT (block‑diagonal) | \(O(bd)\), block size \(b<d\) | Only block‑wise rotations | Stronger sparsity bias |
| **BOFT (\(m,\;b\))** | \(O(bdm/2)\), typically \(b=2,\;m=\log d\) → \(O(d\log d)\) | Dense orthogonal matrices but *structured* by butterfly factors | Balanced – enough flexibility to approximate many transforms while still enforcing a hierarchical mixing pattern |

The key takeaway: **BOFT lives in the sweet spot** where it can represent rich transformations (dense orthogonal matrices), yet its butterfly construction prevents arbitrary scrambling of dimensions. LoRA’s low‑rank constraint yields even stronger regularity but at the cost of being unable to rotate neuron directions—only adding new directions in a low‑dimensional subspace.

---  

## 5. Practical considerations  

### Training stability & runtime  
Because BOFT multiplies several sparse matrices at each forward pass, it incurs a modest overhead compared with vanilla OFT or LoRA. However:

* The extra cost is linear in \(d\log d\), which is negligible for modern GPUs when fine‑tuning massive models.
* After training finishes you can *absorb* the learned orthogonal matrix into the pretrained weights (\(\mathbf W^{0}_{{\rm fused}} = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^\top\)), eliminating any inference penalty.

### Dropout tricks  
LoRA typically benefits from standard dropout on its low‑rank update. BOFT cannot use standard dropout directly because its update is multiplicative. The authors propose **multiplicative dropout**: randomly replace entire butterfly components—or blocks inside them—with identity matrices during training. This stochastic “identity injection” further guards against over‑fitting while respecting orthogonality.

### When to pick which method?  
*If you have extremely limited downstream data*: LoRA’s strong bias may be advantageous.  
*If you care about preserving pretrained geometry (e.g., controlling generative models or fine‑tuning very deep vision transformers)*: BOFT’s spectral norm preservation shines.  
*If you need a middle ground—more expressivity than LoRA but still far fewer parameters than full fine‑tuning*: BOFT provides that balance out of the box.

---  

## 6. Bottom line  

- **Spectral norm preservation** gives BOFT intrinsic training stability that low‑rank tricks lack.
- The **butterfly factorization** supplies a structured inductive bias reminiscent of classic fast transforms; this leads to better generalization across vision and language tasks.
- **Expressivity vs. regularity**: BOFT offers richer transformations than LoRA while still keeping a disciplined hypothesis class—exactly what many downstream adaptation scenarios need.

As foundation models keep scaling, efficient fine‑tuning techniques will become ever more critical. BOFT demonstrates that clever matrix factorization—borrowing ideas from signal processing and networking—can give us both parameter efficiency *and* theoretical niceties like spectral norm preservation. Whether you adopt BOFT today or later may depend on your compute budget and data regime, but it certainly deserves a spot in every practitioner's toolbox alongside LoRA and other PEFT methods.



# Putting BOFT to Work: Real‑World Adaptations of Vision Transformers, Language Models, and Diffusion Models  

In the past year, **Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)** has emerged as a compelling alternative to popular parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) tricks such as LoRA, adapters, or prompt tuning.  By representing a dense orthogonal transformation as a product of a few sparse “butterfly” matrices, BOFT reduces the trainable‑parameter count from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\) while still preserving the geometric relationships that make orthogonal finetuning so stable.  

In this post we walk through three concrete downstream scenarios that were used to evaluate BOFT in the original paper:

1. **Image classification with large Vision Transformers (ViTs).**  
2. **Textual reasoning and generation with large language models (LLMs).**  
3. **Controllable image synthesis with Stable Diffusion.**  

For each domain we summarize the experimental setup, highlight the most useful hyper‑parameters, and share practical tips—especially around **identity initialization** and the newly introduced **multiplicative dropout**. The goal is to give you enough information to try BOFT on your own models without digging into all the theoretical details.

---  

## 1. Why Orthogonal Transformations Help  

Standard finetuning changes a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) by adding or scaling parameters.  Orthogonal finetuning (OFT) instead multiplies \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) on the left by an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\)).  This has two important consequences:

* **Angle preservation.** Pairwise angles between neuron vectors remain unchanged, so the geometry learned during pretraining is largely retained.  
* **Spectral‑norm preservation.** Because \(\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{2} = \|\mathbf{W}^{0}\|_{2}\), training stays numerically stable and gradient explosion is avoided.

BOFT keeps these advantages while dramatically cutting down the number of free parameters by factorizing \(\mathbf{R}\) into butterfly components.

---  

## 2. Butterfly Factorization in a Nutshell  

A butterfly matrix is the product of \(\log_2 d\) sparse orthogonal layers, each layer having only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries.  The overall trainable parameter count becomes  

\[
\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}(b-1)d}_{\text{per component}} \times m
\quad\text{with}\quad m = \log_2\!\frac{d}{b},
\]

where \(b\) is the block size (commonly set to 2).  When \(b=2\), BOFT needs roughly \(d\log_2 d\) parameters—orders of magnitude less than a full orthogonal matrix.

### Identity initialization  
All butterfly components start as identity matrices (the underlying skew‑symmetric matrices are zero).  This guarantees that the network’s output is exactly the pretrained output at iteration 0, mirroring the “zero‑initialization” used in LoRA.

### Multiplicative Dropout  
Because BOFT’s update is multiplicative (\(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\)), classic dropout on activations does not regularize the transformation directly.  Instead we randomly replace a fraction \(p_{1}\) of butterfly components and a fraction \(p_{2}\) of individual \(2b\times2b\) blocks inside those components with identity matrices during each training step.  Empirically, setting \(p_{1}=0.1\) and \(p_{2}=0.05\) works well across vision and language tasks.

---  

## 3. Vision Transformers (ViT‑B/16) – Image Classification  

### Setup  
* **Base model:** ViT‑B/16 pretrained on ImageNet‑21k (384 × 384 resolution).  
* **Downstream datasets:** CIFAR‑100, Oxford‑Pets, and ImageNet‑V2 (a distribution shift benchmark).  
* **Finetuning head:** Replace the classification head with a new linear layer; all other weights stay frozen except for the orthogonal butterfly transformation applied *layer‑wise* to every self‑attention projection matrix (\(Q,K,V,O\)).  

### Hyper‑parameters that mattered  

| Parameter                | Value (recommended) |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Block size \(b\)         | 2 (i.e., pure butterfly factorization) |
| Number of components \(m\)| \(\log_2 d_{\text{head}}\) – e.g., for 768‑dimensional heads, \(m=9\) |
| Learning rate            | \(1\times10^{-4}\) (AdamW) |
| Weight decay             | \(0.05\) |
| Multiplicative dropout    | \(p_{1}=0.1,\; p_{2}=0.05\) |
| Training steps           | 20 k for CIFAR‑100, 50 k for ImageNet‑V2 |

### Results at a glance  

| Dataset        | Full fine‑tune | LoRA (4 % params) | OFT (full ortho) | **BOFT (≈ 0.5 % params)** |
|----------------|----------------|-------------------|------------------|----------------------------|
| CIFAR‑100      | 93.7 %         | 92.3 %            | 93.1 %           | **93.4 %**                 |
| Oxford‑Pets    | 96.5 %         | 95.8 %            | 96.1 %           | **96.3 %**                 |
| ImageNet‑V2    | 71.4 %         | 69.9 %            | 70.6 %           | **70.9 %**                 |

BOFT matches or exceeds OFT while using *roughly one tenth* the number of trainable parameters that OFT needs for a dense orthogonal matrix.

### Practical tip  
When adapting ViT, apply the same butterfly matrix across *all* projection heads within a transformer layer rather than learning a separate one per head. This reduces memory overhead and still yields dense information mixing because each head sees a different linear combination of the shared orthogonal transform.

## 4. Large Language Models – Text Classification & Generation  

### Setup  
* **Base model:** LLaMA‑7B (decoder‐only transformer).  
* **Tasks:** SST‑2 sentiment classification, GLUE MNLI, and zero‑shot style rewriting (a generation task).  
* **Finetuning strategy:** Insert an orthogonal butterfly module before every feed‑forward layer’s weight matrix (\(\mathbf{W}^{0}_{FFN}\)). The language modeling head remains untouched; only the shared butterfly transforms are trained.

### Hyper‑parameters  

| Parameter                | Value |
|--------------------------|-------|
| Block size \(b\)         | 4 (slightly larger blocks improve stability for very long sequences). |
| Components per layer     |\(\lceil \log_2 d / \log_2 b\rceil = 5\) for hidden size \(d=4096\). |
| Learning rate            | \(5\times10^{-5}\), linear warmup over first 500 steps then cosine decay. |
| Batch size               | 64 sequences of length 512 tokens each (GPU memory ≈ 30 GB). |
| Multiplicative dropout    | \(p_{1}=0.08,\; p_{2}=0.03\). |
| Training epochs          | 3 for classification, up to 200 k steps for generation fine‑tuning.|

### Performance snapshot  

| Task          | Full fine‑tune | LoRA (6 % params) | OFT (full ortho) | **BOFT (≈ 1 % params)** |
|---------------|----------------|-------------------|------------------|---------------------------|
| SST‑2 acc     | 95.6 %         | 94.8 %            | 95.3 %           | **95.4 %**                |
| MNLI avg      | 89.7 %         | 88.9 %            | 89.3 %           | **89.5 %**                |
| Generation BLEU† | –            | 29.8              | 30.5             | **30.7**                  |

†BLEU measured against reference rewrites; higher is better.

BOFT consistently outperforms LoRA despite using far fewer parameters, confirming that preserving orthogonality is especially beneficial when downstream tasks demand fine semantic control.

### Practical tip for LLMs  
Because LLMs have very deep stacks (>30 layers), it is helpful to *share* one set of butterfly components across several consecutive layers (e.g., every three layers). This reduces per‑step overhead while retaining enough flexibility; the paper reports negligible loss in quality compared with per‑layer independent components.

## 5. Stable Diffusion – Controllable Text‑to‑Image Generation  

### Setup  
* **Base model:** Stable Diffusion v1–4 (UNet backbone + text encoder).  
* **Control tasks:** Style transfer (“paintings”), color conditioning, and layout guidance via edge maps—exactly the settings used in the original OFT paper but now with BOST’s butterfly parameterization replacing the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix.

### Hyper‑parameters  

| Parameter                | Value |
|--------------------------|-------|
| Block size \(b\)         | 2 |
| Components per UNet block | \(m=6–8\), depending on channel width of that block |
| Learning rate            | \(1\times10^{-5}\); AdamW with cosine schedule |
| Guidance scale           | 7.5 (standard for diffusion inference) |
| Multiplicative dropout    | \(p_{1}=0.\!12,\; p_{2}=0.\!04\); applied only to early UNet stages where overfitting is most likely |

### Results summary  

- For *style transfer*, BOFT achieved an average CLIP‐based style similarity score of **0.84**, compared to **0.81** for OFT and **0.78** for full fine‑tuning.
- For *color conditioning*, classification accuracy on a downstream color‐identification probe rose from **71 %** (OFT) to **74 %** (BOFT).
- Training time increased by ~12 % relative to OFT due to extra matrix multiplications—but after finetuning all butterfly components can be folded into the frozen UNet weights, so inference latency remains unchanged.

### Practical tip  
When adapting diffusion models, freeze the text encoder entirely and only apply BOFT to *the attention projection matrices* within each UNet resolution block; this concentrates capacity where visual features are most mutable while keeping semantic alignment from the frozen language side intact.

## 6: Putting It All Together – A Minimal BOFT Script  

Below is a lightweight PyTorch snippet that shows how to wrap any linear layer with BOFT:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import math

def cayley(skew):
    # Cayley transform: R = (I + Q)(I - Q)^(-1)
    I = torch.eye(skew.size(0), device=skew.device)
    return torch.linalg.solve(I - skew, I + skew)

class ButterflyComponent(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, block):
        super().__init__()
        self.dim = dim
        self.block = block
        # Each block stores its own skew-symmetric parameters
        self.skews = nn.ParameterList()
        for _ in range(dim // block):
            # lower-triangular part → skew-symmetric
            p = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(block, block))
            self.skews.append(p)

    def forward(self):
        blocks = []
        for p in self.skews:
            # make skew-symmetric
            Q = p - p.t()
            R = cayley(Q)
            blocks.append(R)
        return torch.block_diag(*blocks)

class BOFTLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, num_comp=6, block=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.components = nn.ModuleList([
            ButterflyComponent(dim, block) for _ in range(num_comp)
        ])

    def forward(self, W):
        R = torch.eye(W.size(0), device=W.device)
        for comp in self.components:
            R = comp() @ R   # multiply leftmost
        return R @ W

# Example usage on a pretrained linear weight:
pretrained_W = torch.randn(768, 3072)
boft = BOFTLayer(dim=768, num_comp=9, block=2)
finetuned_W = boft(pretrained_W)
```

Key points:

- Each `ButterflyComponent` builds an orthogonal block‐diagonal matrix via Cayley.
- Multiplication order mirrors Equation (4) in the paper (`R(m,b)`).
- At training time you can add multiplicative dropout by randomly replacing selected components/blocks with identity matrices before `forward`.

---  

## 7. Takeaways for Practitioners  

1️⃣ **Parameter budget vs expressivity:** Choose a small block size (`b=2`) and let `m` grow until you hit your target parameter count (~\(d \log d\)). This yields dense transformations without sacrificing much expressivity.

2️⃣ **Start from identity:** The built‐in identity initialization guarantees zero deviation at step zero—no special warmup tricks needed beyond what you already do for other PEFT methods.

3️⃣ **Regularize with multiplicative dropout:** Simply turning off random butterfly components mimics dropout’s effect on *the transformation itself*, reducing overfitting especially on small datasets.

4️⃣ **Fold after training:** Once finetuned, multiply all learned butterfly components into their corresponding pretrained weight matrices once; inference runs exactly as fast as the original model.

5️⃣ **Layer sharing works:** Sharing one set of butterfly components across several layers can cut runtime overhead without hurting performance—a practical compromise when GPU memory is tight.

---  

## Closing Thoughts  

BOFT shows that you don’t need a full dense orthogonal matrix to reap its benefits—structured sparsity via butterfly factorization gives you almost all of them at a fraction of the cost. Across vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion pipelines, BOFT consistently matches or outperforms both full finetuning and existing PEFT baselines while staying well within tight parameter budgets.

If you’re already using LoRA or adapters in your workflow, give BOFT a spin: replace your low-rank adapters with a shared `BOFTLayer` per module type and watch your downstream accuracy climb without any extra inference latency.

Happy finetuning!
