Chapter 7: # 7  Practical Implementation Details  

The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm was devised to eliminate the costly reinforcement learning loop that plagues traditional RLHF pipelines while retaining the same objective of maximizing a human‑consistent reward under a KL‑divergence constraint.  The theoretical derivations presented in Chapter 4 show that the optimal policy for a given β–regularized reward can be expressed directly in terms of the policy’s own log probabilities relative to a reference policy.  In practice, however, one must make a number of concrete engineering choices that can substantially affect training stability, final performance, and computational efficiency.  This chapter translates the theory into a step‑by‑step recipe: how to set the *β* hyperparameter, how to initialise the reference model, how to collect the preference dataset, and how to structure the training loop so that cross‑entropy optimisation converges reliably.

---

## Choosing β Based on Desired KL Tightness  

In DPO the trade‑off between following the reward signal and staying close to the reference policy is controlled by β (see Eq. 15).  Intuitively, a small β makes preference term dominate – the policy will become aggressive in reshaping its output distribution – while a large β pushes the policy to remain near π_ref, preserving diversity and reducing negative side effects such as hallucination or mode collapse.

1. **Why β Appears in Log‑Likelihood** – In Eq. 15 we see  
   \[
     \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[\log
     \sigma\!\bigl(\beta\,\Delta(y_w,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
   \]
   where  
   \[
     \Delta(y_w,y_l)=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
                    -\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)} .
   \]
   The scalar β rescales this log‑ratio; higher values make inactive pairs less influential because σ becomes sharper.

2. **Mapping Beta to Target KL** – For a given reference policy π_ref and current policy π_θ, Lemma 3 of Theory shows  
   \[
     \mathbb{D}_{KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta \;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr]
       = \beta\,(\mathbb{E}_{x}\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi_\theta}r^*(x,y) - H_\theta),
   \]
   where *H_θ* is an entropy term that cannot be explicitly set but typically increases as training progresses when β is small.  We therefore use empirical tuning:  
   *Start with β = 1.*  
   *After every 50–100 gradient updates, compute the average sequence‑level KL on a held‑out validation set.*  
   *If that KL is below half the desired threshold (e.g., ≤0.5), halve β; if it exceeds twice that threshold (≥2), double β.*  

3. **Practical Defaults** – Table 3 in Appendix C shows that for sentiment control on GPT‑2‑Large (≈=1–6B parameters) and for Reddit TL;DR summarization (≈GPT‑J), the sweet spot β∈[0.1, 5] produced KL values between 0.6 and 1.3 on validation prompts while still achieving high win rates (>60%). A common rule of thumb: choose β such that *β × expected reward shift* ≈ *desired KL penalty*. For many tasks this means using a modest β (≈0.5–1) with stronger penalties applied through temperature selection during generation.

## Reference Policy Initialization  

The reference distribution π_ref appears twice in DPO: as the baseline probability of each token sequence and inside every importance weight λ(x,y)=π_θ/π_ref. Discarding or mis‑specifying π_ref leads to two types of failure: (i) numerical instability in λ; (ii) runaway divergence from useful distribution mass.  

1. **Prefer SFT When Available**  
   The most robust choice is ⟨π_ref = π_SFT⟩, exactly as used in existing RLHF pipelines:  
   *SFT* is already constrained by a supervised loss on high‑quality data, ensuring that π_ref has realistic language modeling capacity and has seen many prompts from the downstream domain.
   
2. **When SFT Is Absent**  
   Some downstream tasks have no pre‑existing SFT model (e.g., new dialogue datasets).  In such cases we estimate π_ref by maximizing likelihood only on preferred responses:
   \[
     \pi_{\text{ref}}=\arg\max_{\pi}\;
       \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal D}\!\Bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\Bigr].
   \]
   This ‘pseudo SFT’ model concentrates mass on known good completions but still retains some generative flexibility because it is trained with cross‑entropy rather than arbitrary fine‑tuning objectives.
   
3. **Avoiding Symmetry Collapse** – If all completions come from humans’ manual typing rather than a pre‑trained LM, early sampling requires both directions of bias toward words that are present in human speech but not in your vocabulary size budget; clipping or smoothing of π_ref logits before computing λ helps maintain stable gradients:
   ```python
   log_probs_ref = softmax(ref_logits.clip(min=-10., max=10.))
   lambda_ratio = torch.exp(log_probs_new - log_probs_ref)
   ```
   
4. **Periodic Reinitialisation** – In long training runs one may observe drift of π_θ away from π_ref even though λ remains bounded because of frequent token cancellations (zero‑probability tokens). Periodically re‑compute λ using an updated version of π_ref fine‑tuned on recent μ preferred samples (e.g., every 200K steps) mitigates this drift without changing overall training dynamics.

## Sampling Strategy for Building Preference Dataset  

The quality and quantity of pairwise human preferences directly control DPO’s convergence speed and final alignment quality.

### Number of Samples Per Prompt

- For tasks requiring fine-grained ranking over multiple facets (e.g., safety vs helpfulness), it is beneficial to generate *N* ≥ 10 completions per prompt so that labelers can compare across many options.
- Simpler tasks (sentiment control) only require *N* = 2–4; more sampling reduces annotation time while still furnishing sufficient variability for gradient signals.
- Keep *N* consistent across prompts within one dataset to avoid bias in preference frequencies.

### Temperature During Generation

A higher temperature injects diversity but also noise; lower temperatures yield more deterministic completions:

| Sampling Temperature | Stylized Effect | Typical Use |
|---------------------|----------------|-------------|
| 0      | Greedy execution | Gather “best” candidates for high-confidence preferences |
| 0–0.25 | Moderately diverse | Balanced preference history |
| >0.25  | High diversity | Explore creative or risky settings |

Empirically we found \(T=0.\!25\) works well for both sentiment control and TL;DR summarization datasets when paired with a weak initial SFT LM; \(T=0.\!5\) was exploited only for out-of-distribution news summarization experiments where broader coverage was needed.

### Human Labeling Protocol

- Provide annotators with both completions side-by-side after passing them through an AMP modifier if necessary (to avoid length distortions).
- Use a forced-choice format (“Which completion better satisfies X?”). Randomly remove one option when annotators are indifferent.
- Compute inter-rater agreement; apply majority vote or Bayesian aggregation if necessary.

### Importance Weight Sanity Checks  

Equation (17) shows
\[
    w(x)=\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)).
\]
If you omit w(x)—the “unweighted” version—preliminary ablations reported loss growth and mode collapse within five epochs on GPT‑2-Large sentiment data (see Appendix Table U). Monitor λ over training:

```python
mean_lambda = torch.mean(lambda_ratio).item()
assert mean_lambda > epsilon && mean_lambda < big_constant
```
If λ saturates near zero or diverges past ~10⁶, reduce learning rate or increase ε clip bound.

## Training Schedules: Steps, Batch Sizes, Optimizer Choices

### Step Count & Early Stopping

DPO converges far faster than PPO because it does not require sampling from π_θ mid-training:

| Dataset | Best Performing Step Count |
|---------|----------------------------|
| Sentiment control | < 200k steps |
| TL;DR summarization | ~ 400k steps |
| Single-turn dialogue | ~ 600k steps |

Deploy an early-stopping criterion based on validation win rate improvement over last N checkpoints (e.g., N=15). A plateau indicates either optimal “β” has been reached or overfitting to preference noise.

### Batch Size & Sequence Length

Due to memory demands of large transformer models:

- Batch size per GPU = 16–32 sequences.
- Pad sequences to longest candidate within batch; use attention mask.
- Mixed precision training (`torch.float16`) reduces VRAM consumption without sacrificing stability.

### Optimizer & Learning Rate

AdamW with weight decay ≈ 0·01 is standard; schedule:

```python
# Pseudo-code
optimizer = AdamW(p.parameters(), lr=2e-5)
sched     = get_linear_schedule_with_warmup(
               optimizer,
               num_warmup_steps=5000,
               num_training_steps=max_steps)
```

Larger models benefit from slightly lower LR (~1e-5); smaller models at ~3e-5 produce faster convergence without overshooting due to higher gradients magnitude.

### Stabilizing Cross‑Entropy Training

1. **Gradient Clipping** – Clip at norm = 1 or use `torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_`.
2. **Label Smoothing** – PCR discourages overconfidence when learning initial distributions: assign target probability α/σ for wrong tokens.
3. **Learning Rate Decay** – Linear warmup followed by cosine decay suppresses overshoot late in training.
4. **Batch Normalization not used** – Transformers rely on LayerNorm which handles internal covariate shift adequately.
5. **Checkpoint Reloading** – Every epoch reload last checkpoint if loss spikes >20% relative change indicating possible numerical divergence.

## Common Pitfalls & How to Avoid Them  

| Pitfall | Root Cause | Fix |
|---------|------------|-----|
| Running DPO with an empty reference policy | No base distribution → λ blow-up | Initialize π_ref from SFT or pseudo‐SFT |
| Training with large batch sizes (>64) | VRAM spills causing out-of-memory charges leading to truncated gradients | Reduce batch size & enable gradient accumulation |
| Setting β too low (~<0·01) | Policy becomes too eager → huge KL drift & unstable λ values | Sweep β ≈ [0·05–1·] until estimated KL ~desired value |
| Ignoring token-level masking during loss computation | Softmax over padded positions biases gradients | Mask padded tokens explicitly when computing log probabilities |
| Using unweighted binary cross entropy instead of weighted w(x) | Mode collapse toward trivial solutions where all outputs become identical | Always include w(x) computed via Eq.(17); sanity check λ magnitude |

# Summary  

DPO turns an inherently sequential reinforcement problem into a supervised cross‑entropy task by leveraging an analytic mapping between rewards and policies under hard constraints imposed by human preferences expressed through Bradley-Terry comparisons. To get high quality results:

1️⃣ Choose β so that generated KL stays within task requirements, adjusting empirically via validation checkpoints.

2️⃣ Initialise π_ref from existing SFT whenever possible; otherwise fit it quickly on preferred completions only.

3️⃣ Build your preference dataset with adequate diversity per prompt (2–10 samples) at moderate temperatures (T≈0·25), ensuring you feed well‐labelled pairwise data into DPO.

4️⃣ Train with stable AdamW schedules, small batch sizes, mixed precision, gradient clipping, and close monitoring of importance weights λ throughout training.
