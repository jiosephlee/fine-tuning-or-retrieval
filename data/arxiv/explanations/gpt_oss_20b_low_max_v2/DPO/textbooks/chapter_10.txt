\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 10: # 10. Results – Real Preference Datasets (Summarization & Dialogue)

In this chapter we move beyond controlled toy experiments and present the empirical evidence that *Direct Preference Optimization* (DPO) can be applied to real‑world preference datasets, producing language models that outperform or match existing RLHF baselines while being far simpler to train. We focus on two canonical downstream tasks that are routinely fine‑tuned in the RLHF literature: (1) factual summarization of informal text, and (2) single‑turn dialogue completion.  The experiments use large unsupervised models—GPT‑2‑Large for sentiment, GPT‑J for summarization, and Pythia‑2.8B for dialogue—fine‑tuned with DPO from human preference data collected by prior work.  We reveal how DPO achieves higher *win rates* against strong baselines, how its learning dynamics unfold across training steps, and how robust its performance is to inference temperature.  When comparing against the “Best‑of‑$N$” sampling strategy—an off‑policy hedge that simply resamples many completions from the reference policy and picks the best according to a reward model—DPO comes remarkably close without incurring extra inference cost.

The chapter is organized so that each subtopic receives a full discourse: we motivate the task, describe how datasets are prepared, explain the evaluation metrics, and then present results in the form of tables and figures that we will refer to in subsequent chapters when discussing practical deployment or theoretical guarantees.

## 10.1 Summarization Results

### 10.1.1 Dataset and Preprocessing

The summarization task follows the Reddit TL;DR benchmark introduced by \citet{volske-etal-2017-tl}.  The dataset contains pairs \((x_i, y_i)\) where \(x_i\) is a forum post from /r/TLDR discussions and \(y_i\) is a concise summary produced by an expert human moderator.  To keep in line with prior RLHF work, we use a publicly available **preference collection** from \citet{stiennon2022learning}. There each example consists of a prompt \(x\), two completions \((y_{\mathrm{w}}, y_{\mathrm{l}})\) sampled from an SFT model, and a human label indicating which completion is preferable under quality criteria such as informativeness, brevity, and relevance.

Our training pipeline starts from a **supervised fine‑tuned (SFT)** GPT‑J model (the same checkpoint used by \citet{stiennon2022learning}) trained on the set of high‑quality Reddit summaries.  No further human labeling is required at this point; only the preference dataset above is needed for DPO.

### 10.1.2 Training Procedure

The DPO loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}\) (Equation 4 in the paper) is minimized over batch samples drawn uniformly from the preference dataset.  In practice we:

| Hyperparameter | Value | Rationale |
|-----------------|-------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| \(\beta\)       | \(0.05\) | Keeps KL divergence controlled while still allowing distribution shift |
| Optimizer       | AdamW   | Stable training with weight decay |
| Batch size      | \(512\) | Sufficient to cover diverse examples per step |
| LR              | \(3\times10^{-5}\) | Standard for GPT‑J fine‑tuning |

The reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is initialized as the SFT model.  We keep it fixed during optimization; only \(\pi_{\theta}\)—our DPO policy—is updated.

### 10.1.3 Evaluation Metric: Win Rate

Unlike controlled sentiment experiments where ground‐truth reward can be evaluated directly, summarization lacks an analytic objective function.  The benchmark therefore compares a candidate summary \(y^*\) against a *baseline* summary \(y^{\text{base}}\).  The baseline may be:

- The original SFT summary,
- A non–RLHF variant such as “best of $N$” sampling,
- Or a human reference.

Winner determination uses GPT‑4 to prompt something like:

> “Which of the following two summaries better captures the key points of this forum post?”  
> *Compare including length/quality criteria.*

The proportion of selections favoring \(y^*\) defines the *win rate*. We repeat this comparison over at least several thousand test prompts; statistical significance is measured via binomial confidence intervals.

### 10.1.4 Results Across Temperature

Temperature controls Gibbs sampling’s randomness: lower values generate more deterministic outputs but risk mode collapse; higher values generate diverse but potentially noisy outputs.

Figure 10a (see below) plots win rates for three policies—SFT, PPO, and DPO—evaluated at temperatures \(\tau =0.0,\,0.25,\dots ,1.0\).  
Key observations:

| Policy   | Optimum Temperature | Win Rate @ Optimum |
|----------|---------------------|--------------------|
| SFT      | —                   | ~47 %              |
| PPO      | \(0.0\)             | ~57 %              |
| DPO      | \(0\!-\!0.25\)       | **61 %**           |

*Figure 10a: Win rates vs temperature*  
Economic? This simplified description still captures quantitative differences while focusing reader on temperature robustness.

Interpretation:

- **SFT** serves as a floor; it has no bias toward user preferences beyond what was present in its training data.
- **PPO** performs well but its win rate drops sharply beyond \(\tau=0.\!25\); higher temperatures expose mode collapse or excessive diversity that does not align with preferences.
- **DPO** maintains a high win rate even at \(\tau=0.\!5\); its objective penalizes divergence from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), preventing unwanted shifts while still rewarding preferred completions.

### 10.1.5 Loss Curves Over Training Steps

Figure 10b shows on a log scale how both cross‐entropy loss (Equation 4) and *KL divergence* between policy and reference evolve over training steps for DPO versus PPO (with reward modeling). Despite PPO reaching comparable losses early on, it requires *hundred times more environment interactions* (total parameter updates multiplied by sampling cost).

Moreover, the KL term in the PPO loss tends to dominate after mid-training if not properly clipped; consequently PPO reports unstable gradients leading to occasional loss spikes that attest to common actor–critic instabilities discussed in §9 of this book.

DPO’s gradient remains well‑conditioned: because we backpropagate directly through logits rather than through sampled trajectories, training becomes stable without extensive hyperparameter tuning.

### 10.1.6 Comparison With Best–of–$N$

“Best–of–$N$” training repeatedly samples many generations from the *reference* policy for each prompt during test time
and picks whichever one receives highest score under an externally learned reward function.
It achieves superior win rates but at quadratic runtime cost: inference must translate many times per request.

Table 10a compares win rates and per‐extraction latency (in milliseconds per token).

| Method          | Win Rate (\%) | Latency (ms/?) |
|-----------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Best–of–128     | **64**        | ~2000           |
| DPO             | **61**        | ~55             |
| PPO             | ~57           | ~190            |

DPO almost closes the gap with minimal extra compute—a desirable property for production AI systems that must serve hundreds of thousands of requests daily.

## 10.2 Dialogue Results

### 10.2.1 Defining Preferred Completions

For single-turn dialogue we utilize the Anthropic Helpful & Harmless (HH) dataset as described in §8 of this book.
Each entry comprises an opening query \(x\), two different responses \((y_{\mathrm{w}}, y_{\mathrm{l}})\), and a binary label indicating which response better satisfies user intent while staying safe.
Unlike summarization where both completions are sampled from an SFT policy,
in HH their raw sentences were generated by an unknown large model,
and preference labels were gathered by human annotators who read each pair alongside context.

To build an SFT reference:
- We first collected all human‐selected completions \(y_{\mathrm{w}}\).
- These labels formed a supervised dataset
which we used to fine‑tune Pythia‑2\.8B on “/*preferred response*/”.
This new model serves as our reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
It has larger Pareto advantage over random initialization because training data already reflects human safety requirements.

### 10.2.2 DPO Pipeline Versus PPO Baseline

In order to justify comparisons across algorithms we train three variants:

1. **Preferred–FT** – Fine tune Pythia on only preferred responses.
2. **PPO** – Standard RLHF: learn reward from preference data via Bradley‐Terry MLE,
   then optimize policy using PPO with tuned \(\beta=0.\!05\).
3. **DPO** – Direct Preference Optimization using Equation 4 with same
   $\beta$, no reward learning or RL loops.

All three start from **Pythia‑Preferred–FT**, ensuring identical baseline distributions.

#### Hyperparameters

Critically, PPO required careful learning rate tuning (\(lr=6\times10^{-6}\)) and clipping coefficient adjustment (\(c=0.\!2\)) so that algorithms did not fall into low entropy collapse.
In contrast DPO uses off-the-shelf AdamW (\(lr=3\times10^{-5}\)) without extra regularizers,
showing extreme simplicity with *no hyperparameter sweeps* needed beyond $\beta$ alone.

### 10.2.3 Evaluation Strategy

We evaluate using GPT‑4 again:
the “approve” style prompt compares output w.r.t annotated helpfulness & harmlessness ranking.
Evaluation procedure:
- For each test query pick:
    - Outcome of *base* Pythia,
    - Outcome after Preferred–FT,
    - Outcome after PPO,
    - Outcome after DPO.
- Count how often each candidate wins against *base* yielding *win rate*.
- Compute ±95 % confidence intervals via Wilson interval due to binomial nature.

Because HP/HH dataset contains ~170k dialogues but our tuned policies were trained on only ~100k labeled pairs,
the resulting win rates include both data density effects and algorithmic efficiency terms.

#### Result Summary

Table 10b reports win rates across multiple sampling temperatures (\(\tau =0.\!25,\dots ,1.\)) .

| Method          | Optimal Temperature | Win Rate |
|-----------------|---------------------|----------|
| Preferred–FT    | —                   | ~51 %    |
| PPO             | \(0.\!25\)           | ~56 %    |
| DPO             | \(0.\!25\)           | **58 %** |

Notably:
- Even with limited preference data size,
DPO surpasses PPO while using far fewer environment interactions:
PPO required ≈\(3\times10^6\) token generations
while DPO used ≈\(6\times10^4\).
- A *Best–of–128* baseline achieved ~59 %, confirming near-optimality
but incurring >20× inference time overheads relative to DPO at comparable temperature.

Additionally Figure 10c plots loss curves:
dotted purple trace shows KL divergence remaining close to zero for DPO undershoot
whereas PPO’s KL oscillates due to clip boundaries being violated when input distribution distribution shifts between batches—a known instability identified in earlier work (§9).
