Chapter 6: # 6. Theoretical Properties of Direct Preference Optimization

In this chapter we establish the formal theoretical foundation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  We show that the re‑parameterization used by DPO is *complete* for the class of reward functions that give rise to the Bradley‑Terry (and, more generally, Plackett–Luce) preference model.  That is, every reward function compatible with the observed human preferences can be written in the form
\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} ,
\]
where $\pi_{\theta}$ is some policy over completions and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference policy (typically an SFT model).  This result removes any concern that DPO might exclude useful reward functions simply because it does not learn an explicit value network.

We also discuss how this property implies stability advantages over standard actor–critic approaches such as PPO.  In particular, we view the actor–critic objective as a form of *control‑as‑inference* and observe that the missing normalizing term in many implementations plays exactly the role of a baseline or value function.  Because this term is implicit in DPO’s re‑parameterization, its gradient contains no high‑variance estimate; consequently, DPO sidesteps one of the main sources of instability in RLHF.

The chapter is organized as follows.  First we formalize the notion of *equivalence classes* of reward functions and prove that all members in a class produce identical preference distributions and optimal policies under a KL‑constrained objective.  Next we prove Theorem 1, establishing that the functional family used by DPO spans every equivalence class arising from a Bradley–Terry model.  Finally, we connect these results to actor–critic stability by comparing DPO’s objective with control‑as‑inference.

## 6.1 Equivalence Classes of Reward Functions

When we pose human preferences as a Bradley–Terry model,
\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
    = \sigma\!\Bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\Bigr) ,
\]
the underlying reward function $r^{*}$ need not be unique. Two reward functions that differ by a *context‑only* scalar function
\[
f(x)\;:\;\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}\!\to \mathbb{R}
\]
will induce exactly the same preference distribution: for any pair $(y_{1},y_{2})$,
\[
r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\implies
r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})= r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2}) .
\]
Consequently
\[
\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr)
   = \sigma\!\bigl(r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2})\bigr) .
\]

We can encode this property with an equivalence relation:

> **Definition 6.1 (Equivalence).**  
> Two reward functions $r$ and $r'$ are equivalent iff there exists a function $f$ such that
> \[
> r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\qquad \forall (x,y).
> \]

This relation partitions $\mathcal R$, the set of all conceivable reward functions, into disjoint *equivalence classes*.  
Because preferences depend only on differences of rewards, the entire equivalence class gives rise to a single preference distribution.

### Lemma 6.2 – Preference Invariance

> **Lemma 6.2.**  
> If $r$ and $r'$ belong to the same equivalence class, then for every prompt $x$
> \[
> p^{*}_{\,r}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
> = p^{*}_{\,r'}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr).
> \]

*Sketch.* By plugging $r'=r-f(x)$ into the Bradley–Terry formula one sees that $f(x)$ cancels out.

### Lemma 6.3 – Policy Invariance under KL Constraint

> **Lemma 6.3.**  
> For any two rewards $r,r'$ in the same equivalence class and any reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$,
> the optimal solutions $\pi^{*}_{\,r}$ and $\pi^{*}_{\,r'}$ to
> \[
> \max_{\pi}\Bigl[\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim \pi}[\, r(x,y)\, ]
> - \beta\,D_{\!KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr)\Bigr]
> \]
> are identical.

*Proof idea.* The KL term depends only on $\pi$, not on $f(x)$.
The objective can be rewritten as
$\mathbb{E}_{y}[\, r(x,y)-f(x)+f(x)\,]$
but $f(x)$ is constant with respect to $\pi$, so it does not alter the maximizer.
Therefore every member of an equivalence class yields precisely one optimal policy.

These two lemmas guarantee that working with a single representative from each equivalence class suffices for both modeling preferences and deriving optimal policies.

## 6.2 DPO’s Re‑parameterization Is Complete

Recall that solving the KL‑constrained maximization problem (Eq.~(5) in the paper) yields an optimal policy of the form
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;
    \pi_{r}(y|x)
    = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
      \exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Bigr)
    }
    \label{eq:policy_opt}
\end{equation}
with partition function $Z(x)=
   \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
        \exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y))$.
Solving Eq.~(\ref{eq:policy_opt}) for $r$ gives
\begin{equation}
    r(x,y)=
        \beta\,\log \frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
        + \beta\,\log Z(x) .
    \label{eq:reward_from_policy}
\end{equation}

Equation~(\ref{eq:reward_from_policy}) is key: **any** reward function can be expressed in terms of its induced optimal policy together with a normalizing constant depending only on context $x$.  
Therefore if we replace $\pi_r$ with an arbitrary parametrized policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and drop $\log Z(x)$—which will vanish when differences are taken inside a Bradley–Terry likelihood—we obtain a valid reward representation:
\[
\rho_{\theta}(x,y)
    := \beta\,\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} .
    \tag{6.1}
\]
