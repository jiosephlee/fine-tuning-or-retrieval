### Does DPO require any form of reward scaling or normalization?
Question:
The traditional RLHF pipeline often normalizes reward values to have zero mean so that equivalence classes do not distort learning. In DPO we bypass explicit rewards, yet we still compute an implicit reward as $r_	heta(x,y)=etaigl(	extstyle	ext{log}rac{oldsymbol{	heta}(y|x)}{oldsymbol{m ref}(y|x)}igr)$. Does this implicit reward suffer from scale ambiguities? If so, how does DPO ensure a comparable scale to what is achieved after reward normalisation in standard RLHF?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the classical RL‑HF pipeline the reward model is trained as a *scalar* function of a trajectory.  Because the Bradley–Terry likelihood depends only on differences of rewards, an arbitrary constant can be added to every reward without changing the fitted probabilities.  For this reason former works normalise the learned rewards (e.g., zero‑mean per‑example) before they are fed to the policy optimiser, so that a large or small absolute scale cannot dominate the KL penalty in the objective.

DPO bypasses an explicit reward model altogether, but it still contains an *implicit* reward,
\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\,
      \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
\tag{1}
\]
Two things make this implicit reward free from the same scale ambiguity:

1. **The KL constraint fixes the scale.**  
   The optimal policy for a constrained optimisation problem
   \[
   \max_\pi \;\mathbb{E}[r(x,y)]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,||\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})
   \]
   (Equation (5) in the paper) is given in closed form by  
   \[
   \pi_r(y\mid x)\;\propto\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)
                     \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr).
   \tag{2}
   \]
   If we replace \(r(x,y)\) by \(\hat r_\theta(x,y)\) from (1), the exponential factor becomes
   \[
   \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\hat r_\theta(x,y)\bigr)
     = \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)} ,
   \]
   which cancels with the reference term and leaves  
   \[
   \pi_r(y\mid x)=\pi_\theta(y\mid x).
   \]
   Thus *any* additive constant that could appear in an external reward model would simply multiply both sides of (2) and be absorbed into the partition function \(Z(x)\).  In other words, scale changes do not alter the induced optimal policy because they are rendered irrelevant by the KL regulariser.

2. **The hyper‑parameter \(\beta\) serves as a scaling knob.**  
   The coefficient in front of (1) is exactly the inverse temperature that appears in (2).  Choosing a different value of \(\beta\) changes how sharply the policy prefers higher‑reward completions relative to the reference.  In practice we set \(\beta\) (e.g., \(0.05,0.1,\dots,5\)) once per experiment and keep it fixed during training; no per‑example normalisation is required.

Because DPO directly optimises a cross‑entropy loss (Equation (7) in the paper) with respect to
the *log‑ratio* of probabilities and does not involve any separate baseline or normalisation step, it inherits all of the benefits of an implicitly scaled reward:

- **No extra tuning** – once \(\beta\) is chosen, subsequent training stages use exactly that scale.
- **Stability** – as shown in Section 4, DPO avoids high‑variance policy gradients that can arise when estimating a separate normalising term (the logarithm of the partition function \(Z(x)\)) in standard RLHF.
- **Consistency with RLHF** – Theorem 1 proves that every class of rewards compatible with Bradley–Terry can be represented by some choice of policy via (1).  Therefore the implicit reward in DPO has precisely the same expressive power as any explicitly normalised reward used in RLHF.

In short, DPO eliminates the need for explicit reward normalisation because its objective already embeds a fixed scale thanks to the KL constraint and its closed‑form reparameterisation of rewards.  The single hyper‑parameter \(\beta\) controls that scale globally, ensuring that learned policies remain comparable to those obtained from RLHF with manually normalised rewards.
