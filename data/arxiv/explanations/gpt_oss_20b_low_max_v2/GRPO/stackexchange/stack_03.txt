\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does DeepSeekMath ensure that benchmark contamination is avoided when filtering extracted web pages?
Question:
The authors filter out any 10‑gram substring that appears in GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, or AGIEval. What happens if a benchmark problem contains a unique phrase fewer than 10 grams but appears multiple times across other dumps? Also, how are short prompts (<10 grams) handled differently?
Answer:
**How the DeepSeekMath pipeline keeps its pre‑training corpus free of benchmark leakage**

The authors are very explicit about the de‑contamination rule in § 4.1:

> *“Any text segment containing a **10‑gram string that matches exactly with any sub‑string from the evaluation benchmarks is removed from our math training corpus.”*  
> *For benchmark texts that are shorter than 10 grams but have at least 3 grams, we employ **exact matching** to filter out contaminated web pages.*

So the procedure is a two‑stage string‑matching filter:

1. **Long‑chunks (≥ 10 words)** – an exact sliding‑window comparison is performed against every benchmark excerpt (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval).  
   If any 10‑gram chunk in a candidate page appears verbatim in a benchmark, that whole page is discarded.  

2. **Short‑chunks (3–9 words)** – many benchmark problems are very short; some might even be only a single question or answer clause.  
   For these, the same idea applies: any occurrence of that short fragment in a page triggers removal, using plain equality rather than an n‑gram window.

Because the check is *exact*, it catches every instance where a page contains *any* overlap with a benchmark excerpt, regardless of how many times that excerpt shows up elsewhere.

---

### What if a “unique” problem contains a phrase shorter than 10 words and appears elsewhere?

Suppose a GSM8K problem contains the uncommon clause “*solve for the quadratic equation*”.  
That clause is 4 words long.

1. During de‑contamination the algorithm first assembles every sub‑string up to length 9 from all benchmark documents into a lookup set.
2. The page in question is tokenised and scanned word by word; each contiguous window of length 3–9 words is compared against that set.
3. When the 4‑word window *solve for the quadratic equation* hits the set, the page fails the test and is dropped **regardless of how many other copies of that clause exist elsewhere**.

So even if the same small phrase shows up in dozens of other scraped dumps, it still causes elimination because it matches an exact fragment from a benchmark document.

---

### Why not strip pages based only on full premises?

The authors recognize that interfering with “short prompts” separately is not enough: without this additional step many literally copied or paraphrased queries could slip through because they never hit a 10‑gram boundary. The short‑phrase rule ensures that any *identical* text segment—even as short as three words—cannot survive.

This two‑layer strategy aligns with best practices described by others (e.g., OpenAI’s decontamination guidelines) and guarantees that:

- The pre‑training training data cannot contain verbatim copies of public math benchmarks,
- The risk of inadvertent memorization or overfitting to benchmark answers is minimized,
- The model’s subsequent fine‑tuning and RL stages start from genuinely novel content.

In short: **any match (exact equality) with a benchmark fragment of any length ≥ 3 words triggers removal**, so even unique short phrases that appear repeatedly elsewhere do not survive in the DeepSeekMath corpus.
