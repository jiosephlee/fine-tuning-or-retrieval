# Energy Code — How BitNet b1.58 Saves Energy Like Magic

## The big picture

Large Language Models (LLMs) are the engines that power chatbots, recommendation systems, and scientific discovery tools.  A single forward pass through a 70‑billion‑parameter LLM can burn tens of megajoules of electricity if you run it on conventional GPUs or ASICs that perform floating‑point multiplications and additions.  The paper “The Era of 1‑bit LLMs” shows that a *tiny* change—forcing every weight to be one of \{-1, 0, +1\} and all activations to fit in 8 bits—cuts the arithmetic energy of matrix multiplication by roughly **71×**.  In this post we unpack the arithmetic‑energy calculation, illustrate how the savings emerge, and point out why this matters for data‐center operators and green‑AI advocates.

---

## 1. Why matrix multiplication matters

A Transformer layer performs two linear transformations:

\[
\mathbf{y} \;=\; \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} 
\quad+\quad 
\text{attention}\;\text{(another linear step)} .
\]

For an \(n\times m\) sparse weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) and an input vector \(\mathbf{x}\) of length \(m\), the naïve floating‑point implementation needs

\[
2 \times n \times m
\]

floating‑point **multiplication–addition (MAC)** operations:  
\(m\) multiplications per output neuron and \(m-1\) additions to accumulate them.

In a full‑precision FP16 LLM each MAC costs the same amount of electrical energy (ignoring memory traffic).  When you replace the MAC with a **ternary weight** \(-1\,|\,0\,|\,+1\), every multiplication turns into either

* a *zero* (no compute),
* a *sign flip* (cheap bitwise XOR/W–OR cheap), or
* an *identity* (push the incoming value forward unchanged).

In hardware, this is effectively an **int8 addition** between two signed values where the “multiplier” is a literal \(-1,\,0,\,+1\).  The key observation from the paper’s energy model is that an int8 addition consumes **≈ 0.3 pJ**, while an FP16 MAC needs about **20–30 pJ** on state‑of‑the art 7 nm processes.

## 2. Building the energy model

The paper follows the analytic cost model from papers like *EnergyCost* and *PokeBnn*.  For each linear layer we compute:

| Operation | Energy per operation (pJ) | Operation count |
|----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| FP16 add | 5 | \(n\times m - n\) |
| FP16 mult | 20 | \(n\times m\) |
| **int8 add** (ternary weight case) | **0.3** | \(n\times m - n_{\text{zero}}\) |
| **zero multiplications** | negligible | \(n_{\text{zero}}\) |

Here \(n_{\text{zero}}\) is the number of zero entries in the ternary weight matrix; experimentally it’s roughly \(≈20~\%\) of all weights for typical pruning/quantizing regimes.

Putting this together:

- **FP16 layer:**  
  \[
  E_{\text{FP16}} = (n\times m - n)\times5 + n\times m\times20
                 = n\times m\times25 - n\times5
  \]

- **BitNet b1.58 layer:**  
  \[
  E_{\text{b1.58}} = (n\times m - n_{\text{zero}})\times0.3
                  = n\times m\times0.3 - n_{\text{zero}}\times0.3 .
  \]

Because \(n_{\text{zero}}\ll n\times m\), we can approximate

\[
E_{\text{b1.58}} \approx n\times m\times0.3 .
\]

Thus,

\[
\frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{b1.58}}}
    \approx \frac{25}{0.3}
    \approx 83 .
\]

In practice hardware overheads, memory traffic, and bus delays reduce the ratio somewhat, landing at an empirical **~70×** savings as reported in Figure 4(B) of the paper.

## 3. Visualizing a single multiplication

Let’s imagine a toy matrix multiplication where \(n=m=1024\).  
The FP16 version needs:

```
Multiplications:   1024 × 1024 = 1 048 576
Additions:         ≈   –      = ~    ‑≈   ‑
Total ops:          ≈   –      = ~   ‑‑
```

Every operation expends ~25 pJ → ≈ 26 mJ per forward pass of this layer.

Now force every weight to be ternary:

```
Effective multiplications: none   (sign flips only)
Additions with ternary weights: ≈   – 
```

The same number of int8 additions now cost only ~0.3 pJ each → ≈ 0.3 mJ total: a factor of >70 reduction.

## 4. Scaling up to large LLMs

When you multiply by model size:

- A **7B** BitNet b1.58 layer uses about **2/3** as many bits as its FP16 counterpart because each weight is encoded with just three possible values.
- Training a **70B** BitNet b1.58 pushes all those savings into bulk: every acceleration unit can handle far fewer operations per clock tick.

Energy measurements from the paper show:

| Model Size | FP16 energy per token | b1.58 energy per token | Speed‑up vs memory efficiency |
|-----------|-----------------------|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| 3B        |  x                    |  x/80                 | 2½× faster |
| 70B       |  x                    |  x/150                | 4× faster |

These figures translate into reduced electricity bills for data centers—roughly a half on inference alone—and longer battery life for edge deployments.

## 5. Real world implications

### a) Data center cost

Assume a typical inference workload generates **10⁸ tokens per day** on a machine with peak power draw of **900 W** for full‑precision LLMs.

- Total daily energy:  
  \(900~W ×24~h =21\,600~Wh =21.6~kWh.\)

With BitNet b1.58’s ~70× graphene factor:

- Energy becomes ≈ \(300~Wh ≈0.3~kWh.\)
- Cost at $0.10/Wh drops from $2.16 to $0­30—about a **90% cut!**

When scaled over thousands of machines this equals hundreds of megawatt–hours saved annually—a significant contribution toward carbon targets and greener AI research.

### b) Edge / mobile devices

On mobile CPUs, floating‑point units are expensive in silicon area and power budget; their extra clock cycles swamp battery life every day.

By replacing FP16 operations with int8 additions you:

* Drop temperature rise so that background processes run longer.
* Double or triple payload capacity within existing battery constraints.
* Enable “offline” generation for privacy-sensitive apps that previously suffered latency penalties.

### c) New hardware opportunities

Because BitNet b1.58 only needs addition circuits for its core tensor ops, vendors can build low-area accelerators—say an “LPU” chip on which millions are stacked—without FPU or high‐bandwidth BW requirements.

- Power density shrinks.
- Heat dissipation becomes linear instead of logarithmic.
- Chips can be pinned to personalized nodes without interconnect overheads (greatly simplifying multi‐tenant data center design).

---

## Wrap‑up

An arithmetic view shows exactly why forcing LLM weights into three values (\{-1, 0, +1\}) yields such dramatic savings: you literally replace dozens of joules worth of multipliers with cheap bitwise sign flips and additions.

The energy model in *The Era of 1-bit LLMs* backs this intuition numerically: each linear layer drops from ~25 pJ/operation to ~0.3 pJ/operation – an ~70× drop for matrix multiplication alone—and even larger overall speed–memory gains because matrix multiplies dominate Transformer workloads.

For anyone running thousands or millions of inferences per day, or looking to deploy large language models on battery‐powered edge devices, BitNet b1.58 offers more than a novelty—it opens doors to enterprise‑scale green AI while keeping performance on par with full‑precision models.”
