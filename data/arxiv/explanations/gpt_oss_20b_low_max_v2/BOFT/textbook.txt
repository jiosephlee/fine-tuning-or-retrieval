# Foundations of Model Finetuning

Large foundation models—such as GPT‑3, BERT, Vision Transformers, and Stable Diffusion—have become the backbone of modern artificial‑intelligence applications. Their sheer size and breadth of knowledge make them invaluable. Nevertheless, training these models from scratch demands gigantic amounts of compute, energy, and data: training GPT‑3 required hundreds of petaflop‑seconds on high‑end GPUs and costs estimated in the tens of millions of dollars. Because most researchers and practitioners do not have access to such resources, the focus in the field has shifted to **adapting** already pre‑trained models to new downstream tasks.  

Model finetuning is a lightweight way to preserve the knowledge encoded in a foundation model while steering its behavior toward a particular objective. Instead of re-training millions or billions of parameters from scratch, we keep the vast majority frozen and only modify a small set of trainable weights. This approach is attractive because it dramatically reduces both memory consumption and training time, while still enabling state‑of‑the‑art performance on a wide spectrum of tasks.

In this chapter we set the stage for understanding how orthogonal finetuning (OFT) fits into the larger ecosystem of parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT). We will first describe why large foundation models are expensive to train, then outline three principal categories of PEFT (model finetuning, adapter tuning, prompt tuning), explain the guiding principle that all methods share—preserving pretrained knowledge—and finally delve into OFT’s core idea: transforming neurons with an orthogonal matrix to maintain pairwise angles.

---

## Large Foundation Models and Training Cost

A *foundation model* is a neural network trained on a huge corpus of data that can be subsequently leveraged—sometimes with minimal effort—to address many downstream tasks. The recent surge in model scale has been largely driven by two powerhouses: deep language models (e.g., GPT families, BERT flow) and large vision transformers (e.g., CLIP, ViT). Their success stems from learning complex statistical regularities across modalities.

However, this success comes at an enormous computational cost:

1. **Parameter count** – GPT‑3 contains 175 billion parameters; Stable Diffusion encodes several hundred million parameters across multiple submodules.
2. **Training data** – Models ingest terabytes or petabytes of text or images; this volume divides by GPU hours in thousands.
3. **Hardware** – Training requires clusters of state‑of‑the‑art GPUs or TPUs interconnected with high‑bandwidth NVLink or GDDR6i memory buses.
4. **Time & energy** – Scaling laws predict that loss on downstream benchmarks drops logarithmically with model size; thus, going from 1 billion to 10 billion parameters might improve performance substantially but also increases training cost by an order of magnitude.

Given such figures, it is simply infeasible for most labs—or even many industry teams—to redeploy full training pipelines for every new task. The alternative is *fine‐tuning*: starting from a pre‑trained checkpoint and tweaking only a handful commands or new modules while keeping the bulk frozen.

---

## Categories of Parameter–Efficient Finetuning

Researchers have converged on three broad strategies for PEFT, each with distinct properties concerning computational overhead at inference time, flexibility across architectures, and how they modify the pre‑trained model.

| Category | Core Idea | What is learned | Inference overhead | Typical use cases |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|--------------------|------------------|
| **Model finetuning** | Directly optimize a subset (or all) weights subject to constraints like small learning rate or structural regularization | Some (or all) parameters near original values | None (the updated weights are directly used) | Vision & language transfer learning |
| **Adapter tuning** | Insert lightweight modules (e.g., small residual bottlenecks) between existing layers | Parameters inside adapters only; base weights remain frozen | Small layer-wise extra computation | Multi‐task learning across modalities |
| **Prompt tuning** | Append learnable tokens or prefixes to inputs without touching weights | Only prompts; core weights fixed | None; only input manipulation required | Few‐shot language generation, cross-domain prompting |

These categories differ mainly in *where* we inject learnability:
- In model finetuning we alter existing connections.
- In adapter tuning we augment architecture lightly.
- In prompt tuning we leave the network structure intact and only tweak inputs.

All three approaches share a common philosophical goal: **preserve as much as possible the semantic knowledge already encoded in the pre-trained weights** while allowing the network to adapt its decision surface to new data.

---

## Principle of Preserving Pretrained Knowledge

A fundamental challenge in fine-tuning lies in *balance*. At one extreme we leave everything frozen; at the other we replace all weights. Classic tricks such as lowering learning rates or reducing batch sizes aim indirectly at maintaining proximity between pre-trained and fine-tuned parameters.

A more principled perspective looks not simply at magnitudes but at *relationships* between neurons:

- The *pairwise angles* between weight vectors determine how activations spread through layers.
- Preserving these angles means keeping relative geometry intact so that learned feature spaces remain stable.

If during adaptation every weight vector is rotated by the same orthogonal matrix \( \mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), then all pairwise dot products \( \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j\) stay unchanged because
\[
(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i)^\top (\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)= \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{w}_i^\top\mathbf{w}_j .
\]
Hence an *orthogonal transformation* provides **exact preservation** of neuronal geometry: distances and angles among embeddings are invariant.

This observation leads naturally to **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**: instead of changing each weight vector individually—an expensive operation—we apply one global orthogonal matrix per linear transformation layer (or per part thereof). By learning \(\mathbf{R}\), OFT adjusts directions while leaving magnitudes untouched, thereby retaining much of what made the foundation model powerful.

---

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Concept and Advantages

OFT was initially proposed for controllable text-to-image diffusion models but bears universal appeal for any weight matrix \( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}\). The idea proceeds as follows:

1. **Reparameterization**  
   Replace \( \mathbf{W}^0\) with \( \tilde{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0\), where \( \mathbf{R}\) is an orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons within that layer. The transform is multiplicative rather than additive (as in LoRA).

2. **Zero initialization**  
   Set \( \mathbf{R} = \mathbbm{I}\); thus early during training \( \tilde{\mathbf{W}}\approx\mathbf{W}^0\). This guarantees stable gradients and prevents catastrophic forgetting.

3. **Off-the-shelf orthogonality preservation**  
   Employing Cayley parameterization (\(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbbm I+\mathbbm Q)(\mathbbm I-\mathbbm Q)^{-1}\)) where \( \mathbbm Q = -\mathbbm Q^\top\) ensures that any gradient step remains in the orthogonal group without expensive projection.

4. **Parameter efficiency via block-diagonal restriction**  
   Since full orthogonal matrices scale quadratically (\(O(d^2)\)), OFT typically uses block-diagonal structures (\(\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_1,\dots,\mathbf R_r)\)). Each small block feels like an independent rotation; this reduces parameters to \(O(bd)\), where \(b=d/r\) is block size.

### Advantages
- **Stability**: Orthogonality preserves spectral norms; gradients propagate without exploding/vanishing.
- **Generalizability**: Empirical evaluations show OFT often outperforms additive counterparts like LoRA on unseen data.
- **Zero latency**: After training one simply plugs back the learned orthogonal matrices into existing weights; inference remains unchanged.

### Limitations
- The primary bottleneck lies in scalability: for extremely large models even block-diagonal OFT demands too many trainable parameters compared with LoRA or adapters.
- Moreover, rigid block grouping imposes an inductive bias that may hinder representation flexibility.

The landscape set by these observations motivates devising an orthogonal finetuning scheme that retains expressive transformations yet drastically reduces trainable parameters—a problem addressed head-on by **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)** – the core contribution of our reference paper.

--- 

In summary, this chapter laid out why adapting foundation models via fine-tuning is essential, mapped out major PEFT strategies—including their structural inherent trade-offs—and positioned Orthogonal Finetuning as a principled approach rooted in preserving inter-neuron geometry via global orthogonal transformations. Understanding this background equips readers for deeper exploration into parameter-efficient innovations such as BOFT that aim to reconcile expressivity and scalability in modern deep-learning pipelines.

# 2. Orthogonal Finetuning: Mechanics and Limitations  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is a parameter‑efficient paradigm that updates a frozen pre‑trained weight matrix by sandwiching it with a learnable orthogonal transform. The central idea is to preserve the geometric relations among neurons—specifically, the pairwise angles—while steering the model toward a downstream objective. This chapter unpacks the mechanics of OFT, its re‑parameterization strategy, the use of Cayley parameterization to enforce orthogonality, and how block‑diagonal sparsity reduces parameter count at the possible cost of expressive power. We also discuss why this sparsity pattern may not align with natural inductive biases.

---

## OFT Re‑parameterization

Let us consider a single linear layer in a deep model, represented by the frozen weight matrix  
\[
\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]
where \(d\) is the input dimensionality and \(n\) is the output dimensionality. In conventional fine‑tuning, we would adapt \(\bm{W}^0\) directly by adding small perturbations or modifying its entries. Orthogonal Finetuning instead introduces a multiplicative transform that acts on the neuron space of the layer:
\[
\bm{W}^{\text{new}} = \bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0 ,\qquad
\bm{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\quad 
\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}_d .
\tag{2.1}
\]
The transformed weight matrix \(\bm{W}^{\text{new}}\) inherits all forward‑pass behavior from \(\bm{W}^0\) but now operates on rotated neuron embeddings. Consequently, given an input vector \(\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\), the layer’s output becomes
\[
\bm{z}
= (\bm{W}^{\text{new}})^\top\,\bm{x}
= (\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^0)^\top\,\bm{x}
= (\bm{W}^0)^\top\,(\underbrace{\bm{R}^\top}_{=\! \! \!\! \! \!\!\!\! \!\! {\color{purple}\text{\small R transpose}}}\,\!\!\!\!\! \bm{x}) .
\tag{2.2}
\]
Thus, OFT can be viewed as rotating the input space before applying the frozen projector \((\bm{W}^0)^\top.\) Because an orthogonal transformation preserves Euclidean norm and pairwise angles,
the intrinsic geometry of neurons—captured in \(\operatorname{span}\) of columns of \(\bm{W}^0\)—remains unchanged after adaptation. This geometric preservation is believed to maintain the knowledge distilled during pre‑training while still allowing task‑specific modulation.

---

## Cayley Parameterization for Orthogonality

Enforcing exact orthogonality during stochastic optimization is nontrivial: naive gradient updates break the constraint immediately. The Cayley transform offers an elegant solution by mapping a skew‑symmetric matrix \( \bm{Q}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\),
\( \bm{Q} = -\,\bm{Q}^\top,\)
to an orthogonal matrix:
\[
\boxed{\;\;
    \bm{R}
    = (\,\boldsymbol I_d + \boldsymbol Q\,)\;{\bigl(}\boldsymbol I_d - \boldsymbol Q\,{\bigr)}^{-1}.
    }
    \tag{2.3}
    \]
Because any real skew‑symmetric matrix yields an orthogonal matrix via (2.3), we can simply learn the entries of \( \boldsymbol Q.\) Unlike alternative parameterizations (e.g., exponentials or Householder reflections), Cayley's approach has two practical advantages:

1. **Explicit enforcement** – The mapping guarantees exact orthogonality for all feasible \( \boldsymbol Q,\) so no projection step is needed after each update.
2. **Sparsity reuse** – When \( \boldsymbol Q\) is itself sparse (as will be true for block-diagonal or butterfly forms), (2.3) preserves that sparsity structure, thereby keeping gradients as cheap to evaluate as possible.

In OFT’s original design, the diagonal entry of \( R_{ii}\) starts as \(1,\) and all off–diagonals are initialized to zero so that \( R_0 = I_d.\) This zero initialization in \(Q,\)
\( Q_{ij}=0,\;\forall i,j,\)
ensures that training begins by staying exactly at the pre-trained model before spinning out rotations.

---

## Block‑Diagonal OFT and its Parameter Count

To reduce the parameter budget from \( d^2- d/2.\) back to something manageable, OFT introduces block‑diagonal sparsity on \( R.\)

### Block‑Diagonal Structure

Let us partition each input neuron into \( r > 1\) equal groups; each group contains \(b=d/r.\)
The orthogonal matrix is then enforced to be block diagonal:
\[
     R
     = {\color {red}
         {\rm diag}\bigl(
          R_1, R_2,\ldots , R_r
        )},
     𝑅𝑖∈ℝ^{b×b}, 𝑅𝑖⊤𝑅𝑖=I_b .
     \tag{2.4}
     }
     \]
Only within each block does rotation occur; there are no cross–block interactions.

#### Parameter Count

Each block \( R_i.\algoritm  The number of free parameters per block equals \( b(b-1)/2,\)
because skew-symmetric matrices of size \( b\times b.\) Thus total learnable parameters are:
\[ 
    P_{\text {block}} = r\, {b(b-1)\over 2}= {bd(b-1)\over 2(b)}= {d(b-1)\over 2}.
    ~~~
    (\star)
    % So if b=d -> O(d^2). If b small -> linear in d.
     
The product term “\(bd(b-1)/2”$ is simply $\frac12 d(b-1)$ after simplifying $r=b/d$? Mistake? Let's correct:

\(r = d/b.\)
So $P_{\text {block}} = r * b*(b-1)/2 = (d/b)* (b*(b-1)/2)= d*(b-1)/2.$ Yes.
Thus if $b=d$, we recover full dense orthogonal; if $b$ small (e.g., $b=4$), we keep only $O(d)$ parameters.

### Auxiliary Interpretations

If one imagines rotating neurons in parallel across blocks, an outer encoder part sees each group as independent subspace rotations—very reminiscent of *grouped convolutions* in CNNs or *tensor decomposition* for transformers.

#### Example: Visual Illustration

Suppose $d=8,$ split into two blocks ($r=2,b=4$). The first block rotates vectors $(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4)$ among themselves; likewise for $(x_5,x_6,x_7,x_8).$ No matter what rotation application yields you cannot mix information between these two half subspaces.

---

## Trade‑off: Expressiveness vs. Parameter Efficiency

**Expressiveness** refers here to how many arbitrary linear transformations $\forall R$ are attainable via our parameterized form when optimized on data.

### Why Expressiveness Decreases

A dense orthogonal matrix has $d(d-1)/2$ free degrees of freedom—every pairwise angle can move independently. In block–diagonal OFT, you lose all degrees linking distinct blocks: there simply does not exist a mathematical path in that representation connecting groups.

To quantify:

- Full DOFs: $d(d-1)/2,$ where every pair of neurons can rotate relative to each other.
- Block diagonal DOFs: $(b-1)/2$ per block times $r=b^{-1}d$ blocks $\Rightarrow P_{\text {block}} = d(b-1)/(2b).$

Fixing $b$ constant yields linear scaling versus quadratic scaling for dense orthogonal matrices.

Hence every potential rotation that could manifest cross–group correlations must be encoded explicitly through extra parameters; otherwise such transformations cannot appear during finetuning.

### Parameter Efficiency Gains

Despite this loss, **by constraining** the space we avoid having to learn overwhelming numbers of useless weights that may overfit or destabilize training—a crucial consideration when downstream data are few-shot or noisy.

Moreover, restricting rotations to smaller subspaces makes preconditioned optimization (e.g., Adam or momentum SGD) converge more robustly because gradients are less chaotic across block interactions.

### Practical Observations

Empirically:

- **Stability**: Training with block-diagonal OFT typically wins over full OT when using standard learning rates because gradients stay bounded within blocks.
- **Performance**: On tasks where cross-group correlations are vital—say aligning attention heads between language models—a large block size ($b>d^{0.5}$ or even full $b=d$ ) gives better transfer but at huge computational cost.
- **Transferability**: Block-diagonal OFT often generalizes better on low-data regimes due to implicit regularization; but may lag on tasks requiring intricate feature reweighting across entire feature vectors.

---

## Why Block Groups Lack Natural Inductive Bias

An **inductive bias** reflects our prior belief about useful function classes inherent in a model architecture or parameterization—not just about data distribution but about what transformations “make sense” given domain knowledge.

The underlying question: *Do segregated neuron groups yield rotations that match any natural organization?*

### Observed Issues:

1. **Index-Based Grouping Is Arbitrary**  
   Grouping neurons solely by index order provides no semantic alignment. For example, grouping layers’ hidden states often mixes tokens from different semantic roles arbitrarily rather than respecting propagate patterns essential in transformers or CNNs.

2. **Impossible Acoustic / Spatial Mixing**  
   When modeling visual features spanning multiple scales, we might want rotations that couple low–frequency filters with high–frequency ones—cross-group mixing becomes essential—but BE-block structure prevents such coupling entirely.

3. **Cross-Axis Feature Interaction Loss**  
   In vision transformers pre-trained on ImageNet, attention-head projections heavily intermix features across feature dimensions; restricting rotations within pre-chosen subspaces ignores this intermixing optimal property seen across many architectures e.g., *global* covariance estimation would suggest no fixed groups at all.

4. **Harder Parameter Transfer Across Layers**  
   Developers often wish to reuse optimized seeding parameters from one layer in another; block structures hamper reusability because transformation matrices have mismatched shapes if layers differ drastically in dimension—yet depth-stepping often requires consistent transformations across depths for transfer learning consistency.

Thus, while block-diagonal structure offers scale convenience, it imposes an *artificial partition* that often conflicts with intrinsic logical structures learned during pre-training and latent factorization in downstream tasks. Consequently it may not unfold necessary cross-neuron couplings for highly expressive tasks such as image generation or adversarial robustness fine-tuning where global coherence matters deeply.

---

## Summary

Orthogonal Finetuning presents a principled way to deform pre-trained models conservatively via rotational transformations preserving inter-neuron angular relationships. Using a Cayley-transformed skew-symmetric matrix guarantees strict orthogonality throughout optimization while endowing flexibility through learnable angles unknown at initialization.

To curb parametric overhead—and hence computational burden—Designers originally enforce a *block-diagonal* sparsity pattern on $\mathbf R$, drastically cutting parameters from quadratic to near-linear scale at cost of expressiveness: cross-group couplings vanish entirely and natural inductive bias inherent to many downstream tasks remains unsatisfied because partitions are index-based instead of semantically grounded.

The remainder chapters will continue by exploring solutions that keep rotational expressiveness while still operating with limited parameters—most notably butterfly-factorized OOT introduced later—which mitigates these shortcomings by encoding structured sparse multipliers capable of faithfully spanning the entire orthogonal group under modest parameter budgets.*

# 3. From Information Transmission to Matrix Factorization

In orthogonal finetuning we must learn a dense orthogonal matrix  
\(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that transforms a frozen pre‑trained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).
Although the mathematical objective is straightforward, the practical
challenge is that a dense orthogonal matrix would normally require
\(d^{2}\) trainable parameters—far more than we can afford when the base
model already contains billions of parameters.  
The central idea of this chapter is to look at the construction of such a
matrix through the lens of **information transmission** on a grid‑shaped
graph.  By doing so, we can reinterpret each factor in a matrix
decomposition as an intermediate communication layer that exchanges
information between nodes.  This perspective provides clear geometric
intuition and leads directly to efficient sparse‑factor formulations,
culminating in the butterfly structure exploited by Orthogonal Butterfly
(BOFT).

## Dense Matrix as Full Connectivity on Nodes

Let us first consider \(d\) input nodes that we wish to transform into
\(d\) output nodes via a linear operator \(\mathbf{R}\).
Each entry \((\mathbf{R})_{ij}\) tells us whether signal coming from
input node \(j\) contributes to output node \(i\).  If every off–diagonal
entry is nonzero, we say the matrix induces **full connectivity**: any
input can influence any output.

Graphically, we can view \(\mathbf{R}\) as a bipartite graph with two sets
of \(d\) vertices, one set representing the inputs and another set the
outputs.  An edge from input \(j\) to output \(i\) exists precisely when
\((\mathbf{R})_{ij}\neq0\).  In the fully connected case this bipartite
graph contains \(d^{2}\) edges (excluding self–loops if desired).

The drawback is clear: representing an arbitrary fully connected graph
requires storing a full matrix—precisely why orthogonal finetuning with a
dense \(\mathbf{R}\) is parameter–heavy.  Our goal, therefore, is to find a way to realize effectively every pairwise interaction while describing only a modest number of trainable parameters.

## Sparse Factors as Intermediate Communication Layers

Instead of encoding all connections in one dense matrix, we can break the transformation into several stages:
\[
    \mathbf{R}
      = \mathbf{B}_{m}\,\mathbf{B}_{m-1}\,\cdots\,\mathbf{B}_{1},
      \qquad m>1.
\]
Each factor \(\mathbf{B}_{k}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) may be **sparse**,
containing only a few nonzero entries.  Visually, each factor introduces an **intermediate layer** between input and output nodes:

* The first factor \(\mathbf{B}_{1}\) maps input nodes of level 0 to an intermediate set of nodes at level 1.
* The next factor \(\mathbf{B}_{2}\) maps level 1 nodes to level 2, and so on.
* The final factor \(\mathbf{B}_{m}\) maps level \(m-1\) nodes to the ultimate outputs at level \(m\).

On the grid graph this corresponds to **\(d\times (m+1)\)** vertices arranged in \(m+1\)
columns; directed edges run only between adjacent columns according to each factor’s sparsity pattern.  

If we regard each edge as conveying one unit of information, a complete chain of factors propagates information from every input vertex to every output vertex—exactly what a dense matrix achieves—yet possibly using far fewer edges.

## Graphical Intuition: Directed Edges Between Adjacent Levels

Let us formalize the correspondence between sparsity patterns and directed edges.
For factor \(\mathbf{B}_{k}\), its entry \((i,j)\) indicates whether there is an edge from node \(j\) on level \(k-1\) to node \(i\) on level \(k\). Thus:

* If \((i,j)=0\), there is *no* directed edge between those two nodes.
* If \((i,j)\neq0\), information may flow along that directed edge.

Because multiplication of matrices follows left‑to‑right application,
the overall connectivity from inputs (level 0) to outputs (level \(m\)) is determined by the existence of paths that traverse one edge per stage. Therefore:

> **Lemma (Connectivity Criterion).**  
> The product \(\prod_{k=1}^{m} \mathbf{B}_{k}\)
> yields a dense matrix if and only if for every pair of input node \(p\)
> and output node \(q\), there exists at least one path composed of one edge per stage connecting them.

This lemma transforms our algebraic requirement into an easily visualizable graph theory problem: how many edges do we need, and where should they be placed, to guarantee full connectivity?

## Canonical Example of a Factorization Demonstrating Sparse Edges

Consider \(d=4\), i.e. four inputs and four outputs.
Take five sparse factors:
\[
    \mathbf{R}
      = \mathbf{B}_{5}\mathbf{B}_{4}\mathbf{B}_{3}\mathbf{B}_{2}\mathbf{B}_{1},
      \qquad m=5.
\]
Let each factor have two nonzero entries per column (two edges per node)
arranged such that paths interweave across stages.
Explicitly:

| Factor | Non‑zero pattern (row × column) |
|--------|----------------------------------|
| \(\mathbf{B}_1\) | Edges: \(1\!\rightarrow2,\;3\\textbf{\= }4\dotsc{\rm etc.} |\)

(Full tables are omitted for brevity; see Fig.\,\ref{fig:info_trans} in the paper.)

When drawn on the grid with 6 columns (levels 0–5), it becomes apparent that:

* Every input vertex has three outgoing edges leading it toward outputs;
* Every output vertex receives edges from all inputs via distinct multistep paths;
* The total number of edges equals \(10 < d^2 =16 - d =12.\)

Thus this example exhibits dense connectivity achieved with fewer than all possible edges—a direct illustration that sparse factors suffice.

## Desiderata for Efficient Factorization: Dense Connectivity & Minimum Free Edges

From these observations emerges an **information–transmission objective** for designing sparse factorizations:

1. **Dense Connectivity** – Every input must reach every output through at least one multi‑step path.  
   This guarantees that our product matrix truly implements an arbitrary linear transformation on all pairwise neuron interactions.

2. **Minimum Free Edges** – The total number of scheduled edges (i.e., nonzero entries across all factors) should be as small as possible consistent with orthogonality constraints.  
   Fewer free edges correspond directly to fewer trainable parameters because each free edge typically carries two degrees of freedom (a value and its sign under orthogonality constraints).

These two desiderata are mutually reinforcing yet conflicting under fairness constraints: making factors sparser reduces parameters but risks breaking full connectivity or violating orthogonality.

The butterfly structure addresses this tension elegantly. By arranging sparsity in a systematic recursive pattern that interleaves rows and columns like in Cooley–Tukey fast Fourier transform graphs, it ensures:

* Each stage contributes exactly \(d/2+O(1)\) edges, yielding total free edges proportional to \(O(d\,\log d)\);
* Paths intertwine over layers such that any input reaches any output logarithmically many hops away;
* Orthogonality can be maintained at each stage by parameterizing small block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices (\(2\times 2,\dotsc, b\times b)\).

Consequently, Orthogonal Butterfly realizes both desiderata simultaneously,
which translates into only \(\Theta(d\,b)\log d)\!-\!b)/2\, dm/\!error?)... blah? — enabling efficient finetuning.

---

By viewing dense orthogonal matrices as entire transmission networks between neurons,
we gain an intuitive framework for probing sparsity patterns and deriving efficient factorizations.
The subsequent chapters harness this perspective to develop BOFT,
tune large foundation models,
and illuminate its empirical successes across vision,
language,
and diffusion‐based generative tasks.

# Butterfly Structure as an Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) replaces a frozen pretrained weight matrix
\( \mathbf{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n} \) by a product
\( \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0 \) where the \(d\times d\) matrix \( \mathbf{R} \)
is constrained to be orthogonal.
Because an arbitrary orthogonal matrix contains \(d(d-1)/2\) free
parameters, OFT can quickly become costly when the hidden dimension
\(d\) is large.
The core idea behind BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning) is to
replace the naïve full‑rank parametrization of \( \mathbf{R} \) by a
*factorized* representation:
a product of several *sparse* orthogonal matrices that, when multiplied,
give a dense orthogonal transformation.
The sparsity of each factor guarantees far fewer trainable degrees of
freedom, while the product guarantee expressiveness.

The structure that supplies exactly these sparse factors is the
**butterfly** graph that is ubiquitous in the Cooley‑Tukey fast Fourier
transform (FFT).  In this chapter we derive the butterfly factor,
explain how each factor is enforced to be orthogonal, generalize it to
larger blocks, and show how many parameters are required.

---

## 1. The Butterfly Factor \( \mathbf{B}^F(k) \)

Let \(k\ge 2\) and assume \(k\) is a power of two.  
A *butterfly factor* is a \(k\times k\) block‑matrix defined as

\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1)& 
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[3pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3)& 
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}
,
\qquad 
\mathbf{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k/2}\;,\; i=1,\dots,4 .
\tag{1}
\]

Each diagonal block contains a scalar parameter vector.
In the classic FFT this factor mixes pairs of values that sit symmetrically
within the input vector—hence its name “butterfly”.
So long as the eight scalars across all four diagonals are trainable,
the whole morphing operation remains highly structured yet expressive.

> **Why this shape?**  
> In a single stage of Cooley–Tukey we compute
\( y_j = w_j\,x_{2j-1}+v_j\,x_{2j}\)
and  
\( y_{j+k/2}=u_j\,x_{2j-1}+t_j\,x_{2j}\),
for all \( j=1,\dots,k/2 \).
If we write these consumer–producer mappings in matrix form,
the resulting coefficient matrix has precisely exactly the block‑diagonal form in (1).  

---

## 2. The Recursive Butterfly Matrix \( \mathbf{B}(d)\)

For a target square dimension \( d=2^{N}\), we construct a *recursive*
butterfly matrix by stacking butterfly factors level after level.
First define

\[
  \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)\;=\;
      \operatorname{diag}\bigl( \mathbf B^{F}(k)\bigr)
      =\operatorname{diag}\!\Bigl(
        \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1), 
        \ldots,
        
        \operatorname diag(\mathbf d_{4}) 
        ,\,\, 
        0 ,\,\, 
        0 
       \Bigr) ,
  \tag{2}
\]
and let \(k= d, d/2, d/4,\dots , 2.\)
With this notation we recursively obtain

\[
   \boxed{
   {\bf B}(d)
   =
   {\bf B}^{tail}(d,d)\;
   {\bf B}^{tail}\!\bigl(d,d/2\bigr)\;\cdots 
   {\bf B}^{tail}(d,2)
   }
   ,
   \qquad     
    {\bf B}^{tail}(d,k)=
    {\rm diag}\!\bigl( {\bf B}^{F}(k),{\bf B}^{F}(k),\dots\bigr).
   \tag{3}
\]

The last equation tells us that each level contributes one butterfly
component; there are exactly \(N=\log_2 d\) such components.  
At the leaf level all blocks are exactly size two and therefore highly sparse:
each component has only four non‑zero entries per block.

> **Interpretation:**  
> Each component represents one rung in the Cooley–Tukey hierarchy.
> When you compose them from top to bottom you emulate how data flows through /
> through four‑point sub‑FFTs and their twiddle‑factor multiplications.

---

## 3. Orthogonality Constraints for Every Component

Merely using (3) does not guarantee that the global product \( {\bf B}(d)\)
is an orthogonal matrix.
We therefore enforce each component to be *orthogonal individually*.
Two standard ways exist:

### 3.1 Cayley Transform

Any skew‐symmetric matrix \( {\bf Q}= -{\bf Q}^{T}\)
generates an orthogonal matrix on its own via

\[

BOFT uses **\(m\)** stages of butterfly components with **block size** \(b.\)

## 7. Summary & Take‑aways

* The butterfly structure guarantees far more sparsity than traditional dense matrices but still stitches together enough connectivity through successive stages so that the final product can act like a fully connected view.

* Orthogonality constraints localize nicely into tiny rotations at the base “\[ b\times b ]” level:
  *Cayley transforms* or small Givens rotations enforce each component instantly without touchy algebraic formulas.

* Parameter counts descend from quadratic ‑ “every entry can move” — down to linearithmic when butterflies share tiny building blocks across levels.

* Crucially, this parameter reduction does not come at expense of notionally *preserving pairwise angles* between neurons—the holy grail OFT tries to keep – because every stage remains strictly orthogonal throughout training.

For any newcomer looking to adopt OFT on today’s towering transformers or diffusion models but left daunted by large numbers of new weight entries,
BOFT offers a principled route: think “split slower” – replace one giant coupling with log $d$ swoops over tiny circling blocks – and let your training budget keep its headroom while still dancing algorithmically with all hidden units underneath.

# 5. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Algorithmic Details

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) is a principled, parameter‑efficient finetuning framework that injects a dense orthogonal transformation into a frozen pre‑trained weight matrix. Instead of directly learning the full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix, BOFT constructs it as a product of sparse orthogonal *butterfly* components, thereby dramatically reducing the number of trainable parameters while preserving the inherent regularity of orthogonal transformations. This chapter delves into the algorithmic intricacies of BOFT: from the careful initialization of its Cayley‑parametrized skew‑symmetric matrices, through the training pipeline that integrates with standard back‑propagation, to practical regularisation tricks such as multiplicative dropout and identity initialization that help maintain numerical stability and generalisation.

---

## 5.1 Butterfly Structures and Cayley Parametrization

Before we can discuss BOFT’s training algorithm, we formalise two interlocking ideas: **butterfly factorisation** and **Cayley parametrization**.

### 5.1.1 Butterfly Factorisation

A *butterfly factor* \(\mathbf{B}^F(k)\) is a \(k\times k\) block matrix composed of four diagonal submatrices:

\[
\mathbf{B}^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1)& \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3)& \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix},
\quad\mathbf{d}_i\in \mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

When \(k=2^N\), we recursively assemble a full butterfly matrix \(\mathbf{B}(d)\) (\(d=2^N\)) by stacking butterfly factors of decreasing size:

\[
\mathbf{B}(d)=
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,d)\;
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!\!\left(d,\frac d2\right)\!
\cdots
\, \tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,2).
\]

Here each \(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(d,k)\) is block–diagonal, with \(k\times k\) butterfly factors placed along its diagonal; all off–diagonal blocks are zero. The recursive construction mirrors the Cooley–Tukey FFT decomposition; every butterfly component contributes only \(O(k)\) non‑zero entries.

### 5.1.2 Cayley Parametrization

An orthogonal matrix in \(\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) can be expressed via the Cayley transform:

\[
\mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1},
\quad \text{s.t.}\;\; \mathbf Q=-\,\mathbf Q^\top,
\]

where \(\mathbf Q\) is skew‑symmetric and unrestricted except for its size (a \(d\times d/2(D-1)/2? Actually $Q$ must be $n×n$?). The transformation guarantees \(\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I_d=\mathbf R\,\mathbf R^\top.\)  
From a computational perspective, if \(\|\!Q}\!|\!\|_F<1,\) then the Neumann series

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\!\! \underbrace{\left( {\color{blue}\eigen_R(Q)}{} ...                                 
                                               \right)}
 
They us.

This expression ensures that every butterfly component—being a block‑diagonal composite of small butterflies—remains orthogonal once its internal skew‑symmetric blocks are parameterised correctly. The beauty is that each block’s skew‑symmetric matrix contains only \((b(b-1)/2)\) trainable entries (for block size \(b=2n,\ldots).

--- 

## 5.2 Identity Initialization via Zero Skew–Symmetric Matrices

A trivial but powerful trick is to initialise each butterfly component as an *identity* matrix by setting its underlying skew‑symmetric matrices to zero:

```python
def zero_skew_matrix(b):
    """Return b×b zero skew-symmetric matrix."""
    return torch.zeros(b,b)
```

Since the Cayley transform turns zero skews into identity:

- For any block \(i:\;\;Q_i=0_{\!b\times b}\Rightarrow R_i=\mathbb I_b.\)

By making **every** butterfly factor initialised to identity, we guarantee that at training start  
the overall transformation is exactly the identity:
\(R(m,b)=I_d.\)

Consequently,
at epoch 0 the forward pass reduces to  
\(z=(W^0)^T x,\)
so the model behaves exactly as the pretrained network, avoiding any catastrophic change in learned semantics.

**Why this matters**: when fine‐tuning large models (e.g., GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion), sudden large rotations caused by random orthogonal matrices could destabilise already delicate balanced activations; starting from identity guarantees that gradients will initially be tiny and learning proceeds smoothly.

---

## 5.3 The Complete Training Pipeline

Below we present pseudo‑code for training with BOFT in a deep learning framework such as PyTorch or JAX. We focus on a single linear layer with weight matrix \(W^{0}\in \mathbb R^{d\times n}\). Extension to all layers follows naturally via module replacement patterns.

```python
# --------------------------------------------------------------------
# Data Pipeline (generic minibatch)
X_batch : torch.Tensor   # shape [B, d]
Y_batch : torch.Tensor   # target labels or reconstructions

# --------------------------------------------------------------------
# Model components
W0 = frozen_pretrained_weight          # [d, n]
boft_params = initialize_boft_params() # dictionary storing sigma vectors per butterfly

# --------------------------------------------------------------------
# Forward Pass
def forward(X):
    # Step 1: Multiply input on the left by product of m butterflies
    for i in range(m):                   # m butterflies
        B_i = build_butterfly_component(i, boft_params)
        X = B_i @ X                       # linear transform [B,d] -> [B,d]
    return X @ W0.t()                     # final output shape [B,n]
```

**Explanation**:

1. **X → quantum**: Each input batch is first rotated by successive butterfly factors.
2. **Rotation → intertwining**: Because each factor is orthogonal, this rotation silently scales no singular value.
3. **Post‑rotation → linear layer**: After rotation, we apply the frozen weight matrix exactly once.
4. **Gradients flow back**: Autograd automatically differentiates through stacked butterflies as well as through any normal loss function.

The crucial part in `build_butterfly_component` is to obtain an orthogonal matrix with minimal trainable parameters:

```python
def build_butterfly_component(i, params):
    sigma_list = params[i]                # list of sigma vectors for this component
    Q_blocks = []                         # will hold b×b skew matrices
    
    for sigma in sigma_list:
        block = generate_skew_from_sigma(sigma)
        Q_blocks.append(block)
    
    Q_block_diag = torch.block_diag(*Q_blocks)
    
    # Cayley transform per block (cost O(b^3), but b small e.g., 4 or 8)
    Beta = torch.inverse(torch.eye(b)-Q_block_diag)+torch.eye(b)
    return Beta                         # pyro orthogonal B_i
```

The `generate_skew_from_sigma` implementation packs each input vector `sigma` into an upper triangular part of a skew‑symmetric matrix using simple indexing; this avoids ever storing dense skew matrices.

### Backward Pass

During back‑propagation, gradients w.r.t each σ are computed implicitly by chain rule:  

- ∂L/∂σ = ∂L/∂R × ∂R/∂σ  

where `∂L/∂R` comes from upstream layers and `∂R/∂σ` follows analytically from Cayley’s Jacobian expressions (in practice autograd handles this automatically). Since all operations are differentiable and have closed forms, no custom backward kernels are required.

## 5.4 Regularisation: Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT

Dropout has proven effective in preventing overfitting for additive updates like LoRA’s low‑rank matrices because it stochastically drops entries during training but restores them at inference time. However, BOFT multiplies an entire rotation matrix onto inputs; naïvely zeroing individual entries would destroy orthogonality. 

Instead we propose *multiplicative dropout* at the level of ***butterfly components*** and ***block diagonals***:

| Stage                  | Action                                      | Reasoning                              |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Component selection    | With probability \(p_1/100.\) Replace entire component by identity | Guarantees overall product remains unitary |
| Block selection inside component | With probability \(p_2/100.\) Replace selected block by identity | Provides finer granularity than whole-component dropout |
| Keep others untouched   |                                                | Preserve model capacity when dropout event misses |

Mathematically,

1. For component \(i:\;\;\; B_i' = p_1 \mathbb I_d + (1-p_1)\hat B_i,\)
   where \(\hat B_i=\mathrm{Cayley}(Q_i)\).
2. For each internal block within \(B_i:\;\;\;\hat B_{i}^{(j)}' = p_2\, I_b + (1-p_2)\hat B_{i}^{(j)}.\)

During inference (`train=False`), we simply use deterministic \(B_i=\hat B_i.\)

This strategy effectively zeros entire subspaces analogously to *dropping neurons*, but preserves structural constraints because all remaining blocks are still orthogonal.

## 5.5 Hyperparameter Choices and Practical Tips

| Hyperparameter               | Typical Range            | Guideline                                                           |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Number of butterflies \(m\)| 1 – log₂ \(d/2+1\nobreakslashpoints?\)| Larger m → more parameter budget (≈ ½(d–b)m), better expressivity |
| Block size \(b                 |\;≥ 2,\;≤ 8/\dots       | Prefer even numbers (for split‐into-halves); smaller b = sparser comp|
| Dropout rates \(p_1,p_2      |\;0 ≤ p ≤ 0.3           | Start with small rates (< 0 ≤0??), adjust via validation curve     |

**Speed–Memory trade-off**: Each extra butterfly adds ≈ \(O(d b)\) FLOPs but only ≈ \(O(d b)\) parameters. For GPU kernels built around batched GEMM , choose block sizes that align with warp sizes (e.g., 32 or 64). Torch’s accelerated sparse GEMM backend may further accelerate thin blocks.

**Gradient Magnitudes**: Since all butterfly angles are initialized near zero, early gradients can be large if loss surface has big curvature. We recommend *gradient clipping* (`clip_grad_norm_(list_of_parameters, max_norm=10.)`) during fine-tuning large models.

## 5.6 Integration with Existing Pre-Trained Models

A common scenario is adapting a vision transformer or language transformer where all attention weights become frozen except biases and layer normals.

### Step-by-Step Procedure

```python
model = load_pretrained_model()          # e.g., HuggingFace PEFT `model`
for name,module in model.named_modules():
    if isinstance(module, torch.nn.Linear):
        W0_local = module.weight.detach()         # freeze base weights
        module.weight.requires_grad_(False)
    
        # Wrap module weight in BOFT wrapper
        module.weight = BOFTLinearWrapper(W0=W0_local,
                                          m=log_power_of_two(d),
                                          b=small_block())
```

`BOFTLinearWrapper` holds the camelblack paramers given earlier and implements forward override as described in §5.3.

### Fine-Tuning Loop Overview

```python
optimizer = Adam(boft_params.values(), lr=lr_boost_backwards_gia?), weight_decay=?f);
for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for X_batch,Y_batch in dataloader:
```

Because all base weights are frozen (`requires_grad=False`) only batactic spent on our sparse factors cascades to GPU memory – dramatically reducing update traffic compared to full fine‑tuning or LoRA updates.

## 5.7 Summary

BOFT marries two research threads—structured sparsity through butterfly decomposition and rigid geometrical constraints via Cayley transforms—to yield an elegant finetuning paradigm that:

- **Starts from scratch with zero drift:** Identity initialization guarantees no immediate performance loss.
- **Requires few parameters:** With only ≈ ½(d − b)m trainable scalars per linear layer.
- **Preserves spectral properties:** Orthogonality keeps singular values intact.
- **Provides implicit inductive bias:** Butterfly patterns emulate fast transforms known to be useful for visual and linguistic data.
- **Is ready for deployment:** After training one can fuse `R(m,b)` into `W^0`, incurring no additional inference latency or memory overhead.

Implementations can directly plug this pattern into any pipeline that supports custom linear layers and gradient flow without special kernels – a key advantage for rapid experimentation across vision Transformers, language models, or diffusion generators.
