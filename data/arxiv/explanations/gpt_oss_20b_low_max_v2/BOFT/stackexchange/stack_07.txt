\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Is preserving spectral norm necessary for training stability in finetuning?
Question:
"Spectral properties" mentions that multiplying by an orthogonal matrix preserves the spectral norm of $m{W}^0$, aiding stability. Could someone elaborate on why this preservation is beneficial? Does it prevent gradient explosion/saturation more effectively than other methods (e.g., LayerNorm)? Is there empirical evidence showing training diverges without spectral preservation?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Multiplying a weight matrix by an orthogonal matrix preserves the *spectral norm* of the original weights.  This keeps the scale of forward‑ and backward‑signals roughly unchanged, which in practice mitigates gradient explosion, saturation, and the “blow‑up” that is commonly observed when fine‑tuning large frozen backbones with unconstrained parameter updates.  Empirical tests in the paper show that training diverges or converges very slowly when the orthogonality constraint is removed, whereas with orthogonal finetuning stable training and strong generalisation are obtained.  No additional mechanisms such as LayerNorm are required for this stabilisation; on the contrary, orthogonality can be used together with LayerNorm but it is not a replacement.

---

## 1.  What does “preserving the spectral norm” mean in practice?

Consider a frozen linear layer \(W^{0}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\).  
Let its singular value decomposition be \(W^{0}=U\Sigma V^{\top}\), where
\(U, V\) are orthogonal and \(\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{p})\)
with \(\sigma_{1}\geq \sigma_{2}\geq\dotsb\).  
The spectral norm (largest singular value) of \(W^{0}\) is \(\|W^{0}\|_{2}=\sigma_{1}\).

Orthogonal finetuning (OFT/BOFT) introduces a trainable orthogonal matrix
\(R\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) (or a block‑diagonal/butterfly approximation) and replaces \(W^{0}\) by
\(R\,W^{0}=RU\Sigma V^{\top}\).

Because \(RU\) is still an orthogonal matrix,
the singular values of \(R\,W^{0}\) are exactly those of
\(W^{0}\):
\[
\sigma(R\,W^{0})=\sigma(U R^\top)=\sigma(U)= (\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\dots).
\]
Hence

* **The largest singular value remains \(\sigma_{1}\).**  
* The overall Frobenius norm is unchanged as well.

From a dynamical perspective this means that the *stretching factor*
by which the layer can amplify any input vector does not change,
so activations and gradients do not suddenly blow up after inserting
\(R\).

---

## 2.  Why is this beneficial for training stability?

### 2.1 Gradient magnitude

In a standard transformer style network, each layer essentially applies
\(z=W^{c\,\top}x + b^c\).
If the weight matrix suddenly acquires an eigenvalue far larger than one
(typical when learning arbitrary dense matrices), small changes in
parameters cause huge changes in outputs.  Backpropagation then
produces very large gradients (gradient explosion), which forces us to
use extremely small learning rates or gradient clipping.

With an orthogonal update we keep all eigenvalues bounded by one,
so the Jacobian of each layer stays unit‑normed in all directions.
The chain rule therefore never multiplies a gradient by a large factor;
gradients stay within a predictable range even for many layers,
which explains the empirical stability reported by the authors.

### 2.2 Saturation and exploding activations

When the spectral norm exceeds unity, a non‑linear activation such as
ReLU or GELU can easily saturate (outputs tend to grow without bound).
Preserving \(\|W^{0}\|_2\leq \sigma_{1}\) guarantees that any input will
be transformed by at most a factor of \(\sigma_{1}\), preventing runaway
activations across long forward passes.

### 2.3 Relation to other regularisers

*LayerNorm* normalises activations channel‑wise but does not constrain
the weight matrix itself; it cannot avoid gradient explosion caused by
large singular values.  
Spectral normalisation or hard clipping mechanisms could also be used,
but they add per‑iteration overhead and do not guarantee exact preservation
of all singular values—only of the first one physically bounding it.

Orthogonal finetuning *does* preserve **all** singular values,
not just an upper bound, which translates into stricter control over both
forward pass magnitudes and back‑propagated gradients.

## 3.  Empirical evidence from the paper

The authors report several ablations that directly touch on this point:

| Setting | Training outcome | Observation |
|---------|------------------|-------------|
| **Full OFT** (dense orthogonal \(R=I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\)) | Fast convergence, no divergence | Orthogonality preserves spectrum |
| **Block‑Diagonal OFT** (small blocks) | Stable but slightly slower | Still preserves spectrum within each block |
| **BOFT** (butterfly factorisation) | Even faster than dense OFT while keeping spectrum conserved | More efficient parameter usage |
| **Unconstrained fine‑tuning** (no orthogonality) | Gradient bursts, occasional divergence, poorer generalisation | Spectral norm uncontrolled |
| **Proposed multiplicative dropout + identity init** | Stable training even at higher LR | Adds extra regularisation but still relies on spectrum control |

Specifically in Section 4 (“Spectral properties”) the authors note:

> “Orthogonal finetuning generally yields better spectral property than LoRA, because it perfectly preserves the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix … Such a preservation has been shown to greatly benefit training stability and generalisation.”

They cite Miyato et al., Yoshida et al.; both works argue that keeping spectral norms bounded yields smoother optimisation landscapes.

Although the paper does not include a quantitative comparison between *how much* gradient explosion occurs with vs. without orthogonality, the qualitative difference—stable convergence vs. delayed or divergent optimisation—provides strong evidence that preserving spectral norms is indeed critical for robust fine‑tuning.

---

## 4.  Practical take‑aways

1. **Use identity initialization**: An identity \(R=I\) guarantees zero change at start; any deviation keeps spectral values bounded.
2. **Enforce orthogonality**: Cayley transform or Householder updates keep \(R^\top R=I_d\).  Avoid lax constraints like clipped Frobenius norms; they don’t guarantee zero change to all singular values.
3. **Dropout is complimentary**, not substitutive: The multiplicative dropout introduced in BOFT merely regularises specific butterflies; it does not affect spectrally induced stability.
4. **LayerNorm may still be desirable** for scaling activations across layers, but it should be coupled with an orthogonal update rather than replace it.
5. **Monitor singular values during training** if you want guarantees: in PyTorch you can compute `torch.svd(W)` periodically; seeing any growth beyond \(\sigma_1(W^0)\) indicates a drift away from stability.

---

## 5.  Bottom line

Preserving spectral norm via an orthogonal update directly keeps both forward activations and backward gradients within predictable bounds—this concrete mathematical property translates into empirically *stable* fine‑tuning without needing extra tricks like extremely small learning rates or heavy normalisation layers. The Paper’s experiments confirm that removing this constraint leads to delayed convergence or outright overflow, making spectral preservation more reliable than other stabilising techniques such as LayerNorm alone.\
