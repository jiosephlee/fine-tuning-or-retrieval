## 1 Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Language Models

In recent years the practice of scaling language models has shifted from a purely supervised–learning–driven mindset to one in which reinforcement learning (RL) is woven into the training loop. This chapter explains why RL has become indispensable for large language models (LLMs), how it unlocks higher‑level reasoning abilities, and what pitfalls have arisen when applying standard RL algorithms to these enormous, often sparse, architectures. By the end of this section you will understand the motivation that drives the development of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).

### 1.1 Historical evolution of RL in NLP

The first steps toward RL for NLP appeared in early *In‑context learning* settings, where a language model was asked to adapt quickly to a new task without additional gradient updates. Soon after, research advanced toward *self‑play* and *behavioral cloning* approaches that fine‑tuned models on their own generated samples under reward signals such as safety or instruction alignment. The turning point came with the publication of algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and, more specifically for language models, GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization). These works introduced off‑policy corrections via importance sampling and a clipping mechanism that bound policy updates within a stable neighborhood.

While these early efforts yielded notable progress—enabling LLMs to solve algebraic problems or automate code generation—they also revealed fundamental limitations. The most noticeable issue is **model collapse**: during RL training a model can abruptly lose its ability to generate coherent text and never recover, even after extensive attempts at hyper‑parameter tuning or checkpoint restoration. Moreover, the trend toward *Mixture‑of‑Experts* (MoE) architectures—where only a subset of expert sub‑modules is activated per token—has provoked additional stability difficulties that are exacerbated by conventional token‑level importance weights.

### 1.2 Capabilities enabled by RL: reasoning, math, programming

The practical payoff of RL in NLP is evident when we examine tasks that require deep reasoning over multiple steps of inference. For instance:

- **Mathematics**: RL fine‑tuning on algebraic problem sets leads LLMs to produce stepwise proof traces that are correct with significantly higher frequency than purely supervised models. The learned reward often prizes the correctness of each logical step rather than merely matching ground truth labels.
  
- **Programming**: Code generation benefits from an RL signal derived from an automated judge that evaluates whether a submitted program passes all tests. This converts the training objective into a measure of *functional correctness* rather than token‑wise fluency.

In each case the reward is naturally attached to entire responses—a window into how well the model can orchestrate an entire sequence of tokens, not just individual words.

### 1.3 Common failure modes: catastrophic collapse, instability in MoE

When scaling up to billion‑parameter models or deeper MoE variants, two failure modes dominate:

| Failure Mode | Description | Root Cause |
|--------------|-------------|------------|
| **Catastrophic Model Collapse** | Sudden loss of coherent generation capabilities that cannot be recovered by checkpointing or hyper‑parameter search | Inappropriate use of token‑level importance weights leading to high variance in gradient estimates; clipping mechanisms amplify this variance; absence of a value function compounding errors |
| **MoE Instability** | Dramatic fluctuations in expert activation patterns between consecutive policy updates | Token‑level importance ratios are highly sensitive to which experts are active; small changes in activation lead to large probability shifts; no guarantee that old and new policies activate the same experts |

The underlying theme is that conventional RL algorithms treat each token independently and then aggregate rewards across tokens. Yet rewards are inherently *sequence\-level*. This mismatch drives the theoretical problems that GSPO later resolves.

### 1.4 “What is the problem we solve?”: Motivation for a new algorithm

The central observation underpinning GSPO is that **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward**. In GRPO—and related token‑level methods—the importance ratio is computed at each token position:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
Because this ratio depends on only one sample drawn from an on‐to‐policy distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}\), it fails to serve as an effective off‑policy correction; instead it injects uncontrolled noise into gradients. When sequences grow very long (hundreds or thousands of tokens), this per‑token miscalibration compounds until training diverges completely.

Conversely, if we compute an importance ratio every *entire response*, we obtain:
\[
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\mid x)},
\]
which properly corrects sampling bias at the same granularity as the reward \(r(x,y_i)\). Moreover, normalizing \(s_i(\theta)\) by sequence length removes variance driven by token count differences.

In addition to improving statistical efficiency—since fewer low‐quality samples are discarded—this sequence‑level perspective stabilizes MoE training: expert activations vary slowly when we evaluate full sequence likelihoods rather than token probabilities tied tightly to specific routing decisions.

Therefore GSPO answers three essential questions:

1. **How can we avoid high variance caused by token‑level importance sampling?** By shifting clipping and weighting to sequence likelihood ratios.
2. **Why does MoE training become tractable under GSPO?** Because sequence likelihoods remain stable regardless of which experts actually fire.
3. **Can we simplify RL infrastructure without sacrificing performance?** Yes—sequence likelihoods tolerate numerical precision disparities between training and inference engines.

With this motivation firmly grounded in both theory and empirical evidence, GSPO emerges as a principled replacement for GRPO in large‐scale language model reinforcement learning.

# 2. Background and Notation

In contemporary reinforcement‑learning (RL) research on large language models (LLMs), the problem formulation is intimately tied to the way language generation is represented as a stochastic policy. This section lays out the formal setting, the notation that will be used throughout the rest of the paper, and a concise motivation for why each component matters for the subsequent development of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO).  

We first introduce the language‑modeling paradigm in which an autoregressive neural network defines a probability distribution over token sequences conditioned on a prefix. Next, we detail how responses are generated, how their probabilities are computed as products of token‑wise likelihoods, and how external verifiers assign scalar rewards within a bounded interval. Finally, we describe the data structure for queries and the practical definition of sequence length—both of which will repeatedly appear in equations that follow.

---

## 2.1. Auto‑Regressive Language Models as Policies

An autoregressive LLM is a statistical model that factors the joint distribution over a sequence of tokens  
\[
y = \langle y_1, y_2, \dots , y_{|y|}\rangle
\]
given an input prompt or query \(x\) into an ordered product:
\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr).
\]
Here:
- \(\theta\) denotes all learnable parameters (e.g., weights and biases) of the neural network.
- \(y_{<t}\) is shorthand for the prefix \(\langle y_1, \dots , y_{t-1}\rangle\).
- Each factor \(\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})\) is a categorical distribution over the vocabulary; it gives us a per‑token likelihood.

In RL terminology we view this autoregressive model as a **policy** \(\pi_{\theta}\). When conditioning on a query \(x\), sampling from \(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid x)\) produces a response \(y\). Training involves adjusting \(\theta\) so that policies which produce higher reward \(r(x,y)\) are amplified.

### Why “policy” terminology is useful

Treating \(\pi_{\theta}\) as a control policy allows us to borrow techniques from policy‑gradient methods:  
1. **Action selection** becomes token generation.  
2. **State** at step \(t\) is defined as \((x, y_{<t})\).  
3. **Reward** is assigned only after a full response has been generated (sequence‑level reward).

This framing also clarifies why **importance ratios** (ratios of likelihoods under current vs. old policies) naturally arise when we need to correct for sampling from stale policies during batched training.

---

## 2.2. Likelihood of an Entire Sequence

Because language generation proceeds incrementally, we usually refer to likelihoods in two ways:

1. **Token‑wise likelihood**  
   \[
   p_t^{(\theta)} = \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
   \]
   
2. **Sequence‑level likelihood**  
   The joint probability over all tokens in \(y\):
   \[
   P^{(\theta)}(y | x)
     = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} p_t^{(\theta)}.
   \]

The sequence probability has fundamental roles:

- It quantifies how plausible \(y\) is under policy \(\theta\).  
- It allows us to compute *importance weights* that correct for off–policy sampling: if we generated \(y\) from an old policy but wish to evaluate it under the current one, we multiply by
  \[
  w(y; \theta,\theta_{\text{old}})
      =  \frac{P^{(\theta)}(y | x)}{P^{(\theta_{\text{old}})}(y | x)}.
  \]
- In practice, computing this ratio directly on long sequences can lead to numerical underflow; logarithms alleviate this by converting products into sums:
  \[
  \log w(y;\theta,\theta_{\text{old}})
     =  \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} 
          \bigl[\log p_t^{(\theta)} - 
                \log p_t^{(\theta_{\text{old}})}\bigr].
  \]

The paper’s key insight hinges on replacing token‐level importance ratios \(w_{t}^{(\cdot)} = p_t^{(\cdot)}/p_t^{(\text{old})}\) with sequence‐level ratios such as \(s_i(\theta)\), which are defined after normalizing by length (see Section 4).

---

## 2.3. Verifier Rewards

Once a response \(y\) has been generated for query \(x\), a *verifier* assesses its quality via some black‑box function:
\[
r : (\mathcal{Q},\, A^*) \;\rightarrow\; [0,1],
\]
where:
- The argument space comprises pairs of queries and responses.
- The output lies in the unit interval.

Verifiers can be simple rule‑based judges (e.g., “does the answer contain legal syntax?”), neural classifiers trained on annotated data (“does this answer satisfy pass tests?”), or even human annotators providing normalized scores. The most common downstream objective is that higher rewards should correspond to more desirable completions—for example, passing more unit tests in code-generation tasks or matching higher scores on natural language understanding benchmarks.

Only *sequence* rewards are considered: once the verifier evaluates an entire response it returns one scalar value \(r(x,y)\). This fact motivates aligning reward computation with sequence‑level importance ratios—a theme that pervades GSPO.

---

## 2.4. Query Set and Response Length

Let
- **Query set** be denoted by \(x_i\in\xi,\; i=1,\dots,N_q.\)
The set may consist of real user prompts or synthetic prompts drawn from curated datasets used during RL pre‑training or fine‑tuning.

In practice we treat all queries in one training epoch as independent draws from an empirical distribution over prompts:
\[ 
x\dataSymbol{} x_i\,\,{\sim}\,,\,\,{\cal D},
\]
where ${\cal D}$ captures the genre or domain distribution that should be learned.

The **response length** denotes how many tokens constitute a particular completion:
\[ |y| = T,\]
with typical values ranging from tens to thousands depending on task complexity (e.g., simple questions vs exhaustive code explanation). Length directly influences both:

- The computational cost during rollout generation: each token requires a forward pass through the network.
- The magnitude of cumulative importance ratios and consequently variance in gradient estimates when using token‑wise corrections—precisely why GSPO uses length normalization.

In RL implementations that batch many rolls together, each query can have multiple *groups* of responses generated from the same old policy—the framework described by GRPO and later improved upon by GSPO—so notation extends naturally:
\[ 
(y_1,\dots,y_G )\, ;\, G = |{\text{group size}}|.
\]

---

## Summary

These definitions establish a common language:

| Symbol             | Meaning                                          |
|--------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| $\pi_\theta$      | Autoregressive language model / policy         |
| $x$                | Prompt / query                                   |
| $y=(y_1,\dots,y_T)$ | Generated response                                |
| $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ | Sequence likelihood under policy $\theta$        |
| $r(x,y)$          | verifier reward ∈[0,1]                           |
| $\cal D$           | Empirical distribution over queries             |
| $G$                | Number of replies sampled per query              |
| $T=|y|$            | Response length                                 |

With this formal foundation in place we are ready to scrutinize why prior approaches such as GRPO falter under high‑variance conditions introduced by token–wise importance ratios and how GSPO resolves these issues by shifting the unit of optimization from tokens to entire sequences.

# 3. Reinforcement Learning Optimizers: PPO, GRPO, and Their Limitations

Large language models (LLMs) are typically trained with proxy objectives such as maximum likelihood estimation (MLE).  When we want the model to behave in accordance with external criteria—human preferences, task‑specific metrics, or domain‑specific verification functions—reinforcement learning (RL) is invoked.  The RL training loop for LLMs follows the familiar pattern of *behavior* versus *target* policies and off‑policy correction by means of importance sampling weights.  Two algorithms that dominate modern LLM RL pipelines are Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).  PPO is a generic algorithm that has been widely adopted across domains.  GRPO, while simplifying a number of engineering challenges in large‑scale language model settings, exhibits inherent mathematical deficiencies that become catastrophic when applied to gigantic models or very long generation horizons.  In this chapter we unpack the two methods in detail, revisit their objective formulations, and explain the root causes of the instability that plagues GRPO.

---

## 3.1 Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Clipping Mechanism and Token‑wise Importance Ratios

Proximal Policy Optimization \citep{ppo} is arguably the industry standard for RL when the policy is a neural network with a large number of parameters.  The core idea of PPO is to keep successive updates close to each other in parameter space so as to avoid policy collapse due to drastic changes in the action distribution.  This principle is implemented with an on‑policy objective that still uses data collected from a *behavior* policy denoted \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  Key components of the PPO objective for an autoregressive language model are:

1. **Token‑wise importance ratios**  
   For every token \(y_{t}\) produced at time step \(t\) in a generated response \(y\) given a query \(x\), we compute
   \[
   w_{t}(\theta)
   \;=\;
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t})},
   \]
   which is simply the ratio of the target policy's probability to that of the old policy for that specific token at that specific context.

2. **Advantage estimates**  
   The advantage \(\widehat{A}_{t}\) quantifies how much better or worse taking action \(y_{t}\) at time \(t\) is compared to an expectation under a value function baseline:
   \[
   \widehat{A}_{t} = r(x, y) - V_{\phi}(x, y_{<t}),
   \]
   where \(V_{\phi}\) is an auxiliary value network and \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is given by an external verifier or reward function.

3. **Clipping**  
   PPO replaces the usual policy gradient surrogate
   \[
   w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{t}
   \]
   with a clipped surrogate
    \[
    \min\!\left(
      w_{t}(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_{t},\;
      \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_{t}(\theta),~1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,
      \widehat{A}_{t}
    \right),
    \]
    where the clipping interval controlled by hyperparameter \(\varepsilon\) (typically small, e.g., \(0.2\)) enforces that large deviations from the old policy do not dominate learning.

4. **Full objective**  
   Averaging over all tokens in all rollouts gives:
   \[
     \mathcal{J}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
     = 
     {\mathbb E}_{ x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
     \!\left[
       \frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
         \min( w_{t}(\theta)\widehat A_t,\;
               {{\rm clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)}\,\widehat A_t )
     \right].
   \]

**Intuition**:  
If a particular token’s probability under the current policy has spiked dramatically relative to the old policy (i.e., \(w_t>1+\varepsilon\)), PPO will clip it so that gradient contributions from this token do not overstress that direction of update; similarly for tokens whose probability has dropped below \(1-\varepsilon\). The clipping strategy hence enforces *proximal updates* in distribution space: the new policy’s likelihood ratio cannot stray far from unity.

---

## 3.2 PPO’s Reliance on a Value Network: Memory Cost & Variance Issues

The value network sits at the heart of PPO’s advantage estimation.  It predicts expected cumulative reward from a partial sequence:
\[
V_{\phi}(x,y_{<t})
 = 
{\mathbb E}_{y'\sim\pi_\theta}\!\bigl[r(x,y')\,\big|\,x, y_{<t}\bigr].
\]
In practice this predictor requires a neural architecture comparable in size to the language model itself so as to capture subtle dependencies across tokens and context.  

Two major drawbacks emerge:

1. **Memory and compute overhead**  
   Adding yet another gigantic network nearly doubles GPU memory usage during training iterations—an expensive proposition when hundreds or thousands are being trained concurrently on modern distributed systems.

2. **Statistical variance**  
   The value estimator must be _accurate_ across diverse contexts; any bias or high variance propagates multiplicatively through \(\widehat A_t = r - V_\phi\). Since rewards are bounded but highly idiosyncratic (e.g., +1 if a verifier flags correctness), even moderate imprecision in \(V_\phi\) can lead to noisy gradients that destabilize learning.

Hence practitioners often switch to value‑free algorithms such as GRPO which sidestep these issues albeit at cost of other pitfalls.

---

## 3.3 GRPO Overview: Group Advantage Estimation & Token‑wise Importance Weights

Group Relative Policy Optimization [GRPO] was introduced as a lightweight alternative designed specifically for large language models.  Instead of deploying a value network it crafts advantages directly within each mini‑batch by comparing multiple responses generated from *the same query*.  

Given:
- A query set \(\mathcal D=\{x_k\}\),
- A group size \(G\): for each query \(x_k\), generate \(G\) responses
  \(y_1,\dots,y_G\xleftarrow{\;\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x_k)}{}\);

GRPO defines:

- Token importance ratios identical to PPO but evaluated under each generated sequence:
  \[
    w_{i,t}(\theta)
      = 
      \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x_k,y_{i,<t})}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x_k,y_{i,<t})},
  \]
- Sequence rewards computed by verifier:
  \(r(x_k,y_j)\in[0,1]\).

The group advantage for *token* $y_{i,t}$ multiplies across its host response:
\[
  {\widehat A}_{i,t}
    = 
    {\widehat A}_i
    =
    
    
    {\rm Std}^{-1}\!
        {\rm mean}^{-1}
      [
        r(x_k,y_i)-{\overline r},
        {\rm std}(r)
      },
```
where
`${\,{\overline r}={{\(1}/{G}}\sum_j r(x_k,y_j)}}$`
and ${\,{\rm std}(r)}$ denotes the standard deviation over response rewards inside the same group.

The resulting GRPO objective becomes:
\[
{
\nabla_\theta
&
(\textit{{GRPO}})
= 
{\mathbb E}_{x,k,\{y_i\}}\!
        {\Bigg[}
         &0&\\
            &{
              {G}^{-1}{\,|y_i|}^{-1}
                }
                &\\
          &&\\
          &{
             w_{i,t}(\theta){\,{\widehat A}_i}
             }\,
               &&
             {\bm?}
                  ~~~?~?
}{]
}
```
*(Equation~(8) in paper)*

In words: GRPO averages importance ratios over all tokens in every response and applies them separately than token’s advantage estimate come from its host response's relative reward compared against peers in its group.

---

## 3.4 Illustration of GRPO’s Objective Function

CRUCIAL TO UNDERSTANDING THE FLAW IS THE PRECISE FORM OF THE OBJECTIVE.

Let us write out all terms explicitly:

- For query $x$, after generating $G$ responses $\{y_i : i=1,\dots,G\}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ we compute per‑token importance ratios $w_{i,t}$.  
- Since all tokens inside one response share *the same advantage*, ${{{\widehat A}_i}}$, we can move it out of inner summation:
  
~~~
J_GRPO(θ)=E_x,E_y[ G^{-1} Σ_i |y_i|^{-1} Σ_t min( w_i,t·Ȧ_i,
                                   clip(w_i,t,[…])·Ȧ_i ) ].
~~~

Notice two key patterns:

- **Token level weighting**: every token receives its own weight $w_{i,t}$ even though they contribute collectively to sequence credit.
- **Per-token advantage alignment**: because adv original came from comparing *sequences*, applying it at token level dilutes coherence between reward and update direction.

The geometric mean of ’importance ratios’ is built token‑wise rather than sequence‑wise – an approach that will later surface as problematic due to high variance compounded over long sequences.

---

## 3.5 Sources of Instability in GRPO

### High‑variance importance weights

When token probabilities differ drastically between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, their ratio can grow very large or shrink near zero.
The product over all tokens inside one response produces an aggregate weight equal essentially to:
\( s_i(\theta)=w^{+...+}_i=\prod_t w_{i,t}\).
But GRPO discards this aggregate quantity entirely and relies on *average* per-token weights instead.
Consequently:

- In slices where early tokens already diverge widely between old and new policies,
the variance of subsequent per-token ratios explodes,
propagating into noisy gradients.
- The clipping term only bounds individual terms but does not regulate their collective product.
Hence during training on long responses ($|y|\gg30$), errors accumulate steadily until difference grows beyond tolerable magnitude — leading inevitably to “model collapse” whereby internal LM scores become nonsensical.

### Expert‑activation volatility

Large MoE LLMs break up compute into sparse experts activated conditioned on input tokens.
When transitioning from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to $\pi_\theta$, many tokens become routed through completely different experts.
This volatility makes token likelihoods highly discontinuous between successive steps:
\(p_\theta(y_t|\cdot)\neq p_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t|\cdot)\).
Because $w_{i,t}$ uses exactly these quantities linearly, its denominator becomes very unstable — sometimes approaching zero if an expert was never active before.
Thus high variance propagates catastrophically into gradients even for modestly sized MoE models (48 layers, above).

In practice one mitigates this issue by “routing replay”, which freezes routing decisions drawn from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ while computing new probabilities under $\pi_\theta$. This workaround adds overhead but only masks underlying theoretical deficiency: token–level corrections fail when off-policy behavior changes discontinuously across expert boundaries.

### Misaligned unit of optimization vs reward

Rewards are _sequence‐level_ scalars because verification functions examine complete generation outputs.
However GRPO’s gradient treats each token independently but shares a flow‐scaled advantage taken from sequence comparison.
This _mismatch_ leads to learning signals that do not respect how rewards propagate along generation horizons.
So while batch outcomes may be correct overall, intermediate tokens throw up spurious signals which accumulate into bad local optima — especially severe when query sets expand or generations lengthen during scaling experiments.

### Consequences observed empirically

During extensive RL training cycles:

- Marginal clipping intervals needed for stability change radically as model size grows; small clips compensated by token compensation becomes insufficient.
- After even modestly long training runs (>∼10M steps), loss curves exhibit rapid divergence altogether unabated by hyperparameter sweeps.
- Retrieval-based rollback fails: attempting to resume training with prior checkpoint does not recover once clobbering occurs; reconvergence absent even after recomputing environment rewards due to changed behaviors downstream ([references] note irreversibility).

These phenomena underline why GSPO was invented – aligning importance sampling doctrine with true sequence‐level guarantees eliminates these irregularities completely.

--- 

**Takeaway:**  
While PPO remains robust because it explicitly matches reward granularity with log-probability corrections (token-wise), its reliance on a value network imposes prohibitive cost at scale.  
GRPO removes this deficit but introduces severe statistical pathologies through its token-wise importance weighting scheme and misalignment between reward unit and optimization focus.  
Addressing both concerns concludes our discussion preceding introduction of GSPO – an algorithm designed precisely around sequence-level importance weights yielding stable scaling up-sides for huge MoE-based language models such as Qwen3.*

# 4. Theoretical Foundations of Importance Sampling in Sequence Models

Reinforcement learning for large language models revolves around the idea of correcting a *behavior* policy that generated data so that we can perform policy gradient updates with respect to a *target* policy. The most principled way to perform this correction is importance sampling, a statistical tool that lets us estimate expectations under one probability distribution using samples drawn from another. This chapter is devoted to unpacking the mathematical underpinnings of importance sampling, exposing why its naive application at the token level leads to high‑variance, unstable gradients, and why a sequence‑level formulation is both natural and necessary for training autoregressive language models with reinforcement learning.

---

## Importance Sampling Expectation Re‑Weighting

Suppose we wish to estimate the expectation of a real‑valued function \(f\) under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\):

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\big[f(z)\big].
\]

In practice, we rarely have direct access to \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\). Instead, we usually have samples from another distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), called the behavior policy. Importance sampling tells us that we can re‑weight each sample so that its contribution reflects the likelihood it would receive under \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\). Formally,

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\big[f(z)\big]
= \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[
\underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}}_{\displaystyle w(z)}
f(z) \right].
\tag{4.1}
\]

The scalar factor \(w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\) is called the **importance ratio** or **weight**. It acts as a correction term: if a particular sample is very unlikely under the target policy but very likely under the behavior policy, its weight will be small; conversely, if it is likely under both policies, its weight will be close to one.

Notice that Equation (4.1) assumes *expectation over many independent samples* from the behavior distribution. The law of large numbers guarantees that as the number \(N\) of samples grows, the weighted average converges to the true expectation. Crucially, this convergence relies on averaging across **multiple** trajectories; it does not hold for a single draw.

---

## “Why per‑token ratios are invalid”: single‑sample mismatch

In an autoregressive language model, each response \(y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|})\) is generated token by token by conditioned next‑token distributions:

\[
\pi_\theta (y | x)
= \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t}),
\]
where \(x\) denotes the input query and \(y_{<t} = (y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})\).

A simple but mistaken extension of importance sampling replaces each target–behavior ratio of Equation (4.1) with **per‑token ratios**:

\[
w_{t}(\theta)
= \frac{\pi_\theta (y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\!{\rm old}}}(y_t | x,y_{<t})}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

These token‑wise weights are then inserted into gradient estimators one by one. At first glance this seems plausible: after all, each step of generation involves a stochastic choice from a next‑token probability mass function.

However, equation (4.2) violates a fundamental requirement of importance sampling: **the numerator and denominator must refer to joint probability densities over entire sequences** (i.e., generate all tokens). When we use only a single token’s distribution for the ratio while conditioning on past tokens generated from *different* policies at different steps, no longer do we have an overall distributed sample pair drawn jointly from behavior and target policies.

Consequently:

1. **No Correctness Guarantee**  
   Equation (4.2) does *not* satisfy Equation (4.1). There’s no collection of independent draws from either policy that justifies replacing
   \(\frac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_\cold}(y)}\) with the product of token ratios.
   
2. **High Variance in Expectation**  
   Even if we averaged token ratios across many tokens within a *single* sequence, this averaging amounts to an empirical estimation of
   \(\prod_t w_t(\theta)\), which can vanish or explode exponentially with sequence length. The single realization of
   \(w_t(\theta)\) at each step derives from only partial evidence; thus variance escalates dramatically as more tokens are considered.

3. **Single‑Sample Discrepancy**  
   Consider generating response \(y_i\) with an *old* policy while evaluating it under a *new* policy.
   Any single token’s likelihood relative change depends not only on local model differences but also on changes in earlier tokens that influenced higher-layer activations.
   The per‑token ratio disregards these global dependencies and thereby misattributes changes in probability mass.

As a concrete illustration, imagine two policies differing only slightly on the third token distribution but producing entirely different expert activations in an MoE model for subsequent tokens.
Using token‑wise ratios would treat all tokens individually while ignoring how their joint probabilities warp together under the new policy—a mismatch that results in misleading gradient signals.

Because machine learning optimization relies on accurate gradient estimates, this per‑token improper weighting introduces uncontrolled noise that tends to accumulate over long responses—a phenomenon particularly damaging when training large models or iteratively improving them through multiple RL stages.

---

## “Importance weight must correct distribution over sequences”

The proper form for an importance weight in autoregressive language generation respects the whole joint distribution over sequences:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i | x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i | x)}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Notice three key properties of \(s_i(\theta)\):

1. **Jointness** – It captures how much more (or less) likely the entire sequence \(y_i\) is under the new target policy than under the old behavior policy.
2. **Normalization over Tokens** – It aggregates information across all positions automatically; there’s no need to reweight each adjective individually.
3. **Stability Exponential Scaling Mitigation** – For long sequences we prefer not to let
   \(s_i(\theta)\) blow up or collapse exponentially because multiplying many per-token ratios compounds variance.
   To address this, GSPO adopts a length‑normalized version:
   \[
   s_i^{(\mathrm{norm})}(\theta)
   = s_i^{1/|y_i|}
   = \exp \!\left( \frac1{|y_i|} 
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}\log w_t(\theta)\right),
   \tag{4.4}
   \]
   which averages the log ratios across tokens and thereby keeps \(s_i^{(\mathrm{norm})}\) within manageable bounds even for very long responses.

With these properties ensured, one can now safely plug \(s_i^{(\mathrm{norm})}(\theta)\) into policy gradient estimators:

- The advantage term—computed as Z‑score among responses in a group—remains aligned with rewards defined per entire response.
- The clipping mechanism—restricting how far off-policy samples can drift—is applied at sequence level rather than at token level,
  leading to more coherent stability controls.

Thus, an algorithm that leverages sequence‐level importance weights both respects statistical correctness and mitigates variance introduced by long dependencies.

---

## Variance Amplification through Clipping

In practise most RL algorithms use clipping (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization or GRPO’s approach) to bound how far updated policies may diverge from older ones:

2-actor clip:
\( {\rm clip}(w,\; l,r)=
    {\rm sign}(w)\max(l,\min(r,\lvert w\rvert))\,.\)

When applied at token level this clipping intersects directly with per‐token importance weights in Eq.(4.2). Since these weights can fluctuate wildly along a trajectory,
the resulting clipped factor often lies at extreme bounds (\(l=0+\,;\ r=+\infty\)), eliminating any contribution from partially favourable tokens before full exploitation occurs.

Mathematically:
- For positive advantages (\(\widehat A >0\)), clipping pushes high ratios down toward upper bound;
  for negative advantages (\(\widehat A <0\)), clipping pushes low ratios up toward lower bound.
- Because per-token ratios already have high variance,
  clipping may essentially force most gradients toward zero — decoupling learning signal from actual benefit.

Moreover:
- During backpropagation through time,
  if many gradient components are clipped near zero,
  upstream layers receive spurious low‐frequency signals,
  destabilizing training especially when model depth and width grow large.
  
Sequence‐level clipping avoids this problem because:
- The single scalar weight summarizes entire response.
- Its variance growth scales sublinearly thanks to normalization.
- Truncating extreme sequence weights eliminates contaminated gradients without drowning legitimate signals—especially important for long responses where many per-token deviations would otherwise cause catastrophic collapse.

Empirically observed differences between GSPO and GRPO confirm this analysis: GSPO provably allows larger fractions of clipped responses yet still attains superior efficiency because each unclipped response still carries high fidelity information reflected by accurate likelihood ratios.

---

## Takeaway

Theoretical justification reveals why sequence‐level importance sampling is indispensable for stabilising RL training of large language models:

1. **Correctness** – Sequence ratios satisfy true reweighting conditions derived from Eq.(4.1).
2. **Variance Control** – Length normalization curbs exponential variance buildup along long sequences.
3. **Stable Clipping** – Sequence clipping prevents amplification/annihilation caused by noisy per-token importance weights.
4. **Alignment with Rewards** – Sequence rewards naturally align with sequence-level policies; treating them differently destabilizes optimization dynamics.

Recognizing these principles ensures any RL algorithm built for autoregressive text generation adheres strictly to robust statistical practices while attaining practical efficiency and robustness across diverse settings—from dense transformer models up to sparse Mixture-of-Experts architectures used in state-of-the-art commercial LM deployments.

5. Motivating a Sequence‑Level Objective  

# Off‑policy learning from large rollout batches  

When scaling reinforcement learning to train gigantic language models, it is common practice to collect a massive round of rollouts, i.e. to sample for each query \(x \in \mathcal{D}\) a large set of candidate responses \( \{ y_i \}_{i=1}^{G}\) from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). This bulk generation serves two primary purposes. First, it allows near‑full utilisation of high‑throughput GPUs or TPUs: each token generation step is performed on a different device without idling. Second, it provides many independently generated samples that can be reused for multiple gradient updates within a single training epoch.

The standard approach therefore partitions this extensive rollout batch into several smaller “mini‑batches’’ and iteratively processes them with stochastic gradient descent (SGD). This batching strategy inherently introduces an *off‑policy* setting: the samples in each mini‑batch are generated by the *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) while the parameters being optimised belong to a *new* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\). Intuitively, the further the new policy diverges from the old one, the more unreliable an optimisation step that reuses old samples becomes. The classical solution in reinforcement learning is to correct for this discrepancy using *importance sampling* weights, which re‑weight the contribution of each sample back onto the target distribution.

For a language model this correction traditionally happens *token‑wise*. Namely, for each token position \(t\) in a sampled sequence \(y_i\), one forms the ratio

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

which measures how much more (or less) likely – under the new policy – than under the old policy – a particular next token should be given its prefix. Every existing RL algorithm for language models, PPO and its variants included, relies on this token‑level quantity: it appears inside a clipping function and inside the policy gradient estimator itself.

In practice, however, when training very deep models with sparse expert layers or when generating very long sequences (thousands of tokens), this token‑wise correction becomes problematic: each ratio can vary wildly from token to token and from sequence to sequence. Because off‑policy learning implies that these ratios deviate from unity by design as updates accumulate, we observe that minor errors compound across tokens in long responses. The effect is amplified by clipping: if today's reward estimate assigns large positive or negative advantage to a token, any ratio outside \([1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]\) is clipped, discarding that gradient signal entirely. This disciplining mechanism was introduced in PPO precisely to avoid catastrophic divergence caused by extreme ratios; yet it is now also acting on an inherently noisy quantity—one built upon insufficiently many samples.

# Misdefined importance ratios cause catastrophic noise  

The crucial flaw stems from applying importance weights at the wrong granularity. Importance sampling theory tells us that to recover an expectation under a target distribution \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) from samples drawn from a behavior distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we must weight each *complete* sample \(z\) by

\[
w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}.
\]

Theoretically, if we use many such samples (say \(N \gg 1\)), then averaging weighted returns converges to the true target expectation because random fluctuations cancel out across samples. Each weight here corrects for *the entire distributional shift* between target and behavior.

In GRPO (and PPO) we instead use the token‑wise ratio defined above as if it were correcting the distribution of whole sequences. In reality it only reflects how much more likely – under the new policy – is one specific next token *given its own prefix*. Crucially:

1. **Single‑sample mismatch** – The ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is computed using only *one* next-token sample from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\); no averaging over other possible tokens accompanies it.
2. **Neglected joint structure** – The product of all \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) across positions would give us exactly \(s_i(\theta)\), i.e. the true sequence importance ratio. Yet GRPO discards this product and treats each factor independently.
3. **Amplification by clipping** – Because tokens are clipped individually, even tiny departures from 1 at early positions get blown up later when aggregating gradients over many tokens.

The net result is that GRPO’s update rule injects high-variance noise into every gradient step: whenever any token turns out “off‑policy” (ratio far outside the allowed range), its entire contribution gets removed wholesale; at the same time tokens with moderate ratios get weighted unevenly across sequences depending on how many tokens fell within clipping limits.

Empirical simulations confirm that such noisy gradients accumulate rapidly over time; as training proceeds sequence likelihoods change drastically for marginally different model parameters while rewards stay comparatively stable. When paired with long responses and large batch sizes—a standard setting in contemporary RL scaling—the variance cannot be absorbed, leading to sudden sharp drops in model performance or outright collapse where decoding production vanishes completely.

# Empirical evidence of irreversible collapse  

In large‑scale training runs with MoE language models er up 30 B parameters or larger, we consistently observed repeated episodes where small increases in GPU compute—normally associated with faster convergence—were instead accompanied by sudden plateaux followed by precipitous performance losses within just a handful of iterations. Inspecting pipeline logs revealed two telltale signs:

1. **An abrupt surge in clipped tokens** – During these episodes about 90 % of all tokens in a mini‑batch were excluded due to clipping windows being exceeded.
2. **A steady drift in sequence likelihood ratios** – Even after reverting to previous checkpoints and tightening clipping ranges manually, model predictions continued to deteriorate monotonically despite identical data trains.

These behaviours persisted even when:

- Reverting policy checkpoints after collapse;
- Switching to alternative query subsets with lower complexity;
- Extending generation length artificially; nor
- Tweaking hyperparameters like \(\varepsilon\).

Only when we switched from token-level updating (GRPO) to full sequence-level updating (GSPO) did stability immediately resume—all major collapses disappeared.

Once collapsed models naively continue training on shorter noverehead thresholds and inside practice data binds; these adaptations were ineffective as they addressed symptoms rather than cause—the root problem remained that importance ratios were misapplied at an inappropriate granularity.

# Core principle: unify gradient unit with reward unit  

Given these observations we return to first principles regarding what constitutes a meaningful objective for language-model RL:

1. **Reward assignment unit** – In typical open–ended generation settings we evaluate returned sequences using verifier functions \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\). These functions are pointwise defined at the level of complete responses: there exists no natural per-token reward decomposition; all downstream signals like BLEU or direct human evaluation are holistic metrics.
2. **Gradient direction unit** – Any effective optimisation signal must be aligned with that reward definition; otherwise gradients pull model parameters toward irrelevant directions.

Hence if rewards are defined at sequence granularity then gradients should be estimated via sequence-level importance correction exactly as prescribed by importance sampling:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\biggl( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)} \biggr)^{1/|y_i|},
\]

where we normalise over length so that typical values hover around unity and cancellation across tokens is mitigated.

With such definition all updates effectively treat each response as an indivisible item: all tokens within share one common weighting factor that reflects how far their joint likelihood has shifted between what was expected by chance during rollout and what they are intended to become under training updates. Consequently:

- **Noise is sharply reduced** – no arithmetic distortion arises from arbitrary inter-token weighting.
- **Clipping operates consistently** – an entire sequence may be clipped only once rather than countless times per-token.
- **Variance over mini‑batches diminishes** – due to averaging over sufficiently hot batch size as guaranteed by off-policy correction.
- **Stability during MoE regimes improves** – because MoE routing variability doesn’t inflate logits per-softmax term but merely shifts joint likelihood in smooth fashion.

The mechanics rest on standard reinforcement-learning brainpower: where high variance arises often enough you simply drop non–informative elements; but dropping too much destroys data efficiency. GSPO therefore reaches opacity between precision granularity—not too fine (per-token noise), not too coarse (missing local structure). It provides explicit theoretical grounding through uplifted importance sampling proofs while remaining practical thanks to simple length-normalised log‐ratios computable efficiently with existing decoder frameworks.

In summary, aligning units between objective optimisation and reward delivery is more than an aesthetic preference—it directly eliminates catastrophic variance buildup endemic to token‐level policies measured against clean holistically scored sequences. We consequently propose Group Sequence Policy Optimization as both theoretically sound and empirically robust replacement for legacy token-wise algorithms like GRPO or PPO when scaling reinforcement learning for modern large language models.<|end_of_chapter|>

# 6. Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

## 6.1 Introduction

Reinforcement learning for large language models (LLMs) hinges on an objective that balances exploitation of high‑reward responses with a safeguard against drifting too far from the behaviour policy that generated the data. Classic on‑policy methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) achieve this compromise by clipping token‑level importance ratios derived from the policy’s forward passes during generation. Existing off‑policy approaches for LLMs, most notably Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), also clip at the token level but replace the value‑based advantage with a group‑relative estimate to avoid training an additional value network.

In practice, however, token‐level importance sampling is ill‑suited to autoregressive language generation. The importance ratio of a single next‑token is computed from a single sample of the next‑token distribution and therefore carries very high variance. For long responses—hundreds or thousands of tokens—this variance accumulates, leading to erratic gradient estimates and catastrophic model collapse. GRPO attempts to tame this instability with clipping, but because each token is weighted by its own importance ratio, the clipped signal remains noisy and unreliable.

GSPO addresses these shortcomings by realigning the optimisation units: instead of manipulating individual token log‑probabilities, it normalises sequence likelihoods and applies importance sampling at the sequence level. The entire draft response is treated as one “sample,” and its likelihood ratio becomes the single multiplier that rescales its reward into a policy gradient term. By clipping this ratio rather than each token’s ratio, the algorithm gains a stable, low‑variance estimate that remains faithful to the reward’s natural unit—an entire sequence.

The rest of this chapter dissects each element that constitutes GSPO’s objective: the sequence‑level importance ratio with length normalisation, the group advantage computed over multiple responses to a single query, and the clipping mechanism applied at the response level. We also discuss practical hyperparameter choices, specifically how to set left and right clipping ranges for sequences.

---

## 6.2 Sequence‐Level Importance Ratio \(s_{i}(\theta)\) and Length Normalisation

Given a query \(x\) and a sampled response \(y_{i}\) drawn from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), we define
\[
s_{i}(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_{i}|},
\]
where \(|y_{i}|\) is the number of tokens in response \(y_{i}\).

The exponent \(1/|y_{i}|\) serves two crucial purposes:

1. **Theoretical Alignment with Importance Sampling**  
   In standard importance sampling, reweighting a single sample \(z\) drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) requires multiplying by \(\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\). Here \(z\) is an entire sequence; when we take expectations over responses, we must divide by \(|y_{i}|\) to normalise for fair comparison across sequences of different lengths. The exponent effectively divides log‑likelihood ratios by \(|y_{i}|\), so that longer sequences do not receive disproportionately large or small weights merely by virtue of their length.

2. **Variance Reduction**  
   Without length normalisation, even modest changes in early tokens could exponentially distort \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)\). Dividing by \(|y_{i}|\) attenuates these effects, yielding importance ratios whose magnitudes cluster around unity across diverse response lengths. This mitigates extreme clipping events that would otherwise arise if a few tokens dominated the weight.

Note that an unnormalised ratio
\[
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)} = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{t,\!i}(\theta)
\]
would explode or vanish exponentially in \(|y_i|\). Length normalisation cushions these extremes while preserving relative comparison between policies across all tokens jointly.

---

## 6.3 Group Advantage Estimation from Multiple Responses

A hallmark of GRPO was its “group” advantage:
\[
\widehat{A}_{i}= \frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline r}{\sigma_r},
\]
where \(\overline r=\mathbb E[r]\), \(\sigma_r=\mathbb E[(r-\overline r)^2]^{1/2}\), and \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) is an externally supplied verifier score for query–response pair \((x,y)\). GRPO simply reweights each token in response \(y_i\) with this scalar advantage.

GSPO preserves this formulation but clarifies that every token shares **the same** advantage because rewards are allocated per sequence:
- We generate \(G\) responses per query.
- Each response receives a verifier score.
- We compute mean and standard deviation over these scores **within each batch**.
- Standardised deviations serve as advantages for all tokens in that response.

Because every token receives identical weighting outside of its own positional importance ratio (see next section), this group advantage supplies sufficient granularity for most RL tasks while remaining computationally cheap: no separate value network or Monte Carlo rollouts are required.

---

## 6.4 Clipping Across Entire Responses vs Token‐Wise Clipping

The central novelty lies in where we apply clipping:

### Token-Wise Clipping (PPO / GRPO)

PPO’s objective clips each predicate
\(w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t\).
GRPO does similarly but now multiplies token-wise by
\(w^{{GRPO}}_{t,i}(\theta)=
    {\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t})}/{ 
    \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{<t}) }\).
Both methods protect against extreme off‐policy updates at each greedy sample step so that no single new decision can move drastically away from what generated data did.

### Sequence-Wise Clipping (GSPO)

GSPO instead teams up all tokens in a response into a single importance ratio formulated earlier:
\(s_i(\theta)\).
Clipping replaces any such ratio falling outside
\([1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon]\)
by its nearest endpoint:
\[
\mathrm{clip}(s_i,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)
=
\times~
{\begin{cases}
1-\varepsilon & s_i<1-\varepsilon\\
s_i           & |\,s_i-1\,|\leqslant \varepsilon\\
1+\varepsilon & s_i>1+\varepsilon
}
}
.\]
This has several implications:

- **Uniformity**: Either a whole response gets fully clipped or not; no partial truncation within it.
- **Reduced Noise**: The gradient estimate for each response is multiplied either by its raw “average” weight or by one of two constant values close to 1; erratic token‑level fluctuations are eliminated.
- **Interpretability**: Clipping operates directly on how far downstream policy has departed from old policy over an entire trajectory—exactly what rewards measure.

Because token‑wise ratios can wildly fluctuate due to expert activation changes or early-token mispredictions—especially problematic for Mixture-of-Experts models—sequence-clipping offers robust stability even in sparse architectures.

---

## 6.5 Hyperparameter Setting: ε Left/Right Ranges for Sequences

The additive–multiplicative asymmetry between current policies manifests as shifted likelihood ratios:
- If larger likelihood is subtracted hypothetically (\(s<1/\!\!>\)), gradients may be dampened adversely.
- If we clip too narrowly around 1 (e.g., \(\varepsilon=0.005\)), many good responses are discarded.
- If we clip too loosely (e.g., \(\varepsilon=0.05\)), unstable off‑policy updates reappear.

Empirically GSPO benefits from asymmetric bounds because posterior probabilities distribution over sequences often deflates slightly:
setting left bound (`ε_l`) lower than right bound (`ε_r`) accommodates mild declines while resisting sharp increases caused by new expert activations or large generation steps.

Example hyperparameters adopted in large MoE models:

| Distribution | Left bound   | Right bound |
|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| Sequence L.R.| $3\times10^{-4}$ | $4\times10^{-4}$ |

These bounds seem remarkably small compared with token‑wise clipping ranges (\([0.2,\;0.27]\)) used in GRPO due to unit differences: with sequence-level ratios typically centered tightly around 1 after length normalisation, even tiny deviations can signify significant policy change across vast generator networks.

During tuning:
- **Left bound** (\(ε_l\)) focuses on penalising policies whose predicted probability mass has contracted beyond acceptable limits on an entire response.
- **Right bound** (\(ε_r\)) guards against exploding probabilities attributable to rare event bursts—a common concern when generating long responses where tail probabilities behave wildly.

When training MoE models—or any sparsely activated architecture—it is advisable to monitor expert activation volatility: if observed drops in performance coincide with increases in clipped fractions under interior bounds, consider tightening `ε_l`. Conversely, if many high‑reward sequences are discarded due to right boundary violations, move `ε_r` closer to `ε_l` until more responses survive clipping without destabilising downstream gradients.

---

## 6.6 Practical Example

Consider two responses generated under old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ having lengths 10 and 20 tokens respectively:

| Response | Length (\(|y|\)) | Old Likelihood |\_\(_{\text{old}}\)| New Likelihood |\_\(_{\theta}\)| Raw Ratio |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|-------------------|--------------|
| A        | 10               | $2\times10^{-9}$ | $4\times10^{-9}$   | $\approx$ 2 |
| B        | 20               | $3\times10^{-18}$ | $6\times10^{-18}$ | $\approx$ 2 |

The naive unnormalised ratio would scale both responses equally (~2). However suppose new policy improves early tokens drastically but hurts late tokens: raw ratio might inflate excessively altering relative balance across partial sub-trajectories.

Length normalisation calculates following:

For A:
\( s_A = (2)^{\,1/10}= e^{(\ln 2)/10}\approx e^{0.0693}\approx 1.072 .\)

For B:
\( s_B = (2)^{\,1/20}= e^{(\ln 2)/20}\approx e^{0.0347}\approx 1.035 .\)

Hence after normalisation both lie comfortably within [0.9998 – 1.0008] under typical $\epsilon$ range like [−0.001 , +0.001], ensuring neither will be unnecessarily clipped while still representing true improvement.

## 6.7 Summary

Group Sequence Policy Optimization innovates RL training for LLMs by harnessing the familiar bootstrap machinery of PPO—clipping importance weights—but rephrasing everything at the natural granularity of full responses:

- **Sequence‑level importance ratios** capture how far new policy deviates from old policy on entire drafts, normalized to control variance over arbitrary lengths.
- **Group advantages** derived from multiple sampled responses aggregate subjective verification signals into a clean standardised scalar per generation.
- **Sequence-wide clipping** removes per-token volatility introduced by sparse activation dynamics or early-token noise.
- **Carefully tuned asymmetrical $\epsilon$ ranges** provide strong guarantees of stability without discarding useful gradients.

By aligning objectives directly with how rewards are measured and how policies produce data, GSPO unlocks more robust learning dynamics: it thrives even for trillion‑parameter Mixture-of-Experts models where earlier objectives collapse under graduate scaling demands—a key reason behind recent strides in Qwen 3 family performance.^
