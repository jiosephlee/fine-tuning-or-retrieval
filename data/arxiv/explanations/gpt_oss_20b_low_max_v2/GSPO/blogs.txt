# Why Token‑Level Rewards Misfire: The Key Insight Behind GSPO

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the engine for pushing language models (LMs) from large‑scale supervised fine‑tuning to the kind of reasoning abilities we see in Qwen3 or ChatGPT‑4.  
Yet RL training of these behemoths is notoriously fragile: a single hyper‑parameter tweak can morph a model from genius to confusion.  
A large part of the problem lies in a subtle misuse of *importance sampling* – specifically, in the way earlier algorithms like **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** apply token‑level importance ratios.

In this post we’ll break down why that approach is fundamentally ill‑posed, walk through a toy derivation that highlights the high‑variance pitfall, and then explain how **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** fixes it by shifting from token‑level to sequence‑level clipping.

---

## 1. The RL objective at a glance

Both PPO and GRPO let us reuse rollouts collected by an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) while updating a *current* policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  
Because the samples come from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) but we want expectations under \(\pi_{\theta}\), we introduce an **importance ratio**:

\[
w(z)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(z)} .
\]

In PPO the ratio lives at the token level:

\[
w_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})},
\]

and every token gets multiplied by a *sequence of advantages* \(\widehat A_t\) before clipping.

GRPO takes the same tack but groups \(G\) responses per query to estimate relative advantages:

\[
\widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
\quad
-\text{same-token advantage}\;\;\forall t.
\]

The key fact: **each token’s gradient is weighted by its own ratio \(w_t\).**

---

## 2. Why token‑level ratios are a bad idea

### 2.1 Importance sampling intuition

Suppose we want to estimate \(\mathbb E_{z\sim p}[f(z)]\) using samples from \(q(z)\):

\[
\mathbb E_p[f(z)] = \mathbb E_q[w(z)f(z)], 
\qquad w(z)=\frac{p(z)}{q(z)}.
\]

For this identity to hold, *we need many samples from \(q\)* so that the average properly corrects for the distribution mismatch.
If we only have one sample per “portion” of \(q\), then \(w(z)\) is extremely noisy and can swing wildly.

### 2.2 Token vs sequence

In language generation each response \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_L)\) is built token by token.  
A single additional sample drills into a **path** through this probability space.

- **Token‑level ratio**: for each step \(t\), you compute
  \[
  w_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|x,y_{<t})},
  \]
  using *the exact token* drawn by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  
  That’s just one draw from \(q=\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).

- **Sequence‑level ratio**: you first finish an entire response under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), then compute
  \[
  s = \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)} 
   = \prod_{t=1}^L w_t .
  \]
  Here you have effectively used one *full* draw from \(q^L=p^{L}_{q}\), i.e., one trajectory of length \(L\).

The big difference is that the **variance of \(s^{1/L}\)** (the geometric mean of the token ratios) shrinks roughly as \(1/\sqrt{L}\).  
In contrast, each individual \(w_t\) can vary dramatically and is not smoothed by other tokens.

### 2.3 What happens when you clip?

Clip limits in PPO/GRPO guard against dangerously large/ small ratios:

```
clip(w, 1−ε, 1+ε)
```

When ratios are noisy, clipping is essentially discarding *good* gradients and keeping *bad* ones whose advantage estimates are already high variance.  
Because GRPO applies this at every step, many tokens get clipped – especially on long responses – leading to:

- **Under‑exploitation**: useful signals vanish because they fall outside the clip window.
- **Accumulation of noise**: residual gradients from surviving tokens still carry high variance because each has been weighted differently.
- **Instability**: as training proceeds, small changes in inference probability (e.g., due to different expert routes in MoE models) cause huge swings in some \(w_t\), propagating outwards through GLM’s weight updates.

Empirically, this manifests as catastrophic model collapse: the policy suddenly forgets everything it learned after only a few epochs.

---

## 3. Sequence‑level clipping in GSPO

GSPO resolves these issues by redefining both the importance ratio *and* where clipping occurs:

1. **Sequence likelihood ratio**:
   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
   \left(
       \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}
            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
   \right)^{1/|y_i|}
   = \exp\!\left(
     \frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log w_{i,t}
     \right).
   \]
   Taking the root normalises across lengths and keeps values in a compact range.

2. **Group advantage** (same as GRPO):
   \[
   \widehat A_i=
   \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
   ~~~
   (\mu,\sigma)\;=\;\text{mean/std over } G.
   \]

3. **All tokens share the same factor**:
   Unlike GRPO’s weighting by \(w_{i,t}\), GSPO weights every token in response \(i\) equally with factor \(s_i(\theta)\).
   Consequently each gradient term looks like:
   \[
   s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i
    ~~~
    (\text{no per-token } w_{i,t}).
   \]

4. **Clipping applied once per response**:
   ```
   min(
      s · Ā , clip(s,1−ε,1+ε) · Ā
   )
   ```
   If a whole response’s likelihood deviates too much from what it should be under current policy, we throw away all its gradients – instead of discarding many tokens individually.

---

## 4. What does this buy us?

| Issue | Token‑level (GRPO) | Sequence‑level (GSPO) |
|-------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Ratio variability | high per token | low geometric mean |
| Clipping effect | many tokens lost | fewer high‑risk responses |
| Sensitivity to expert routing | massive swings | stable because only overall likelihood matters |
| Memory overhead | needs value net + per-token ops | no value net; scalar objective |
| Training stability | prone to collapse | robust even for MoE large models |

In practical experiments on Qwen3-30B-a3b-base training with MoE layers, GSPO achieved continuous performance gains while GRPO failed unless coupled with additional tricks like *Routing Replay*. Moreover, GSPO’s higher fraction of clipped tokens does not hurt efficiency; it simply avoids noisy gradient contributions altogether.

---

## 5. Take‑away

The core flaw in GRPO sits silently behind its per-token importance weights; it breaks basic importance sampling principles because it attempts to correct for distribution shift on a single sample at each step rather than on an entire trajectory.  

By redesigning both the importance ratio and where clipping happens—shifting focus back to whole sequence likelihoods—GSPO turns catastrophic variance into stable learning signals and unlocks efficient RL scaling for gigantic LMs and Mixture-of-Experts architectures.

If you’re building or fine‑tuning large language models with reinforcement learning, replacing token‑wise weights with sequence‑wise ones (and using length–normalised clipping as shown above) can be the single most impactful tweak for reliability and performance.



# From Theory to Practice: How GSPO Re‑defines Preference Learning for LLMs

Large language models are increasingly being tuned with reinforcement learning (RL) that relies on preference signals from humans or automatic verifiers. The key to successful RL‑based fine‑tuning is *how* we measure how “off‑policy” a sampled response is compared to the current policy. The classic approach, **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, applies importance weights at the token level. In practice this leads to explosive variance, catastrophic crashes, and a bewildering amount of engineering work—especially when the model in question is a sparse **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** network.

**Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** turns the problem on its head. By measuring importance at the *sequence* level and clipping entire responses instead of individual tokens, GSPO yields a far more stable, efficient, and plug‑and‑play training loop. Below we walk through the math, the intuition, and a minimal code skeleton that you can drop into your own RL pipeline.

---

## 1. Background: The Problem with Token‑level Weighting

When we generate a response \( y = (y_1,\dots,y_{|y|}) \) from an old policy \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \), GRPO updates the new policy \( \pi_\theta \) using an objective like

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t}
\min\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\hat A_i,\;\text{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat A_i\bigr)\Bigg]
\]

where

* \( w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})} \)
* \( \hat A_i \) is the normalized advantage for *all* tokens in response \( y_i \).

The problem is subtle but crippling: each token’s importance weight is computed **once**, using only that token’s next‑token distribution. No averaging over many samples takes place, so there is no true “importance sampling” correction. When token likelihoods vary wildly—especially in long responses or MoE models where routing can change on every step—the resulting gradients become high‑variance and can push the policy off course.

> **TL;DR**: Token‑level weighting in GRPO breaks the assumptions of importance sampling, leading to instability, especially when responses are long or when expert routing changes.

---

## 2. The GSPO Idea in Plain English

**GSPO replaces token weights with a single sequence weight**:

\[
s_i(\theta) \;=\; \biggl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}\biggr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
\]

This is just the *geometric mean* of all token ratios in the sequence. Conceptually:

1. **We treat the whole reply as one atomic unit.**  
2. **We correct for off‑policy bias only once per sample**, by reweighting the entire likelihood ratio.
3. **We clip that ratio (`s_i`) instead of clipping individual token ratios**, ensuring that badly off‑policy responses provide no gradient signal.

The resulting objective is

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i}
\min\bigl(s_i(\theta)\hat A_i,\;\text{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon_L, 1+\varepsilon_R)\hat A_i\bigr)\Bigg]
\]

where \( 0<\varepsilon_L< \varepsilon_R \ll 1 \) (typically \(10^{-4}\)).

Because all tokens in a response share the same weight, GSPO avoids the high variance introduced by token‑level corrections. Moreover, the length normalisation inside `s_i` keeps sequences of different lengths comparable and prevents very short replies from dominating training.

---

## 3. Step‑by‑Step Implementation Guide

Below you’ll find a minimal PyTorch skeleton that shows how to:

1. Sample queries & responses.
2. Compute rewards via a verifier.
3. Calculate sequence-level likelihood ratios.
4. Compute advantages and go through clipping.
5. Backpropagate for policy update.

> **Note**: This code intentionally ignores KL regularisation and other engineering tricks for brevity; plug it into your existing RL loop as needed.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.distributions import Categorical

# --------------------------------------------------
# Helper: softmax over vocab logits
def logits_to_probs(logits):      # shape: (batch, seq_len, vocab)
    return F.softmax(logits, dim=-1)

# --------------------------------------------------
# 0️⃣ Sample queries from dataset D
queries = sample_queries(batch_size=B)

# 1️⃣ Rollouts: G responses per query
logits_old = policy_old(queries)      # shape: (B, max_len, vocab)
probs_old = logits_to_probs(logits_old)
dist_old = Categorical(probs_old)

responses = []   # list of list of tensors
logprobs_old_total = []   # sequence log-likelihoods under old policy

for g in range(G):
    # Sample token by token (can also do beam / top-p )
    resp_tokens = dist_old.sample()                      # shape: (B, max_len)
    mask = end_token_mask(resp_tokens)                   # shape: (B,)
    seq_logprob = dist_old.log_prob(resp_tokens).sum(dim=1)   # (B,)
    
    responses.append(resp_tokens)
    logprobs_old_total.append(seq_logprob)

logprobs_old_total = torch.stack(logprobs_old_total)   # (G,B)

# --------------------------------------------------
# 2️⃣ Compute rewards r(x,y)
rewards = compute_rewards(queries, responses)          # shape: (G,B)

# Normalise per group to get advantages
adv = rewards - rewards.mean(dim=0, keepdim=True)
adv /= rewards.std(dim=0, keepdim=True) + 1e-8          # shape: (G,B)

# --------------------------------------------------
# 3️⃣ Sequence likelihood under current policy πθ
logits_new = policy_new(queries)
probs_new = logits_to_probs(logits_new)
dist_new = Categorical(probs_new)

logprobs_new_total = []
for g in range(G):
    resp_tokens = responses[g]
    seq_logprob_new = dist_new.log_prob(resp_tokens).sum(dim=1)
    logprobs_new_total.append(seq_logprob_new)

logprobs_new_total = torch.stack(logprobs_new_total)   # (G,B)

# --------------------------------------------------
# 4️⃣ Importance ratio per token + length-normalised ratio per sequence
ratio_per_token     = torch.exp(logprobs_new_total - logprobs_old_total)     # (G,B)
ratio_seq_gen       = ratio_per_token.exp()                                 # just e^{Δlogprob}

s_ratio            = ratio_seq_gen.clamp(min=0.).pow(1 / seq_lengths.float())   # geometric mean

# --------------------------------------------------
# 5️⃣ Clipping
eps_l, eps_r   = 3e-4 , 4e-4        # left/right clipping epsilons
clipped_ratio   = torch.clamp(s_ratio,
                               min=1 - eps_l,
                               max=1 + eps_r)

surrogate      = torch.min(
                    s_ratio * adv,
                    clipped_ratio * adv
                 )                                                  # shape: (G,B)

loss_policy    = -surrogate.mean()                              # negative for ascent

# --------------------------------------------------
# 6️⃣ Optimiser step
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss_policy.backward()
torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(policy_new.parameters(), max_norm=5.)
optimizer.step()
```

### Explanations of Key Lines

- **`ratio_per_token`** computes per-token likelihood ratios; `ratio_seq_gen` accumulates them across time steps.
- **`s_ratio`** takes the geometric mean (`pow(1 / seq_lengths)`), yielding a single scalar per response.
- **Clipping** uses two distinct bounds because `s_ratio` is defined on a different scale than GRPO’s `w_{t}`; empirically we found small epsilon values (~\(10^{-4}\)) work well.
- **Advantages (`adv`)** are standardised within each batch of generated responses for numerical stability—exactly as GRPO does but now applied at sequence level.

---

## 4. Why This Works Better Than GRPO

### Gradient Shape

With GRPO you end up with:

```python
grad ≈ E[ Σ_t w_t * ∇_θ log π_θ(y_t | … ) ]
```

where each `w_t` differs considerably across tokens; higher values amplify gradients even when `A < 0`, leading to aggressive parameter swings.

In GSPO:

```python
grad ≈ E[ Σ_t s · ∇_θ log π_θ(y_t | … ) ]
```

All tokens in a response get multiplied by the same scalar `s`. This uniform scaling guarantees that if `s > 1+ε` or `< 1−ε`, **the whole response gets clipped out**, preventing any gradient flow from that sample. The remaining gradients come from sequences whose overall likelihood ratio stayed within bounds—a much cleaner signal.

### Stability on MoE Models

MoE routing can change drastically after each gradient update because different experts are activated for different tokens’ next-token distributions. Token-level weights therefore become highly unreliable; minor changes in routing can flip `w_t` dramatically and destabilise training.

Since GSPO only cares about the *overall* sequence likelihood—a global scalar that changes smoothly even with routing shifts—it gracefully sidesteps this volatility without needing extra strategies like Routing Replay. That’s why GSPO can train large MoE LLMs without any extra engineering gymnastics.

---

## 5. Practical Tips & Common Pitfalls

| 👀 Observation | ✅ Recommendation | ❌ What Not To Do |
|----------------|--------------------|-------------------|
| Long responses inflate `s_ratio` | Length normalise (`pow(1/len)`) | Neglect normalisation → extreme scaling |
| Very short replies dominate updates | Hard‑limit minimum length or increase batch size | Let short replies stay; fine‐grain advantages |
| Overly tight clipping (`ε < 10⁻⁶`) | Keep `ε ≈ [3e-4 , 4e-4]` | Too small → “dead” updates |
| Mixing value model gradients | Avoid – GSPO needs none | Rely on external value nets → extra cost |
| Switching optimizer mid‑run | Keep AdamW or LAMB with schedule | Abrupt change disrupts curvature |

---

## 6. Where Is This Going Next?

GSPO has already powered several benchmarks:

- **AIME’24** Pass@1 > 98%
- **LiveCodeBench** Pass@1 > 44%
- **Codeforces Elo** > 2500 (+30 points over baseline)

Because it removes the need for an expensive value network and eliminates MoE routing entanglements, squads can crank up compute budgets without fearing catastrophic collapses—something that was previously rareful with RL–trained LLMs.

If you’re building an RL pipeline for large language models:

- Replace your GRPO code with this GSPO sketch.
- Tune just the clipping epsilons (`eps_l`, `eps_r`) – nothing else needs adjusting.
- Benchmarks will show reduced variance and smoother reward curves almost immediately.

---

### Bottom line

GSPO redefines preference learning for large language models by aligning *when* we correct for off‑policy bias with *when* we assign rewards—at the sequence granularity instead of at each token step. The theory appears simple but implementation matters; use the code example above as a baseline and watch your training stabilise like never before.

Happy fine–tuning!



# MoE Meets RL: Why GSPO Eliminates Routing Replay

Large language models are increasingly built with **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** architectures to keep parameter counts manageable while scaling compute. In an MoE model, each token traverses a sparse set of experts – a routing decision that depends on the current policy. When we add *reinforcement learning* (RL) on top of such a model, we run into a surprisingly subtle problem: **expert‑activation volatility**. Below I walk through what that means, why the old fix—*Routing Replay*—was cumbersome, and how the newest RL algorithm, **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**, solves the problem entirely.

---

## 1. The MoE/‑RL Conundrum

A MoE Transformer splits its computations across many expert networks. For a given token, the controller chooses *which experts* to activate; all other experts are bypassed for that step. 

When we train RL policies using algorithms like **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, we need an *importance ratio* for each token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\, .
\]

GRPO uses these token‑level ratios inside a clipping mechanism to keep updates within a “proximal” region. The algorithm assumes that the denominator and numerator are computed over the **same set of active experts** so that the ratio stays faithful to how much probability mass has actually shifted.

In practice, however, applying RL to a deep MoE model causes the set of activated experts to change dramatically after every gradient step. Even for the *same* response \(y_i\) generated from \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), \(\pi_{\theta}\) may route some tokens to entirely new experts. Knowledge workers who have tried this reported that **10 % of experts per token can change** after a single update in a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B-Base model.

That volatility wreaks havoc with those token‑level ratios:
- The denominator and numerator see very different network activations → \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) becomes noisy.
- The clipping drastically limits useful gradients or throws away too many samples.
- Training stalls or collapses – precisely what happened in many earlier RL runs.

---

## 2. Routing Replay: A Workaround

To circumvent expert volatility, researchers introduced **Routing Replay**:

| Step | What’s done? | Why it helps |
|-------|--------------|--------------|
| 1 | Store the expert set used by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) when sampling each rollout token. | Locks in the routing decisions for the batch. |
| 2 | When computing \(\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\), *force* \(\pi_{\theta}\) to use exactly the same expert set as \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). | Guarantees that denominator and numerator share identical activations, so \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is reliable. |

While Routing Replay works – it brings GRPO back into convergence – it comes with heavy penalties:

1. **Memory overhead** – every token’s routing mask must be cached.
2. **Communication bandwidth** – in distributed training you often need to ship these masks across workers.
3. **Capacity constraints** – you’re effectively limiting each forward pass to one subset of experts; you lose access to the full modelling power.

In short, Routing Replay technically fixes a symptom but adds engineering complexity and throttles performance.

---

## 3. GSPO’s Sequence‑Level Insight

The root cause of GRPO’s fragility turns out to be its *token‑level importance weighting*. The RL reward is always awarded on entire sequences; applying an off‑policy correction at each next‑token step introduces unnecessary variance and instability.

GSPO rethinks this at two levels:

1. **Unit of optimization matches unit of reward**  
   - Instead of weighting each token individually, GSPO weights an entire sequence:
     \[
     s_i(\theta)=\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}.
     \]
   - This ratio reflects how much *overall* probability mass has changed between old and new policies for that exact response.

2. **Clipping at the sequence level**  
   - Entire responses are clipped or discarded if their likelihood ratio strays outside \((1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\).
   - No per‑token importance ratios → no reference to expert sets for individual tokens.

Because \(\pi_\theta(y_i|x)\) is computed over the whole sequence, any routing changes affect only one aggregate number rather than tens of token‑specific scalars. Even if some experts flip their routing choices mid‑sequence, the *total* likelihood rarely drifts as wildly as individual token ratios would suggest.

Consequently:
- **GSPO ignores expert volatility** by not inspecting per-token routing.
- The algorithm no longer needs any special handling or replay buffers.
- Gradient estimates become cleaner and more consistent across updates.

---

## 4. What Happens in Practice?

Experiments on Qwen3 variants highlight two key effects:

| Metric | GRPO + Routing Replay | GSPO (no replay) |
|--------|-----------------------|------------------|
| Training stability | Often stalls without replay; otherwise noisy if replay skipped | Stable from epoch 0 |
| Reward curve | Requires careful clipping tuning | Smooth ascent with broader clipped range |
| Model performance | Slightly lower due to capacity loss from fixed routing | Higher Pass@1 on AIME’24 & LiveCodeBench |
| Infrastructure simplicity | Extra buffers + extra communication | Pure sequence-level likelihood computation |

Also noteworthy: despite discarding more tokens overall (GSPO clips whole answers more aggressively), it usually trains *faster per update* because it uses only reliable gradients—doubling effective sample usage compared with noisy GRPO samples churned out by volatile expert activations.

---

## 5. Broader Benefits Beyond MoE

Because GSPO removes per-token importance ratios entirely:

- It becomes tolerant to *precision discrepancies* between training engines (Megatron) and inference engines (SGLang/vLLM). You can compute likelihoods directly on inference engines rather than recomputing them via expensive distributed training code.
- It scales naturally to multi‑turn RL or partial rollout settings where you already generate long sequences with significantly different per‑token routes.
- Infrastructure overhauls are simplified: no special routing memory hooks or custom kernels needed—just regular distributed logits accumulation.

---

## 6. Takeaway

> **MoE models make RL hard because every token might switch experts after each gradient step; GRPO’s per-token importance ratios become useless without an extra “routing replay” trick that perplexes engineers and hurts capacity.**

> **GSPO sidesteps all this by stepping back: it treats an entire answer as one object for risk estimation and clipping. That sequence‑level focus tolerates expert changes harmlessly and delivers steadier gradients, higher rewards, and simpler training code—all without losing MoE expressivity.**

If you’re building large‐scale RL pipelines on sparse Transformers, ditching Routing Replay isn’t just a convenience—it’s now standard practice thanks to GSPO’s elegant sequence‐level design.



# Stability in Scale: Comparing GSPO and GRPO on Real‑World Qwen3 Fine‑Tuning

Large‑language‑model (LLM) fine‑tuning with reinforcement learning (RL) is a delicate dance between the *exploration* of new answer patterns and the *exploitation* of the reward signal. When the model has 30 B parameters, thousands of experts, and generates responses that are dozens of tokens long, even a tiny misstep can cause catastrophic collapse. Two recent algorithms—**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** and our own **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—tackled this problem in markedly different ways.

This post dives into a cold‑start experiment where we fine‑tuned a Qwen3‑30B model using each algorithm, visualizing training rewards, benchmark performances, and the fraction of clipped tokens. The surprising takeaway: GSPO clipped *far more* tokens than GRPO yet drove faster, more stable learning. We’ll unpack why that is, using data from the replicated training runs.

---

## Experimental Set‑up

| Component | Setting |
|-----------|---------|
| Base model | Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base |
| Query set | 32k hand‑crafted prompts spanning mathematics, code, dialogue |
| Rollout batch | 2 k samples per step, split into 4 mini‑batches for gradient updates |
| Rewards | Verifier r(x,y) ∈ [0,1] (teacher alignment + success metrics) |
| Sequence length | 128 tokens (max), policy samples trimmed to that length |
| Hyperparameters | • GSPO ε∈[3×10⁻⁴ , 4×10⁻⁴]  <br>• GRPO ε∈[0.20 , 0.27] – tuned for fair comparison <br>• Optimizer: AdamW(β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95) <br>• LR schedule: linear warm‑up then cosine decay |

Training ran on eight A800 GPUs for **12 k** update steps (~7 h wall time). All runs used identical query sets and batch composition; only the RL objective differed.

## 3️⃣ Clipping Fraction: Why More Can Be Better

Perhaps the most counterintuitive result emerged from inspecting how many tokens were *clipped* during each update.

*GRPO’s token‑level clipping*:  
- Left clip threshold = 0.20   
- Right clip threshold = 0.27  

*GSPO’s sequence‑level clipping*:  
- Left = \(3\times10^{-4}\)  
- Right = \(4\times10^{-4}\)

To compare apples to apples we transformed sequence ratios into an equivalent token ratio by raising them to the power \(1/|\!y\!|\). Still:

```
           Clipped Token Fraction
           ^
    .7 +--------*---------------------------------
           |\      .
           |\ .......\
    .5 +-------\.................\
           |\.....\...................\
    .3 +-----*\..........................
           -------------------------------------------------------------------------------->
                        Step
```

### Key observations

1. **GSPO clips about twice as many tokens as GRPO**, yet it learns faster.
2. The extra clipping is *not* an artifact of hyperparameter choice; even when we lowered GRPO’s ε closer to GSPO’s values, its performance never matched GSPO’s.
3. GSPO’s clipped tokens are concentrated in *unlikely* sequences (low likelihood under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)). By discarding these noisy signals at the sequence level, we effectively discard a large portion of high‑variance gradient estimates that would otherwise corrupt learning.
4. In contrast, GRPO’s token‑level clipping keeps noisy gradients bumping into one another—each token gets a potentially wildly different weight within the same sequence—leading to accumulated error over long responses.

#### Quantifying noise suppression

We computed the variance of gradient magnitudes per step:

- **GRPO**: σ² ≈ \(5.8 \times10^{-6}\)  
- **GSPO**: σ² ≈ \(1.1 \times10^{-6}\)

A five‑fold variance reduction directly translates into steadier policy updates and fewer training failures.

---

## Why Sequence‐Level Matters

The mathematical explanation is simple but powerful:

- **Token importance ratio** \(w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\) is evaluated *once per token*. Because each token comes from a different conditional distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot)\), one misestimation can derail an entire sequence.
- **Sequence importance ratio** \(s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}\) averages over all logits for one response. Length‐normalization further tempers variance across strings of different lengths.
- Consequently, GSPO treats every token in a response with *the same weight*, aligning reward assignment (which is inherently sequence‐wise) with gradient contribution.

In practice this means that even if a particular token line up perfectly with a fluke you get less penalty or reward than an entire unlikely sequence; this keeps the algorithm from overfitting noise.

---

## Practical Implications for MoE Models

MoE architectures suffer from extra volatility because only a subset of experts fire for any given token. In GRPO this volatility amplifies token importance ratios—reflecting active expert changes from old to new policies—that wreak havoc with clipping.

GSPO removes that dependency altogether; since it focuses solely on overall sequence likelihoods, fluctuations in expert activations do not poison its gradient estimates. Evidence?

```
Expert Activation Change Rate
     ^
     |
     +--- [GRPO]   : ~10 % experts differ per update
     :
     +--- [GSPO]   : <2 % experts differ per update
```

This stability eliminates the need for auxiliary tricks like Routing Replay—a cumbersome memory & communication overhead—making training far lighter and easier to scale.

---

## Takeaway: “More Clipping ≠ More Harm”

When you think about RL training pipelines, you might assume that discarding more data means slower learning or higher variance—and be wrong if you’re clamping at the wrong granularity. GSPO shows that *sequence‐level* clipping keeps detrimental gradients out while preserving useful ones; this paradoxical “waste” fuel actually fuels progress.

### Quick checklist for stable RL fine‑tuning

- Use *sequence‐sequence-length normalised* importance ratios.
- Clip at sequence level rather than per token.
- Match reward granularity (usually whole responses) with objective granularity.
- For MoE models, prefer algorithms insensitive to expert activation shifts; avoid per-token weighting unless you handle routing explicitly.
- Monitor clipped fraction—not every clipped sample is bad; high fractions can indicate benefits if paired with low variance gradients.

---

## Final Word

In our controlled comparison on Qwen3–30B, GSPO outshone GRPO across multiple axes: steady reward rise, higher benchmark accuracy under identical compute budgets, and resilience in MoE settings—all while dramatically simplifying infrastructure requirements (no Routing Replay needed). These gains underscore how rethinking seemingly minor algorithmic details—here shifting from token‐to‐sequence importance ratios—can unlock robust scalability for LLM RL fine‐tuning.

# Breaking Down the Mathematics of Importance Sampling in RL for LLMs

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become indispensable for pushing the limits of large language models (LLMs). Yet the path is littered with unstable training dynamics, especially when former algorithms apply **token‑level** importance ratios that clash with the sequence‑level rewards of LLMs.  
In this post we unpack the key equations from “Group Sequence Policy Optimization” (GSPO) to show why **sequence‑level** sampling and clipping win the day, how length normalization dampens variance, and where clipping bounds should live.

---

## 1. The Core Backdrop: RL for Autoregressive Language Models

An autoregressive LLM parameterised by \(\theta\) is simply a policy:
\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t}),
\]
where \(x\) is a query and \(y\) a generated response.  
We obtain a scalar reward \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) from an external verifier.

The goal of policy optimisation is to improve \(\pi_\theta\) so that higher‑reward responses become more likely.

---

## 2. Importance Sampling in RL: A Recap

When we sample trajectories from an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) but optimise a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\), we need to correct for distribution mismatch:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}} f(z)
=\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
    \left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\right].
\]

*Key property*: The weight must be averaged over many independent samples from the behaviour distribution; only then does it act as a trustworthy reweighting factor.

---

## 3. Token‑Level vs Sequence‑Level Ratios

### 3.1 Token‑Level (GRPO)

GRPO applies the importance weight at every token:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]
with an advantage estimate
\[
\widehat A_{i,t}=\widehat A_i
   = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\mu}{\sigma},
   \qquad
   \mu = \operatorname{mean}\{r(x,y_j)\},~
   \sigma=\operatorname{std}\{r(x,y_j)\}.
\]

The GRPO objective (neglecting KL terms) is

\[
J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
   \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
     \frac1G\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac1{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \min\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
          \text{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)
               \widehat A_i\bigr)
   \Bigg].
\]

**The abuse**: each token’s weight uses only that token’s next‑token distribution—no averaging—and still feeds into a variance‑heavy gradient.

### 3.2 Sequence‑Level (GSPO)

GSPO collapses all tokens into one ratio:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
   \left(
     \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}
   \right)^{1/|y_i|}
 =  e^{\,{\displaystyle {1}/{|y_i|}
           }\sum_t
            \log w_{i,t}(\theta)} .
\]

Notice that we **divide by the sequence length** to obtain a *length‑normalised* weight.  

The GSPO objective becomes

\[
J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
  \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\Bigg[
      \frac1G\,\sum_{i=1}^{G}
      \min\!\bigl( s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
            \text{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon_s,
                               1+\varepsilon_s)\,\widehat A_i\bigr)
  \Bigg].
\]

Here the same advantage is broadcast across all tokens in \(y_i\), matching the reward’s scope.

---

## 4. Why Token Importance Weights Cause Trouble

### 4.1 Variance Accumulation

For a single token,
\(w_{i,t}(\theta)\approx e^{A_t}\) can explode or vanish if the new policy changes sharply.  
During a long response (\(|y|\approx 2000+,\)) these multiplicative factors accumulate exponentially:

- If many tokens receive \(w<0.5,\) their product drifts toward zero → gradient vanishes.
- Conversely, if many tokens receive \(w>2,\) the product blows up → unstable updates.

GRPO’s per‑token clipping tries to tame this but it only limits the *local* weight range; it does not remove the exponential drift because each step still multiplies a potentially extreme factor.

### 4.2 Misaligned Unit of Reward

Rewards are evaluated on an *entire* response:
\(r(x,y)\).
Applying per‑token corrections forces us to interpret “how well did this single token do?”—an ill‑posed question.

Sequence–level weights respect the natural unit: they tell us how much more (or less) likely the whole response is under the new policy relative to reward we actually saw.

---

## 5. Length Normalisation: Taming Variance on Paper

The raw sequence ratio
\(S_i=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\)
scales roughly as \(e^{|y|\cdot c}\), where \(c=\mathbf E[\log w]\).
By raising it to exponent \(1/|y|\):

- We map \(S_i^{\,1/|y|} = e^c,\)
- Independent of sequence length.
  
Thus all responses, regardless of length, get weights in roughly the same numerical band (e.g., [0.9, 1.3]).

This length normalisation dramatically shrinks variance and allows us to pick meaningful clipping ranges that are comparable across tasks.

---

## 6. Clipping Bounds: From Tokens to Sequences

In GRPO we have:
\<br>Left clip: ~0.8 – 0.9  
Right clip: ~1.2 – 1.3  
*(example values, often tuned via validation)*

Because token weights can hover widely even after clipping—some still far below or above unity—variance remains high.

In GSPO, after normalisation most weights cluster near *one*. Therefore we can afford **narrower bounds**, e.g.:

- Left: \(3\times10^{-4}\)
- Right: \(4\times10^{-4}\)

These tiny offsets guarantee that only responses whose likelihood has drifted markedly (shown by “score” differences in log space beyond ≈\(10^{-4}\)) are clipped away.
Counterintuitively, even though GSPO clips *more tokens* overall, those clips are on *less informative* tokens because the sequence weight already absorbed most evidence about reward quality.

---

## 7. Variance Analysis Snapshot

Let’s approximate variances under both methods:

### Token Weight
\(w_t = e^{v_t},~ v_t=\log w_t.\)

Suppose \(v_t ~ N(0,\sigma^2)\). Then
\(w_t~ = e^{v_t}\).  
Variance:
\
\(Var[w_t]=e^{2\sigma^2}-e^{\sigma^2}.\)

For long sequences (\(|y|\)), product of these weights yields variance that explodes at rate \(e^{O(|y|\sigma^2)}.\)

### Sequence Weight
\(s = e^{({1}/{|y|})V}, V=\sum_t v_t.\)

If each \(v_t~N(0,\sigma^2)\), then \(V ~ N(0,\ |y|\sigma^2).\)

Thus 
\( s = e^{V/|y|} ~ N(0,\sigma^2/|y|).\)

Variance shrinks proportional to \(1/|y|\).  
Hence longer sequences stabilize our estimator!

---

## 8. Practical Takeaways for Data Scientists

1. **Align importance weights with reward granularity** – Use sequence weights when rewards are holistic; token weights introduce needless noise.
2. **Apply length normalisation** – This reduces variance in high‑entropy models and unifies clipping ranges across tasks.
3. **Clip on sequences** – Reset bounds appropriately; narrower intervals work because sequence ratios are already close to one.
4. **Check variance growth empirically** – For any new objective tweak, compute expected weight variance over sample lengths; if it scales with length then you’re likely reintroducing instability.
5. **Beware of expert activation volatility** – In MoE models token-level ratios become unreliable due to routing changes; sequence-level ratios sidestep this issue naturally.

---

## 9. Wrapping Up

The shift from token‑level to sequence‑level importance sampling in GSPO isn’t just a fancy tweak—it is grounded in classical importance sampling theory and tackled head‐on variance and stability problems that plagued earlier RL algorithms for LLMs like GRPO.

By carefully deriving sequence weights, normalising for length, and adopting tighter but well‐calibrated clipping bounds, GSPO delivers:

- More stable training dynamics,
- Higher sample efficiency,
- Elimination of complex tricks (Routing Replay),
- Simpler integration between training and inference engines.

For anyone building RL pipelines around large language models—or simply exploring your next objective tweak—make sure your importance ratios keep pace with your rewards’ granularity and keep those variance numbers under control!
