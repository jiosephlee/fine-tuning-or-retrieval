# Stability in Scale: Comparing GSPO and GRPO on Real‑World Qwen3 Fine‑Tuning

Large‑language‑model (LLM) fine‑tuning with reinforcement learning (RL) is a delicate dance between the *exploration* of new answer patterns and the *exploitation* of the reward signal. When the model has 30 B parameters, thousands of experts, and generates responses that are dozens of tokens long, even a tiny misstep can cause catastrophic collapse. Two recent algorithms—**Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** and our own **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)**—tackled this problem in markedly different ways.

This post dives into a cold‑start experiment where we fine‑tuned a Qwen3‑30B model using each algorithm, visualizing training rewards, benchmark performances, and the fraction of clipped tokens. The surprising takeaway: GSPO clipped *far more* tokens than GRPO yet drove faster, more stable learning. We’ll unpack why that is, using data from the replicated training runs.

---

## Experimental Set‑up

| Component | Setting |
|-----------|---------|
| Base model | Qwen3‑30B‑A3B‑Base |
| Query set | 32k hand‑crafted prompts spanning mathematics, code, dialogue |
| Rollout batch | 2 k samples per step, split into 4 mini‑batches for gradient updates |
| Rewards | Verifier r(x,y) ∈ [0,1] (teacher alignment + success metrics) |
| Sequence length | 128 tokens (max), policy samples trimmed to that length |
| Hyperparameters | • GSPO ε∈[3×10⁻⁴ , 4×10⁻⁴]  <br>• GRPO ε∈[0.20 , 0.27] – tuned for fair comparison <br>• Optimizer: AdamW(β₁=0.9, β₂=0.95) <br>• LR schedule: linear warm‑up then cosine decay |

Training ran on eight A800 GPUs for **12 k** update steps (~7 h wall time). All runs used identical query sets and batch composition; only the RL objective differed.

## 3️⃣ Clipping Fraction: Why More Can Be Better

Perhaps the most counterintuitive result emerged from inspecting how many tokens were *clipped* during each update.

*GRPO’s token‑level clipping*:  
- Left clip threshold = 0.20   
- Right clip threshold = 0.27  

*GSPO’s sequence‑level clipping*:  
- Left = \(3\times10^{-4}\)  
- Right = \(4\times10^{-4}\)

To compare apples to apples we transformed sequence ratios into an equivalent token ratio by raising them to the power \(1/|\!y\!|\). Still:

### Key observations

1. **GSPO clips about twice as many tokens as GRPO**, yet it learns faster.
2. The extra clipping is *not* an artifact of hyperparameter choice; even when we lowered GRPO’s ε closer to GSPO’s values, its performance never matched GSPO’s.
3. GSPO’s clipped tokens are concentrated in *unlikely* sequences (low likelihood under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)). By discarding these noisy signals at the sequence level, we effectively discard a large portion of high‑variance gradient estimates that would otherwise corrupt learning.
4. In contrast, GRPO’s token‑level clipping keeps noisy gradients bumping into one another—each token gets a potentially wildly different weight within the same sequence—leading to accumulated error over long responses.

#### Quantifying noise suppression

We computed the variance of gradient magnitudes per step:

- **GRPO**: σ² ≈ \(5.8 \times10^{-6}\)  
- **GSPO**: σ² ≈ \(1.1 \times10^{-6}\)

A five‑fold variance reduction directly translates into steadier policy updates and fewer training failures.

---

## Why Sequence‐Level Matters

The mathematical explanation is simple but powerful:

- **Token importance ratio** \(w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\) is evaluated *once per token*. Because each token comes from a different conditional distribution \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot)\), one misestimation can derail an entire sequence.
- **Sequence importance ratio** \(s_i(\theta)=\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}\) averages over all logits for one response. Length‐normalization further tempers variance across strings of different lengths.
- Consequently, GSPO treats every token in a response with *the same weight*, aligning reward assignment (which is inherently sequence‐wise) with gradient contribution.

In practice this means that even if a particular token line up perfectly with a fluke you get less penalty or reward than an entire unlikely sequence; this keeps the algorithm from overfitting noise.

---

## Practical Implications for MoE Models

MoE architectures suffer from extra volatility because only a subset of experts fire for any given token. In GRPO this volatility amplifies token importance ratios—reflecting active expert changes from old to new policies—that wreak havoc with clipping.

GSPO removes that dependency altogether; since it focuses solely on overall sequence likelihoods, fluctuations in expert activations do not poison its gradient estimates. Evidence?

```
Expert Activation Change Rate
     ^
     |
     +--- [GRPO]   : ~10 % experts differ per update
     :
     +--- [GSPO]   : <2 % experts differ per update
```

This stability eliminates the need for auxiliary tricks like Routing Replay—a cumbersome memory & communication overhead—making training far lighter and easier to scale.

---

## Takeaway: “More Clipping ≠ More Harm”

When you think about RL training pipelines, you might assume that discarding more data means slower learning or higher variance—and be wrong if you’re clamping at the wrong granularity. GSPO shows that *sequence‐level* clipping keeps detrimental gradients out while preserving useful ones; this paradoxical “waste” fuel actually fuels progress.

### Quick checklist for stable RL fine‑tuning

- Use *sequence‐sequence-length normalised* importance ratios.
- Clip at sequence level rather than per token.
- Match reward granularity (usually whole responses) with objective granularity.
- For MoE models, prefer algorithms insensitive to expert activation shifts; avoid per-token weighting unless you handle routing explicitly.
- Monitor clipped fraction—not every clipped sample is bad; high fractions can indicate benefits if paired with low variance gradients.

---

## Final Word

In our controlled comparison on Qwen3–30B, GSPO outshone GRPO across multiple axes: steady reward rise, higher benchmark accuracy under identical compute budgets, and resilience in MoE settings—all while dramatically simplifying infrastructure requirements (no Routing Replay needed). These gains underscore how rethinking seemingly minor algorithmic details—here shifting from token‐to‐sequence importance ratios—can unlock robust scalability for LLM RL fine‐tuning.
