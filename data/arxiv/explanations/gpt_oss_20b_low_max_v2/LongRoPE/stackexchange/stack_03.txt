\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the relationship between the extension ratio s and the maximum rescale factor allowed in LongRoPE's search space?
Question:
According to Table 2 (search space), the max lambda_i is s×1.25. Why multiply by 1.25 instead of using exactly s? What effect does allowing a slightly larger lambda have on interpolation and on keeping the positional encoding within reasonable bounds?
Answer:
**Answer**

LongRoPE’s search space is constructed so that the evolutionary algorithm can explore a wide range of interpolation behaviours while still keeping the optimisation tractable.  
For a target extension ratio \(s=L'/L\) each RoPE dimension \(i\) is assigned a rescale factor \(\lambda_i\) (the reciprocal of the factor that actually multiplies the rotation angle).  
In Table 2 the authors state that \(\lambda_i\) can take values from **1.0** (no scaling, i.e. simply extrapolating the original angles) up to **\(s\times1.25\)**, and that \(\hat n\), the token‑position threshold below which no interpolation is applied, can be any of {0, 1, 2, 4,…, 256}.

---

### Why *\(s\times1.25\)* instead of exactly *\(s\)* ?

1. **Linear PI uses \(\lambda=s\).**  
   In plain (linear) positional interpolation the idea is to down‑scale every rotation angle by exactly the factor \(s\).  This makes every new position look as if it were only \(1/s\) as far away as an original position with the same index, which is a very strong contraction for large \(s\).

2. **Interpolation is “crowding”** – when positions are brought too close together, tokens that should be disting­uished by their RoPE vectors start to overlap in value.  This crowding hurts attention and hurts perplexity.

3. **Allowing a slightly larger scale mitigates crowding** – by permitting \(\lambda\) to be as large as \(1.25\,s\) we give the evolutionary search a safety margin: for some dimensions it can choose to *interpolate less* (i.e., a smaller \(\lambda\)) than PI would prescribe or even *extrapolate* (\(\lambda< s\)).  The 25 % buffer gives the search algorithm flexibility to move just enough away from pure PI while still staying in the “same” scaling regime.

4. **Practical necessity** – if \(\lambda_i>s\) the new positions are effectively pushed farther apart than they would be under PI.  This prevents catastrophically small angles that would cause numeric under‑flow or too‑large angles that would overflow when computing sines and cosines.  The 1.25 multiplier was chosen empirically: it’s large enough to let good solutions emerge but small enough to avoid extreme values that would destabilise training or inference.

---

### What does this extra headroom do for interpolation?

- **Richness of solutions** – With the range \([1,\;1.25\,s]\) instead of a single value \(s\), the search can assign *different* rescale factors to different RoPE dimensions depending on whether they carry high‑frequency or low‑frequency information (high‑frequency dims tend to need less scaling).

- **Preserving positional fidelity on early tokens** – Combined with the second non‑uniformity (the token‑position threshold \(\hat n\)), a smaller \(\lambda\) for initial tokens keeps their angles close to what the model was trained on, reducing catastrophic forgetting on short contexts while still giving newer tokens more spread.

- **Stabilising long‑term attention** – Larger \(\lambda_i\) values help prevent positions in very long sequences from collapsing into a few discrete points in angle space, which would make self‑attention diffuse over many unrelated tokens.

---

### Effect on “reasonable bounds”

The paper’s experiments show that with vanilla PI (\(\lambda=s\)) perplexity spikes as soon as you double or quadruple the sequence length because of position crowding.  By contrast, allowing \(\lambda_i\le 1.25\,s\) lets LongRoPE discover
configurations where

* high‑frequency RoPE dimensions are barely scaled (λ≈1), keeping early positional signals sharp,
* low‑frequency dimensions are scaled up to at most \(1.25\,s\), spreading out later positions just enough to avoid collapse but not too far that they become unrelated.

These changes keep all sine/cosine arguments within intervals where numerical precision is maintained and where the Transformer’s attention matrix remains well conditioned—what “reasonable bounds” means in practice.

---

### Bottom line

- The extra **25 % slack** over pure linear PI gives the evolutionary search room to trade between under‑ and over‑scaling across dimensions and token ranges.
- This flexibility directly translates into *better* interpolation: positions stay distinguishable even when you extend from 4 k to millions of tokens.
- Empirically it allows non‑fine‑tuned models to already work at an 8× longer context and gives a solid starting point for extreme extensions like 2048k with only modest fine‑tuning.

So, multiplying by 1.25 instead of using exactly \(s\) is not an arbitrary hyperparameter; it is an intentional design choice that balances expressive power against numerical stability and reflects the empirical observation that slightly looser scaling yields lower perplexity across all tested extensions.(gamma))
