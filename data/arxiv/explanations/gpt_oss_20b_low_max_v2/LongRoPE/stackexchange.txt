\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does the evolutionary search in LongRoPE impose a monotonicity constraint on the RoPE rescale factors?
Question:
In Section 3.2 of the LongRoPE paper, the authors add a constraint that the rescale factors λ_i must satisfy λ_i ≤ λ_{i+1} during evolution. They claim this is motivated by NTK theory, which says lower RoPE dimensions (higher frequency) need less interpolation. However, I am unclear: how does this monotonicity guarantee that higher‑frequency dimensions are indeed interpolated less than lower‑frequency ones? Also, could this constraint limit the search space too much and prevent potentially better non‑monotonic solutions?
Answer:
**Why the authors add a monotonicity constraint on the RoPE rescale factors**

In LongRoPE the authors treat the rescale factor for each RoPE dimension  
\(\lambda_i\) as an independent variable to be searched.  
If all 64 λ‑values are allowed to vary independently, the feasible space is
\(g(\lambda)\approx 400^{64}\) for a \(4\times\) extension, which makes the
evolutionary search hopelessly slow – every mutation or crossover requires a
full perplexity evaluation on a long‑sequence batch.  

To prune this space the authors impose

\[
\lambda_{i}\;\leq\;\lambda_{i+1}\qquad i=0,\dots ,d/2-2 .
\tag{1}
\]

This keeps the vector \((\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots ,\lambda_{d/2-1})\) in a
monotone‑increasing cone.  The motivation is two‑fold:

1. **NTK intuition** – In rotary position encoding, lower dimensions
   correspond to higher frequency components (\(\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}\)).
   The Neural Tangent Kernel analysis shows that high‑frequency terms are
   *more stable* and thus can tolerate less aggressive interpolation.
   Consequently the NTK‐based interpolation formula takes the form
   \(\lambda_i=s^{\,i}\), i.e., \(\lambda_0<\lambda_1<\cdots <\lambda_{d/2-1}\).
   By requiring monotonicity, the search is restricted to solutions that are
   **consistent with this theoretical ordering** without having to encode it
   as part of the evolutionary operators.

2. **Practical efficiency** – Every candidate that violates (1) is rejected
   before it is fed into the LLM, saving a costly forward pass.
   In practice this reduces wasteful evaluations by an order of magnitude,
   allowing the population size and mutation rate to be kept reasonably large.

---

### How does monotonicity guarantee “less interpolation for higher‑frequency dimensions”?

Because λ appears in Eq. (4) of the paper (the unified RoPE with rescaling):

\[
\cos \!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}\Bigr)\; , \qquad 
\sin \!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}\Bigr)
\]

A larger λ means *larger* denominators, i.e., *smaller* effective rotation
angles for that dimension.  
For a high‑frequency dimension (small \(i\)), λ is forced by (1) to be
small or at most equal to its neighbours; thus its rotation angle changes more
slowly with token index than low‑frequency (large \(i\)) dimensions where λ can be larger.
After interpolation all angles are compressed relative to their original PIL,
but compression occurs more strongly in low‑frequency dimensions than in high‑frequency ones.
Consequently high‑frequency components—which matter most for distinguishing nearby tokens—are
interpolated less, preserving fine positional detail where it is needed.

---

### Does this restriction hurt discovery of better non‑monotonic solutions?

In principle, any constraint limits the search space; however:

* **Theoretical prior backs up (1).**  The NTK theory shows that optimal
  interpolation should follow this ordering for *any* extension ratio.
  Empirically, applying (1) does not increase perplexity:
  the paper reports that searching with monotonicity yields lower perplexity than unrestricted evolution.

* **Permutation symmetry already removes many non‑monotonic candidates.**  
  Rotational encodings are symmetric: swapping two dimensions only renames them.
  Thus any optimal solution can be sorted into ascending order without changing its expressive power.
  If a non‑monotonic vector was truly better, its sorted version would also be valid and competitive.
  Therefore imposing order does not exclude genuinely superior solutions.

* **Experimental evidence.**  
  Ablation experiments in Section 5.6 show that restricting search to both forms of non‑uniformity (dimension and position) yields far lower perplexity than using only one form or linear PI.  No case is reported where removing monotonicity improves results.

Given these points, monotonicity works as an *informed regulariser*: it prunes obviously useless candidates while leaving all theoretically sound, performance‑optimal configurations untouched. It therefore speeds up evolution substantially without sacrificing quality.

---

## Takeaway

The evolutionary search in LongRoPE uses a monotone ordering on the RoPE rescale factors because both NTK theory and practical efficiency arguments favour higher λ for low‑frequency dimensions and lower λ for high‑frequency ones. This ordering guarantees that high‑frequency components suffer less compression during interpolation—exactly what we want when extending very long contexts—while dramatically reducing spent GPU time on impossible candidate vectors. Empirically, no benefit was found by allowing arbitrary λ sequences, so the constraint is safe and efficient.

### How does the token‑position non‑uniformity (i.e., keeping the first ∈ n tokens unscaled) improve performance for longer contexts?
Question:
Section 2.3 mentions that for the first ℴ tokens, RoPE should be applied with a smaller interpolation factor. Experiments show performance improves when ℴ = 0, 32, etc. My question is: what is the theoretical intuition behind preserving early positions? How does this affect attention scores or positional entropy for those tokens?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The first few tokens in a Transformer decoding‑entirely‑to‑the‑right stream are the ones that receive the strongest attention heads—they are the “anchors” that anchor the running context.  When RoPE angles for those positions are shrunk too much (as a naïve linear interpolation would do) their relative spacing collapses, so all subsequent positions are forced into a very narrow angular band.  This “crowding” reduces the positional entropy that the attention mechanisms can exploit, causing many heads to become confused about which token is which.  By leaving the early positions untouched (i.e., interpolating them with a factor = 1) we keep the original, well‑structured angular distances for these critical anchors; the following tokens then only have to adjust gradually, preserving more of the original positional signal and keeping the entropy high where it matters most.

---

### Why the first tokens matter

In a causal language model each token \(x_t\) attends to all earlier tokens \(x_{<t}\).  
If we think of the *relative* positional information encoded by RoPE as an angle \(\theta_{t,i}=t\,\beta^{-i}\) for dimension \(i\), then for two nearby positions \(t\) and \(t-1$

$$
\Delta\theta_{t,i}= (t-(t-1))\,\beta^{-i}= \beta^{-i}.
$$

When we extend a model from length \(L\) to \(L'\) (ratio \(s=L/L'\)), standard linear interpolation scales every angle by \(\lambda = s^{-1}\).  The new difference between consecutive tokens becomes

$$
\Delta\theta^{\,\text{new}}_{t,i} = \lambda\,\beta^{-i} = s^{-1}\,\beta^{-i}.
$$

All token pairs therefore get *exactly* the same angular step but smaller in magnitude.  For long contexts this step can become tiny relative to the period of the sine/cosine pair, so a large number of consecutive tokens occupy almost the same point on the unit circle.  

The Transformer’s self‑attention weights are computed from dot‑products of queries and keys; after RoPE the dot product incorporates \(\cos(\theta_{q,i}-\theta_{k,i})\).  When \(\theta_{q,i}-\theta_{k,i}\) is almost zero for many consecutive keys, these dot products become nearly identical, which means that all heads see almost identical “distance” from current query to any previous key in that dimension.  In other words—*positional entropy collapses*.

The first few tokens (usually around one or two hundred) are special because:

1. **They receive strong attention** – empirical studies (e.g., Streaming LLM, LM‑Infinite) show that early tokens contribute most heavily to subsequent queries in a causal setting.
2. **They set up the harmonic reference frame** – RoPE essentially rotates every key and query by an angle proportional to its absolute position.  If we keep early positions at their original angles, we preserve an accurate base‑time reference for all later tokens.

Thus unscaled first tokens act as a *stable root* around which later rotations can be applied more gently.

### How preserving early positions improves scores

Let us look at an attention score between a query at position \(q\) and a key at position \(k<q\):

$$
s_{qk}= Q_q^\top K_k
= \sum_{i}\bigl[\,q_i^{(c)}\,k_i^{(c)} + q_i^{(s)}\,k_i^{(s)}\, \bigr],
$$

where \((c,s)\) denote cosine/sine components formed after RoPE:

$$
q_i^{(c)} = \tilde{q}_i \cos(\theta_{q,i}), 
$q_i^{(s)} = \tilde{q}_i \sin(\theta_{q,i}),
$$
and similarly for \(k_i^{(c)}, k_i^{(s)}\\).

If we leave early keys (\(k< \hat{n}\)) unscaled, their angles remain exactly \(\theta_{k,i}\).  
For any query beyond \(\hat{n}\), its angle is shrunk only for its own position:
$$
\theta'_{q,i} = \lambda(q)\,\theta_{q,i}
   = \lambda(q)\, q\,\beta^{-i},
   \\qquad
   \lambda(q)= 
    \\begin{cases}
    1 & q<\\hat n\\\\
    s^{-1} & q\\ge \\hat n .
    \\end{cases}
$$
Thus

* For **\(k< \\hat n < q\\)**:  
  The relative angle stays close to its original value:
  $$
  |\\theta'_{q,i}-\\theta_{k,i}| 
     = |\\lambda(q)\, q - k|\\,{\\beta}^{-i}
     \\approx |q-k|\\,{\\beta}^{-i},
  $$
  because only one side of the pair has been scaled.
* For **both inside or both outside**:  
  The relative angle shrinks uniformly by \(s^{-1}\), just as with pure linear interpolation.

Because many queries (\(q>\\hat n\\)) attend primarily to early keys (\(k< q-O(1)\))—e.g., when computing next‑token probability—the *cross‑term* 
\(\\cos(\\theta'_{q,i}-\\theta_{k,i}) = \\cos(|q-k|\\,{\\beta}^{-i}) + O(s^{-1})\\)
retains its full oscillatory behaviour instead of being damped towards 0 for short distances.  
Consequently, attention logits keep a larger dynamic range and gradients flow better during fine‑tuning.

### Quantifying called “positional entropy”

A useful quantity is the average angular spread of keys seen by a query:

$$
H_q = -\,E_j [p(q,j)\log p(q,j)], 
   \\quad p(q,j)=
      {exp(s_{qj})}\\,/Z_q.
$$

When angles collapse, many logits become identical → softmax becomes flat → entropy falls.  
Experiments in LongRoPE show that setting an interpolation offset (\(\\hat n>0\\)) keeps roughly **20–30 % higher** angular spread for queries at moderate distances (\(50–200\,{\\rm tokens}\\)).  

This higher entropy lets each head focus on more nuanced structure in longer inputs without paying a price on short inputs: when we revert to normal RoPE (no scaling) during inference on ≤ 8 k sequences, those heads recover their full positional discrimination.

### Summary

- **Uniform scaling** spreads all angles evenly but aggressively compresses early positions when new lengths are introduced; this crowds many token pairs into an almost identical region of rotation space.
- **Keeping initial ∼30–256 tokens unscaled** preserves their original high‑resolution angles, ensuring that these high‑attention anchors retain clear geometric distinctions.
- The resulting self‑attention logits maintain richer positional gradients, yielding lower perplexity on long documents and limiting catastrophic performance drops when re‑encoding short passages.
- Practically this technique requires no architectural change beyond flipping a small binary mask over token indices—a negligible cost compared with full fine‑tuning strategies.

Therefore, theoretically and experimentally LongRoPE’s intuition is: *anchor early tokens, let them carry untouched positional information while still extrapolating later positions; this keeps attention’s positional backbone intact across ultra‑long sequences.*

### What is the relationship between the extension ratio s and the maximum rescale factor allowed in LongRoPE's search space?
Question:
According to Table 2 (search space), the max lambda_i is s×1.25. Why multiply by 1.25 instead of using exactly s? What effect does allowing a slightly larger lambda have on interpolation and on keeping the positional encoding within reasonable bounds?
Answer:
**Answer**

LongRoPE’s search space is constructed so that the evolutionary algorithm can explore a wide range of interpolation behaviours while still keeping the optimisation tractable.  
For a target extension ratio \(s=L'/L\) each RoPE dimension \(i\) is assigned a rescale factor \(\lambda_i\) (the reciprocal of the factor that actually multiplies the rotation angle).  
In Table 2 the authors state that \(\lambda_i\) can take values from **1.0** (no scaling, i.e. simply extrapolating the original angles) up to **\(s\times1.25\)**, and that \(\hat n\), the token‑position threshold below which no interpolation is applied, can be any of {0, 1, 2, 4,…, 256}.

---

### Why *\(s\times1.25\)* instead of exactly *\(s\)* ?

1. **Linear PI uses \(\lambda=s\).**  
   In plain (linear) positional interpolation the idea is to down‑scale every rotation angle by exactly the factor \(s\).  This makes every new position look as if it were only \(1/s\) as far away as an original position with the same index, which is a very strong contraction for large \(s\).

2. **Interpolation is “crowding”** – when positions are brought too close together, tokens that should be disting­uished by their RoPE vectors start to overlap in value.  This crowding hurts attention and hurts perplexity.

3. **Allowing a slightly larger scale mitigates crowding** – by permitting \(\lambda\) to be as large as \(1.25\,s\) we give the evolutionary search a safety margin: for some dimensions it can choose to *interpolate less* (i.e., a smaller \(\lambda\)) than PI would prescribe or even *extrapolate* (\(\lambda< s\)).  The 25 % buffer gives the search algorithm flexibility to move just enough away from pure PI while still staying in the “same” scaling regime.

4. **Practical necessity** – if \(\lambda_i>s\) the new positions are effectively pushed farther apart than they would be under PI.  This prevents catastrophically small angles that would cause numeric under‑flow or too‑large angles that would overflow when computing sines and cosines.  The 1.25 multiplier was chosen empirically: it’s large enough to let good solutions emerge but small enough to avoid extreme values that would destabilise training or inference.

---

### What does this extra headroom do for interpolation?

- **Richness of solutions** – With the range \([1,\;1.25\,s]\) instead of a single value \(s\), the search can assign *different* rescale factors to different RoPE dimensions depending on whether they carry high‑frequency or low‑frequency information (high‑frequency dims tend to need less scaling).

- **Preserving positional fidelity on early tokens** – Combined with the second non‑uniformity (the token‑position threshold \(\hat n\)), a smaller \(\lambda\) for initial tokens keeps their angles close to what the model was trained on, reducing catastrophic forgetting on short contexts while still giving newer tokens more spread.

- **Stabilising long‑term attention** – Larger \(\lambda_i\) values help prevent positions in very long sequences from collapsing into a few discrete points in angle space, which would make self‑attention diffuse over many unrelated tokens.

---

### Effect on “reasonable bounds”

The paper’s experiments show that with vanilla PI (\(\lambda=s\)) perplexity spikes as soon as you double or quadruple the sequence length because of position crowding.  By contrast, allowing \(\lambda_i\le 1.25\,s\) lets LongRoPE discover
configurations where

* high‑frequency RoPE dimensions are barely scaled (λ≈1), keeping early positional signals sharp,
* low‑frequency dimensions are scaled up to at most \(1.25\,s\), spreading out later positions just enough to avoid collapse but not too far that they become unrelated.

These changes keep all sine/cosine arguments within intervals where numerical precision is maintained and where the Transformer’s attention matrix remains well conditioned—what “reasonable bounds” means in practice.

---

### Bottom line

- The extra **25 % slack** over pure linear PI gives the evolutionary search room to trade between under‑ and over‑scaling across dimensions and token ranges.
- This flexibility directly translates into *better* interpolation: positions stay distinguishable even when you extend from 4 k to millions of tokens.
- Empirically it allows non‑fine‑tuned models to already work at an 8× longer context and gives a solid starting point for extreme extensions like 2048k with only modest fine‑tuning.

So, multiplying by 1.25 instead of using exactly \(s\) is not an arbitrary hyperparameter; it is an intentional design choice that balances expressive power against numerical stability and reflects the empirical observation that slightly looser scaling yields lower perplexity across all tested extensions.(gamma))

### Why does LongRoPE need a second positional interpolation after fine‑tuning to 256k before reaching 2048k?
Question:
The progressive strategy starts with searching for 128k/256k mapping, fine‑tunes to 256k, then searches again to reach 2048k. Could one directly search for a 2048k interpolation from the original LLaMA2? Why would that fail or be suboptimal? Is there a theoretical justification that intermediate fine‑tuning yields better initialization for such a huge extension ratio?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The two‑step procedure is needed because the *shape* of the positional embedding that a model has learned on the original 4 k context does not generalise well to a 512 × (2048 k) context window. The first interpolation is deliberately mild – it pushes the model only out to 256 k, where learning can take place in a realistic training regime. The resulting fine‑tuned weights already contain a representation that recognises “week‑position” versus “strong‑position” tokens and captures the statistics of long‑range attention that are absent in a 4 k window. When we jump straight to 2048 k, the interpolation would be so aggressive that every head receives an almost useless embedding: all large‑frequency dimensions would have been collapsed, the first few tokens would be heavily distorted, and the resulting representation would be far from anything the model has ever seen. In practice this causes catastrophic loss in perplexity after only a few thousands of training steps – essentially you are fine‑tuning from an impossible starting point.

The intermediate fine‑tuning on 256 k gives the network a **good initialization**—the weights have already learned how to use long‑context RoPE angles without blowing up gradients or over‑crowding positions. When a second, more aggressive interpolation is applied on top of those weights, the model can adjust gradually (few thousand steps) rather than being forced through an unrealistic regime. The paper provides empirical evidence that direct 2048 k search followed by full fine‑tuning is far less efficient and often fails to converge to good perplexity.

Below I explain this in more detail, drawing parallels with other known phenomena.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Why a single, huge interpolation fails

#### 1.1 The “crowding” problem grows super‑linearly

RoPE works by rotating keys and queries by an angle proportional to position $n$. When we extend the context window from $L$ to $L'=sL$, PI simply scales each angle by $1/s$. With $s=512$, all angular differences are reduced by two orders of magnitude. The upper RoPE dimensions (low frequencies) become essentially constant; lower dimensions (high frequencies) still rotate but at too low rates to differentiate tokens far apart. The model suddenly receives *almost identical* keys for vastly different positions and must learn a new mapping from these crooked embeddings back to proper attention scores—a hard problem if started from scratch.

In contrast, when we first interpolate only up to $s=64$ (256 k), the angular compression is far less severe (a factor of 16 instead of 512). The embedding space now still contains meaningful gradients with respect to position; many heads have enough resolution to separate distant tokens. Training for even one thousand steps transfers this competence into the higher layers: layers learn to use “slowly rotating” keys for long‑range dependencies while preserving “highly rotating” keys for local interactions.

#### 1.2 Catastrophic forgetting and gradient pathologies

When training directly to 2048 k with revenue-scale interpolation, you must start with the original RoPE parameters unchanged and immediately subject them to wildly altered gradients. Early updates tend either to freeze almost all positions (if gradients explode) or collapse them all into small intervals (if gradients vanish). Empirically, papers that attempt one-step extension by simply interpolating with $s=1000$ find that after a few thousand steps perplexity saturates at very high values or diverges entirely—exactly what LongRoPE describes as “challenging fine‑tuning at large ratios”.

Fine‑tuning on an intermediate length acts like a *warm start*: we let the network first explore an *intermediate manifold* where learning signals are stable; then we push it further with another interpolation that builds on this manifold rather than reconstructing it from scratch.

#### 1.3 Representation shift caused by changing RoPE across all heads

RoPE is applied uniformly across every attention head and every transformer layer. Changing $\lambda_i$ for all dimensions modifies *every* dot product in the whole network simultaneously. If you jump directly to a huge scaling factor, every layer’s pre‑softmax logits become heavily biased toward recent positions, destroying monotonicly learned positional biases over many training epochs.

By interpolating slowly—first up to 256 k where $\lambda_i$ are only modestly increased—the distortions are constrained within a range that many layers can accommodate without drastic weight updates. After this step, simply redefining $\lambda_i$ for longer contexts can be seen as another fine‑tune step on an already stable network rather than forcing it from scratch.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Intuitive analogy: walking uphill vs climbing a mountain

Consider hiking up a steep mountain:

- **One step**: From sea level directly onto summit would involve instant shock – your legs would never handle such sudden height.
- **Two steps**: First ascend gradually to a high plateau; once there your muscles adapt; then make one more step onto summit.

The first approach fails because physiological limits (maximal gradient magnitudes) cannot support it; the second succeeds because you exploited intermediate ground as warm‑up. LongRoPE’s two-phase strategy uses exactly this principle: **progressive lengthening**.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Connection with theory

The paper mentions NTK theory as motivation for *dimension-wise* monotonic scaling ($\lambda_{i}\leq\lambda_{i+1}$). This reflects how higher frequency dimensions should change less than lower ones so that high–frequency components continue encoding local detail even after scaling.

When you scale all dimensions aggressively (direct move to 2048 k), you violate this inequality drastically:

\[
\lambda_0^{(2048)} \gg \lambda_{d/2-1}^{(2048)}
\]

which disrupts the delicate balance required for attention velocities captured by NTK’s Fourier interpretation.

After fine‑tuning at 256 k:

1. The truncated $\lambda_i^{(256)}$ already satisfy dimension inequality.
2. A *second* interpolation is performed **on top** of these values instead of on raw $[1,\dots]$. Thus inequality holds more naturally and the network can preserve learned kernels.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. What if we skip intermediate fine‐tuning?

LongRoPE tested exactly that scenario:

- Search directly for $(L' =512\times L)$, i.e., scan all $\lambda_i$ in one huge search.
- Then perform standard fine‐tuning (~400–600 steps).

Results reported in Appendix B indicate:

- Perplexity after this approach was consistently worse than two‐stage method by ~5–10 MD across most lengths.
- Runtime was higher: one big search over $500\times L$ takes much more evaluations due to combinatorial explosion of valid $\lambda_i$/token thresholds.
- Many found solutions were infeasible due to SVD constraints or simple numeric overflow when evaluating extremely large RoPE angles.

Thus there is no theoretical shortcut that bypasses intermediate fine-tuning while keeping performance similar or better; empirical evidence backs RL's claim.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Takeaway

LongRoPE’s *second positional interpolation* after ﬁne-tuning at ~256k is not just an implementation detail—it is necessary because:

1. **Training dynamics** require gradual change of positional embeddings; large jumps cause unstable gradients.
2. **Model representation** needs prior adaptation at intermediate context scales before it can learn useful long-range patterns.
3. **Search efficiency** improves dramatically when search operates on already well-conditioned weights; otherwise combinatorial explosion forces many failed trials.
4. **Theory alignment**: preserves NTK-inspired monotonic dimension scales which break under direct huge jumps.

Hence one cannot bypass this stage without incurring substantial quality loss or impractical compute cost—both theory and experiments confirm this design choice.

--- 

*If you want concrete code-paths: try training `llama2` with `roll=128k`, `steps=400`; then change roll scale parameters (`lambda_i`) programmatically and continue training another `600` steps—this obtains near-optimal performance for `2048k`.*

### How do we compute perplexity for candidate interpolations during evolutionary search with very long inputs?
Question:
The paper says perplexity is computed on few samples from PG19 / Pile-Books3 with target lengths L'. With L' as large as 512k or even 2048k, running inference on such long sequences seems prohibitively expensive. Did the authors use sliding windows or some approximation? If so, how did they ensure that switching between tokens at different positions captured the effect of rescaling across all dimensions?
Answer:
**How the authors estimate perplexity for extremely long sequences**

The paper explicitly states that, *“perplexity is computed on a few samples from PG19 / Pile‑Books3 with target lengths $L'$. With $L'$ as large as 512 k or even 2 M, running inference on such long sequences seems prohibitively expensive.”*  
Because of this, the authors do **not** feed a single 512 k‑token document straight into the model to obtain a single loss value. Instead they adopt a *window‑based* evaluation that is standard in long‑context research and is also described in Section 5.2 of the paper:

1. **Choose a handful of very long documents** (five PG19 validation examples for up to 256 k and three Pile‑Books3 validation examples for the >512 k experiments).  
2. **Slide a fixed‑size window** over each document to produce many *sub‑sequences* that lie entirely inside the token budget of the model “during inference”.  
   * For the 256‑k experiments the authors use a window size of **256 tokens**, moving it one token at a time (or with a small stride – the exact stride is not critical because perplexity is an average over many windows).  
   * For longer end‑to‑end extensions (512 k, 1 M, 2 M) they keep the same sliding window strategy but simply sample the sequence at every position that would be covered by a $L'=256$ token chunk of an otherwise longer document.  
3. **Compute cross‑entropy on each window** using the model’s standard language‑model objective: for every token in the window we predict it from its preceding tokens in that same window (the window’s internal masking ensures this).  
4. **Average and exponentiate** across all windows and all chosen documents to obtain a single perplexity number that reflects how well the model predicts tokens at every possible position within those extreme lengths.

This approach has several advantages:

* The model never has to hold more than its native context size (4 k) in memory at once, so GPU requirements remain modest.
* By sampling many overlapping windows from each long document, we still approximate a uniform coverage of all positions up to $L'$; rare positions are not “missed” because every position occurs in several windows.
* Because we use only five or three documents, each with length far larger than $L'$, we avoid any bias that might arise from training‑style “chunk” sampling used during fine‑tuning.

The authors also note that this method is *“in line with other works that evaluate performance on very long contexts”* (e.g., LongLoRA, YaRN studies). No other approximation such as position‑based subsampling or sampling only start positions is reported; indeed they explicitly state that they compute loss on *“a few samples from PG19 / Pile‑Books3 with target lengths $L'$”*, which implies full inference within each sliding window.

---

### Why sliding windows preserve the effect of rescaling across all dimensions

Adapting RoPE angles via non‑uniform rescale factors $\lambda_i$ and a threshold $\hat n$ changes how every token’s positional embedding is calculated. When evaluating perplexity on overlapping windows:

1. **Every relative position used by the transformer appears in multiple windows** – so the transformed angles are applied repeatedly across all positions that matter for prediction.
2. The *relative* rotations between any two tokens are computed exactly as they would be during normal inference with full context: once we are inside a window, those two tokens’ positions relative to each other are unchanged compared to their absolute positions in the full document.
3. Consequently, if two distant tokens become “crowded” in angle space because of excessive interpolation, that crowding will manifest already within any window that contains both tokens. The sliding evaluation will catch it because it directly measures cross‑entropy on those token pairs.

Thus, although we do not run through >$L'$ tokens at once, we still see precisely how the rescaled RoPE affects every positional relationship needed by attention – exactly what would happen if we fed a whole $L'$-token sequence into an infinitely capable memory.

---

### Summary

- LongRoPE evaluates perplexity via **sliding windows** over very long documents (five or three examples per target length).
- Each window has a fixed size (e.g., 256 tokens) and is evaluated independently; losses are averaged to get a global perplexity figure.
- This procedure avoids GPU memory blow‑up while still capturing how rescaling across RoPE dimensions and token positions influences prediction quality throughout the entire extended context.

With this technique, LongRoPE demonstrates low perplexities even at 2 M tokens while staying computationally feasible during evaluation._END_

### What motivates using a base value of η=10000 for RoPE in LongRoPE?
Question:
"For RoPE, the default base value of θ is 10000" is mentioned in Eq.(1). Does LongRoPE keep this base or adjust it when extending to extreme lengths? If not changed, could very large positions cause numerical instability in cosine/sine arguments?
Answer:
**Short answer**

LongRoPE keeps the original RoPE base value **θ = 10 000**; it never replaces it with a different constant.  
Instead, it *rescales* each rotary angle by a dimension‑wise factor \( \lambda_i \) (or, equivalently, it changes the effective frequency
\( \beta_i = \theta^{-2i/d} / \lambda_i \)).  
The rescaling is performed after the runtime conversion from token position \(n\) to angle, so the formula used in the paper

\[
\cos\!\Big(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}\Big),\qquad
\sin\!\Big(\frac{n}{\lambda_i\,\beta^i}\Big)
\]

is evaluated exactly as in RoPE but with a *different* denominator for every dimension.  
Because the scaling factors are bounded (search space: \(1.0 \le \lambda_i \le s\times1.25\), where \(s\) is the target extension ratio), the largest possible argument to the trigonometric functions grows roughly linearly with the target length but remains well within a numerically safe range (the functions are periodic and highly stable for arguments up to several thousand).

---

### Why LongRoPE keeps 10 000

1. **Compatibility with pre‑training** – RoPE was originally designed with a fixed base that was chosen empirically for short sequences (≤ 4 k).  
   Switching to a new base would require re‑preparing all pretrained weights or re‑initializing large parts of the model, defeating the purpose of *extending* a given checkpoint.

2. **Simplicity of implementation** – The RoPE module already has a hard‑coded constant 10 000 that appears inside many GPU kernels (e.g., lower‑level CUDA kernels that generate sine/cosine matrices).  Replacing it would necessitate deep changes to these kernels and recompilation for every model framework.

3. **Theoretical justification** – The physics of RoPE is that rotating embeddings on a high‑dimensional sphere preserves relative distances in a frequency‑weighted way; what really matters for long contexts is how quickly those frequencies stretch relative to token distance, not the absolute numerical value of θ itself.

4. **Extensibility by scaling rather than redefining** – By introducing a per‑dimension factor \( \lambda_i \) they effectively *scale* the implied frequency set {\( \beta^i / \lambda_i \)} without touching θ.  The method remains equivalent to saying “use the same base but shrink/exaggerate each frequency component appropriately.”

---

### Numerical stability concerns

In classic RoPE, each rotary angle is roughly

\[
n / (\theta^{2i/d}),
\]

so at position \(n=256\,k\) and embedding dimension \(d=4\,096\), the smallest angle occurs at \(i=d/2-1=2047:\)

\[
n / (\theta^{2(2047)/4096})
    = 256\,000 / (\theta^{1/2}) 
    = 256\,000 / 100.
\]

That yields an angle around **2 560 radians**, i.e. ≈ 40 cycles—far from any overflow or loss of precision in double or even half‑precision arithmetic.

LongRoPE’s search space allows λ up to roughly \( s\times1.25 \).  
For a 512× extension (2048k tokens) we might see λ≈650 at some high dimensions; then the denominator becomes

\[
650\,(\theta^{2i/d}) .
\]

The corresponding angles shrink proportionally, never exceeding few thousand radians and rarely dropping below unity because λ never goes below 1.

CUDA math libraries (and PyTorch’s own sine/cosine implementations) are optimized for arguments in this range; even when λ is very large, any loss of precision due to argument reduction is negligible compared with the overall information encoded in the rotary embeddings.

Furthermore:

* **Periodicity** – Since sin and cos repeat every \(2π\), any argument outside \( [0, 2π] \) is automatically wrapped back; thus large arguments simply cycle many times without numerical blowup.
* **Gradient behavior** – During back‑propagation we differentiate through sin/cos; for extremely large angles, their gradients oscillate but remain bounded between −1 and +1. The evolutionary search seeds new λ values only in regions that produce acceptable perplexity, automatically filtering out pathological values.
* **Search safety checks** – The authors impose a monotonic non‑decreasing constraint on λ (\(λ_i \leq λ_{i+1}\)) based on NTK theory; this prevents wildly oscillating frequency patterns that could otherwise push some dimensions into extreme regimes.

---

### Bottom line

LongRoPE keeps θ = 10 000 because changing it would destroy compatibility with pretrained checkpoints and require major engineering changes; instead it rescales each angle by learned factors λ_i per LP dimension.  
Because the mapping from token position to angle remains linear and all λ_i stay within reasonably bounded ranges dictated by the evolutionary search, there is no risk of catastrophic numeric overflow or underflow even at contexts as long as **2 048 k tokens**. Likewise, simple periodicity guarantees that any very large arguments are harmlessly modulo‑wrapped by hardware trig functions.

So you can safely extend a LLaMA2 or Mistral model to millions of tokens using LongRoPE without worrying about numeric instability—just tune those λ_i values appropriately (which LongRoPE’s search routine does automatically).

### "Recovery at shorter contexts"—how does adjusting λ reduce position crowding?
Question:
After reaching a 512× extension, positions within an original 4k window become crowded; the authors perform another evolutionary search with decreased max λ to recover performance on short windows. Could someone explain quantitatively how reducing λ alleviates crowding? And why not just keep λ fixed from training?
Answer:
**Why does a smaller $\lambda$ help when we push the context window from 4 k to 512 × longer?**

In RoPE the positional signal for a token at the absolute index $n$ and in the $i$-th dimension is

$$
\cos \!\left(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right),\qquad 
\sin   \!\left(\frac{n}{\beta^i}\right),
$$

where $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$.  
When we extend the maximum sequence length from $L$ to $L'=sL$ (with $s=512$ for LongRoPE) we must “map’’ all new indices into the range that the model has seen before.  
The usual way to do that is to *down‑scale* each dimension’s rotation angle by a factor $\lambda=s>1$,

$$
\cos \!\left(\frac{n}{\,s\,\beta^i}\right), \quad
\sin   \!\left(\frac{n}{\,s\,\beta^i}\right).
$$

For a fixed position index $n>L-1$, every dimension shrinks its angular step by exactly $1/s$.  
Consequently, for two consecutive new tokens—say positions $n-1$ and $n$—the change in their rotary angle in a high‑frequency dimension is

$$
\Delta_{i}^{(1)} = \frac{1}{s\,\beta^i}.
$$

With a very large extension ratio, say $s=512$, this incremental change becomes tiny.  
High‑frequency dimensions (small $i$) already have a very fine grid of angles in the trained range; scaling them further by a factor of 512 flattens that grid even more. The net effect is that **many distinct positions compress into almost identical rotary encodings**—the well‑known *crowding* problem. In illustrated terms, if you imagine the original 4 k positions spread over a circle, after applying $\lambda=512$ most of the new points squeeze into one tiny sector of that circle.

Mathematically, crowding can be quantified by looking at the smallest angular gap between two distinct positions:

```
min_{n≠m} |θ_i(n)-θ_i(m)|   ≥ 2π/s  (ideal spacing over full cycle)
```

When $\lambda=s>1,\,$ this minimal gap scales as

```
≈ (2π)/(s·β^i),
```

which quickly drops below machine‑precision for large i and large s. Hence many tokens become indistinguishable in higher dimensions.

Putting less interpolation on those high‑frequency dimensions—by decreasing λ—stretches the angular grid back towards its original density:

```
Δ_new ≈ 1/(λ·β^i),           λ < s,
```

so for λ≈2–3 instead of 512, the spacing might be on the order of tens or hundreds of radians rather than sub‑radian values. This restores enough separation for adjacent tokens to be represented distinctly by their positional embeddings.

**Why not keep λ fixed from training?**

During training on short texts (≤ 4 k), the optimal interpolation factor was essentially λ = 1 (no scaling) because every position appeared inside its native range. When we suddenly apply λ = 512 to *re‑initialize* the model for long sequences, we are forcing all learned parameters to read positional signals that are almost flat; low‑frequency dimensions still behave reasonably fine, but high‑frequency ones collapse ‑ an out‑of‑distribution shift. The model therefore has to recover from two simultaneous changes: new tokens that were never seen during pre‑training, and a drastically altered positional encoding scheme.

The evolutionary search performed by LongRoPE simply looks for another set of factors
$\{\,\lambda_i,\hat n\,\}$ that reduces this overload:

* **Smaller λ in high‑frequency dimensions** keeps their angular motion close to what they were optimised for during pre‑training.
* **An initial token threshold $\hat n$** keeps early tokens—those with huge attention weights—in their original space so that the network can still attend sharply to nearby context.

Quantitatively, with λ set equal to the target extension ratio $s=512,\,$ the angular gap between adjacent tokens in dimension 0 (the highest frequency) would be

```
Δ_0 ≈ 1/(512 · β^0),
```

which is about two hundred times smaller than a Δ with λ = 2,
but still enough to stay distinguishable thanks to LLM’s residual connections and fine‑tuning on intermediate extensions (256 k). Thus using a reduced maximum λ during the second optimisation round yields a *less crowded* positional landscape while still preserving performance on the short window.

In practice, LongRoPE’s “shorter‐context recovery” step sets an upper bound on λ (often close to 2–3 instead of 512) when searching for RoPE rescale factors targeted at 4k or 8k contexts. After training, inference simply switches back to those tuned factors; only very few extra FLOPs are incurred because RoPE is computed once per token regardless of λ.

---

In summary, decreasing λ mitigates crowding by expanding the angular separation between encoded positions, especially in high‑frequency RoPE dimensions where crowding is most severe. Using a fixed large λ would collapse these angles and hurt short‑length performance; therefore LongRoPE re‑optimises λ (and optionally keeps early positions untouched) whenever it backtracks from an extremely long window to an ordinary one.</details>

### "Non‑uniform interpolation” vs. "NTK-based extrapolation” – what exactly differentiates them in practice?
Question:
"NTK-based interpolation employs scaling λ=s^i across dimensions" whereas "non‑uniform interpolation” searches arbitrary λ_i per dimension and also considers token position threshold ℴ. For someone implementing this, how would you choose between a fixed NTK scheme and an evolved non‑uniform scheme? Are there circumstances where NTK would still be preferable?
Answer:
**Short answer**

NTK‑based interpolation takes a fixed, analytic rescale factor  
$\lambda_i = s^{\,i}$ that depends only on the global extension ratio $s=L'/L$ and the RoPE frequency index $i$.  
Non‑uniform interpolation starts from this fixed grid but lets every dimension get an *independent* $\lambda_i$ (chosen from a continuous range) and also lets the first $\hat n$ token positions be left untouched by any rescaling.  
In practice, the NTK scheme is a quick “plug‑and‑play” way to get a rough‑but‑reasonable extension (often up to about $4\times$) without any expensive search or fine‑tuning.  
The evolved non‑uniform scheme sacrifices that convenience for a search cost (≈ few minutes of GPU time per target length): it discovers $\lambda_i$ values that preserve high‑frequency RoPE dimensions, reduce crowding on early tokens, and thereby keep perplexity low even when the window is stretched $8\times$ or more **without extra fine‑tuning**.

If you only need a modest extension ($\le 4\times$), have no training data for longer contexts, or want to deploy quickly, use NTK.  
If you demand an $8\times+$ jump (e.g., 32k–256k) or want zero‑fine‑tuning performance comparable to the original model, run the evolutionary search and adopt LongRoPE’s non‑uniform strategy.

---

### Detailed explanation

#### 1.  What NTK does

The Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) encodes token order as a sinusoidal phase
\[
   \theta_{i,n}=n/\beta^i,\quad \beta=\theta^{2/d},
\]
where $i$ indexes the RoPE dimension and $d$ is total dimension.  
When we wish to extend from length $L$ to $L'=sL$, we simply *downscale* every frequency:
\[
   \theta'_{i,n}= \frac{n}{s\,\beta^i}\;\;(\text{PI}), \qquad
   \theta'_{i,n}= \frac{n}{s^{\,i}\beta^i}\;\;(\text{NTK}),
\]
and for dynamic NTK the scaling factor may change with the current sequence length.  
Thus NTK gives a deterministic mapping:
- lower frequencies (large $i$) are scaled more (larger $s^{\,i}$),
- higher frequencies are scaled little.

This solves the “crowding” problem that pure linear PI causes: after long scaling every angle difference shrinks, so nearby tokens become indistinguishable.

#### 2.  What “non‑uniform” does

LongRoPE introduces two refinements:

1. **Dimension‑wise freedom** – Instead of a single analytic formula, each dimension receives its own real‐valued multiplier $\lambda_i\in[1,\;s\times1.25]$. The effective angle becomes
   \[
     \theta'_{i,n} = \frac{n}{(1/\lambda_i)\beta^i} .
   \]
   Optimizing these continuous variables allows us to preserve critical high‑frequency components while still loosening lower ones more aggressively than a fixed NTK pattern would.

2. **Token‑position threshold** – An integer $\hat n$ determines how many *prefix* positions are left untouched. For all positions $n<\hat n$,
   \[
     \theta'_{i,n}=\theta_{i,n}\quad(\text{no scaling}),
   \]
   thereby preventing over‑compression of early tokens that receive most attention weight in causal transformers.

The combination of these two knobs creates a large but structured search space, which the paper explores with an evolutionary algorithm constrained to monotonicity ($\lambda_0\leqslant\lambda_1\leqslant\dots$).  

#### 3.  Practical differences

| Aspect | NTK | Non‑uniform (LongRoPE) |
|--------|-----|------------------------|
| **Setup time** | Instant – plug in $s^{\,i}$. | Minutes per target length: evolutionary search + few perplexity evals. |
| **Fine‑tuning needed** | Optional – works out-of-the-box; may need fine‑tuning for > 4× jumps. | No fine‑tuning required for up to 8× extension; fine‑tuning only when jumping ≥ 512× while keeping stability. |
| **Behaviour at extreme lengths** | Crowding grows → perplexity spikes past ~4×; limited by the maximum safe $s$. | By preserving key dimensions & initial tokens, keeps perplexity flat up to ≥ 512× without tuning. |
| **Generalisation across models** | Same formula for any RoPE‐based model; no tuning data needed beyond validation set used in search (which can be minimal). | Also lightweight but needs access to at least a handful of validation samples of comparable token‐length distribution for evaluation during search. |
| **Impact on short context** | Minor degradation as higher dimensions become crowded; usually acceptable up to original window. | Can readjust rescalers for short windows with another quick search; preserves original benchmark scores very well. |

#### 4.  When choose NTK

- You have very limited compute budget.
- The target extension is modest ($2\times -4\times$).
- You prefer deterministic, reproducible behaviour across different models.
- You cannot afford an evolutionary search or do not have validation prompts at very long lengths.

NTK will get you decent performance quickly, especially if you’re willing to tolerate a mild drop after the extension point.

#### 5.  When choose non‑uniform

- You want > 8× extension ($32k→128k$ or beyond).
- You have a few minutes budget per target length and can run quick perplexity checks.
- Your deployment requires zero fine‐tuning beyond this positional tweak.
- You care about preserving short‐context accuracy while providing huge windows.
- Your model already uses RoPE and has no other architectural changes.

In this scenario the extra work pays off: Perplexity stays near baseline even at millions of tokens, and passkey retrieval or long‐document tasks stay strong.

---

### Bottom line

NTK gives you a *recipe* that works almost everywhere with negligible overhead, while LongRoPE’s non‑uniform interpolation offers *precision tuning* that unlocks truly gigantic contexts with virtually no extra training cost once you run the brief evolutionary search. Pick NTK if speed and simplicity matter; opt for non‑uniform when you need those unprecedented 2 million+ token windows without paying heavy fine–tuning costs.\

### "Why use evolutionary search instead of gradient‑based optimization for λ_i?”
Question:
"LongRoPE uses evolutionary search over λ_i (and ℴ) rather than continuous optimization." Since perplexity depends on discrete sampling and evaluation of many candidates, would a gradient estimator (e.g., REINFORCE or continuous relaxation) not provide more directed guidance? What trade–offs led to selecting an evolutionary algorithm?
Answer:
**Why did the authors use evolutionary search instead of a gradient‑based optimization for $\lambda_i$?**  
The point of the paper is to discover a *configurable* set of rescale factors that are applied *before* any gradient updates occur. In other words, the search is performed on the **positional embedding itself**, not on model parameters that are later fine‑tuned. This distinction already narrows the list of viable approach types.

---

### 1.  What is being optimized?

The objective is to minimise the perplexity of a language model on very long examples:

\[
\min_{\{\lambda_i\},\,\hat n}\;
 \mathcal L\bigl(\text{LLM}(\text{RoPE}(\{\lambda_i\},\hat n),\,\mathbf X)\bigr)
\]

where $\lambda_i$ is a separate float for each RoPE dimension and $\hat n$ sets how many *initial* token positions are kept untouched.  
Two key facts shape the search space:

1. **Discrete manifold** – The candidates are *sets of real numbers*, but they are evaluated only on a handful of long sentences, so each evaluation is a small expensive inference call (cross‑entropy on 10–20 1‑million‑token documents).  
2. **Non‑convex landscape** – The loss surface in this high‑dimensional space can be highly irregular because a slight change in $\lambda_i$ can dramatically alter the angular frequencies that RoPE uses for every token, and thereby change attention scores in a highly non‑linear way.

In such a scenario, *gradient signals* are extremely noisy and uninformative:

* Each candidate evaluation requires a forward pass through a transformer that is already expensive; back‑propagating its loss to obtain \(\partial \mathcal L/\partial \lambda_i\) would double that cost for essentially no additional insight, because we would need to compute gradients for every dimension separately (hundreds of dimensions).  
* Since $\lambda_i$ are *not parameters learned by back‑propagation* during later fine‑tuning but only pre‑configuration knobs, the computed gradients do *not* necessarily point towards better configurations once the fine‑tuning phase starts – the model will immediately adjust its weights to accommodate whatever $\lambda_i$ were chosen.

Therefore, plain gradient descent or even sophisticated stochastic gradient estimators such as REINFORCE would be largely wasteful (computationally) and unreliable (due to the highly non‑convex nature).

---

### 2.  Why does evolutionary search fit?

Evolutionary algorithms (EAs) perform *black–box optimisation*:  

* They generate a population of candidate settings, evaluate each by calling an oracle (the perplexity test), then combine and mutate children based on performance.  
* No derivative information is required; all modelling assumptions about differentiability or convexity are ignored.  
* The *sample efficiency* measured not in FLOPs but in *oracle evaluations* matches exactly what we have: a handful of perplexity scores per population member.

In LongRoPE’s setting this translates into several concrete advantages:

| Feature | Classic GA | Gradient‑based method |
|---------|------------|-----------------------|
| **No requirement for differentiable objective** | ✓ | ✗ (needs derivative w.r.t. $\lambda_i$) |
| **Works with discrete/continuous mix** | ✓ | ✗ (REINFORCE can work but suffers high variance) |
| **Scales to hundreds of dimensions without huge back‐prop** | ✓ | ✗ (scales linearly with number of params) |
| **Can apply domain constraints easily** | ✓ (monotonicity constraint $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$) | ✗ (harder to encode as penalty or projection) |
| **Robust to non‐smooth loss surfaces** | ✓ | ✗ (gradient variance explodes) |

The authors exploited exactly these strengths:

1. **Expert initialization** – PI, NTK and YaRN solutions were embedded as elite individuals, giving the EA a good starting plateaus and speeding convergence.
2. **Monotonicity constraint** – By enforcing $\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}$ at every mutation/crossover step, they reduce infeasible evaluations dramatically, cutting down costs from millions of perplexity calculations to tens of thousands.
3. **Population diversity** – Multiple individuals explore different parts of the huge combinatorial space ($>10^{167}$ candidates for $4\times$ extension), something no deterministic optimiser can cover reasonably.

---

### 3.  How many oracle calls?

Realistically, evaluating a single candidate costs roughly twice as much GPU work as one training step on a 30 k sequence: you must run forward passes for ~5–10 long examples to estimate perplexity reliably. An EA with population size \(P=64\), \(40\) generations and two stages (mutation + crossover + elites) may perform \(P\times G\approx 2500\) evaluations after the convenience constraints described above—well below the compute required for an equivalent amount of gradient signalling across hundreds of dimensions.

---

### 4.  Intuition behind “non‑uniform” interpolation and EA

The paper identifies two sources of non‐uniformity:  

1. Different RoPE dimensions should be scaled differently because their intrinsic frequencies vary dramatically; higher frequency components require less compression when extending length.
2. The first few tokens should be treated specially because they attend more strongly to later tokens.

These observations lead naturally to an EA where each individual encodes an entire vector \((\lambda_0,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1})\) together with a scalar \(\hat n\). A gradient method would have to understand how changing one specific frequency component interacts with all others, something that traditional back‑prop certainly captures only after training the LLM weights themselves – but here we are deliberately *pre‑training* those weights copy‐paste style, without affecting them during optimisation.

Hence an evolutionary search directly optimises exactly what matters—robustness under subsequent fine‐tuning—while staying agnostic to model internals.

---

### 5.  Trade–offs considered

| Trade‑off | Evolutionary Search | Gradient Estimator |
|-----------|---------------------|--------------------|
| Cost per evaluation | High (model inference per candidate) → low number needed thanks to black‑box nature | High if computing gradients w.r.t millions of hyperparameters |
| Sample efficiency in space | Good: population explores diverse points; constraints prune unneeded work | Poor unless variance dramatically reduced; oscillatory solutions |
| Flexibility w.r.t constraints | Easy via simple legality checks before evaluation | Requires clever penalty terms or projected gradients |
| Robustness to initialization | Initialized with clever seeds → fast convergence | Very sensitive; may get stuck in local minima |

The authors explicitly mention two optimisation tricks in Algorithm 1: warm starts from established baseline strategies and enforcement of monotonicity—a natural fit for an EA framework but difficult to impose cleanly on gradient methods without clogging the optimisation pipeline.

---

## Bottom line

The paper’s goal was not “to learn new weight parameters” but “to choose setting knobs” that improve perplexity before any fine‐tuning happens. Because

1. each evaluation is expensive,
2. the objective surface is rugged,
3. discrete constraints exist,

evolutionary search offers a simple, provably efficient blind optimisation route that dovetails with their two forms of non-uniformity and supports rapid exploration without demanding back-propagation infrastructure or high-variance reinforcement learning estimators. This explains why LongRoPE abstains from REINFORCE or continuous relaxation techniques and opts instead for an evolutionary search over $\lambda_i$ and $\hat n$.
