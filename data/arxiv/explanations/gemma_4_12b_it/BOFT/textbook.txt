\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Foundations of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

# The rise of foundation models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has been transformed by the emergence of "foundation models." These are large-scale models trained on vast amounts of data that demonstrate a remarkable ability to generalize across a wide array of tasks. Prominent examples of these models include ChatGPT, which has revolutionized natural language processing, and Stable Diffusion, which has made significant strides in text-to-image generation. The success of these models is largely attributed to their massive scale; for instance, the GPT-3 model contains approximately $175$ billion parameters. 

The significance of foundation models lies in their ability to serve as a versatile base. Instead of building a new, specialized model for every individual task—such as summarizing a specific type of legal document or identifying specific objects in a medical image—researchers can start with a foundation model that already possesses a deep, broad understanding of language, images, or other data modalities. This shift toward using large-scale pre-trained models as a starting point has fundamentally changed how research is conducted, moving the focus from "training from scratch" to "efficient adaptation."

# The cost-prohibitive nature of training from scratch

While foundation models offer immense power, their creation is an incredibly resource-intensive process. Training a model with hundreds of billions of parameters requires an enormous amount of computational power, high-end hardware, and significant time. For most researchers, organizations, and even many large companies, training a foundation model from scratch is prohibitively expensive. The sheer scale of the data and the hardware requirements create a barrier to entry that prevents the rapid development of new base models for every niche application.

Because training from scratch is often infeasible, the primary challenge in the field has shifted toward adaptation. The goal is to take a powerful, pre-trained foundation model and modify it so that it performs exceptionally well on a specific downstream task. This process must be done efficiently. If the adaptation process requires as much computation or memory as training the original model, it defeats the purpose of using a foundation model. Therefore, developing methods that can "fine-tune" or "adapt" these models without retraining them from the ground up is a critical area of modern machine learning research.

# Taxonomy of adaptation methods: Model Finetuning, Adapter Tuning, and Prompt Tuning

To address the need for efficient adaptation, several primary strategies have emerged. These methods are generally categorized based on how they modify the original foundation model to suit a new task.

The first category is model finetuning. In this approach, the underlying architecture of the model remains completely unchanged. Instead of training the entire model, only a specific subset of the existing parameters is updated. This allows the model to learn the nuances of a new task while still utilizing the vast majority of the knowledge stored in the frozen parameters of the foundation model.

The second category is adapter tuning. Rather than modifying the existing parameters of the foundation model, adapter tuning involves inserting new, trainable parameters into the architecture. These "adapters" are typically small modules added between the existing layers of the model. During the adaptation process, only the weights of these added modules are updated, while the weights of the original foundation model remain fixed.

The third category is prompt tuning. This method takes a different approach by keeping the model's parameters entirely frozen. Instead, the researcher attaches additional trainable "prefix tokens" to the input. These tokens are learned during the adaptation process and serve to "steer" the pre-trained model toward the desired output for a specific task. By modifying the input representation rather than the weights, prompt tuning achieves adaptation with very few trainable parameters.

# The advantages of model finetuning: Simplicity and zero inference latency

Among the three primary methods of adaptation, model finetuning is often distinguished by its balance of power and simplicity. Because it works by updating a subset of the weights within the original architecture, it is a very straightforward approach to implementation. It does not require the structural modifications seen in adapter tuning or the complex input engineering sometimes required in prompt tuning.

A major technical advantage of model finetuning is that it introduces zero inference latency. In production environments, "latency" refers to the time it takes for a model to produce an output once it receives an input. Methods like adapter tuning add extra layers to the model's forward pass, and prompt tuning increases the length of the input sequence. Both of these can add computational overhead during the inference phase. However, because model finetuning simply replaces a portion of the existing weights with new ones, the final model's architecture is identical to the original. The model "runs" exactly as it did before, meaning there is no additional time cost when the model is deployed for use.

# Core principles of finetuning: Preserving pretraining knowledge and relational information

The fundamental goal of any finetuning method is to ensure that the model adapts to a new task without "forgetting" the extensive knowledge it acquired during its initial pretraining phase. To achieve this, the finetuned model must remain "similar" to the pretrained model according to specific measurements. 

One common, albeit somewhat ad hoc, way to ensure this similarity is to use a very small learning rate. By making the changes to the weights very small, researchers hope to prevent the model from drifting too far from its original state. However, there is a more principled way to approach this problem by looking at the structured nature of weight matrices. Specifically, researchers have observed that preserving the "relational information" of the weight matrices—such as the pairwise angles between neurons—is crucial for maintaining the semantic knowledge of the model.

This insight leads to the framework of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In OFT, rather than just making small changes to the weights, the neurons in the same layer are transformed by a learnable orthogonal matrix. By using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved throughout the finetuning process. For a pretrained linear layer with weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, the forward pass is modified from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output. By ensuring $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the model can adapt to a new task while maintaining the underlying geometric structure of the pre-trained representations.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principles and Limitations

# The geometry of weight matrices: Pairwise angles between neurons

When adapting large foundation models to specific downstream tasks, a primary challenge is ensuring that the model retains the vast amount of general knowledge acquired during its initial pretraining phase. Standard model finetuning often attempts to achieve this by using very small learning rates, which implicitly keeps the new weight matrices close to the original ones. However, the paper argues that this is an ad hoc approach. A more principled way to preserve pretraining knowledge is to focus on the "relational information" contained within the weight matrices. Instead of just looking at the magnitude of the weights, we should consider the geometry of the neurons.

Specifically, the geometry of a weight matrix can be characterized by the pairwise angles between its neurons. Each neuron can be viewed as a vector in a high-dimensional space. The relative angles between these vectors represent how the model distinguishes between different features and concepts. If we can modify a weight matrix in a way that preserves these pairwise angles, we ensure that the semantic relationships learned during pretraining remain intact, even as we adapt the model to a new task. This preservation of angular relationships is a fundamental insight that provides the motivation for the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework.

By focusing on the geometry of the weight space, we shift the goal of finetuning from simple weight modification to a structured geometric transformation. The objective is to find a transformation that allows the model to learn new patterns while maintaining the "shape" of the pretraining knowledge. This geometric perspective provides a theoretical foundation for why certain finetuning methods might generalize better than others, as it provides a clear metric for what "preserving knowledge" actually means in the context of high-dimensional linear layers.

# Orthogonal transformation as a weight update mechanism

The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework implements the geometric preservation described above by using an orthogonal transformation to update the weights. Unlike popular methods such as LoRA, which update the pretrained weights by adding a low-rank matrix, OFT employs a multiplicative approach. Given a pretrained linear layer with a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT introduces a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The forward pass of the layer is then modified from the original $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to a new form: $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the output.

The use of an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is critical because orthogonal transformations are specifically designed to preserve the inner products and, consequently, the pairwise angles between vectors. Because $\bm{R}$ preserves these angles, the transformation $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$ maintains the relational information of the neurons in the same layer. To ensure that the model starts from its original state before training begins, OFT initializes the matrix $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This "zero initialization" ensures that at the start of the finetuning process, the transformation has no effect, and the model behaves exactly like the original pretrained model.

This multiplicative mechanism distinguishes OFT from additive methods like LoRA. While LoRA introduces a small update to the weights, OFT applies a global rotation or reflection to the entire weight space of the layer. By constraining the transformation to be orthogonal, OFT provides a principled way to explore the space of possible weight configurations while strictly adhering to the requirement of preserving pretraining knowledge. This provides a stable and generalizable framework for adapting models to various downstream tasks.

# The Cayley parameterization for ensuring orthogonality

A significant technical challenge in OFT is ensuring that the matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the entire training process. In standard gradient descent, updating the entries of a matrix will naturally move it away from the manifold of orthogonal matrices. To solve this, the paper adopts a specific mathematical formulation known as the Cayley parameterization. Instead of optimizing the entries of $\bm{R}$ directly, we parameterize $\bm{R}$ using a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$.

The Cayley parameterization is defined by the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. A matrix $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric if it satisfies the condition $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. By using this construction, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ is mathematically guaranteed to be orthogonal regardless of the values within $\bm{Q}$. This allows the optimization process to freely update the entries of $\bm{Q}$ using standard backpropagation while the "orthogonality constraint" is automatically satisfied by the structure of the formula itself.

By employing this parameterization, OFT avoids the need for complex constrained optimization techniques or manual projection back onto the orthogonal manifold after every gradient step. This ensures that the training remains stable and that the primary goal—preserving the pairwise angles of the neurons—is never violated during the learning process. It provides a robust mathematical bridge between the goal of orthogonal transformation and the practical realities of training neural networks using gradient descent.

# The block-diagonal structure in original OFT: Trade-offs between parameter efficiency and expressivity

While the original OFT framework is theoretically sound, it faces a practical hurdle regarding parameter efficiency. A full $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters. For large foundation models where the dimension $d$ is very high, this number of parameters can be prohibitively large, making OFT less efficient than other methods like LoRA. To mitigate this, the original OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure to the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$.

In this structure, $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a smaller orthogonal matrix. Here, $b$ represents the block size and $r$ is the number of blocks, such that $br = d$. By making the blocks smaller, the number of trainable parameters is significantly reduced. For example, as the block size $b$ decreases, the model becomes more parameter-efficient. However, this efficiency comes at a cost to the model's expressivity.

The primary drawback of the block-diagonal structure is that it assumes the dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ independent groups. In this setup, the dimensions in different groups are transformed separately by different orthogonal matrices. This introduces a rigid structure where information cannot flow between the groups during the transformation. While this saves parameters, it limits the range of transformations the model can represent, potentially hindering its ability to learn complex relationships that span across the entire $d$-dimensional space.

# The problem of fixed sparsity patterns and hidden inductive biases

A deeper limitation of the original OFT arises from the fixed nature of the block-diagonal sparsity pattern. Because the blocks are pre-defined by their indices in the matrix, the sparsity pattern of the transformation is "hard-coded." This creates a significant problem: the block-diagonal structure is unable to approximate many classic linear transforms. Because the transformation is restricted to independent blocks, it cannot represent any operation that requires interaction between different indices in the weight matrix. This lack of expressiveness is a direct result of the chosen sparsity pattern.

Furthermore, this fixed structure introduces undesirable inductive biases. An inductive bias is an implicit assumption the model makes about the data or the task. By choosing a block-diagonal structure, we are forcing the model to believe that the features represented by different indices are independent of one another. This may not be true for many downstream tasks, and such a strong, arbitrary assumption can limit the model's generalizability. The paper notes that finding a "good" sparsity pattern—one that is both parameter-efficient and expressive enough to avoid these negative biases—remains a significant mystery.

In summary, while the original OFT was a breakthrough in principled finetuning, its reliance on block-diagonal matrices creates a tension between efficiency and capability. It achieves parameter efficiency by sacrificing the ability to model dense connections, and it introduces a rigid architectural bias that may not suit all applications. These limitations provide the motivation for the development of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method, which seeks to find a more sophisticated way to achieve dense connectivity with sparse parameterization.

The Information Transmission Framework

# Interpreting Dense Matrices as Connectivity Between Nodes

To understand the core innovations of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), we must first establish a conceptual foundation for how we view weight matrices during the finetuning process. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), we are interested in an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that transforms a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Instead of viewing this matrix simply as a collection of $d^2$ numbers, the authors propose a shift in perspective: viewing the matrix as a map of information flow.

In this framework, a $d$-dimensional dense square matrix $\bm{R}$ is interpreted as a system of connectivity between $d$ source nodes and $d$ receiver nodes. Each element $R_{ij}$ in the matrix represents the "pathway" between these nodes. If an entry $R_{ij}$ is non-zero, it signifies that information from the $j$-th node is capable of flowing to the $i$-th node. Conversely, if an entry $R_{ij}$ is zero, it indicates a break in the circuit, meaning that no information from node $j$ can reach node $i$.

By adopting this perspective, the goal of constructing a "dense" orthogonal matrix becomes a problem of network design. A dense matrix is one where every source node has a functional pathway to every receiver node. This connectivity is vital because it ensures that the transformation applied to the neurons preserves the necessary relational information and expressiveness required for the model to adapt to new downstream tasks. If the matrix were too sparse or disconnected, certain parts of the model's knowledge would become isolated from others, limiting the model's ability to learn.

# Matrix Factorization as Sequential Information Exchange

The authors extend this connectivity intuition to the problem of matrix factorization. When we represent a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple smaller matrices, such as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \dots \bm{B}_1$, we are essentially describing a multi-step relay system for information. In this view, the transformation is not happening in a single leap; rather, information is passed sequentially through a series of intermediate stages.

Each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ in the product serves as a specific "exchange" step. Information enters the first matrix $\bm{B}_1$, is transformed and passed to the next level, enters $\bm{B}_2$, and continues this process until it finally exits the last matrix $\bm{B}_m$. This is conceptually similar to how a signal might pass through several different routers in a computer network to reach its final destination. Each router (matrix) performs a partial transformation, and the cumulative effect of all these transformations results in the final dense transformation $\bm{R}$.

This interpretation is powerful because it allows us to analyze the complexity of the model based on the "bottlenecks" or "paths" created at each step. By breaking down a large, dense matrix into a product of sparse matrices, we are trading "space" (the number of parameters required to represent the matrix) for "time" (the computational cost of performing multiple sequential multiplications). The goal of the BOFT method is to find the most efficient way to structure these sequential exchanges so that the final output remains dense and expressive while using the fewest possible trainable parameters.

# Grid-Structured Graph Construction from Matrix Products

To formalize the sequential information exchange described above, the authors construct a grid-structured graph. This graph provides a visual and mathematical map of how information travels through the factorization $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \dots \bm{B}_1$. The graph is designed with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, organized into $m+1$ columns (or levels). Each column contains $d$ nodes, representing the state of the information at each stage of the factorization.

In this grid, the matrix $\bm{B}_i$ is interpreted as the connectivity matrix between the $i$-th level and the $(i+1)$-th level. Specifically, the $(j_1, j_2)$ element of the matrix $\bm{B}_i$ determines whether there is a directed edge from the $j_2$-th node in level $i$ to the $j_1$-th node in level $i+1$. If the element is zero, no edge exists. For the final product $\bm{R}$ to be a dense matrix, the graph must satisfy a specific condition: there must be a valid path of directed edges from every single node in the first level (the source) to every single node in the final level (the receiver).

This graphical representation makes it much easier to identify which sparsity patterns will result in a dense matrix. For example, if we consider a product of four matrices, $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$, and we find that there is no sequence of edges that allows node 1 in the first level to reach node 3 in the fifth level, then the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ will have a zero at the $(3,1)$ position. By analyzing the grid, we can systematically design the non-zero patterns of the sparse matrices $\bm{B}_i$ to ensure that every node in the source level is connected to every node in the receiver level.

# Defining Desiderata for Efficient Orthogonal Matrices: Dense Connectivity vs. Minimum Free Edges

Using the information transmission framework, the authors define two primary desiderata (requirements) for designing an efficient orthogonal matrix. The first is **dense connectivity**. As established, for the matrix $\bm{R}$ to be expressive enough to approximate general linear transforms, every node in the first level of the grid must have at least one path to every node in the last level. This ensures that no information is lost or isolated during the transformation.

The second desiderata is **minimum free edges**. Since the goal of BOFT is parameter efficiency, we want to achieve dense connectivity using as few edges as possible. In this context, an "edge" corresponds to a non-zero entry in one of the factorized matrices $\bm{B}_i$. However, the authors make an important distinction regarding "free" edges. Because the matrices $\bm{B}_i$ must be orthogonal, they must be full-rank. This implies that for each matrix $\bm{B}_i$, there must be at least $d$ edges to form a bijection between the nodes of level $i$ and level $i+1$. These $d$ edges are necessary for the mathematical property of orthogonality and are often non-trainable (for instance, they might be fixed as $\pm 1$). 

The "free" edges are the additional trainable parameters beyond these necessary $d$ connections. A naive way to achieve dense connectivity is to use a single dense orthogonal matrix, which requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges. The authors show that by using a more clever graph topology, such as the butterfly structure, one can achieve dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. The objective of BOFT is to find the optimal sparsity pattern that satisfies the dense connectivity requirement while minimizing the number of free edges.

# Analyzing the Limitations of Block-Diagonal Structures within this Framework

Finally, the information transmission framework provides a clear explanation for why the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) method faces limitations in terms of expressiveness. In the original OFT, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as a block-diagonal matrix: $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. While this is highly parameter-efficient, the framework reveals a significant structural flaw.

When we visualize a block-diagonal matrix as a grid graph, we see that the nodes are partitioned into $r$ separate groups. The transformations $\bm{R}_i$ only allow information to flow between nodes within the same group. There are no edges connecting a node in group $i$ to a node in group $j$ if $i \neq j$. In the language of the information transmission framework, the block-diagonal structure fails the **dense connectivity** requirement. Because there is no path from a node in one group to a node in another, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ cannot be dense; it will always have large blocks of zeros.

This explains why block-diagonal OFT has an inherent "inductive bias" that limits its expressiveness—it cannot approximate classic linear transforms that require cross-group interaction. By contrast, the butterfly structure proposed in BOFT is designed specifically to ensure that every node can communicate with every other node across the grid, providing a much smoother interpolation between the identity matrix (perfect regularity) and the full orthogonal group (full expressiveness).

# Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) Construction

To understand the construction of Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), one must first understand the problem it aims to solve within the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In standard model finetuning, we seek to adapt a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to a downstream task while preserving the semantic knowledge learned during pretraining. OFT achieves this by applying a multiplicative orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the weights, such that the new weights become $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because orthogonal matrices preserve the pairwise angles between neurons, the relational information of the model remains intact.

However, a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is inefficient for large models. Previous attempts to improve efficiency used a block-diagonal structure, where the matrix is divided into independent smaller blocks. While this reduces the number of parameters, it introduces a heavy inductive bias: it assumes that dimensions are grouped into isolated blocks that do not interact. BOFT provides a solution by constructing a dense orthogonal matrix through the factorization of multiple sparse matrices, inspired by the efficiency of the Cooley-Tukey algorithm.

# The Cooley-Tukey algorithm and the butterfly graph topology

The foundation of BOFT lies in the butterfly structure, a topology famously used in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The core intuition behind using this structure is its ability to perform efficient information transmission. In the context of matrix multiplication, a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ represents a dense connectivity where every input dimension can influence every output dimension. If we represent $\bm{R}$ as a product of $m$ matrices $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, we can visualize this as a grid-structured graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes.

In this information transmission framework, each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ defines the connectivity between level $i$ and level $i+1$. For the resulting product $\bm{R}$ to be dense, the graph must satisfy the "dense connectivity" desideratum: there must be a path from every node in the first level to every node in the last level. A naive way to achieve this is to use a single dense matrix, but this is parameter-heavy. The butterfly graph topology is an elegant solution because it can densely connect $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. By leveraging this topology, BOFT can generate a dense orthogonal matrix while keeping the number of trainable parameters significantly lower than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ required by a full dense matrix.

# Mathematical definition of butterfly factors and butterfly matrices

To formalize this, we first define the butterfly factor. Assuming $k \geq 2$ is a power of 2, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as a $k \times k$ matrix:

$$\bm{B}^F(k) = \begin{bmatrix} \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\ \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$$

where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}$ is a vector. This structure shows that each node in one level connects to exactly two nodes in the next level (or vice versa), creating the "butterfly" shape. 

Next, we define the $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ recursively. For a dimension $d = 2^N$, the matrix is constructed as:

$$\bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} \bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\ \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2}) \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2}) \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$$

Here, $\bm{B}_1$ and $\bm{B}_2$ are themselves butterfly matrices of dimension $\frac{d}{2}$. The components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ are known as butterfly components. Each $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$ with block size $k$, where each block is a butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$. This recursive structure ensures that the final product of these sparse components results in a dense matrix, satisfying the information transmission requirements of the model.

# Recursive construction of the butterfly component

The power of the butterfly matrix lies in its recursive decomposition. By breaking down a large $d$-dimensional matrix into a product of components $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ where $k$ decreases geometrically (from $d$ down to 2), we effectively distribute the connectivity across multiple stages. Each stage $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ handles a specific level of information exchange. 

This recursive construction is what allows BOFT to bridge the gap between the high expressivity of a dense orthogonal matrix and the high parameter efficiency of a sparse matrix. In the original OFT, the block-diagonal structure is essentially a "one-step" transformation where the blocks never interact. In BOFT, the butterfly components allow for a multi-step transformation. Because the matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ is a product of these components, a value at any position in the final dense matrix $\bm{R}$ is a result of information flowing through the butterfly graph. This allows the model to approximate a much wider range of linear transforms than the block-diagonal structure could ever achieve.

# Designing orthogonal butterfly components via block-wise permutations

A critical requirement for BOFT is that the final matrix $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal. To ensure this, every individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ in the product must also be orthogonal. The researchers achieve this by starting with the simplest possible butterfly component: the one with a block size of 2, denoted as $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$. 

To ensure $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$ is orthogonal, each $2 \times 2$ block is parameterized using the Cayley transform (which is equivalent to a 2-dimensional rotation). Because the non-zero pattern of any other butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, k)$ for $k > 2$ is simply a block-wise permutation of the non-zero pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d, 2)$, we can ensure the orthogonality of all components by applying the same rotation logic to the corresponding blocks. 

The framework is further generalized into "block butterfly components" $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d, k)$. In this version, each entry in the butterfly factor $\bm{d}_i$ is replaced by a $b \times b$ matrix. To maintain orthogonality, each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix is parameterized to be orthogonal. This allows BOFT to maintain a flexible block size $b$ while still benefiting from the butterfly structure's sparse factorization.

# The BOFT($m, b$) parameterization and its complexity analysis

BOFT is formally defined by two hyperparameters: $m$, the number of butterfly components in the product, and $b$, the block size. The forward pass for a linear layer is modified as follows:

$$\bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}, \quad \text{s.t. } \bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$$

where each $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is an orthogonal butterfly component, and $\bm{I}_d$ is the identity matrix. The term $\bm{R}(m,b)$ represents the total orthogonal transformation applied to the weights. 

The parameter efficiency of this construction is significant. For a linear layer of size $d \times n$, BOFT($m, b$) requires $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters. We can compare this to other methods:
1. **Original OFT:** Uses a full dense orthogonal matrix, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.
2. **Block-diagonal OFT:** Uses a block-diagonal structure with block size $b$, requiring $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ parameters.
3. **BOFT:** If we set $b=2$ and $m = \log d$, the number of parameters becomes $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$.

This demonstrates that BOFT can achieve a dense orthogonal matrix (which provides high expressivity) with a parameter count that is only logarithmic in growth relative to the dimension, making it much more efficient than the original OFT while being more expressive than the block-diagonal version.

# Initialization strategies: Identity initialization and Multiplicative Dropout

To ensure that the finetuning process starts from a stable point, BOFT employs specific initialization and regularization strategies. Since the goal of finetuning is to start with the pretrained model and deviate only as much as necessary, BOFT initializes all butterfly components as identity matrices. In the context of the Cayley transform, this is achieved by initializing the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ as a zero matrix. When $\bm{Q} = \bm{0}$, the resulting orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, ensuring that at the start of training, the model is identical to the pretrained foundation model.

Because BOFT uses a multiplicative update ($\bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$), standard Dropout—which typically zeros out elements of an additive update—is not directly applicable. Instead, the authors propose "Multiplicative Dropout." This method exploits the fact that the butterfly components can be permuted into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal matrices. Multiplicative Dropout works by randomly selecting a percentage $p_1$ of the butterfly components and a percentage $p_2$ of the diagonal blocks within those components. The selected blocks are then replaced with identity matrices. This effectively "turns off" certain parts of the orthogonal transformation, providing a regularization mechanism to prevent overfitting while maintaining the structural integrity of the orthogonal parameterization.

Theoretical Insights and Inductive Biases

# Expressivity analysis: Approximating the full orthogonal group

In the context of parameter-efficient finetuning, a critical question arises regarding the "expressivity" of the chosen transformation. Expressivity refers to the ability of a model's parameterization to represent a wide enough range of possible functions or transformations to effectively adapt to a downstream task. For Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the goal is to transform the pretrained weight matrix using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. While a dense, full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ offers the highest possible expressivity because it can represent any rotation or reflection in $d$-dimensional space, it is computationally expensive, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

To achieve parameter efficiency, we must move toward structured matrices that use fewer parameters, such as the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT or the butterfly factorization used in Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). However, there is an inherent tension between expressivity and regularity. A model with universal expressivity (like full finetuning) can theoretically represent any transformation, yet it often performs poorly on specific downstream tasks because it can overfit to noise. Conversely, a model with high regularity (a very restricted hypothesis class) might be too simple to capture the necessary nuances of the task.

BOFT seeks a "smoother interpolation" between these two extremes. By using the butterfly structure, BOFT constructs a hypothesis class that is more expressive than the block-diagonal structure but more regular than a fully dense orthogonal matrix. The goal is to find a structured subset of the orthogonal group that is rich enough to capture the necessary features of the downstream task while remaining constrained enough to prevent overfitting. This suggests that the effectiveness of BOFT lies not in its ability to represent *every* possible transformation, but in its ability to represent a *useful* subset of transformations efficiently.

# Theorem 1: BOFT vs. OFT expressivity

To formally understand the advantage of the butterfly structure over the block-diagonal structure, we can compare their expressiveness. The original OFT uses a block-diagonal matrix $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small orthogonal matrix of size $b \times b$. This structure imposes a very rigid constraint: dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ independent groups, and transformations are applied to these groups separately. This significantly limits the model's ability to learn correlations between different groups of dimensions.

The paper introduces a formal comparison through Theorem 1, which states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT when using the same block size $b$. This is because the butterfly structure, combined with permutations, can recover many classic linear transforms that the block-diagonal structure simply cannot. For example, the block-diagonal structure cannot approximate any classic fast linear transforms because it cannot create cross-block dependencies.

Furthermore, the paper highlights that the butterfly matrix can be generalized even further. By composing multiple butterfly matrices with their transposes—a construction known as a kaleidoscope hierarchy—one can actually represent the entire orthogonal group. Specifically, if we define a generalized orthogonal matrix as a product of butterfly matrices and their transposes, such as $\bm{R}^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l) = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1, b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2, b_2) \cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1, b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_1, b_2)$, and set the parameters to $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d - 1$, the framework can represent the entire orthogonal group. This theoretical insight confirms that BOFT provides a scalable path toward full expressivity while maintaining the ability to restrict the hypothesis class for better generalization.

# Spectral properties: Preservation of the spectral norm and training stability

One of the most significant mathematical advantages of using orthogonal transformations for finetuning is the preservation of the spectral properties of the weight matrices. In many finetuning methods, such as LoRA, the pretrained weights are updated by adding a low-rank matrix. This addition can alter the singular values of the weight matrix, potentially leading to unstable training or a loss of the original model's characteristics.

In contrast, both OFT and BOFT modify the weights multiplicatively by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. To understand why this is beneficial, consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, which can be written as $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. In this decomposition, $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values. When we apply BOFT, the new weight matrix becomes $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{U}$ are both orthogonal matrices, their product $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is also an orthogonal matrix. 

Consequently, the singular values (the entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$) remain completely unchanged. The spectral norm of the weight matrix, which is defined by its largest singular value, is perfectly preserved throughout the finetuning process. Research has shown that preserving the spectral norm is highly beneficial for training stability and helps the model generalize better by ensuring that the "energy" or scale of the weights does not explode or vanish during adaptation. This is a key reason why orthogonal-based methods are often more stable than additive low-rank methods.

# BOFT as a form of learning bilinear similarity

Beyond its spectral benefits, BOFT can be interpreted through the lens of bilinear similarity. When we apply the transformation $\bm{R}$ to the weights, the forward pass involves calculating the similarity between the input $\bm{x}$ and the transformed weight vectors. Specifically, if $\bm{w}^0_i$ represents the $i$-th neuron (the $i$-th column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$), the output is essentially learning the bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i \bm{R} \bm{x}$.

By viewing the task this way, BOFT is framed as a method for learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ that is subject to a strong regularity constraint: the matrix $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal. This connects the method to established fields like distance metric learning and bilinear forms. In these fields, the goal is often to learn a transformation that preserves important geometric relationships between data points. By enforcing orthogonality, BOFT ensures that the transformation $\bm{R}$ preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, which the paper identifies as a crucial step in preserving the semantic knowledge captured during the original pretraining phase.

# The role of structured inductive biases: Linking to FFT, Hadamard, and Cosine transforms

A fundamental concept in machine learning is the inductive bias—the set of assumptions a model uses to predict outputs for inputs it has not encountered before. BOFT introduces a specific, structured inductive bias by utilizing the butterfly factorization. The butterfly structure is not an arbitrary choice; it is the same underlying topology used in many classic and highly efficient linear transforms, including the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), the Hadamard transform, and the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT).

By choosing the butterfly structure, BOFT implicitly biases the model toward these types of transformations. Because these transforms are widely used and have well-understood properties in signal processing and data analysis, this bias acts as a powerful regularizer. It encourages the model to find solutions that share structural patterns with these classic transforms, which can help prevent the model from overfitting to the specific noise of the downstream dataset.

Additionally, the use of sparse matrix factorization in BOFT provides another layer of implicit inductive bias. Sparse structures naturally limit the complexity of the hypothesis class. When combined with the butterfly structure, these biases work together to ensure that the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ remains within a manageable and geometrically meaningful subset of the orthogonal group. This combination of "structured sparsity" and "classic transform topology" is what allows BOFT to achieve high parameter efficiency without sacrificing the ability to generalize to new tasks.

Empirical Evaluation and Conclusion

# Experimental setup across Vision Transformers (ViT), Large Language Models (LLM), and Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

To validate the effectiveness of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) framework, the researchers conducted an extensive empirical study that spanned several different modalities of foundation models. Rather than limiting the evaluation to a single domain, the study included Large Vision Transformers (ViT), Large Language Models (LLM), and text-to-image diffusion models. This multi-modal approach was designed to demonstrate that BOFT is a generic and versatile method for model adaptation, rather than a niche solution for a specific type of architecture or data.

By testing the framework on Vision Transformers, the researchers could observe how BOFT handles spatial information and visual feature extraction. The inclusion of Large Language Models allowed for an assessment of the method's ability to preserve and adapt linguistic knowledge and sequential reasoning. Finally, by applying BOFT to text-to-image diffusion models, the study built upon the existing success of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in controllable generation, showing that the new butterfly-based parameterization maintains those benefits while introducing greater efficiency.

This broad experimental scope is crucial for establishing the "principled" nature of the proposed finetuning paradigm. By showing that BOFT works across vision, language, and generative tasks, the authors argue that the underlying mechanics—specifically the use of butterfly factorization to generate dense orthogonal matrices—provide a robust inductive bias that is applicable to the wide variety of tasks researchers face when adapting large-scale foundation models.

# Comparative performance against LoRA and standard OFT

The empirical results serve to position BOFT within the existing landscape of parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods. The primary points of comparison are the widely popular Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). LoRA achieves parameter efficiency by adding a product of two low-rank matrices to the pretrained weights. In contrast, OFT achieves its goal by multiplying the pretrained weights by a learnable orthogonal matrix. While OFT offers stronger generalization and more stable training than LoRA by preserving the pairwise angles between neurons and the spectral norm of the weights, it traditionally suffers from high parameter counts because dense orthogonal matrices require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

BOFT is designed to bridge the gap between these two methods. It retains the multiplicative orthogonal transformation of OFT—ensuring that the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is preserved—but utilizes butterfly factorization to drastically reduce the number of trainable parameters. The experiments demonstrate that BOFT outperforms current state-of-the-art methods by a considerable margin. Specifically, it achieves a level of parameter efficiency that is competitive with, and often superior to, LoRA, while maintaining the structural advantages of orthogonal transformations.

Furthermore, the study shows that BOFT is more expressive than the block-diagonal structure used in standard OFT. While the block-diagonal approach introduces a fixed sparsity pattern that can limit the model's ability to approximate certain linear transforms, BOFT's butterfly structure can recover many classic linear transforms, such as the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) or the Hadamard transform. This allows BOFT to find a more effective trade-off between the regularity required for generalization and the expressivity required for downstream task adaptation.

# Analysis of training overhead and zero inference latency

One of the practical considerations in any finetuning method is the trade-off between training complexity and the speed of the final model during use. The researchers analyze these two factors to show that BOFT is highly suitable for production environments. During the training phase, BOFT does introduce a slight overhead compared to standard OFT. This is because the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is not a single matrix but a product of $m$ different butterfly components: $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Consequently, during each training iteration, the model must perform multiple matrix multiplications to compute the effective transformation, which slightly increases the runtime.

However, this training-time overhead does not translate to any cost during the inference phase. This is a critical advantage for deploying large foundation models. Because the learned butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ are static after the finetuning process is complete, they can be pre-multiplied into the original pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Once this multiplication is performed, the resulting weight matrix can be used exactly as it would be in a standard linear layer. Therefore, BOFT provides "zero inference latency," meaning the final model runs just as fast as the original pretrained model, without the need to compute additional products or extra layers during each forward pass.

# Limitations and future directions: Network topology optimization and runtime efficiency

Despite the successful empirical results, the authors acknowledge certain limitations and areas for future research. The first primary limitation is the training runtime overhead mentioned previously. While the overhead is acceptable for many applications, it remains an open problem to optimize the speed at which these butterfly products are calculated during the training process. The researchers suggest that more efficient ways to compute these sparse factorizations could be explored to minimize the time spent in the backward and forward passes of the training loop.

Another area for future exploration is the underlying topology of the butterfly structure itself. The paper introduces the "information transmission" framework, which views the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as the transmission of information across a grid-structured graph. Under this view, the butterfly structure is simply one way to achieve dense connectivity with $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. The authors suggest that the field could benefit from drawing inspiration from computer networking to identify even more efficient network topologies. By treating the construction of the orthogonal matrix as a network design problem, researchers may find new, even more efficient ways to transmit information, potentially leading to new parameterization methods that further reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining high expressivity.
