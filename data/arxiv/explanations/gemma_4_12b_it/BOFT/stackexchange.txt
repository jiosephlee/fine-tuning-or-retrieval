\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ actually get parameterized for training?
Question:
In the paper, Equation 1 defines the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ as a matrix composed of four diagonal matrices $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1)$ through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)$. While the paper states these are 'some vectors', it doesn't explicitly say how these vectors are learned or if they are constrained in any way other than the block-wise rotation. Could someone clarify what the actual trainable parameters are within this specific component?
Answer:
To understand how the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is parameterized, it is helpful to look at the underlying goal of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) framework: ensuring that the resulting global matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is orthogonal. Because $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is defined as a product of several butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, each individual component must be an orthogonal matrix for the product to remain orthogonal.

The paper simplifies the parameterization of these components by observing a structural property of the butterfly graph. Specifically, the non-zero pattern of any butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ (regardless of the size $k$) is simply a permutation of the non-zero pattern of the base case $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$. Because of this permutation symmetry, the authors only need to define a valid parameterization for the smallest possible block—the $2b \times 2b$ block—and then "propagate" that parameterization to the larger structures.

For the base case $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, the paper explicitly states that orthogonality is guaranteed using the Cayley transform. The Cayley transform is a standard method to parameterize the orthogonal group $\mathcal{O}(d)$. It defines an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (where $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$):
$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$
In this formulation, the trainable parameters are the elements of the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, it contains significantly fewer independent parameters than a general matrix, which aligns with the goal of parameter efficiency.

Now, we can connect this back to Equation 1. For the simplest case where the block size $b=1$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is composed of four diagonal matrices $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1)$ through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)$ that form a $k \times k$ matrix. When $k=2$, this becomes a $2 \times 2$ matrix:
$$\bm{B}^F(2) = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & d_2 \\ d_3 & d_4 \end{bmatrix}$$
To ensure this $2 \times 2$ matrix is orthogonal (a rotation matrix), the values in the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ must satisfy the properties of a rotation. For example, if we parameterize this as a rotation by an angle $\theta$, then $d_1 = \cos \theta$, $d_2 = -\sin \theta$, $d_3 = \sin \theta$, and $d_4 = \cos \theta$. 

In the general case where $b > 1$ or $k > 2$, the "vectors" $\bm{d}_i$ are the components derived from the Cayley transform applied to the $2b \times 2b$ blocks. Essentially, the authors are training the parameters of the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ for the base blocks, and the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ in Equation 1 are the resulting entries that populate the butterfly structure. By enforcing the Cayley transform on the base blocks and using permutations to arrange them into the butterfly pattern, the model ensures that every component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal without having to learn $d^2$ independent parameters for each matrix in the product.

### Why does multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserve the 'pairwise angles' between neurons?
Question:
The paper mentions that the core principle of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) is preserving the relational information of weight matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. I understand that an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Can someone explain the linear algebra behind why this property ensures that the angles between the vectors in the weight matrix remain unchanged after the transformation $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$?
Answer:
To understand why multiplying by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, it is helpful to look at the geometric definition of an angle in linear algebra. The angle $\theta$ between two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ is determined by the dot product and the magnitudes (norms) of those vectors, as expressed in the formula:

$$\cos \theta = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\| \|\mathbf{v}\|}$$

In the context of the paper, the "neurons" are represented by the columns of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Let $\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{w}_j$ be two such columns (neurons) in the pretrained model. After the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) transformation, these neurons become $\mathbf{w}'_i = \bm{R}\mathbf{w}_i$ and $\mathbf{w}'_j = \bm{R}\mathbf{w}_j$. To see if the angle remains unchanged, we need to check if the numerator (the dot product) and the denominator (the product of the norms) are invariant under the transformation $\bm{R}$.

First, let's look at the dot product of the transformed neurons. Using the property of transposes, the dot product can be written as:

$$\mathbf{w}'_i \cdot \mathbf{w}'_j = (\bm{R}\mathbf{w}_i)^\top (\bm{R}\mathbf{w}_j) = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \mathbf{w}_j$$

Because $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix, it satisfies the definition $\bm{R}^\top \bm{R} = \bm{I}$. Substituting this into the equation, we get:

$$\mathbf{w}_i^\top \bm{I} \mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j = \mathbf{w}_i \cdot \mathbf{w}_j$$

This shows that the dot product between any two neurons is perfectly preserved. Similarly, we can check the norm of a single transformed neuron:

$$\|\mathbf{w}'_i\|^2 = (\bm{R}\mathbf{w}_i)^\top (\bm{R}\mathbf{w}_i) = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \bm{R}^\top \bm{R} \mathbf{w}_i = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_i = \|\mathbf{w}_i\|^2$$

Since both the dot product and the individual norms are preserved, the value of $\cos \theta$ remains constant, meaning the angle $\theta$ between the neurons is unchanged.

Intuitively, you can think of an orthogonal matrix as a transformation that performs only rotations and reflections in high-dimensional space. Unlike a general linear transformation, which can stretch, squash, or shear the space (thereby changing the relative angles and distances between vectors), an orthogonal transformation is a "rigid" transformation. It moves the entire coordinate system as a single unit without warping it. 

This is the core reason why the paper emphasizes preserving "relational information." By ensuring that the relative geometry of the neurons remains identical to the pretrained state, the model retains the "semantic knowledge" acquired during pretraining while still allowing the weights to be adapted for a new task through the rotation provided by $\bm{R}$.

### If BOFT involves a product of $m$ matrices, why is there no additional inference latency?
Question:
I am a bit confused about the efficiency claim. The paper states that BOFT has no additional inference latency, but it also mentions that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of a product of multiple butterfly components $\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. If we have to perform $m$ matrix multiplications during the forward pass at inference time, wouldn't that add latency compared to the original model? Or is there a way to 'collapse' these matrices first?
Answer:
The key to understanding why there is no additional inference latency lies in the distinction between the **parameterization** used during training and the final **weight application** used during inference.

During the training phase, BOFT utilizes the butterfly factorization $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ specifically to achieve parameter efficiency. By representing the orthogonal matrix as a product of $m$ sparse matrices, the model only needs to store and optimize $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters instead of the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required for a dense orthogonal matrix. This is the "trade-off" mentioned in the paper: the model accepts a slightly higher training runtime (because it must perform $m$ matrix multiplications to compute the transformation in every iteration) in exchange for a much smaller memory footprint and a more structured hypothesis class.

However, once the training process is finished, the butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ are no longer changing. Because these matrices are fixed, you can "collapse" them into a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ by pre-computing their product: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. 

The paper explains this in the concluding remarks: "after the finetuning stage, the BOFT-learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model." Mathematically, if the original forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, the finetuned pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Since $\bm{R}$ is a constant $d \times d$ matrix after training, you can pre-calculate a new weight matrix $\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$. 

At inference time, the model no longer sees the product of $m$ matrices or even the matrix $\bm{R}$. It simply uses the new weight matrix $\bm{W}_{new}$. Because $\bm{W}_{new}$ has the exact same dimensions as the original $\bm{W}^0$, the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) and the memory required for the forward pass remain identical to the original model.

This makes BOFT a **reparameterization-based** method, similar to LoRA. In LoRA, the low-rank updates $\Delta \bm{W}$ are merged into the original weights ($\bm{W} + \Delta \bm{W}$) after training. In BOFT, the orthogonal transformation is merged into the weights via multiplication ($\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$). Both techniques ensure that the "extra" parameters used to guide the learning process disappear during deployment, leaving the model's inference speed untouched.

### How does the 'minimum free edges' desideratum interact with the orthogonality constraint?
Question:
In Section 3, the authors describe an information transmission framework where they want to achieve 'minimum free edges' while maintaining 'dense connectivity.' However, they also mention that 'orthogonality injects a delicate constraint to the edges.' I'm struggling to understand this: if we are trying to minimize the number of edges to save parameters, but orthogonality forces certain edges to exist (or forces others to be zero), how does the authors' framework actually 'find' the optimal sparsity pattern? Is it a search problem or a structural construction?
Answer:
To understand how the "minimum free edges" desideratum interacts with the orthogonality constraint, it is helpful to view the problem through the authors' "information transmission" lens. The authors frame the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a path-finding problem on a grid-structured graph. If we factorize the matrix into $m$ matrices, $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$, each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents the connectivity between two adjacent levels of nodes. For the final matrix $\bm{R}$ to be dense, every node in the first level must have at least one directed path to every node in the final level.

The "minimum free edges" desideratum is essentially a search for the most parameter-efficient way to satisfy this "dense connectivity." However, the orthogonality constraint makes this non-trivial. For each intermediate matrix $\bm{B}_i$ to be orthogonal, it must be full-rank. Mathematically, this implies that there must be at least $d$ edges between any two adjacent levels to form a bijection between the nodes. The authors make a crucial distinction here: these $d$ edges are "fixed" because, in an orthogonal matrix with only $d$ non-zero entries, those entries can only be $\pm 1$. Therefore, these edges do not count toward the "trainable" parameters. The "free edges" are the additional non-zero entries that provide the necessary paths to ensure dense connectivity while allowing for learnable weights.

Regarding your question on whether this is a search problem or a structural construction, the paper leans heavily toward **structural construction**. While the information transmission framework provides a mathematical "scorecard" to evaluate how well a sparsity pattern performs (i.e., does it provide dense connectivity? how many free edges does it use?), the authors do not propose an algorithm to search for an arbitrary optimal graph. Instead, they use this framework to identify and justify the use of known, mathematically elegant topologies.

The authors specifically identify the **butterfly structure** from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm as the ideal construction. The butterfly structure is a known topology that achieves dense connectivity using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ edges. By using this construction, the authors bypass the need for an exhaustive search; they leverage a pre-existing structural prior that is already known to be efficient in other fields (like signal processing and computer networking).

In summary, the framework acts as a theoretical bridge. It translates the abstract goal of "parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning" into a concrete graph-theory problem (finding a graph with dense paths and few edges). Once the problem is translated, the authors apply a structural solution (the butterfly factorization) that satisfies those graph-theory requirements efficiently. The orthogonality constraint acts as the "floor" for the number of edges required (the $d$ bijection edges), and the butterfly structure provides the "ceiling" for the most efficient way to build dense paths on top of that floor.

### Why is a block-diagonal structure incapable of generating a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ in the information transmission view?
Question:
The paper argues that BOFT is more expressive than the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT because it can generate a dense matrix. Using the information transmission view (the grid-structured graph), can someone explain why a block-diagonal structure fails the 'dense connectivity' requirement? I'm trying to visualize the paths from the first level to the last level nodes.
Answer:
To understand why a block-diagonal structure fails the "dense connectivity" requirement in the information transmission view, we have to look at how the paper maps matrix multiplication to graph reachability. In this framework, a matrix $\bm{R}$ is represented as a product of $m$ matrices $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$. This multiplication is visualized as a grid of $d \times (m+1)$ nodes, where each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ defines the directed edges (connectivity) between level $i$ and level $i+1$. For the final product $\bm{R}$ to be a dense matrix, the paper specifies a fundamental requirement: every node in the first level must have at least one valid path to every node in the last level.

When we consider the block-diagonal structure used in the original Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the matrix is defined as $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$. In this structure, the dimensions are partitioned into $r$ independent groups. In the information transmission graph, this means that the edges provided by the matrices $\bm{B}_i$ are restricted entirely within these groups. If a node in the first level belongs to the indices of the first block $\bm{R}_1$, the only available edges in the subsequent levels will point to other nodes that also belong to the indices of $\bm{R}_1$. There are no edges—no "cross-talk"—that allow information to jump from the first block to the second block, or any other subsequent block.

You can visualize this by imagining the grid as a series of neighborhoods. In a block-diagonal setup, these neighborhoods are completely isolated from one another; there are no roads connecting one neighborhood to the next. If you start at a node in the first neighborhood at the first level, you can travel to any node within that same neighborhood at the final level, but you can never reach a node in a different neighborhood. Because there is no path between nodes in different blocks, the reachability matrix of the graph remains block-diagonal. Since a dense matrix requires that every source node can reach every target node, the block-diagonal structure is mathematically incapable of generating a dense $\bm{R}$ because it lacks the necessary global connectivity.

This is the core reason why the authors transition to the butterfly structure. While the butterfly structure is also sparse (saving parameters), its recursive topology is specifically designed to ensure that information "mixes" across the entire dimension $d$. By using butterfly factors, the graph allows a node at the first level to eventually reach every node at the last level through a series of swaps and combinations, thereby satisfying the dense connectivity requirement while maintaining a much lower parameter count than a fully dense matrix. In essence, while the block-diagonal structure creates isolated "islands" of information, the butterfly structure creates a "web" that connects every point to every other point.

### How does 'Multiplicative Dropout' differ from standard Dropout in the context of BOFT?
Question:
I understand that standard Dropout (zeroing out elements) doesn't work for BOFT because it's a multiplicative weight update. The paper proposes 'Multiplicative Dropout' which replaces blocks with identity matrices. Does this mean the 'dropped' components effectively do nothing to the weight matrix? And how does this maintain the orthogonality of the total matrix $\bm{R}$ throughout the training process?
Answer:
The distinction between standard Dropout and Multiplicative Dropout in the context of BOFT stems from the fundamental algebraic nature of how the weights are updated. In standard neural networks, weights are typically updated additively (e.g., $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W}$). In such a setting, Dropout works by zeroing out elements of $\Delta \bm{W}$, which effectively "turns off" certain learned features. However, BOFT uses an orthogonal finetuning approach where the new weight matrix is a multiplicative transformation of the pretrained weights: $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because this is a multiplicative update, zeroing out elements in the matrix $\bm{R}$ would destroy the orthogonality of the transformation and likely lead to a collapse of the signal, as the matrix would no longer be a rotation/reflection in the high-dimensional space.

To solve this, the authors propose Multiplicative Dropout, which operates on the factorized butterfly components of $\bm{R}$. Instead of zeroing out elements to produce a zero matrix, the "dropped" components are replaced with identity matrices $\bm{I}$. Intuitively, because $\bm{I}$ is the identity element for matrix multiplication, replacing a component with $\bm{I}$ means that specific part of the transformation "does nothing" to the data. It allows the information to pass through to the next layer in the factorization unchanged. In the same way that BOFT is initialized with identity matrices to ensure the model starts as the pretrained version, Multiplicative Dropout ensures that the dropped components act as "pass-through" gates, preserving the original features while preventing the model from over-relying on specific butterfly factorizations during training.

Regarding the maintenance of orthogonality, the beauty of this approach lies in the structural properties of the butterfly factorization. The total matrix $\bm{R}$ is defined as a product of several butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. For $\bm{R}$ to remain orthogonal, each individual component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ must be orthogonal. The authors note that these components can be permuted into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. Since the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix, replacing a block within these structures with $\bm{I}$ preserves the orthogonality of that specific component. Because the product of any number of orthogonal matrices is always orthogonal, the total matrix $\bm{R}$ remains perfectly orthogonal throughout the training process, even as different components are randomly "dropped" (turned into identities) in each forward pass. This allows the model to benefit from the regularization of Dropout without violating the geometric constraints required by Orthogonal Finetuning.
