\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ actually get parameterized for training?
Question:
In the paper, Equation 1 defines the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ as a matrix composed of four diagonal matrices $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1)$ through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)$. While the paper states these are 'some vectors', it doesn't explicitly say how these vectors are learned or if they are constrained in any way other than the block-wise rotation. Could someone clarify what the actual trainable parameters are within this specific component?
Answer:
To understand how the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is parameterized, it is helpful to look at the underlying goal of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) framework: ensuring that the resulting global matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is orthogonal. Because $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is defined as a product of several butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, each individual component must be an orthogonal matrix for the product to remain orthogonal.

The paper simplifies the parameterization of these components by observing a structural property of the butterfly graph. Specifically, the non-zero pattern of any butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ (regardless of the size $k$) is simply a permutation of the non-zero pattern of the base case $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$. Because of this permutation symmetry, the authors only need to define a valid parameterization for the smallest possible block—the $2b \times 2b$ block—and then "propagate" that parameterization to the larger structures.

For the base case $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, the paper explicitly states that orthogonality is guaranteed using the Cayley transform. The Cayley transform is a standard method to parameterize the orthogonal group $\mathcal{O}(d)$. It defines an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in terms of a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ (where $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$):
$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$
In this formulation, the trainable parameters are the elements of the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. Because $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric, it contains significantly fewer independent parameters than a general matrix, which aligns with the goal of parameter efficiency.

Now, we can connect this back to Equation 1. For the simplest case where the block size $b=1$, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is composed of four diagonal matrices $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1)$ through $\text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)$ that form a $k \times k$ matrix. When $k=2$, this becomes a $2 \times 2$ matrix:
$$\bm{B}^F(2) = \begin{bmatrix} d_1 & d_2 \\ d_3 & d_4 \end{bmatrix}$$
To ensure this $2 \times 2$ matrix is orthogonal (a rotation matrix), the values in the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ must satisfy the properties of a rotation. For example, if we parameterize this as a rotation by an angle $\theta$, then $d_1 = \cos \theta$, $d_2 = -\sin \theta$, $d_3 = \sin \theta$, and $d_4 = \cos \theta$. 

In the general case where $b > 1$ or $k > 2$, the "vectors" $\bm{d}_i$ are the components derived from the Cayley transform applied to the $2b \times 2b$ blocks. Essentially, the authors are training the parameters of the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ for the base blocks, and the vectors $\bm{d}_i$ in Equation 1 are the resulting entries that populate the butterfly structure. By enforcing the Cayley transform on the base blocks and using permutations to arrange them into the butterfly pattern, the model ensures that every component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal without having to learn $d^2$ independent parameters for each matrix in the product.
