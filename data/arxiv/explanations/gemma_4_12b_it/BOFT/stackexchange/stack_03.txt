\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### If BOFT involves a product of $m$ matrices, why is there no additional inference latency?
Question:
I am a bit confused about the efficiency claim. The paper states that BOFT has no additional inference latency, but it also mentions that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of a product of multiple butterfly components $\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. If we have to perform $m$ matrix multiplications during the forward pass at inference time, wouldn't that add latency compared to the original model? Or is there a way to 'collapse' these matrices first?
Answer:
The key to understanding why there is no additional inference latency lies in the distinction between the **parameterization** used during training and the final **weight application** used during inference.

During the training phase, BOFT utilizes the butterfly factorization $\bm{R}(m,b) = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ specifically to achieve parameter efficiency. By representing the orthogonal matrix as a product of $m$ sparse matrices, the model only needs to store and optimize $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters instead of the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters required for a dense orthogonal matrix. This is the "trade-off" mentioned in the paper: the model accepts a slightly higher training runtime (because it must perform $m$ matrix multiplications to compute the transformation in every iteration) in exchange for a much smaller memory footprint and a more structured hypothesis class.

However, once the training process is finished, the butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ are no longer changing. Because these matrices are fixed, you can "collapse" them into a single dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ by pre-computing their product: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. 

The paper explains this in the concluding remarks: "after the finetuning stage, the BOFT-learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model." Mathematically, if the original forward pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, the finetuned pass is $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$. Since $\bm{R}$ is a constant $d \times d$ matrix after training, you can pre-calculate a new weight matrix $\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$. 

At inference time, the model no longer sees the product of $m$ matrices or even the matrix $\bm{R}$. It simply uses the new weight matrix $\bm{W}_{new}$. Because $\bm{W}_{new}$ has the exact same dimensions as the original $\bm{W}^0$, the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) and the memory required for the forward pass remain identical to the original model.

This makes BOFT a **reparameterization-based** method, similar to LoRA. In LoRA, the low-rank updates $\Delta \bm{W}$ are merged into the original weights ($\bm{W} + \Delta \bm{W}$) after training. In BOFT, the orthogonal transformation is merged into the weights via multiplication ($\bm{R} \bm{W}^0$). Both techniques ensure that the "extra" parameters used to guide the learning process disappear during deployment, leaving the model's inference speed untouched.
