\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does 'Multiplicative Dropout' differ from standard Dropout in the context of BOFT?
Question:
I understand that standard Dropout (zeroing out elements) doesn't work for BOFT because it's a multiplicative weight update. The paper proposes 'Multiplicative Dropout' which replaces blocks with identity matrices. Does this mean the 'dropped' components effectively do nothing to the weight matrix? And how does this maintain the orthogonality of the total matrix $\bm{R}$ throughout the training process?
Answer:
The distinction between standard Dropout and Multiplicative Dropout in the context of BOFT stems from the fundamental algebraic nature of how the weights are updated. In standard neural networks, weights are typically updated additively (e.g., $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W}$). In such a setting, Dropout works by zeroing out elements of $\Delta \bm{W}$, which effectively "turns off" certain learned features. However, BOFT uses an orthogonal finetuning approach where the new weight matrix is a multiplicative transformation of the pretrained weights: $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because this is a multiplicative update, zeroing out elements in the matrix $\bm{R}$ would destroy the orthogonality of the transformation and likely lead to a collapse of the signal, as the matrix would no longer be a rotation/reflection in the high-dimensional space.

To solve this, the authors propose Multiplicative Dropout, which operates on the factorized butterfly components of $\bm{R}$. Instead of zeroing out elements to produce a zero matrix, the "dropped" components are replaced with identity matrices $\bm{I}$. Intuitively, because $\bm{I}$ is the identity element for matrix multiplication, replacing a component with $\bm{I}$ means that specific part of the transformation "does nothing" to the data. It allows the information to pass through to the next layer in the factorization unchanged. In the same way that BOFT is initialized with identity matrices to ensure the model starts as the pretrained version, Multiplicative Dropout ensures that the dropped components act as "pass-through" gates, preserving the original features while preventing the model from over-relying on specific butterfly factorizations during training.

Regarding the maintenance of orthogonality, the beauty of this approach lies in the structural properties of the butterfly factorization. The total matrix $\bm{R}$ is defined as a product of several butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. For $\bm{R}$ to remain orthogonal, each individual component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ must be orthogonal. The authors note that these components can be permuted into $2b \times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices. Since the identity matrix $\bm{I}$ is itself an orthogonal matrix, replacing a block within these structures with $\bm{I}$ preserves the orthogonality of that specific component. Because the product of any number of orthogonal matrices is always orthogonal, the total matrix $\bm{R}$ remains perfectly orthogonal throughout the training process, even as different components are randomly "dropped" (turned into identities) in each forward pass. This allows the model to benefit from the regularization of Dropout without violating the geometric constraints required by Orthogonal Finetuning.
