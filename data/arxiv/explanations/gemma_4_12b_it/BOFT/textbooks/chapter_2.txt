\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 2: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principles and Limitations

# The geometry of weight matrices: Pairwise angles between neurons

When adapting large foundation models to specific downstream tasks, a primary challenge is ensuring that the model retains the vast amount of general knowledge acquired during its initial pretraining phase. Standard model finetuning often attempts to achieve this by using very small learning rates, which implicitly keeps the new weight matrices close to the original ones. However, the paper argues that this is an ad hoc approach. A more principled way to preserve pretraining knowledge is to focus on the "relational information" contained within the weight matrices. Instead of just looking at the magnitude of the weights, we should consider the geometry of the neurons.

Specifically, the geometry of a weight matrix can be characterized by the pairwise angles between its neurons. Each neuron can be viewed as a vector in a high-dimensional space. The relative angles between these vectors represent how the model distinguishes between different features and concepts. If we can modify a weight matrix in a way that preserves these pairwise angles, we ensure that the semantic relationships learned during pretraining remain intact, even as we adapt the model to a new task. This preservation of angular relationships is a fundamental insight that provides the motivation for the Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework.

By focusing on the geometry of the weight space, we shift the goal of finetuning from simple weight modification to a structured geometric transformation. The objective is to find a transformation that allows the model to learn new patterns while maintaining the "shape" of the pretraining knowledge. This geometric perspective provides a theoretical foundation for why certain finetuning methods might generalize better than others, as it provides a clear metric for what "preserving knowledge" actually means in the context of high-dimensional linear layers.

# Orthogonal transformation as a weight update mechanism

The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework implements the geometric preservation described above by using an orthogonal transformation to update the weights. Unlike popular methods such as LoRA, which update the pretrained weights by adding a low-rank matrix, OFT employs a multiplicative approach. Given a pretrained linear layer with a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, OFT introduces a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. The forward pass of the layer is then modified from the original $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to a new form: $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the output.

The use of an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is critical because orthogonal transformations are specifically designed to preserve the inner products and, consequently, the pairwise angles between vectors. Because $\bm{R}$ preserves these angles, the transformation $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)$ maintains the relational information of the neurons in the same layer. To ensure that the model starts from its original state before training begins, OFT initializes the matrix $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix $\bm{I}$. This "zero initialization" ensures that at the start of the finetuning process, the transformation has no effect, and the model behaves exactly like the original pretrained model.

This multiplicative mechanism distinguishes OFT from additive methods like LoRA. While LoRA introduces a small update to the weights, OFT applies a global rotation or reflection to the entire weight space of the layer. By constraining the transformation to be orthogonal, OFT provides a principled way to explore the space of possible weight configurations while strictly adhering to the requirement of preserving pretraining knowledge. This provides a stable and generalizable framework for adapting models to various downstream tasks.

# The Cayley parameterization for ensuring orthogonality

A significant technical challenge in OFT is ensuring that the matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal throughout the entire training process. In standard gradient descent, updating the entries of a matrix will naturally move it away from the manifold of orthogonal matrices. To solve this, the paper adopts a specific mathematical formulation known as the Cayley parameterization. Instead of optimizing the entries of $\bm{R}$ directly, we parameterize $\bm{R}$ using a skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$.

The Cayley parameterization is defined by the formula $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. A matrix $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric if it satisfies the condition $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. By using this construction, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ is mathematically guaranteed to be orthogonal regardless of the values within $\bm{Q}$. This allows the optimization process to freely update the entries of $\bm{Q}$ using standard backpropagation while the "orthogonality constraint" is automatically satisfied by the structure of the formula itself.

By employing this parameterization, OFT avoids the need for complex constrained optimization techniques or manual projection back onto the orthogonal manifold after every gradient step. This ensures that the training remains stable and that the primary goal—preserving the pairwise angles of the neurons—is never violated during the learning process. It provides a robust mathematical bridge between the goal of orthogonal transformation and the practical realities of training neural networks using gradient descent.

# The block-diagonal structure in original OFT: Trade-offs between parameter efficiency and expressivity

While the original OFT framework is theoretically sound, it faces a practical hurdle regarding parameter efficiency. A full $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters. For large foundation models where the dimension $d$ is very high, this number of parameters can be prohibitively large, making OFT less efficient than other methods like LoRA. To mitigate this, the original OFT introduces a block-diagonal structure to the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$.

In this structure, $\bm{R}$ is parameterized as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times b}$ is a smaller orthogonal matrix. Here, $b$ represents the block size and $r$ is the number of blocks, such that $br = d$. By making the blocks smaller, the number of trainable parameters is significantly reduced. For example, as the block size $b$ decreases, the model becomes more parameter-efficient. However, this efficiency comes at a cost to the model's expressivity.

The primary drawback of the block-diagonal structure is that it assumes the dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ independent groups. In this setup, the dimensions in different groups are transformed separately by different orthogonal matrices. This introduces a rigid structure where information cannot flow between the groups during the transformation. While this saves parameters, it limits the range of transformations the model can represent, potentially hindering its ability to learn complex relationships that span across the entire $d$-dimensional space.

# The problem of fixed sparsity patterns and hidden inductive biases

A deeper limitation of the original OFT arises from the fixed nature of the block-diagonal sparsity pattern. Because the blocks are pre-defined by their indices in the matrix, the sparsity pattern of the transformation is "hard-coded." This creates a significant problem: the block-diagonal structure is unable to approximate many classic linear transforms. Because the transformation is restricted to independent blocks, it cannot represent any operation that requires interaction between different indices in the weight matrix. This lack of expressiveness is a direct result of the chosen sparsity pattern.

Furthermore, this fixed structure introduces undesirable inductive biases. An inductive bias is an implicit assumption the model makes about the data or the task. By choosing a block-diagonal structure, we are forcing the model to believe that the features represented by different indices are independent of one another. This may not be true for many downstream tasks, and such a strong, arbitrary assumption can limit the model's generalizability. The paper notes that finding a "good" sparsity pattern—one that is both parameter-efficient and expressive enough to avoid these negative biases—remains a significant mystery.

In summary, while the original OFT was a breakthrough in principled finetuning, its reliance on block-diagonal matrices creates a tension between efficiency and capability. It achieves parameter efficiency by sacrificing the ability to model dense connections, and it introduces a rigid architectural bias that may not suit all applications. These limitations provide the motivation for the development of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method, which seeks to find a more sophisticated way to achieve dense connectivity with sparse parameterization.
