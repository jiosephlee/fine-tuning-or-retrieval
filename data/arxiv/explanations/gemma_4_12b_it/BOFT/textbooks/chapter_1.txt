\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 1: Foundations of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)

# The rise of foundation models

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has been transformed by the emergence of "foundation models." These are large-scale models trained on vast amounts of data that demonstrate a remarkable ability to generalize across a wide array of tasks. Prominent examples of these models include ChatGPT, which has revolutionized natural language processing, and Stable Diffusion, which has made significant strides in text-to-image generation. The success of these models is largely attributed to their massive scale; for instance, the GPT-3 model contains approximately $175$ billion parameters. 

The significance of foundation models lies in their ability to serve as a versatile base. Instead of building a new, specialized model for every individual task—such as summarizing a specific type of legal document or identifying specific objects in a medical image—researchers can start with a foundation model that already possesses a deep, broad understanding of language, images, or other data modalities. This shift toward using large-scale pre-trained models as a starting point has fundamentally changed how research is conducted, moving the focus from "training from scratch" to "efficient adaptation."

# The cost-prohibitive nature of training from scratch

While foundation models offer immense power, their creation is an incredibly resource-intensive process. Training a model with hundreds of billions of parameters requires an enormous amount of computational power, high-end hardware, and significant time. For most researchers, organizations, and even many large companies, training a foundation model from scratch is prohibitively expensive. The sheer scale of the data and the hardware requirements create a barrier to entry that prevents the rapid development of new base models for every niche application.

Because training from scratch is often infeasible, the primary challenge in the field has shifted toward adaptation. The goal is to take a powerful, pre-trained foundation model and modify it so that it performs exceptionally well on a specific downstream task. This process must be done efficiently. If the adaptation process requires as much computation or memory as training the original model, it defeats the purpose of using a foundation model. Therefore, developing methods that can "fine-tune" or "adapt" these models without retraining them from the ground up is a critical area of modern machine learning research.

# Taxonomy of adaptation methods: Model Finetuning, Adapter Tuning, and Prompt Tuning

To address the need for efficient adaptation, several primary strategies have emerged. These methods are generally categorized based on how they modify the original foundation model to suit a new task.

The first category is model finetuning. In this approach, the underlying architecture of the model remains completely unchanged. Instead of training the entire model, only a specific subset of the existing parameters is updated. This allows the model to learn the nuances of a new task while still utilizing the vast majority of the knowledge stored in the frozen parameters of the foundation model.

The second category is adapter tuning. Rather than modifying the existing parameters of the foundation model, adapter tuning involves inserting new, trainable parameters into the architecture. These "adapters" are typically small modules added between the existing layers of the model. During the adaptation process, only the weights of these added modules are updated, while the weights of the original foundation model remain fixed.

The third category is prompt tuning. This method takes a different approach by keeping the model's parameters entirely frozen. Instead, the researcher attaches additional trainable "prefix tokens" to the input. These tokens are learned during the adaptation process and serve to "steer" the pre-trained model toward the desired output for a specific task. By modifying the input representation rather than the weights, prompt tuning achieves adaptation with very few trainable parameters.

# The advantages of model finetuning: Simplicity and zero inference latency

Among the three primary methods of adaptation, model finetuning is often distinguished by its balance of power and simplicity. Because it works by updating a subset of the weights within the original architecture, it is a very straightforward approach to implementation. It does not require the structural modifications seen in adapter tuning or the complex input engineering sometimes required in prompt tuning.

A major technical advantage of model finetuning is that it introduces zero inference latency. In production environments, "latency" refers to the time it takes for a model to produce an output once it receives an input. Methods like adapter tuning add extra layers to the model's forward pass, and prompt tuning increases the length of the input sequence. Both of these can add computational overhead during the inference phase. However, because model finetuning simply replaces a portion of the existing weights with new ones, the final model's architecture is identical to the original. The model "runs" exactly as it did before, meaning there is no additional time cost when the model is deployed for use.

# Core principles of finetuning: Preserving pretraining knowledge and relational information

The fundamental goal of any finetuning method is to ensure that the model adapts to a new task without "forgetting" the extensive knowledge it acquired during its initial pretraining phase. To achieve this, the finetuned model must remain "similar" to the pretrained model according to specific measurements. 

One common, albeit somewhat ad hoc, way to ensure this similarity is to use a very small learning rate. By making the changes to the weights very small, researchers hope to prevent the model from drifting too far from its original state. However, there is a more principled way to approach this problem by looking at the structured nature of weight matrices. Specifically, researchers have observed that preserving the "relational information" of the weight matrices—such as the pairwise angles between neurons—is crucial for maintaining the semantic knowledge of the model.

This insight leads to the framework of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT). In OFT, rather than just making small changes to the weights, the neurons in the same layer are transformed by a learnable orthogonal matrix. By using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved throughout the finetuning process. For a pretrained linear layer with weights $\bm{W}^0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, the forward pass is modified from $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$ to $\bm{z} = (\bm{R} \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$, where $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the input and $\bm{z} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the output. By ensuring $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal, the model can adapt to a new task while maintaining the underlying geometric structure of the pre-trained representations.
