\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 5: Theoretical Insights and Inductive Biases

# Expressivity analysis: Approximating the full orthogonal group

In the context of parameter-efficient finetuning, a critical question arises regarding the "expressivity" of the chosen transformation. Expressivity refers to the ability of a model's parameterization to represent a wide enough range of possible functions or transformations to effectively adapt to a downstream task. For Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the goal is to transform the pretrained weight matrix using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. While a dense, full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ offers the highest possible expressivity because it can represent any rotation or reflection in $d$-dimensional space, it is computationally expensive, requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters.

To achieve parameter efficiency, we must move toward structured matrices that use fewer parameters, such as the block-diagonal structure used in the original OFT or the butterfly factorization used in Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). However, there is an inherent tension between expressivity and regularity. A model with universal expressivity (like full finetuning) can theoretically represent any transformation, yet it often performs poorly on specific downstream tasks because it can overfit to noise. Conversely, a model with high regularity (a very restricted hypothesis class) might be too simple to capture the necessary nuances of the task.

BOFT seeks a "smoother interpolation" between these two extremes. By using the butterfly structure, BOFT constructs a hypothesis class that is more expressive than the block-diagonal structure but more regular than a fully dense orthogonal matrix. The goal is to find a structured subset of the orthogonal group that is rich enough to capture the necessary features of the downstream task while remaining constrained enough to prevent overfitting. This suggests that the effectiveness of BOFT lies not in its ability to represent *every* possible transformation, but in its ability to represent a *useful* subset of transformations efficiently.

# Theorem 1: BOFT vs. OFT expressivity

To formally understand the advantage of the butterfly structure over the block-diagonal structure, we can compare their expressiveness. The original OFT uses a block-diagonal matrix $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i$ is a small orthogonal matrix of size $b \times b$. This structure imposes a very rigid constraint: dimensions of a neuron are divided into $r$ independent groups, and transformations are applied to these groups separately. This significantly limits the model's ability to learn correlations between different groups of dimensions.

The paper introduces a formal comparison through Theorem 1, which states that BOFT is more expressive than OFT when using the same block size $b$. This is because the butterfly structure, combined with permutations, can recover many classic linear transforms that the block-diagonal structure simply cannot. For example, the block-diagonal structure cannot approximate any classic fast linear transforms because it cannot create cross-block dependencies.

Furthermore, the paper highlights that the butterfly matrix can be generalized even further. By composing multiple butterfly matrices with their transposes—a construction known as a kaleidoscope hierarchy—one can actually represent the entire orthogonal group. Specifically, if we define a generalized orthogonal matrix as a product of butterfly matrices and their transposes, such as $\bm{R}^G(m_1, b_1, m_2, b_2, l) = \bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1, b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2, b_2) \cdots \bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1, b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_1, b_2)$, and set the parameters to $m_1 = m_2 = \log d$, $b_1 = b_2 = 2$, and $l = d - 1$, the framework can represent the entire orthogonal group. This theoretical insight confirms that BOFT provides a scalable path toward full expressivity while maintaining the ability to restrict the hypothesis class for better generalization.

# Spectral properties: Preservation of the spectral norm and training stability

One of the most significant mathematical advantages of using orthogonal transformations for finetuning is the preservation of the spectral properties of the weight matrices. In many finetuning methods, such as LoRA, the pretrained weights are updated by adding a low-rank matrix. This addition can alter the singular values of the weight matrix, potentially leading to unstable training or a loss of the original model's characteristics.

In contrast, both OFT and BOFT modify the weights multiplicatively by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. To understand why this is beneficial, consider the singular value decomposition (SVD) of a pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, which can be written as $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. In this decomposition, $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing the singular values. When we apply BOFT, the new weight matrix becomes $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0 = \bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. Because $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{U}$ are both orthogonal matrices, their product $\bm{R}\bm{U}$ is also an orthogonal matrix. 

Consequently, the singular values (the entries of $\bm{\Sigma}$) remain completely unchanged. The spectral norm of the weight matrix, which is defined by its largest singular value, is perfectly preserved throughout the finetuning process. Research has shown that preserving the spectral norm is highly beneficial for training stability and helps the model generalize better by ensuring that the "energy" or scale of the weights does not explode or vanish during adaptation. This is a key reason why orthogonal-based methods are often more stable than additive low-rank methods.

# BOFT as a form of learning bilinear similarity

Beyond its spectral benefits, BOFT can be interpreted through the lens of bilinear similarity. When we apply the transformation $\bm{R}$ to the weights, the forward pass involves calculating the similarity between the input $\bm{x}$ and the transformed weight vectors. Specifically, if $\bm{w}^0_i$ represents the $i$-th neuron (the $i$-th column vector of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$), the output is essentially learning the bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i \bm{R} \bm{x}$.

By viewing the task this way, BOFT is framed as a method for learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ that is subject to a strong regularity constraint: the matrix $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal. This connects the method to established fields like distance metric learning and bilinear forms. In these fields, the goal is often to learn a transformation that preserves important geometric relationships between data points. By enforcing orthogonality, BOFT ensures that the transformation $\bm{R}$ preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, which the paper identifies as a crucial step in preserving the semantic knowledge captured during the original pretraining phase.

# The role of structured inductive biases: Linking to FFT, Hadamard, and Cosine transforms

A fundamental concept in machine learning is the inductive bias—the set of assumptions a model uses to predict outputs for inputs it has not encountered before. BOFT introduces a specific, structured inductive bias by utilizing the butterfly factorization. The butterfly structure is not an arbitrary choice; it is the same underlying topology used in many classic and highly efficient linear transforms, including the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), the Hadamard transform, and the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT).

By choosing the butterfly structure, BOFT implicitly biases the model toward these types of transformations. Because these transforms are widely used and have well-understood properties in signal processing and data analysis, this bias acts as a powerful regularizer. It encourages the model to find solutions that share structural patterns with these classic transforms, which can help prevent the model from overfitting to the specific noise of the downstream dataset.

Additionally, the use of sparse matrix factorization in BOFT provides another layer of implicit inductive bias. Sparse structures naturally limit the complexity of the hypothesis class. When combined with the butterfly structure, these biases work together to ensure that the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ remains within a manageable and geometrically meaningful subset of the orthogonal group. This combination of "structured sparsity" and "classic transform topology" is what allows BOFT to achieve high parameter efficiency without sacrificing the ability to generalize to new tasks.
