# Simplifying the Giants: How GSPO Unlocks Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become the "heavy hitters" of the modern AI landscape. By activating only a subset of parameters (experts) for each token, these models can achieve massive scale with greater computational efficiency. However, this sparse architecture introduces a significant hurdle: training them using Reinforcement Learning (RL) is notoriously unstable. This instability often stems from a phenomenon known as "expert volatility," which can lead to catastrophic model collapse during training.

A new algorithm, Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), offers a way to bypass these headaches. By shifting the fundamental unit of optimization from the token level to the sequence level, GSPO stabilizes MoE training and removes the need for complex technical workarounds.

## The Challenge: Expert Volatility

To understand why GSPO is necessary, we first have to look at why current RL algorithms, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), struggle with MoE models. 

In a standard RL setup, we use "importance sampling" to correct for the fact that we are training on data sampled from an old policy ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) rather than the current policy ($\pi_\theta$). GRPO performs this correction at the token level. It calculates an importance ratio for every individual token:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

In MoE models, this approach is problematic because of expert-activation volatility. Because the model is sparse, even a small gradient update can cause the model to route a token to a different expert than it did in the previous step. In a 48-layer model, for example, roughly 10% of the experts activated for a response might change after a single update. These tiny shifts in routing cause the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly, introducing high-variance noise into the gradients and eventually causing the training process to collapse.

## The "Hack": Routing Replay

Before the introduction of GSPO, researchers had to use a workaround to keep MoE RL training on track, known as "Routing Replay." 

To stabilize the training, developers would cache the specific experts activated by the old policy and "replay" those exact routing modes when calculating the importance ratios for the new policy. This ensured that the gradients were updated on a consistent set of experts. While effective, Routing Replay comes with significant costs:
*   It introduces additional memory and communication overhead.
*   It can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model by forcing it to stick to specific routing paths.
*   It adds significant complexity to the RL infrastructure.

## The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Optimization

GSPO provides a more elegant solution by changing the unit of optimization. Instead of trying to correct the distribution for every single token, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence.

The GSPO importance ratio, $s_{i}(\theta)$, is defined as:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

By using the sequence likelihood, GSPO achieves several major advantages:

**1. Immunity to Volatility**
While individual tokens might be routed to different experts between updates, the model’s overall ability to generate a coherent sequence remains relatively stable. Because GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood ($\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)$) rather than the specific path taken by every token, it is naturally resistant to the expert-activation volatility that plagues GRPO.

**2. Elimination of Workarounds**
Because GSPO is inherently stable for MoE models, it completely obviates the need for Routing Replay. This allows models to leverage their full capacity without artificial routing constraints and simplifies the training pipeline significantly.

**3. Infrastructure Simplification**
GSPO also eases the burden on engineering teams. Because it relies on sequence-level likelihoods, it is much more tolerant of the precision discrepancies that often exist between training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like vLLM). This makes it easier to use likelihoods directly from the inference engine, simplifying the design of RL infrastructure for multi-turn and large-scale training.

## Conclusion

By moving the goalposts from token-level precision to sequence-level stability, GSPO solves one of the most persistent problems in scaling MoE models. It provides a robust, efficient foundation that has already contributed to the performance of the latest Qwen3 models. For developers looking to push the boundaries of large-scale RL, GSPO offers a path toward simpler, more stable, and more performant training.
