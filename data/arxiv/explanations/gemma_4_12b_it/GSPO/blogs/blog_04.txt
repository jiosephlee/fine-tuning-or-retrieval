\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# Bridging the Gap: Making AI Infrastructure Easier to Build

One of the most persistent "hidden" headaches in large-scale AI engineering is the friction between the training environment and the inference environment. While we often focus on the nuances of model architecture or the math of reinforcement learning (RL), the physical infrastructure required to run these processes introduces a significant challenge: precision discrepancy.

When training large language models (LLMs) using reinforcement learning, we typically use different software stacks for different stages of the pipeline. For example, training engines like Megatron are optimized for massive gradient updates, while inference engines like vLLM or SGLang are optimized for high-throughput generation. Because these engines use different kernels, optimizations, and sometimes different floating-point precisions, they can produce slightly different results for the exact same input. In many RL algorithms, these tiny discrepancies can compound into major problems, complicating the development of stable training pipelines.

## The Problem with Token-Level Optimization

To understand why this is a problem, we have to look at how current state-of-the-art RL algorithms, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), handle "off-policy" data. When we train on a batch of data, we are often optimizing a current policy ($\pi_\theta$) using responses that were sampled from an older policy ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$). To make this work, we use importance sampling to correct for the fact that the data came from a different distribution.

In GRPO, this correction happens at the token level. The algorithm calculates an importance ratio for every single token $y_{i,t}$ in a response:

$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$

Because this ratio is calculated for every token, even a microscopic difference in how the training engine and inference engine calculate $\pi(y_t)$ can lead to "noise." Over a long sequence of tokens, these small errors can accumulate, leading to unstable gradients and, in some cases, catastrophic model collapse. This forces engineers to build complex workarounds to ensure that the probabilities used during the update perfectly match those used during generation.

## The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Stability

The authors of the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) paper propose a shift in perspective: instead of correcting the distribution at the token level, we should optimize at the sequence level. 

The core innovation of GSPO is defining the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$

By using the sequence likelihood, GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward (since rewards are typically given for the entire response, not individual tokens). More importantly, from an infrastructure standpoint, this approach is inherently more "tolerant" of precision discrepancies. 

Because $s_i(\theta)$ looks at the probability of the whole response, the tiny floating-point jitters that occur at the individual token level are smoothed out. The sequence-level likelihood is a much more robust metric, making it far less sensitive to the fact that a training engine and an inference engine might calculate probabilities slightly differently.

## Simplifying the RL Infrastructure

This shift from token-level to sequence-level optimization has profound implications for how we build AI infrastructure. 

Currently, to ensure consistency, many teams have to perform "recomputation"—meaning they take the responses generated by the inference engine and run them through the training engine again just to get a "canonical" likelihood score for the RL update. This is computationally expensive and adds significant complexity to the data pipeline.

GSPO changes the game by potentially allowing us to:
*   **Directly use inference engine outputs:** Developers can use the likelihoods returned directly by engines like vLLM or SGLang for the optimization step.
*   **Simplify Disaggregated Frameworks:** In setups where training and inference are physically separated on different hardware clusters, GSPO removes the need for the training cluster to "re-verify" the generation results.
*   **Scale Multi-turn and Partial Rollouts:** It simplifies the logic required for complex tasks like multi-turn dialogue or partial rollouts, where managing token-level consistency across multiple turns becomes an engineering nightmare.

By solving a fundamental mathematical mismatch in how we weight importance ratios, GSPO doesn't just improve model performance—it clears a major engineering hurdle, making it easier to build the robust infrastructure needed to scale the next generation of AI.
