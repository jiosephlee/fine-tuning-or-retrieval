\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why is there a $1/|y_i|$ exponent in the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$?
Question:
In the definition of GSPO, the sequence-level importance ratio is given as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. I understand that $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} (y_i | x)}$ is the standard importance sampling ratio for a sequence. Why is it necessary to take the $|y_i|$-th root? Does this normalization simply keep the value near 1, or does it fundamentally change the gradient $\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}$ compared to a standard sequence-level ratio?
Answer:
The presence of the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent in the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ serves two primary purposes: it acts as a variance reduction technique through length normalization and it fundamentally alters the weighting of token-level gradients compared to algorithms like GRPO.

From a numerical perspective, the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ is a product of probabilities for each token in the sequence. Because probabilities are $\le 1$, the likelihood value decays exponentially as the sequence length $|y_i|$ increases. Without the root, the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}$ would become extremely sensitive to the length of the response; even a tiny change in the model's output distribution for a single token would be compounded by the total number of tokens in the sequence. By taking the $|y_i|$-th root, the authors are essentially calculating the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios. This normalization keeps $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range, ensuring that the importance ratio remains close to 1 regardless of whether the response is short or long. This is crucial for the clipping mechanism, as it allows the researchers to use a consistent clipping range $\varepsilon$ across the entire batch. Without this normalization, a short sequence and a long sequence would require vastly different clipping thresholds to achieve the same level of training stability.

Beyond numerical stability, the exponent fundamentally changes the gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}$ in a way that addresses the "ill-posed" nature of GRPO. In GRPO, the gradient involves a summation where each token's log-likelihood gradient is weighted by its own specific token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})}$. Because these individual ratios can vary significantly between tokens, the resulting gradient becomes noisy, and this noise accumulates as the sequence length grows. 

In contrast, the gradient analysis in the paper shows that by using $s_i(\theta)$, GSPO weights all tokens in a response equally. Looking at the derived gradient in Equation 10:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_{i}(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$$
Here, the term $s_i(\theta)$ is factored out of the inner summation. This means that for a given sequence $i$, the gradient update treats every token $y_{i,t}$ with the same weight, scaled by the overall sequence-level importance ratio. By moving from token-level weighting to sequence-level weighting, GSPO eliminates the high-variance fluctuations caused by individual token ratios, which the authors identify as a primary driver of model collapse in previous methods. Thus, the exponent doesn't just "keep the value near 1"; it transforms the optimization objective from a weighted sum of independent token probabilities into a smoothed update where the reward is distributed uniformly across the sequence.

### If rewards are sequence-level, why does the GRPO objective still include a summation over tokens $t$?
Question:
The paper argues that 'the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward' and suggests that token-level optimization is problematic because rewards are granted to the entire sequence. However, in Equation 2, the GRPO objective $\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}$ still contains the term $\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}$. If the optimization is supposed to be sequence-level, why aren't we removing the summation over $t$ entirely, rather than just changing how the importance weight is calculated?
Answer:
The reason the GRPO objective retains the summation over tokens $t$ despite having sequence-level rewards lies in the fundamental way GRPO (and PPO) treats the importance sampling weight. 

To understand this, we have to look at the "unit" of the importance weight. In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is defined at the token level: 
$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t}) }.$$

Even though the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ is a single scalar shared by all tokens in a sequence (because the reward is sequence-level), GRPO applies a unique importance weight to every single token $y_{i,t}$ within that sequence. Because the weights are calculated per-token, the objective function must sum these weighted rewards across all tokens to produce a single scalar value for the gradient update. Mathematically, this is expressed as the average of the weighted advantages: $\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i}$. This summation is necessary because the optimization is still technically occurring at the token level; we are updating the policy to favor individual tokens that contribute to a high-reward sequence.

The paper argues that this is "ill-posed" because a token-level importance weight $w_{i,t}$ is based on a single sample from a single next-token distribution. In standard importance sampling, a weight is only valid if it correctly corrects the distribution mismatch; doing this at the token level without averaging over many samples introduces high-variance noise.

This is exactly why GSPO removes the summation. In GSPO, the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined at the sequence level:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}.$$

Because $s_i(\theta)$ is a single scalar that represents the deviation of the entire sequence, it acts as the weight for the entire sequence's reward. You no longer need to sum over $t$ because you aren't weighting individual tokens anymore; you are weighting the entire response. 

We can see this clearly by comparing the gradients provided in the paper. In the GRPO gradient (Equation 8), the importance weight $w_{i,t}$ sits *inside* the summation:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta) \propto \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} w_{i,t}(\theta) \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}).$$

In the GSPO gradient (Equation 6), the sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$ sits *outside* the summation:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) \propto s_i(\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}).$$

By moving the weight outside the sum, GSPO ensures that all tokens in a response are weighted equally. This aligns the "unit of optimization" (the sequence weight $s_i$) with the "unit of reward" (the sequence advantage $\widehat{A}_i$), which is the core improvement the authors are proposing to solve the stability issues found in GRPO.

### How does the $\mathrm{sg}$ operation in GSPO-token maintain numerical identity with GSPO?
Question:
In the GSPO-token variant, the paper defines $s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} [ s_{i}(\theta) ] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] }$. The authors claim that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_{i}(\theta)$. I am confused about how the stop-gradient ($\mathrm{sg}$) operation works here. If we stop the gradient on the denominator, doesn't that mean the ratio $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] }$ effectively behaves like a constant during the backward pass? How does this allow the gradient to correctly reflect the log-likelihood of the tokens?
Answer:
To understand how the $\mathrm{sg}$ operation maintains numerical identity while preserving the intended gradient, it helps to look at the mathematical properties of the stop-gradient operation and how it interacts with the ratio in the GSPO-token objective.

The stop-gradient operation, $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$, essentially treats its input as a constant during the backward pass. It does not change the numerical value of the expression (i.e., $x = \mathrm{sg}[x]$), but it ensures that $\nabla_{\theta} \mathrm{sg}[f(\theta)] = 0$.

In the GSPO-token variant, the term is defined as:
$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} [ s_{i}(\theta) ] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] }$$

To see why this is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$, we look at the value of the expression. Because $\mathrm{sg}$ preserves the numerical value, the fraction $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] }$ is always exactly $1$. Consequently, the numerical value of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is simply $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$, which is equal to $s_i(\theta)$. This is why the authors state that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_i(\theta)$ for purposes like the clipping condition and the forward pass of the objective function.

The confusion regarding the gradient arises from the assumption that the entire ratio behaves like a constant. However, only the **denominator** of the ratio is stopped. The numerator, $\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$, is still fully differentiable with respect to $\theta$. 

When we compute the gradient of the GSPO-token objective $\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}}$ with respect to $\theta$ (ignoring clipping for clarity), we apply the gradient to $s_{i,t}(\theta)$. Since $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ is treated as a constant, it is pulled out of the gradient operation:
$$\nabla_{\theta} s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] } \right)$$

Because the denominator $\mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta}(\dots)]$ is a constant, the gradient of the fraction is simply the gradient of the numerator divided by that constant:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) ] } \right) = \frac{ \nabla_{\theta} \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) } = \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$

Substituting this back into the gradient of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$, we get:
$$\nabla_{\theta} s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$

When you multiply this by the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ in the full objective, the resulting gradient is:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO-token}} \propto \sum_{t} \widehat{A}_{i,t} \cdot \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$

In the standard GSPO algorithm, the gradient is derived as:
$$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} \propto \sum_{t} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot s_i(\theta) \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$$

The two are theoretically identical because $s_i(\theta)$ in the GSPO gradient and $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ in the GSPO-token gradient both act as the same scalar weight for the log-likelihood gradients. The stop-gradient trick is essentially a mathematical "bridge": it allows the model to use the sequence-level importance ratio as a constant weight to scale the token-level updates, preventing the token-level gradients from "bleeding back" into the sequence-level ratio calculation while still providing the flexibility to use different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ for each token.

### Why does 'expert-activation volatility' specifically invalidate importance sampling in MoE models?
Question:
In Section 5.3, the authors explain that in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, the experts activated for a response can change significantly between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. They claim this 'invalidates' the importance sampling weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$. I am struggling to understand the theoretical link here: if $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is simply the ratio of the probabilities of the tokens, why does the fact that different experts are active in the two models make that ratio mathematically 'invalid' rather than just high-variance?
Answer:
The term "invalidates" in this context refers to a violation of a fundamental assumption underlying importance sampling: that the two distributions being compared are related by a smooth, continuous change in parameters. In a standard dense model, as $\theta$ moves toward $\theta_{\text{old}}$, the probability $\pi_\theta(y_t)$ changes predictably because the same set of weights is being updated. However, in a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, the probability of a token is determined by a routing mechanism that selects a specific subset of experts.

When the authors describe "expert-activation volatility," they are referring to a situation where the router in $\pi_{\theta}$ selects a different set of experts than the router in $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for the same token. Mathematically, this means the numerator $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ and the denominator $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})$ are being computed by entirely different sub-networks. In this scenario, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is no longer comparing two slightly different versions of the same function; it is comparing two different functions altogether. Because the underlying "pathway" of the computation has changed, the ratio fails to provide a valid correction of the distribution. It ceases to be a meaningful measure of how much the policy has shifted and instead becomes a source of extreme, non-representative noise because the denominator provides no coherent baseline for the numerator's value.

This distinction is why the authors claim the objective is "ill-posed" rather than just "high-variance." High variance implies that the ratio is still a valid estimator of the importance weight but happens to have a large standard deviation. "Invalid" implies that the fundamental requirement for importance sampling—that the behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$ provides a valid support for the target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$—is broken. In MoE, if the expert selection flips, the two models are effectively performing different operations for that specific token.

GSPO addresses this by shifting the unit of optimization to the sequence level. The paper argues that while internal expert routing may be volatile, the overall sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ remains relatively stable because the model's primary goal is to maintain its language modeling capability. By using the sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$, the objective focuses on the final output distribution rather than the brittle, per-token routing paths. This ensures that the importance sampling weight correctly reflects how the entire response deviates between the two policies, remaining robust even if the specific experts used to generate the tokens fluctuate between updates.

### Why does clipping a much larger fraction of tokens in GSPO lead to higher training efficiency?
Question:
Figure 4 shows that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO. In most RL settings, clipping more data usually implies we are discarding more of the signal, which should theoretically slow down learning or reduce efficiency. Why does the paper claim this counter-intuitive result is a sign of better efficiency? Is it because the gradients from the non-clipped tokens are significantly more accurate?
Answer:
The reason why clipping a much larger fraction of tokens in GSPO leads to higher training efficiency—despite the intuition that discarding more data should slow down learning—lies in the difference between **signal quality** and **signal quantity**. The paper argues that the core issue with GRPO is that its gradient estimates are "inherently noisy and inefficient," meaning that while GRPO might be using more tokens to compute a gradient, the information provided by those tokens is less reliable for model optimization.

To understand this, we must look at the fundamental difference in how these two algorithms define importance weights. GRPO applies importance sampling at the token level, using the weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t}) }$. The authors point out that this is technically an ill-posed application of importance sampling. Because a reward is granted to an entire sequence, but the weights are applied to individual tokens based on a single sample from the behavior distribution, these weights fail to perform the intended distribution correction. Instead, they introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. Because these individual token weights $w_{i,t}$ fluctuate significantly, they frequently cross the clipping thresholds, leading to the very high fraction of clipped tokens observed in the experiments.

In contrast, GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward by using a sequence-level importance ratio: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. This ratio represents how much the entire response deviates from the old policy. When we derive the gradient for GSPO, we see that the sequence-level ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ acts as a global multiplier for the entire sequence. Consequently, GSPO weights all tokens within a response equally during the gradient update. This eliminates the "instability factor" of GRPO, where different tokens in the same sequence would be weighted differently based on volatile, unreliable ratios.

The "counter-intuitive" efficiency of GSPO stems from this stability. Even though GSPO might clip more tokens (because its clipping mechanism is applied to the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than the volatile token-level $w_{i,t}$), the gradients it *does* use are significantly more accurate and stable. In GRPO, the high fraction of clipped tokens is a symptom of the "noisy" and "ill-posed" nature of its gradient estimates. By moving to a sequence-level approach, GSPO ensures that the optimization signal remains clean and consistent, allowing the model to exploit the training samples more effectively. In short, GSPO is more efficient because it prioritizes the reliability of the learning signal over the raw volume of tokens processed.

### How does GSPO allow the use of inference engine likelihoods while GRPO does not?
Question:
The paper mentions that GSPO is more tolerant of precision discrepancies between training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like vLLM). Why does using a sequence-level likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y|x)$ solve this problem, while the token-level likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x, y_{<t})$ used in GRPO does not? Is it because of the accumulation of floating-point errors over the length of the sequence $|y|$?
Answer:
The difference in how GSPO and GRPO handle inference engine likelihoods stems from the mathematical "unit" of the importance ratio and how errors are propagated through the objective function.

In GRPO, the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is calculated at the token level for every position $t$. This means the algorithm requires highly precise, consistent likelihoods for every single token in a sequence. If there is a small floating-point discrepancy between the training engine (e.g., Megatron) and the inference engine (e.g., vLLM), these errors occur independently at each token position. Because $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is used directly to weight the gradient of each token, these discrepancies are not "averaged out"; instead, they introduce high-variance noise into the gradient estimation. As the paper notes, this noise can accumulate over long sequences, leading to the instability and model collapse observed in GRPO.

GSPO addresses this by using a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Mathematically, GSPO defines this ratio as:
$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$$
This formula reveals why GSPO is more tolerant of precision discrepancies. By taking the sum of the log-likelihoods and dividing by the sequence length $|y_i|$, GSPO essentially computes the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios. In this structure, any precision discrepancy or floating-point error at a specific token position $t$ is "diluted" by the total length of the sequence. 

To put it intuitively, while GRPO is sensitive to the *exact* value of every individual token's weight, GSPO is only sensitive to the *average* log-probability of the sequence. Even if an inference engine has slightly different numerical outputs than a training engine, the average over many tokens remains much more stable. This smoothing effect makes the optimization objective significantly more robust to the differences in kernels, precision (like FP16 vs. BF16), or hardware-specific floating-point behaviors between different software stacks. Consequently, GSPO can reliably use the likelihoods provided by fast inference engines without the risk of the training noise that plagues token-level methods like GRPO.
