# Why AI Models 'Collapse' During Training—and How GSPO Fixes It

Scaling reinforcement learning (RL) is currently one of the most effective ways to push the boundaries of what large language models (LLMs) can do. By using RL, models can learn to perform complex reasoning, such as solving high-level mathematics or programming tasks. However, as we try to scale these models to larger sizes and longer reasoning paths, we hit a major roadblock: catastrophic model collapse.

In many cases, training a massive model using state-of-the-art RL algorithms can result in an irreversible breakdown of intelligence. To understand how to fix this, we need to look at the underlying mechanics of how these models learn from their mistakes.

## The Root of the Problem: Token-Level Noise

Current popular RL algorithms, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), face significant stability issues when training gigantic models. The researchers behind the new Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm identified that the culprit is a fundamental misapplication of "importance sampling."

In reinforcement learning, we often use an "old" policy to generate samples and then update a "new" policy. To do this correctly, we use importance weights to correct for the difference between the old and new policies. GRPO performs this correction at the **token level**. Specifically, it calculates a weight for every single token produced in a response:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

The problem is that this weight is based on a single sample from a distribution. Because it doesn't average over multiple samples, it introduces high-variance noise into the training gradients. As a response gets longer, this noise accumulates and compounds. When combined with the "clipping" mechanisms used to keep training stable, this noise can eventually lead to a total collapse of the model's weights, making it impossible to recover even with hyperparameter tuning.

## The GSPO Solution: Optimization at the Sequence Level

The core philosophy of GSPO is simple: **the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward.** Since a reward is typically given for an entire sequence (e.g., "did the model solve the math problem?"), the importance weights should also be calculated at the sequence level.

Instead of weighting every token individually, GSPO uses a sequence-level importance ratio:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

By using the likelihood of the entire sequence $y_i$ and applying length normalization, GSPO provides a much more stable and theoretically grounded signal. This approach ensures that the gradient updates are guided by the overall success of the response rather than being distracted by the high-variance noise of individual token probabilities.

## Key Advantages of GSPO

The transition from token-level to sequence-level optimization provides three major benefits:

*   **Stability in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models:** MoE models are notoriously difficult to train with RL because the "experts" activated for a specific token can change drastically between training steps. GRPO often requires complex workarounds like "Routing Replay" to handle this volatility. GSPO inherently resolves this issue because it focuses on the sequence likelihood, which remains stable even if the internal expert routing fluctuates.
*   **Superior Training Efficiency:** Empirically, GSPO shows that it can achieve higher accuracy and better benchmark performance (on tasks like AIME'24 and CodeForces) using the same amount of training compute as GRPO. Interestingly, even though GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO, it produces a more reliable learning signal that leads to faster progress.
*   **Infrastructure Simplification:** Because GSPO is more tolerant of small numerical discrepancies, it allows developers to use likelihoods generated by inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang) directly for training. This removes the need to recompute likelihoods using a separate training engine, simplifying the overall RL infrastructure.

## Conclusion

GSPO represents a shift toward more robust, scalable reinforcement learning. By aligning the optimization objective with the way rewards are actually delivered—at the sequence level—GSPO eliminates the "noise" that leads to catastrophic collapse. This algorithmic foundation has already contributed to the remarkable performance of the latest Qwen3 models, providing a stable path forward for scaling the next generation of AI intelligence.



# The Power of the Whole: Why Sequence-Level Logic is the Future of RL

In the current landscape of Large Language Model (LLM) training, reinforcement learning (RL) has become the standard for pushing models toward complex reasoning tasks like mathematics and programming. However, as models grow larger and responses become longer, maintaining training stability becomes a significant hurdle. A core issue lies in a fundamental misalignment: while we typically reward a model for producing a complete, correct response (a sequence), we often still optimize the model based on individual words (tokens).

This blog explores the shift toward Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new RL algorithm that corrects this misalignment by moving from token-level to sequence-level optimization.

## The Problem with Token-Level Importance Sampling

To understand the innovation of GSPO, we first need to look at the limitations of current state-of-the-art methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). In these frameworks, the importance ratio—which tells the model how much to adjust its weights based on an old policy—is calculated at every token position $t$.

Specifically, GRPO defines the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ as:
$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$

The problem is that this calculation is fundamentally ill-posed for language generation. Importance sampling relies on averaging over many samples to correct for distributional mismatches. Because $w_{i,t}$ is based on a single sample for each next-token distribution, it fails to act as a proper distribution correction. Instead, it introduces high-variance noise into the gradients. As sequence lengths increase, this noise accumulates, often leading to "catastrophic and irreversible model collapse," where the training dynamics break down entirely.

## The GSPO Solution: Aligning Optimization with Reward

The guiding principle behind GSPO is simple: the unit of optimization should match the unit of reward. Since rewards are granted to the entire sequence, the importance ratio should also be defined at the sequence level.

GSPO introduces a sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ based on sequence likelihood:
$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$

By using this ratio, GSPO performs clipping, rewarding, and optimization on the entire response rather than individual tokens. To ensure numerical stability across different response lengths, GSPO incorporates length normalization. This prevents a few highly probable tokens from causing dramatic fluctuations in the ratio, keeping the training signal steady.

From a gradient perspective, the difference is profound. In GRPO, tokens are weighted unequally by their respective importance weights. In GSPO, every token in a response is weighted equally. By eliminating the variance caused by unequal token weights, GSPO provides a much more reliable learning signal.

## Real-World Impact: Stability and Efficiency

The benefits of this shift are not just theoretical; they have significant practical implications for training large-scale models like the Qwen3 series.

### 1. Stabilizing Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
Training MoE models via RL is notoriously difficult due to "expert-activation volatility." In models like GRPO, the specific experts activated for a token can change drastically between the old and new policies, causing the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly. GSPO sidesteps this issue entirely. Because GSPO focuses on the overall sequence likelihood—which remains stable even if internal routing changes—it eliminates the need for complex workarounds like "Routing Replay" to keep training from collapsing.

### 2. Improving Infrastructure Efficiency
GSPO also simplifies the engineering side of RL. Currently, many systems must recompute likelihoods using a training engine (like Megatron) because of precision discrepancies between training and inference engines (like vLLM). Because GSPO relies on sequence-level likelihoods, it is far more tolerant of these discrepancies. This allows developers to use likelihoods directly from the inference engine, streamlining the training pipeline for multi-turn RL and disaggregated frameworks.

## Conclusion

By shifting the focus from the individual token to the complete sequence, GSPO provides a theoretically grounded and practically robust framework for RL. It solves the instability issues of previous methods, simplifies the training of complex MoE architectures, and improves overall training efficiency. As we continue to scale language models toward more sophisticated reasoning, sequence-level logic represents a vital step toward more stable and performant AI.



# Simplifying the Giants: How GSPO Unlocks Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become the "heavy hitters" of the modern AI landscape. By activating only a subset of parameters (experts) for each token, these models can achieve massive scale with greater computational efficiency. However, this sparse architecture introduces a significant hurdle: training them using Reinforcement Learning (RL) is notoriously unstable. This instability often stems from a phenomenon known as "expert volatility," which can lead to catastrophic model collapse during training.

A new algorithm, Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), offers a way to bypass these headaches. By shifting the fundamental unit of optimization from the token level to the sequence level, GSPO stabilizes MoE training and removes the need for complex technical workarounds.

## The Challenge: Expert Volatility

To understand why GSPO is necessary, we first have to look at why current RL algorithms, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), struggle with MoE models. 

In a standard RL setup, we use "importance sampling" to correct for the fact that we are training on data sampled from an old policy ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) rather than the current policy ($\pi_\theta$). GRPO performs this correction at the token level. It calculates an importance ratio for every individual token:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

In MoE models, this approach is problematic because of expert-activation volatility. Because the model is sparse, even a small gradient update can cause the model to route a token to a different expert than it did in the previous step. In a 48-layer model, for example, roughly 10% of the experts activated for a response might change after a single update. These tiny shifts in routing cause the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate wildly, introducing high-variance noise into the gradients and eventually causing the training process to collapse.

## The "Hack": Routing Replay

Before the introduction of GSPO, researchers had to use a workaround to keep MoE RL training on track, known as "Routing Replay." 

To stabilize the training, developers would cache the specific experts activated by the old policy and "replay" those exact routing modes when calculating the importance ratios for the new policy. This ensured that the gradients were updated on a consistent set of experts. While effective, Routing Replay comes with significant costs:
*   It introduces additional memory and communication overhead.
*   It can limit the actual capacity of the MoE model by forcing it to stick to specific routing paths.
*   It adds significant complexity to the RL infrastructure.

## The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Optimization

GSPO provides a more elegant solution by changing the unit of optimization. Instead of trying to correct the distribution for every single token, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence.

The GSPO importance ratio, $s_{i}(\theta)$, is defined as:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

By using the sequence likelihood, GSPO achieves several major advantages:

**1. Immunity to Volatility**
While individual tokens might be routed to different experts between updates, the model’s overall ability to generate a coherent sequence remains relatively stable. Because GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood ($\pi_{\theta} (y_i | x)$) rather than the specific path taken by every token, it is naturally resistant to the expert-activation volatility that plagues GRPO.

**2. Elimination of Workarounds**
Because GSPO is inherently stable for MoE models, it completely obviates the need for Routing Replay. This allows models to leverage their full capacity without artificial routing constraints and simplifies the training pipeline significantly.

**3. Infrastructure Simplification**
GSPO also eases the burden on engineering teams. Because it relies on sequence-level likelihoods, it is much more tolerant of the precision discrepancies that often exist between training engines (like Megatron) and inference engines (like vLLM). This makes it easier to use likelihoods directly from the inference engine, simplifying the design of RL infrastructure for multi-turn and large-scale training.

## Conclusion

By moving the goalposts from token-level precision to sequence-level stability, GSPO solves one of the most persistent problems in scaling MoE models. It provides a robust, efficient foundation that has already contributed to the performance of the latest Qwen3 models. For developers looking to push the boundaries of large-scale RL, GSPO offers a path toward simpler, more stable, and more performant training.



# Bridging the Gap: Making AI Infrastructure Easier to Build

One of the most persistent "hidden" headaches in large-scale AI engineering is the friction between the training environment and the inference environment. While we often focus on the nuances of model architecture or the math of reinforcement learning (RL), the physical infrastructure required to run these processes introduces a significant challenge: precision discrepancy.

When training large language models (LLMs) using reinforcement learning, we typically use different software stacks for different stages of the pipeline. For example, training engines like Megatron are optimized for massive gradient updates, while inference engines like vLLM or SGLang are optimized for high-throughput generation. Because these engines use different kernels, optimizations, and sometimes different floating-point precisions, they can produce slightly different results for the exact same input. In many RL algorithms, these tiny discrepancies can compound into major problems, complicating the development of stable training pipelines.

## The Problem with Token-Level Optimization

To understand why this is a problem, we have to look at how current state-of-the-art RL algorithms, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), handle "off-policy" data. When we train on a batch of data, we are often optimizing a current policy ($\pi_\theta$) using responses that were sampled from an older policy ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$). To make this work, we use importance sampling to correct for the fact that the data came from a different distribution.

In GRPO, this correction happens at the token level. The algorithm calculates an importance ratio for every single token $y_{i,t}$ in a response:

$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$

Because this ratio is calculated for every token, even a microscopic difference in how the training engine and inference engine calculate $\pi(y_t)$ can lead to "noise." Over a long sequence of tokens, these small errors can accumulate, leading to unstable gradients and, in some cases, catastrophic model collapse. This forces engineers to build complex workarounds to ensure that the probabilities used during the update perfectly match those used during generation.

## The GSPO Solution: Sequence-Level Stability

The authors of the Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) paper propose a shift in perspective: instead of correcting the distribution at the token level, we should optimize at the sequence level. 

The core innovation of GSPO is defining the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence. The sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$

By using the sequence likelihood, GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward (since rewards are typically given for the entire response, not individual tokens). More importantly, from an infrastructure standpoint, this approach is inherently more "tolerant" of precision discrepancies. 

Because $s_i(\theta)$ looks at the probability of the whole response, the tiny floating-point jitters that occur at the individual token level are smoothed out. The sequence-level likelihood is a much more robust metric, making it far less sensitive to the fact that a training engine and an inference engine might calculate probabilities slightly differently.

## Simplifying the RL Infrastructure

This shift from token-level to sequence-level optimization has profound implications for how we build AI infrastructure. 

Currently, to ensure consistency, many teams have to perform "recomputation"—meaning they take the responses generated by the inference engine and run them through the training engine again just to get a "canonical" likelihood score for the RL update. This is computationally expensive and adds significant complexity to the data pipeline.

GSPO changes the game by potentially allowing us to:
*   **Directly use inference engine outputs:** Developers can use the likelihoods returned directly by engines like vLLM or SGLang for the optimization step.
*   **Simplify Disaggregated Frameworks:** In setups where training and inference are physically separated on different hardware clusters, GSPO removes the need for the training cluster to "re-verify" the generation results.
*   **Scale Multi-turn and Partial Rollouts:** It simplifies the logic required for complex tasks like multi-turn dialogue or partial rollouts, where managing token-level consistency across multiple turns becomes an engineering nightmare.

By solving a fundamental mathematical mismatch in how we weight importance ratios, GSPO doesn't just improve model performance—it clears a major engineering hurdle, making it easier to build the robust infrastructure needed to scale the next generation of AI.
