\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 3: # Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

# The GSPO Objective: Moving to sequence-level importance ratios

To understand Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), one must first understand the limitations of current reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms used to train large language models. In large-scale RL, we often face an "off-policy" learning setting. This occurs when we sample a batch of responses using an old policy, $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but attempt to update the parameters of the current policy, $\pi_\theta$. To make this update valid, we use importance sampling to correct for the discrepancy between the old and new distributions.

In standard algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), this correction happens at the token level. For every token $y_t$ in a generated response, the algorithm calculates an importance ratio $w_t(\theta)$ by comparing the probability of that specific token under the new policy versus the old policy. However, the authors of this paper identify a fundamental flaw in this approach: the objective is "ill-posed." While the importance sampling weights are applied to individual tokens, the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence.

This mismatch between the unit of optimization (tokens) and the unit of reward (sequences) creates significant issues. In GRPO, the importance weights are calculated using a single sample from each next-token distribution. Because these weights are not averaged over multiple samples, they introduce high-variance noise. As the length of the generated response increases, this noise accumulates, leading to unstable training dynamics and, in many cases, catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. GSPO addresses this by shifting the unit of optimization to match the unit of reward. Instead of correcting for distribution shifts at every token, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence.

# Mathematical derivation of the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$

The core innovation of GSPO lies in its definition of the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. In previous methods like GRPO, the weight for a token $y_{i,t}$ is simply the ratio of the probabilities of that token given the preceding context. In contrast, GSPO defines the ratio based on the total sequence likelihood. Given a query $x$ and a response $y_i$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the sequence-level importance ratio is defined as the ratio of the likelihood of the full sequence under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ compared to the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

To ensure this ratio remains numerically stable and comparable across responses of different lengths, GSPO incorporates length normalization. The importance ratio is expressed as:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$

where $|y_i|$ denotes the number of tokens in the response. This can be rewritten using the properties of logarithms to show its relationship to the individual token probabilities:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$

By taking the $1/|y_i|$ power, GSPO effectively calculates the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios. This normalization is crucial because, without it, the likelihood of a sequence would fluctuate dramatically based on the number of tokens, making it impossible to apply a unified clipping range $\varepsilon$. With this definition, the GSPO objective function becomes:

$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right]$

In this formulation, $\widehat{A}_i$ represents the normalized advantage of the entire response $y_i$, calculated relative to the other responses in a group of size $G$.

# Length Normalization: Controlling variance and ensuring unified numerical ranges

Length normalization is not merely a mathematical convenience in GSPO; it is a functional requirement for stable training. In autoregressive language modeling, the probability of a sequence is the product of the probabilities of its constituent tokens. As the sequence length $|y_i|$ increases, the raw sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ becomes extremely small. Because the likelihood of a sequence is highly sensitive to even minor changes in the probabilities of individual tokens, the raw importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}$ would exhibit extreme variance.

By applying the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent, GSPO constrains the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range. This allows the algorithm to use consistent clipping parameters $\varepsilon$. Without this normalization, a response with 100 tokens would require a vastly different clipping range than a response with 10 tokens, as the magnitude of the ratio would differ by orders of magnitude. 

Furthermore, this approach provides a more reliable indicator of how far the response $y$ sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy $\pi_\theta$. It ensures that the "off-policy" correction remains meaningful regardless of the generation length. This is particularly important for tasks requiring long-form reasoning, such as mathematics or programming, where long responses are standard. By stabilizing these ratios, GSPO prevents the exploding gradients and noise accumulation that typically lead to model collapse in long-sequence RL training.

# Gradient Analysis: Comparing the gradient weights of GSPO vs. GRPO

To understand why GSPO is more stable than GRPO, we can examine the gradients of their respective objectives. By deriving the gradients (and omitting the clipping term for clarity), we can see how the two algorithms weight the updates to the model parameters $\theta$. 

The gradient of the GRPO objective is:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

In this equation, each token $y_{i,t}$ in the sequence is weighted by its own specific importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. These weights can vary significantly across the sequence, potentially falling anywhere within the range allowed by the clipping mechanism. These unequal weights act as noise that accumulates over the length of the sequence.

In contrast, the gradient of the GSPO objective is:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

The fundamental distinction is clear: in GSPO, the entire sum of token gradients for a response is multiplied by a single, sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$. This means that every token in a given response is weighted equally during the gradient update. By eliminating the token-specific weights, GSPO removes the primary source of high-variance noise that plagues GRPO.

# Eliminating instability: How equal weighting of tokens stabilizes training

The elimination of token-level weighting has profound implications for training stability, particularly when dealing with complex architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE models, the "expert-activation volatility" presents a unique challenge. Because these models are sparse, the specific experts activated for a token can change significantly after a single gradient update. In GRPO, this volatility causes the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically, as the model might suddenly switch to a different network path for the same token. To compensate, researchers previously had to use "Routing Replay" strategies—caching and replaying old routing modes—to force stability.

GSPO fundamentally resolves this issue because it is not sensitive to individual token likelihoods. Since GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$, it leverages the fact that while individual experts might change, the model's overall language modeling capability remains consistent. GSPO can converge normally on MoE models without the need for complex workarounds like Routing Replay, allowing the model to utilize its full capacity without artificial constraints.

Additionally, GSPO improves RL infrastructure. Because GSPO is more tolerant of precision discrepancies, it allows researchers to use the likelihoods returned directly by inference engines (like SGLang or vLLM) for optimization. This avoids the need to recompute likelihoods using a separate training engine (like Megatron), which is a common bottleneck in production RL pipelines. Ultimately, by aligning the unit of reward with the unit of optimization and equalizing the weighting of tokens, GSPO provides a robust, efficient, and scalable foundation for training the next generation of large language models.
