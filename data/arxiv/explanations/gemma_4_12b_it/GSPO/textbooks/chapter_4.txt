\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 4: Advanced Variants and Mathematical Properties

# GSPO-token: Enabling fine-grained, token-wise advantage adjustment

While the standard Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm provides a robust framework for training large language models by optimizing at the sequence level, there are specific scenarios where a more granular approach is required. In many complex reinforcement learning (RL) tasks, a single reward assigned to an entire response may not provide enough information to guide the model toward the most efficient or correct reasoning path. This is particularly true in multi-turn reinforcement learning, where the model must navigate a conversation or a series of interactions where different parts of a response might have different qualitative values.

To address this need for flexibility, the authors introduce GSPO-token. This variant is designed to allow for token-wise advantage adjustment while still maintaining the stability benefits of the sequence-level importance ratio. In standard GSPO, the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ is applied uniformly to every token in a response $y_i$. In contrast, GSPO-token allows for the use of token-specific advantages, denoted as $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, where each token $y_{i,t}$ within a response can potentially have a different advantage score.

By enabling token-wise advantages, GSPO-token provides a middle ground between the coarse-grained optimization of GSPO and the potentially unstable token-level optimization of GRPO. It allows researchers to customize the learning signal for specific parts of a sequence—for example, providing higher rewards for correct reasoning steps in a math problem while penalizing irrelevant filler words. This flexibility ensures that the model can be fine-tuned to handle more sophisticated requirements where the "goodness" of a response is not just a binary outcome but a sequence of progressive improvements or errors.

# Mathematical formulation of GSPO-token and its gradient derivation

To understand how GSPO-token achieves this flexibility while remaining stable, we must examine its mathematical objective and how it handles gradients. The objective function for GSPO-token is defined as:

\begin{align}
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right]
\end{align}

In this equation, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is a specialized importance ratio. It is defined as:

\begin{align}
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right]  \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }
\end{align}

Here, $s_{i}(\theta)$ is the sequence-level importance ratio defined in previous sections: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The notation $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ refers to an operation that takes the numerical value of the expression but stops the gradient (equivalent to the \texttt{detach} operation in common deep learning frameworks). This is a critical design choice: it ensures that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_{i}(\theta)$ because the ratio of the policy probability to its "stopped-gradient" version is always 1.

When we derive the gradient of this objective (omitting the clipping for clarity), we see the following:

\begin{align}
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=&\ 
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right]
\end{align}

The primary takeaway from this derivation is that the gradient of GSPO-token weights the log-likelihood gradient of each token by the *sequence-level* importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, rather than a unique token-level ratio. This architectural choice prevents the gradient noise and instability that plague GRPO, while still allowing the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to vary per token.

# Theoretical Equivalence: Proving numerical identity between GSPO and GSPO-token in standard settings

One of the most important properties of GSPO-token is its relationship with the original GSPO algorithm. The authors prove that GSPO-token is not a fundamentally different algorithm in its standard application, but rather a generalized version that simplifies back to GSPO under specific conditions.

Specifically, if we assume a standard setting where the advantage of every token in a response is the same (i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), GSPO-token becomes numerically identical to GSPO. To see why, consider the gradient of GSPO-token derived above:

\begin{align}
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=&\ 
\mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] \\
=&\ 
\mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]
\end{align}

In this simplified form, the expression exactly matches the gradient of the GSPO objective. This numerical identity is significant because it provides a theoretical guarantee: when researchers use the simplest form of advantage estimation (where one reward is given per sequence), GSPO-token provides the exact same optimization path as GSPO.

Furthermore, since the clipping condition in GSPO-token relies on $s_{i,t}(\theta)$, and $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically identical to $s_i(\theta)$, the clipping behavior remains consistent between the two versions in standard settings. This means that GSPO-token acts as a "superset" of GSPO, offering the same stability and performance guarantees as the base algorithm while providing an optional "dial" to increase granularity when the task demands it.

# Applications in multi-turn RL and complex advantage customization

The ultimate utility of GSPO-token lies in its application to complex reasoning and conversational tasks. In standard reinforcement learning for language models, we often use a "verifier" or a reward model to score a final answer. However, as models begin to tackle multi-turn interactions, the "correctness" of a model's output is often distributed across different parts of a conversation.

For instance, in a multi-turn dialogue, a model might provide a correct answer in the third turn, but its reasoning in the second turn might have been inefficient or slightly off-track. By using GSPO-token, researchers can assign different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to the tokens in each turn. This allows the gradient update to reward the "correct" logic in the third turn while simultaneously providing a corrective signal for the "inefficient" logic in the second turn, all while using the stable sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ to ensure that the gradients do not explode or vanish.

Beyond multi-turn RL, GSPO-token allows for sophisticated advantage customization. This is particularly useful in "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) reasoning. In such tasks, a model generates a long sequence of internal thoughts before reaching a final answer. It is possible that certain intermediate thoughts are crucial for the correct conclusion, while others are merely auxiliary. GSPO-token gives developers the tool to weight these intermediate steps differently. By providing a fine-grained way to shape the policy without sacrificing the stability of sequence-level optimization, GSPO-token serves as a highly flexible foundation for the next generation of complex RL training for large language models.
