\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does clipping a much larger fraction of tokens in GSPO lead to higher training efficiency?
Question:
Figure 4 shows that GSPO clips a much larger fraction of tokens than GRPO. In most RL settings, clipping more data usually implies we are discarding more of the signal, which should theoretically slow down learning or reduce efficiency. Why does the paper claim this counter-intuitive result is a sign of better efficiency? Is it because the gradients from the non-clipped tokens are significantly more accurate?
Answer:
The reason why clipping a much larger fraction of tokens in GSPO leads to higher training efficiency—despite the intuition that discarding more data should slow down learning—lies in the difference between **signal quality** and **signal quantity**. The paper argues that the core issue with GRPO is that its gradient estimates are "inherently noisy and inefficient," meaning that while GRPO might be using more tokens to compute a gradient, the information provided by those tokens is less reliable for model optimization.

To understand this, we must look at the fundamental difference in how these two algorithms define importance weights. GRPO applies importance sampling at the token level, using the weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t}) }$. The authors point out that this is technically an ill-posed application of importance sampling. Because a reward is granted to an entire sequence, but the weights are applied to individual tokens based on a single sample from the behavior distribution, these weights fail to perform the intended distribution correction. Instead, they introduce high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. Because these individual token weights $w_{i,t}$ fluctuate significantly, they frequently cross the clipping thresholds, leading to the very high fraction of clipped tokens observed in the experiments.

In contrast, GSPO aligns the unit of optimization with the unit of reward by using a sequence-level importance ratio: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. This ratio represents how much the entire response deviates from the old policy. When we derive the gradient for GSPO, we see that the sequence-level ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ acts as a global multiplier for the entire sequence. Consequently, GSPO weights all tokens within a response equally during the gradient update. This eliminates the "instability factor" of GRPO, where different tokens in the same sequence would be weighted differently based on volatile, unreliable ratios.

The "counter-intuitive" efficiency of GSPO stems from this stability. Even though GSPO might clip more tokens (because its clipping mechanism is applied to the sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ rather than the volatile token-level $w_{i,t}$), the gradients it *does* use are significantly more accurate and stable. In GRPO, the high fraction of clipped tokens is a symptom of the "noisy" and "ill-posed" nature of its gradient estimates. By moving to a sequence-level approach, GSPO ensures that the optimization signal remains clean and consistent, allowing the model to exploit the training samples more effectively. In short, GSPO is more efficient because it prioritizes the reliability of the learning signal over the raw volume of tokens processed.
