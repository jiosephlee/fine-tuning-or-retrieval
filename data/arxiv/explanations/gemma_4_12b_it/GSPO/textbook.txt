Foundations of Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

# Role of RL in scaling LLMs for reasoning, mathematics, and programming

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a fundamental paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models (LLMs). While initial training stages of these models often rely on supervised learning to predict the next token in a sequence, RL is uniquely suited to develop sophisticated capabilities that require deep, multi-step reasoning. For instance, in domains such as competition-level mathematics and complex programming tasks, the model must navigate a long chain of logical deductions to reach a correct final answer. RL provides the framework necessary to optimize these models for such "reasoning processes" by rewarding successful outcomes.

By utilizing RL, researchers can train models to undertake deeper and longer reasoning processes rather than just generating surface-level text. Instead of simply learning the most probable next word, the model learns to explore different paths of thought. If a path leads to a correct mathematical proof or a functioning piece of code, the model is rewarded, thereby reinforcing the specific sequence of reasoning steps taken. This capability to scale RL is essential for pushing the boundaries of what language models can achieve, as it allows for the development of models that can solve problems requiring structured, high-level intelligence.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Mechanics, clipping mechanisms, and the value model burden

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a cornerstone algorithm in the field of reinforcement learning, designed to optimize a policy while ensuring that updates remain stable. In the context of training language models, PPO seeks to update the policy parameters $\theta$ by evaluating responses generated from an older version of the model, denoted as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Because the model being trained ($\pi_\theta$) is slightly different from the model that generated the data ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$), the algorithm uses an importance ratio to correct for this discrepancy. For a token $y_t$ at position $t$, this ratio is defined as $w_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t | x, y_{<t})}$.

To prevent the model from making excessively large updates that could lead to instability, PPO introduces a clipping mechanism. The objective function $\mathcal{J}_\text{PPO}(\theta)$ calculates the minimum of two terms: the weighted advantage and a clipped version of that advantage. The advantage, $\widehat{A}_t$, represents how much better a specific token is compared to the average expected outcome. By clipping the importance ratio $w_t(\theta)$ within a range of $1 \pm \varepsilon$, the algorithm ensures that the policy does not change too drastically in a single update step. This "proximal" approach is vital because if the policy changes too much, the data collected from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ becomes an unreliable guide for the new policy, potentially leading to a collapse in performance.

A significant practical challenge with PPO is its heavy reliance on a "value model." To estimate the advantage $\widehat{A}_t$ of each individual token, PPO typically requires a separate value model that predicts the expected reward from any given state. In practice, this value model is often as large as the policy model itself. This introduces a massive computational and memory burden, as the system must maintain and update two enormous models simultaneously. Furthermore, the effectiveness of PPO is tied to the accuracy of this value model; if the value model fails to provide reliable estimates, especially for very long responses or complex tasks, the training process can become ineffective.

# Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO): Eliminating the value model via group-based relative advantages

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was developed to address the overhead associated with the value model in PPO. Instead of using a separate, massive critic model to estimate the advantage of every token, GRPO computes the relative advantage of a response by comparing it to a group of other responses generated for the same query. When the model generates a group of $G$ different responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ for a single query $x$, it can determine the advantage of each response based on its performance relative to the group's average.

The advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ for a response $y_i$ is calculated by subtracting the mean reward of the entire group from the reward of that specific response, and then dividing by the standard deviation of the rewards within that group. Specifically, $\widehat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \mathrm{mean}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}{\mathrm{std}(\{r(x, y_i)\}_{i=1}^G)}$. Because all tokens in a specific response $y_i$ share the same reward, they are all assigned the same advantage $\widehat{A}_i$. This approach allows the algorithm to bypass the need for a value model entirely, significantly reducing the memory and computational requirements of the RL infrastructure.

Despite its efficiency, GRPO still utilizes an importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ at the token level to perform off-policy updates. This ratio, $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})}$, measures the likelihood of each individual token under the current policy compared to the old policy. While this removes the value model, it introduces a different set of dynamics where every token in a sequence is weighted differently. This token-level weighting is a key characteristic of GRPO that distinguishes it from sequence-level optimization methods.

# Standard Notation: Autoregressive policies, sequence likelihoods, and reward verifiers

To understand the mathematical framework of these algorithms, it is necessary to establish a standard notation. We represent an autoregressive language model as a policy $\pi_\theta$, where $\theta$ denotes the parameters of the model. The model takes a query $x$ from a query set $\mathcal{D}$ and generates a response $y$. The model is "autoregressive" because it generates the response one token at a time, where the probability of the current token depends on the query and all preceding tokens in the response.

The likelihood of an entire response $y$ under the policy $\pi_\theta$ is expressed as the product of the probabilities of each individual token. If $|y|$ denotes the total number of tokens in the response, the likelihood is $\pi_\theta(y | x) = \prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$. This notation allows us to see how the probability of a sequence is built up step-by-step. In the context of reinforcement learning, we are interested in how these probabilities change as we update the parameters $\theta$.

Finally, the "quality" of a response is determined by a verifier $r$. This verifier takes a query-response pair $(x, y)$ and produces a reward $r(x, y)$, typically normalized within the range $[0, 1]$. This reward serves as the primary signal for the reinforcement learning algorithm. Whether a response is "good" or "bad" is determined by this scalar value, which the RL algorithms (like PPO, GRPO, or GSPO) use to calculate the advantages that guide the optimization of the policy $\pi_\theta$.

The Problem of Token-Level Optimization in GRPO

# Off-policy learning and the necessity of clipping in large-scale RL

In the context of training large language models (LLMs) using reinforcement learning (RL), the objective is to scale training to maximize the capabilities of the model in complex domains such as mathematics and programming. To achieve this scaling, researchers must utilize large rollout batch sizes to ensure that hardware is fully utilized. However, because the computational demands of these models are so high, it is a standard practice to partition a single large batch of rollout data into multiple mini-batches. This process for performing gradient updates creates an off-policy learning environment.

Off-policy learning occurs when the data used to calculate the gradient updates for the current policy, $\pi_\theta$, was actually sampled from a different, older policy, $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Because the model being optimized is slightly different from the model that generated the responses, the gradients calculated from that data may not perfectly represent the direction the current policy should move in. If the discrepancy between the current policy and the old policy becomes too large, the training dynamics can become unstable, leading to erratic updates that can degrade the model's performance.

To mitigate the risks associated with off-policy learning, reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) employ a clipping mechanism. This mechanism acts as a safety buffer by constraining the policy update within a proximal region of the old policy. By clipping the importance ratios, the algorithm ensures that no single update can move the policy too far away from the data distribution it was sampled from. Without this constraint, the high variance inherent in off-policy data could cause the model to diverge during the training process.

# The Principle of Importance Sampling: Correcting distributional mismatch

To understand why certain RL algorithms fail, one must first understand the theoretical foundation of importance sampling. Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to estimate the expectation of a function $f$ under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ when samples are actually drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. The mathematical principle states that the expectation under the target distribution can be calculated by re-weighting the samples from the behavior distribution:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} \, f(z) \right]$$

The core purpose of the importance weight, $\frac{\pi_\text{tar}(z)}{\pi_\text{beh}(z)}$, is to correct for the distributional mismatch between where we are sampling from and where we want to optimize. For this correction to be mathematically effective and provide a reliable estimate, the principle relies on averaging over a large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) from the behavior distribution. By averaging many samples, the weights effectively "re-shape" the behavior distribution to match the target distribution, allowing for stable gradient estimation.

In the setting of reinforcement learning for language models, this principle is used to bridge the gap between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the current policy $\pi_\theta$. When we use data from the old policy to update the new one, we use importance weights to adjust the objective function. If these weights are applied correctly across a sufficient number of samples, they allow the model to learn from "old" data as if it were "new" data, thereby improving sample efficiency and allowing for more stable updates during large-scale training.

# The 'Ill-posed' nature of GRPO: Why token-level weights fail to correct distributions

A significant finding in recent research is that the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm possesses an "ill-posed" objective when applied to large-scale language model training. The issue stems from a fundamental misapplication of the importance sampling principle at the token level. In GRPO, the importance ratio is defined as:

$$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$$

Here, the weight is calculated for every individual token $y_{i,t}$ within a sequence. However, because each token $y_{i,t}$ is a single sample drawn from the next-token distribution of the old policy, the importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ fails to perform the intended role of distribution correction. Because we are not averaging over multiple samples of the same token distribution to calculate this specific weight, the weight does not "correct" the distribution as importance sampling theory requires.

Instead of providing a stable correction, these token-level weights introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients. Because the weights are calculated for every single step of a response, the errors associated with these weights are not isolated; they permeate the entire gradient calculation for every token in the sequence. This flaw means that GRPO is fundamentally misapplying the math of importance sampling, leading to a training objective that is theoretically unsound for the task of sequence generation.

# High-variance noise accumulation and the phenomenon of catastrophic model collapse

The consequence of using ill-posed, token-level importance weights is the accumulation of high-variance noise throughout the training process. Because these noisy weights are applied to every token in a response, the variance does not stay constant; rather, it accumulates as the response length increases. For long-response tasks, such as complex reasoning or coding, this accumulation becomes severe. The noise progressively destabilizes the gradient updates, making it increasingly difficult for the model to find a stable path toward optimization.

This instability is further exacerbated by the clipping mechanism. While clipping is intended to provide stability, when it interacts with the high-variance noise of token-level weights, it can lead to unpredictable consequences. The researchers observed that this combination often precipitates a phenomenon known as catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. During such a collapse, the model's performance drops sharply and cannot be recovered.

The researchers found that once this collapse occurs, it is often impossible to resume training successfully. Even if the researchers revert the model to a previous checkpoint and meticulously retune hyperparameters—such as adjusting the clipping ranges $\varepsilon$, extending the generation length, or switching to entirely different RL queries—the model remains stuck. This suggests that the collapse is not a simple hyperparameter issue but a fundamental failure of the training dynamics caused by the noisy gradient estimates inherent in the GRPO design.

# The Core Principle: Aligning the unit of reward with the unit of optimization

The proposed solution to these issues is grounded in a core principle: the unit of the optimization objective must match the unit of the reward. In most language model RL tasks, the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence $y$ based on its completion. Since the reward is a sequence-level signal, the optimization should also occur at the sequence level rather than the token level. By moving the optimization to the sequence level, the algorithm can utilize importance weights that have a clear theoretical meaning.

In Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the importance ratio is redefined based on sequence likelihood:

$$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$

This sequence-level ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ reflects how much the entire response $y$ sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy. By using this ratio, GSPO aligns the off-policy correction with the sequence-level reward. Furthermore, GSPO employs length normalization (the exponent $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$) to reduce variance and ensure that the importance ratios of responses of different lengths fall within a unified numerical range.

By optimizing at the sequence level, GSPO ensures that the clipping mechanism acts on the entire response. This eliminates the instability caused by individual token weights and ensures that the learning signal is both reliable and effective. This shift in perspective—moving from individual tokens to the holistic sequence—resolves the expert-activation volatility in Mixture-of-Experts models and provides a more robust foundation for scaling reinforcement learning in large language models.

# Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

# The GSPO Objective: Moving to sequence-level importance ratios

To understand Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), one must first understand the limitations of current reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms used to train large language models. In large-scale RL, we often face an "off-policy" learning setting. This occurs when we sample a batch of responses using an old policy, $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, but attempt to update the parameters of the current policy, $\pi_\theta$. To make this update valid, we use importance sampling to correct for the discrepancy between the old and new distributions.

In standard algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), this correction happens at the token level. For every token $y_t$ in a generated response, the algorithm calculates an importance ratio $w_t(\theta)$ by comparing the probability of that specific token under the new policy versus the old policy. However, the authors of this paper identify a fundamental flaw in this approach: the objective is "ill-posed." While the importance sampling weights are applied to individual tokens, the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence.

This mismatch between the unit of optimization (tokens) and the unit of reward (sequences) creates significant issues. In GRPO, the importance weights are calculated using a single sample from each next-token distribution. Because these weights are not averaged over multiple samples, they introduce high-variance noise. As the length of the generated response increases, this noise accumulates, leading to unstable training dynamics and, in many cases, catastrophic and irreversible model collapse. GSPO addresses this by shifting the unit of optimization to match the unit of reward. Instead of correcting for distribution shifts at every token, GSPO defines the importance ratio based on the likelihood of the entire sequence.

# Mathematical derivation of the sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$

The core innovation of GSPO lies in its definition of the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$. In previous methods like GRPO, the weight for a token $y_{i,t}$ is simply the ratio of the probabilities of that token given the preceding context. In contrast, GSPO defines the ratio based on the total sequence likelihood. Given a query $x$ and a response $y_i$ sampled from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the sequence-level importance ratio is defined as the ratio of the likelihood of the full sequence under the current policy $\pi_\theta$ compared to the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$.

To ensure this ratio remains numerically stable and comparable across responses of different lengths, GSPO incorporates length normalization. The importance ratio is expressed as:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$

where $|y_i|$ denotes the number of tokens in the response. This can be rewritten using the properties of logarithms to show its relationship to the individual token probabilities:

$s_{i}(\theta) = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$

By taking the $1/|y_i|$ power, GSPO effectively calculates the geometric mean of the token-level importance ratios. This normalization is crucial because, without it, the likelihood of a sequence would fluctuate dramatically based on the number of tokens, making it impossible to apply a unified clipping range $\varepsilon$. With this definition, the GSPO objective function becomes:

$\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \min \left( s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i}, \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i} \right) \right]$

In this formulation, $\widehat{A}_i$ represents the normalized advantage of the entire response $y_i$, calculated relative to the other responses in a group of size $G$.

# Length Normalization: Controlling variance and ensuring unified numerical ranges

Length normalization is not merely a mathematical convenience in GSPO; it is a functional requirement for stable training. In autoregressive language modeling, the probability of a sequence is the product of the probabilities of its constituent tokens. As the sequence length $|y_i|$ increases, the raw sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$ becomes extremely small. Because the likelihood of a sequence is highly sensitive to even minor changes in the probabilities of individual tokens, the raw importance ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}$ would exhibit extreme variance.

By applying the $\frac{1}{|y_i|}$ exponent, GSPO constrains the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ within a unified numerical range. This allows the algorithm to use consistent clipping parameters $\varepsilon$. Without this normalization, a response with 100 tokens would require a vastly different clipping range than a response with 10 tokens, as the magnitude of the ratio would differ by orders of magnitude. 

Furthermore, this approach provides a more reliable indicator of how far the response $y$ sampled from the old policy deviates from the current policy $\pi_\theta$. It ensures that the "off-policy" correction remains meaningful regardless of the generation length. This is particularly important for tasks requiring long-form reasoning, such as mathematics or programming, where long responses are standard. By stabilizing these ratios, GSPO prevents the exploding gradients and noise accumulation that typically lead to model collapse in long-sequence RL training.

# Gradient Analysis: Comparing the gradient weights of GSPO vs. GRPO

To understand why GSPO is more stable than GRPO, we can examine the gradients of their respective objectives. By deriving the gradients (and omitting the clipping term for clarity), we can see how the two algorithms weight the updates to the model parameters $\theta$. 

The gradient of the GRPO objective is:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GRPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

In this equation, each token $y_{i,t}$ in the sequence is weighted by its own specific importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. These weights can vary significantly across the sequence, potentially falling anywhere within the range allowed by the clipping mechanism. These unequal weights act as noise that accumulates over the length of the sequence.

In contrast, the gradient of the GSPO objective is:

$\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]$

The fundamental distinction is clear: in GSPO, the entire sum of token gradients for a response is multiplied by a single, sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$. This means that every token in a given response is weighted equally during the gradient update. By eliminating the token-specific weights, GSPO removes the primary source of high-variance noise that plagues GRPO.

# Eliminating instability: How equal weighting of tokens stabilizes training

The elimination of token-level weighting has profound implications for training stability, particularly when dealing with complex architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE models, the "expert-activation volatility" presents a unique challenge. Because these models are sparse, the specific experts activated for a token can change significantly after a single gradient update. In GRPO, this volatility causes the token-level importance ratios to fluctuate drastically, as the model might suddenly switch to a different network path for the same token. To compensate, researchers previously had to use "Routing Replay" strategies—caching and replaying old routing modes—to force stability.

GSPO fundamentally resolves this issue because it is not sensitive to individual token likelihoods. Since GSPO focuses on the sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$, it leverages the fact that while individual experts might change, the model's overall language modeling capability remains consistent. GSPO can converge normally on MoE models without the need for complex workarounds like Routing Replay, allowing the model to utilize its full capacity without artificial constraints.

Additionally, GSPO improves RL infrastructure. Because GSPO is more tolerant of precision discrepancies, it allows researchers to use the likelihoods returned directly by inference engines (like SGLang or vLLM) for optimization. This avoids the need to recompute likelihoods using a separate training engine (like Megatron), which is a common bottleneck in production RL pipelines. Ultimately, by aligning the unit of reward with the unit of optimization and equalizing the weighting of tokens, GSPO provides a robust, efficient, and scalable foundation for training the next generation of large language models.

Advanced Variants and Mathematical Properties

# GSPO-token: Enabling fine-grained, token-wise advantage adjustment

While the standard Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) algorithm provides a robust framework for training large language models by optimizing at the sequence level, there are specific scenarios where a more granular approach is required. In many complex reinforcement learning (RL) tasks, a single reward assigned to an entire response may not provide enough information to guide the model toward the most efficient or correct reasoning path. This is particularly true in multi-turn reinforcement learning, where the model must navigate a conversation or a series of interactions where different parts of a response might have different qualitative values.

To address this need for flexibility, the authors introduce GSPO-token. This variant is designed to allow for token-wise advantage adjustment while still maintaining the stability benefits of the sequence-level importance ratio. In standard GSPO, the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ is applied uniformly to every token in a response $y_i$. In contrast, GSPO-token allows for the use of token-specific advantages, denoted as $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$, where each token $y_{i,t}$ within a response can potentially have a different advantage score.

By enabling token-wise advantages, GSPO-token provides a middle ground between the coarse-grained optimization of GSPO and the potentially unstable token-level optimization of GRPO. It allows researchers to customize the learning signal for specific parts of a sequence—for example, providing higher rewards for correct reasoning steps in a math problem while penalizing irrelevant filler words. This flexibility ensures that the model can be fine-tuned to handle more sophisticated requirements where the "goodness" of a response is not just a binary outcome but a sequence of progressive improvements or errors.

# Mathematical formulation of GSPO-token and its gradient derivation

To understand how GSPO-token achieves this flexibility while remaining stable, we must examine its mathematical objective and how it handles gradients. The objective function for GSPO-token is defined as:

\begin{align}
\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} 
\min \left( s_{i,t}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i,t},  \, \mathrm{clip} \left( s_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - {\varepsilon}, 1 + {\varepsilon} \right) \widehat{A}_{i,t} \right) 
\right]
\end{align}

In this equation, $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is a specialized importance ratio. It is defined as:

\begin{align}
s_{i,t}(\theta) 
= \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right]  \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }
\end{align}

Here, $s_{i}(\theta)$ is the sequence-level importance ratio defined in previous sections: $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. The notation $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ refers to an operation that takes the numerical value of the expression but stops the gradient (equivalent to the \texttt{detach} operation in common deep learning frameworks). This is a critical design choice: it ensures that $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically equal to $s_{i}(\theta)$ because the ratio of the policy probability to its "stopped-gradient" version is always 1.

When we derive the gradient of this objective (omitting the clipping for clarity), we see the following:

\begin{align}
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=&\ 
\mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) }
\left[ 
\frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\widehat{A}_{i,t} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})  
\right]
\end{align}

The primary takeaway from this derivation is that the gradient of GSPO-token weights the log-likelihood gradient of each token by the *sequence-level* importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, rather than a unique token-level ratio. This architectural choice prevents the gradient noise and instability that plague GRPO, while still allowing the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to vary per token.

# Theoretical Equivalence: Proving numerical identity between GSPO and GSPO-token in standard settings

One of the most important properties of GSPO-token is its relationship with the original GSPO algorithm. The authors prove that GSPO-token is not a fundamentally different algorithm in its standard application, but rather a generalized version that simplifies back to GSPO under specific conditions.

Specifically, if we assume a standard setting where the advantage of every token in a response is the same (i.e., $\widehat{A}_{i,t} = \widehat{A}_{i}$), GSPO-token becomes numerically identical to GSPO. To see why, consider the gradient of GSPO-token derived above:

\begin{align}
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO-token}(\theta)
=&\ 
\mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \widehat{A}_{i} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] \\
=&\ 
\mathbb{E} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_i (\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right]
\end{align}

In this simplified form, the expression exactly matches the gradient of the GSPO objective. This numerical identity is significant because it provides a theoretical guarantee: when researchers use the simplest form of advantage estimation (where one reward is given per sequence), GSPO-token provides the exact same optimization path as GSPO.

Furthermore, since the clipping condition in GSPO-token relies on $s_{i,t}(\theta)$, and $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically identical to $s_i(\theta)$, the clipping behavior remains consistent between the two versions in standard settings. This means that GSPO-token acts as a "superset" of GSPO, offering the same stability and performance guarantees as the base algorithm while providing an optional "dial" to increase granularity when the task demands it.

# Applications in multi-turn RL and complex advantage customization

The ultimate utility of GSPO-token lies in its application to complex reasoning and conversational tasks. In standard reinforcement learning for language models, we often use a "verifier" or a reward model to score a final answer. However, as models begin to tackle multi-turn interactions, the "correctness" of a model's output is often distributed across different parts of a conversation.

For instance, in a multi-turn dialogue, a model might provide a correct answer in the third turn, but its reasoning in the second turn might have been inefficient or slightly off-track. By using GSPO-token, researchers can assign different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ to the tokens in each turn. This allows the gradient update to reward the "correct" logic in the third turn while simultaneously providing a corrective signal for the "inefficient" logic in the second turn, all while using the stable sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ to ensure that the gradients do not explode or vanish.

Beyond multi-turn RL, GSPO-token allows for sophisticated advantage customization. This is particularly useful in "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) reasoning. In such tasks, a model generates a long sequence of internal thoughts before reaching a final answer. It is possible that certain intermediate thoughts are crucial for the correct conclusion, while others are merely auxiliary. GSPO-token gives developers the tool to weight these intermediate steps differently. By providing a fine-grained way to shape the policy without sacrificing the stability of sequence-level optimization, GSPO-token serves as a highly flexible foundation for the next generation of complex RL training for large language models.

Empirical Evaluation and Practical Implications

# Benchmark Results

To validate the theoretical advantages of Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the researchers conducted a series of empirical evaluations comparing it against the existing state-of-the-art Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm. The experiments were performed using a cold-start model that was fine-tuned from the Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base architecture. To assess the model's capabilities in sophisticated reasoning and problem-solving, the researchers utilized three primary benchmarks: AIME'24, LiveCodeBench (specifically the period from 202410-202502), and CodeForces. These benchmarks are standard for measuring a model's performance in competition-level mathematics and programming tasks, which require deep and long reasoning processes.

During the evaluation, the researchers observed that GSPO provides a significantly more stable training trajectory than GRPO. While GRPO often faces issues with model collapse—a state where the model's performance degrades catastrophically and irreversibly—GSPO maintains stability throughout the entire training process. This stability allows GSPO to consistently deliver performance improvements as the training compute is increased. Furthermore, GSPO demonstrates a high degree of scalability; the model continues to improve as the researchers regularly update the query set and extend the allowed generation length for responses.

In terms of efficiency, GSPO outperformed GRPO in achieving higher training accuracy and better benchmark results under the same amount of training compute and the same number of consumed queries. These results were not merely theoretical; the researchers successfully applied GSPO to the RL training of the latest Qwen3 models. The success of GSPO in these high-stakes environments serves as a powerful proof of the algorithm's ability to unleash the potential of reinforcement learning scaling for large language models. For the sake of a fair comparison, the researchers meticulously tuned the clipping ranges for GRPO (setting them to 0.2 and 0.27) to ensure that the performance gains observed in GSPO were due to the algorithmic design rather than hyperparameter tuning.

# The Paradox of Clipping

One of the most intriguing findings in the empirical evaluation of GSPO is what the researchers call a "counter-intuitive" paradox regarding the clipping mechanism. In reinforcement learning, clipping is used to manage the discrepancy between the "old" policy (which generated the data) and the "current" policy being optimized. If the two policies diverge too much, the importance sampling weights become unreliable, introducing high-variance noise into the gradients. By clipping these weights, the algorithm prevents "off-policy" samples from causing wild, unstable updates to the model.

The research highlights a significant difference in how GRPO and GSPO handle this clipping. GRPO applies clipping at the token level, meaning the importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ is checked for every single token in a response. In contrast, GSPO performs sequence-level clipping, which means the importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ is checked once for the entire response. When the researchers analyzed the fraction of clipped tokens, they discovered a massive disparity: GSPO actually clips a significantly larger fraction of tokens—by a factor of two orders of magnitude—than GRPO does.

Under normal circumstances, one might expect that clipping more data would result in less information being used for training, thereby leading to lower efficiency. However, the researchers found that GSPO achieves higher training efficiency despite clipping much more of the data. This paradox reveals a fundamental truth about the quality of the learning signal in GRPO. Because GRPO uses token-level importance weights, the gradients it produces are inherently noisy. These noisy gradients make it difficult for the model to exploit the samples it *does* collect. GSPO, by using sequence-level rewards and optimization, provides a much cleaner and more reliable learning signal. Because GSPO's signal is so much more effective, it does not suffer from the "waste" of clipping more data; instead, it successfully filters out the high-variance noise that plagues GRPO.

# MoE Stability

The benefits of GSPO are particularly evident when training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. MoE models are large-scale architectures that use "sparse activation," meaning only a small subset of the model's parameters (the "experts") are active for any given token. This sparsity introduces a unique stability challenge: "expert-activation volatility." In these models, the experts selected for a specific response can change significantly between the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$. The researchers observed that in a 48-layer Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base model, approximately 10\% of the experts activated under the new policy would differ from those activated under the old policy after just one gradient update.

This volatility is catastrophic for GRPO. Because GRPO relies on token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$, any change in which experts are active causes the likelihood of the token to fluctuate drastically. This invalidates the importance sampling weight and introduces the same high-variance noise discussed previously, preventing the model from converging. To combat this, researchers previously had to use a "Routing Replay" strategy. This involved caching the specific experts used in the old policy and "replaying" those same routing modes in the new policy to force the importance ratios to remain stable. While effective, Routing Replay is cumbersome, requiring extra memory and communication overhead, and it can limit the model's capacity by artificially constraining which experts can be used.

GSPO fundamentally solves this problem without any workarounds. The core reason is that GSPO focuses on the sequence-level likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)$ rather than the token-level likelihood $\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}|x, y_{i,<t})$. While the specific "routing" of experts might fluctuate slightly for an individual token, the model's overall ability to generate a coherent sequence remains stable. Because the sequence likelihood does not fluctuate as drastically as the individual token likelihoods, GSPO is naturally robust to expert-activation volatility. By obviating the need for Routing Replay, GSPO simplifies the training pipeline, reduces overhead, and allows the MoE model to utilize its full capacity without artificial constraints.

# Infrastructure Benefits

Beyond the algorithmic and mathematical advantages, GSPO offers significant practical benefits for the infrastructure used to train large language models. In real-world production environments, there is often a "precision discrepancy" between the engines used for training (such as Megatron) and the engines used for inference (such as SGLang or vLLM). These engines may use different floating-point precisions or different computational kernels, which can lead to slight differences in the calculated likelihoods of the responses.

For algorithms like GRPO, these small discrepancies can be problematic. Because GRPO multiplies many token-level likelihoods together to compute the importance ratio, these tiny errors can accumulate across a long sequence, leading to significant numerical drift. This often forces engineers to recompute the likelihoods of sampled responses using the training engine, which is computationally expensive and slows down the training loop.

GSPO is inherently more tolerant of these discrepancies because it uses sequence-level likelihoods. Because the optimization is performed on the sequence as a whole rather than being built from the product of many individual token ratios, the impact of minor precision differences is greatly diminished. This tolerance allows researchers to directly use the likelihoods returned by the inference engine for the optimization step. This capability is highly valuable in advanced scenarios such as partial rollout, multi-turn reinforcement learning, and "training-inference disaggregated" frameworks, where the goals of the inference and training systems are separated to maximize hardware efficiency.

# Conclusion

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) represents a significant advancement in the reinforcement learning of large language models. By shifting the unit of optimization from the token level to the sequence level, GSPO aligns the importance sampling weights with the way rewards are actually granted—at the completion of a response. This shift provides a theoretically grounded and practically robust framework that addresses the core instability issues found in previous methods like GRPO.

The empirical evidence provided in this study demonstrates that GSPO is not only more stable but also more efficient and performant. It solves the critical "expert-activation volatility" problem in MoE models without the need for complex workarounds like Routing Replay. Furthermore, it simplifies the underlying infrastructure by being more resilient to the precision discrepancies between different software engines. By providing a stable and scalable foundation for RL, GSPO has already contributed to major improvements in the Qwen3 models and stands as a cornerstone for future efforts to scale intelligence through reinforcement learning.
