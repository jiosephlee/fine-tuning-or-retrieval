\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Mining Gold from the Web: How DeepSeekMath Built a Massive Math Corpus

Mathematical reasoning remains one of the most significant hurdles for large language models (LLMs) due to its highly structured and complex nature. While proprietary models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have set high bars for performance, the open-source community has often lagged behind. The researchers behind DeepSeekMath addressed this gap by focusing on a fundamental ingredient of AI success: the data. By constructing a massive, high-quality mathematical corpus from the vast wilderness of the internet, they developed DeepSeekMath 7B, which achieves an impressive 51.7% on the competition-level MATH benchmark.

## The Challenge of Sifting Through the Noise

The primary challenge in training a math-specialized model is identifying high-quality mathematical content within the massive volume of web data. Simply scraping the internet is insufficient because the "signal"—actual mathematical reasoning—is often buried under noise. To solve this, the researchers developed a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline designed to mine "gold" from the Common Crawl (CC) dataset.

The strategy began with a seed corpus: OpenWebMath. This is a collection of high-quality mathematical web texts that provided the "ground truth" for what the model should learn. Using this seed, the team trained a fastText classifier to distinguish between mathematical content and general web pages. They used 500,000 data points from the seed corpus as positive examples and 500,000 diverse web pages from Common Crawl as negative examples.

## An Iterative Pipeline for Scale

Rather than a single pass, the researchers used an iterative process to expand their dataset. The classifier was used to recall mathematical pages from the deduplicated Common Crawl. To ensure quality, the team ranked these pages based on their classifier scores and kept only the top-ranking instances.

However, because the initial seed corpus lacked diversity, many relevant mathematical pages were missed in the first pass. To fix this, the researchers:
* Organized the Common Crawl into disjoint domains based on base URLs.
* Identified domains where more than 10% of the pages were already collected as "math-related."
* Manually annotated specific URLs within those domains that contained mathematical content (e.g., specific sub-sections of MathOverflow).
* Added these uncollected but high-quality URLs to the seed corpus.

This loop was repeated four times. By refining the seed corpus with human-annotated examples and retraining the classifier each time, the team successfully mined a corpus of 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120 billion tokens. This is significantly larger than previous efforts, being nearly seven times the size of the math web pages used by Minerva and nine times the size of OpenWebMath.

## Data Quality Over Model Size

One of the most compelling findings of the DeepSeekMath study is that high-quality data can compensate for model scale. The researchers validated the DeepSeekMath Corpus by training a relatively small 1.3B parameter model on different datasets. 

The results showed that the DeepSeekMath Corpus provided a steeper learning curve and more lasting improvements compared to other corpora like MathPile or Proof-Pile-2. Furthermore, because the corpus included multilingual data, it significantly improved performance on Chinese mathematical benchmarks—an area where many English-centric math datasets underperform.

The researchers also discovered an unexpected result regarding academic papers. While many models rely heavily on arXiv papers for training, DeepSeekMath’s experiments suggested that arXiv papers were not particularly effective at improving mathematical reasoning in their specific benchmarks. Instead, the diverse, high-quality web data provided a more robust foundation for the model's capabilities.

## Refining the Reasoning with GRPO

While data selection provided the foundation, the researchers also introduced a novel reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to polish the model's reasoning. GRPO is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) that removes the need for a critic model, significantly reducing memory and computational overhead. 

Instead of a separate model estimating a baseline, GRPO estimates the baseline from the average reward of a group of sampled outputs for the same question. This approach allowed DeepSeekMath-Instruct to jump from 46.8% to 51.7% on the MATH benchmark. By combining a massive, meticulously mined corpus with efficient reinforcement learning, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that the path to state-of-the-art reasoning lies in the quality and scale of the underlying data.
