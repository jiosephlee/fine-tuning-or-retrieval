\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Does RL Actually Make AI Smarter? Decoding the 'Robustness' Factor

When we observe a large language model (LLM) achieve a higher score on a complex mathematics benchmark after undergoing Reinforcement Learning (RL), it is tempting to conclude that the model has suddenly become "smarter." However, the researchers behind **DeepSeekMath** offer a more nuanced perspective. Their findings suggest that RL might not be expanding the model's fundamental knowledge or core reasoning capabilities as much as it is making the model's existing capabilities more **robust**.

To understand this distinction, we have to look at how the researchers evaluated the transition from the instruction-tuned model (DeepSeekMath-Instruct) to the RL-enhanced model (DeepSeekMath-RL).

## The Metrics: Pass@K vs. Maj@K

The researchers used two specific metrics to dissect the impact of RL on mathematical reasoning: **Pass@K** and **Maj@K**. These metrics help differentiate between a model's ability to "stumble upon" a correct answer and its ability to consistently produce it.

*   **Pass@K**: This measures the probability that at least one correct answer exists within a set of $K$ samples generated by the model. If a model has a high Pass@K, it means that even if it's inconsistent, it is capable of generating a correct reasoning path eventually.
*   **Maj@K**: This measures the probability that the *majority* of the $K$ samples are correct. A high Maj@K score indicates that the model is consistently arriving at the correct conclusion.

By comparing these two, the researchers were able to isolate whether RL was teaching the model "new tricks" or simply refining its current behavior.

## The Finding: Robustness over Raw Capability

The results from the DeepSeekMath experiments provide a striking insight: **RL significantly enhances Maj@K but has a negligible effect on Pass@K.**

This suggests that the "pool" of correct answers the model is capable of generating does not significantly expand during the RL phase. Instead, RL works by narrowing the model's output distribution. In simpler terms, while the model isn't necessarily learning a vast array of new mathematical proofs, it is becoming much more reliable at choosing the correct reasoning path from the options it already knows. 

The researchers describe this as boosting the correct response from the "Top-K" results. Rather than "expanding" the model's fundamental intelligence, RL acts as a corrective force that stabilizes the model's reasoning. It suppresses the "noise" of incorrect paths and reinforces the "signal" of the correct ones, making the model's performance more dependable and robust.

## How GRPO Drives this Robustness

To achieve this, the team introduced **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. GRPO is an efficient variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) that eliminates the need for a separate "critic" model. 

Instead of using a critic to estimate a baseline, GRPO samples a group of outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ for the same question and uses the average reward of that group to calculate the advantage. This "group relative" approach aligns well with how reward models are typically trained—on comparisons between different outputs for the same prompt. 

By rewarding the model for producing outputs that are better than its own group average, GRPO effectively "prunes" the less reliable reasoning paths. Whether using **Outcome Supervision (OS)**, which rewards the final answer, or **Process Supervision (PS)**, which rewards each individual reasoning step, the result is a model that is increasingly disciplined in its logic.

## The Unified Paradigm

The research also places these findings into a broader context by proposing a unified paradigm for training methods like SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO. They argue that all these methods can be viewed as different ways of processing a **Data Source**, a **Reward Function**, and an **Algorithm** to produce a **Gradient Coefficient**.

In this framework, the distinction between these methods lies in how they handle the reward signal:
*   **SFT** relies on human-selected data.
*   **RFT and DPO** use offline sampling to refine the model.
*   **Online RFT and GRPO** use real-time exploration to provide more dynamic feedback.

By understanding RL as a way to optimize these coefficients, we can see that its primary role in mathematical reasoning is to act as a fine-tuning mechanism for reliability. It ensures that when a model is asked a difficult question, it doesn't just have the *potential* to get it right—it is statistically much more likely to do so.
