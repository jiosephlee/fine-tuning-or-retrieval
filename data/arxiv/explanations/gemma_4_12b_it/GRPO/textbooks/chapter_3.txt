\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 3: # Training Architectures and Empirical Findings

The development of a high-performing mathematical language model requires more than just a massive amount of raw data; it requires a strategic approach to how that data is introduced to the model and how different types of information interact during the learning process. To understand how DeepSeekMath achieves its results, we must examine the specific architecture of its training, the types of data mixtures used to build its "intelligence," and the empirical findings regarding how specific data sources—such as computer code and academic papers—actually influence the model's reasoning capabilities.

# Initialization Strategy: Why DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 is an ideal starting point

In the lifecycle of training a large language model (LLM), the "initialization" phase refers to the state of the model before specialized training begins. Choosing the correct starting point is critical because a model's foundational weights determine its latent capacity to learn certain types of logic. For DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, the researchers deliberately chose to initialize the model using DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B rather than starting from a general-purpose language model.

The intuition behind this choice lies in the structural similarities between computer programming and mathematical reasoning. Both code and mathematics require the model to understand strict logical sequences, handle precise syntax, and decompose complex problems into a series of discrete, manageable steps. A model pre-trained on code is already "primed" to think in these structured ways. By starting with a "coder," the model begins its mathematical journey with a sophisticated understanding of procedural logic, which provides a much stronger foundation than a general model that might be more attuned to the fluid, often ambiguous nature of everyday conversation.

Furthermore, this choice reflects a move toward domain-specific initialization. By starting with a model that already excels at a related cognitive task—coding—the researchers can focus the subsequent pre-training on refining and expanding those logic-heavy capabilities into the mathematical domain. This strategy helps ensure that the model doesn't have to learn the "basics" of logical consistency from scratch; instead, it can leverage existing weights that are already optimized for the kind of rigorous, step-by-step processing that mathematical problem-solving demands.

# Training Recipe: Mixture of math, code, and natural language data

Once the model is initialized, the "recipe" for its pre-training determines its final capabilities. DeepSeekMath-Base 7B is trained for a total of 500B tokens using a carefully balanced mixture of different data types. This mixture is designed to ensure that the model gains deep mathematical expertise without losing its ability to communicate naturally or maintain its logical "edge."

The distribution of this 500B-token training set is highly specific. The primary driver of the model's performance is the DeepSeekMath Corpus, which accounts for 56% of the training data. This large portion of math-related tokens provides the core domain knowledge required to solve problems ranging from elementary school arithmetic to college-level calculus and geometry. To complement this, the recipe includes 20% GitHub code tokens. This serves as a continuous "logic anchor," ensuring that the model maintains its ability to structure information and perform procedural reasoning throughout the training process.

The remaining portion of the mixture is dedicated to maintaining general-purpose utility. This includes 10% natural language data from Common Crawl in both English and Chinese, which prevents the model from becoming "over-specialized" to the point where it can no longer understand or generate standard human language. Additionally, 10% of the data comes from arXiv papers, and 4% from AlgebraicStack. This diverse mixture ensures that the model becomes a well-rounded assistant: it possesses the specialized knowledge of a mathematician, the logical structure of a programmer, and the linguistic fluency of a polyglot.

# The Code Training Hypothesis: How code training enhances mathematical reasoning

One of the most significant empirical contributions of this research is the investigation of the "code training hypothesis." There has long been a debate in the AI community regarding whether training a model on programming languages actually improves its general reasoning abilities. The researchers in this paper provide compelling evidence that code training is a powerful catalyst for mathematical reasoning, regardless of whether the model is actually being asked to write code.

The researchers tested this through several experimental settings. In a "Two-Stage Training" setup, they compared a model that underwent 400B tokens of code training followed by 150B tokens of math training against a model that received 400B tokens of general-purpose data followed by the same 150B math tokens. They found that the code-pre-trained model significantly outperformed the general-pre-trained model in solving both program-aided and non-tool mathematical problems. This suggests that code training teaches the model a "meta-skill" of logic that is transferable to other structured domains.

Even more interesting was the "One-Stage Training" experiment, where code and math tokens were mixed together. The results showed that mixing these two types of data actually synergizes their benefits. Specifically, while two-stage training can sometimes lead to "catastrophic forgetting" (where the model loses its coding ability as it learns math), the mixed approach allowed the model to retain its high coding performance while simultaneously boosting its math reasoning. This confirms that the logical patterns found in code are fundamentally aligned with the patterns found in mathematics, allowing the model to learn both effectively and concurrently.

# The ArXiv Paradox: Analyzing why arXiv papers may not provide the expected boost

In many previous works, academic papers—particularly those from arXiv—are treated as the "gold standard" for high-quality mathematical training data. The intuition is that because these papers contain complex proofs and advanced theories, they should naturally lead to a more capable model. However, the researchers found a surprising result: arXiv papers appeared to be largely ineffective at improving mathematical reasoning on the benchmarks tested.

To investigate this, the researchers trained models of different sizes (1.3B and 7B parameters) on various arXiv-centric corpora, including MathPile (which is over 85% arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama. Despite the high academic quality of this data, the models showed no notable improvements—and in some cases, even a deterioration—across various mathematical benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-STEM. This "paradox" suggests that the way information is structured in academic papers might not be the most efficient way for a model to learn the underlying mechanics of problem-solving.

The authors offer a few nuanced caveats to this finding. They note that their experiments may not have captured specific tasks that arXiv papers might excel at, such as the "informalization" of theorems (translating formal math into human-readable text). Furthermore, they acknowledge that the benefits of such dense, academic data might only manifest at a much larger model scale than the 7B parameters used in this study. Nevertheless, for the current scale of open-source models, the researchers suggest that large-scale web-based mathematical content is currently more effective than a heavy reliance on academic papers.

# Generalization: Impact of math training on MMLU and BBH benchmarks

A critical question in AI research is whether training a model on a specific domain—like mathematics—causes it to lose its general intelligence or "overfit" so much that it can no longer perform unrelated tasks. The results from the DeepSeekMath-Base 7B experiments provide a very positive answer to this question: math training actually appears to amplify general reasoning capabilities across different domains.

The researchers evaluated the model on the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark and the BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) benchmark. MMLU covers a wide array of subjects, while BBH consists of challenging tasks that require multi-step reasoning. The results showed that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B exhibited significant enhancements in these areas compared to its precursor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5. This implies that the "reasoning muscles" developed during math pre-training are not isolated; they provide a structural benefit to the model's ability to understand language and navigate complex logic in any context.

Additionally, the researchers demonstrated that this math-heavy training did not come at the cost of technical skills. By including code tokens in the final mixture, the model maintained its performance on coding benchmarks like HumanEval and MBPP. This suggests a harmonious "multi-tasking" capability where the model can switch between mathematical reasoning, computer programming, and general linguistic tasks without significant degradation in any single area. The take-away is that mathematical reasoning is a foundational cognitive pillar; improving it provides a "rising tide" that lifts the model's overall intelligence.
