\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 6: A Unified Paradigm for Preference Optimization

# The Unified Objective Function: Data Source, Reward Function, and Algorithm

To understand the diverse landscape of modern large language model alignment, it is helpful to view different techniques not as isolated methods, but as variations of a single underlying objective. The research presented in the DeepSeekMath paper proposes a unified paradigm to analyze methods such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). This framework conceptualizes the gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ as a product of three fundamental components: the data source, the reward function, and the algorithm.

The mathematical expression for this unified objective is:
$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\underbrace{(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}}_{Data \ Source}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|}  \underbrace{GC_{{\mathcal{A}}}(q, o, t, \pi_{{rf}})}_{Gradient \ Coefficient}  \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right)$$

In this equation, the data source $\mathcal{D}$ defines where the training examples come from. The reward function $\pi_{rf}$ provides the signal that determines the quality or preference of an output. Finally, the algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ acts as the mechanism that processes the data and the reward signal to produce the gradient coefficient $GC$. This coefficient is essentially a multiplier that determines how much the model's parameters should be updated—either reinforcing a specific token or penalizing it—based on the reward signal. By isolating these three components, we can clearly see how different alignment strategies differ in their mechanics.

# Online vs. Offline Sampling: Comparing RFT/DPO with Online RFT/GRPO

One of the primary distinctions within this unified paradigm is whether the training data is collected "offline" or "online." Offline sampling refers to situations where the data source $\mathcal{D}$ is derived from the sampling results of an initial, fixed model, such as the model produced after SFT. This is the case for RFT and DPO. In these methods, the model generates multiple outputs, and those that are deemed "correct" or "preferred" are used to fine-tune the model. Because the model producing the data does not change during the sampling phase, the diversity and quality of the data are constrained by the initial model's capabilities.

In contrast, online sampling involves data generated by the real-time policy model being trained. This is the characteristic of Online RFT and GRPO. In these methods, the model (the "actor") explores and generates new outputs during the training process itself. The paper provides a significant observation regarding this distinction: Online RFT significantly outperforms RFT, particularly in the later stages of training. This happens because, in the early stages, the actor and the SFT model are very similar, making the data they produce nearly identical. However, as training progresses, the actor begins to deviate from the SFT model. At this point, online sampling provides a distinct advantage because the model is exploring and learning from its own evolving outputs, rather than being limited to the distribution of the original SFT model.

# Gradient Coefficients: How different algorithms penalize or reinforce outputs

The gradient coefficient $GC$ is the core "logic" of an alignment algorithm. It dictates how the reward signal is translated into a change in model weights. The research distinguishes between two types of reward functions: "Rule" and "Model." A Rule-based reward is a binary or deterministic judgment, such as whether a mathematical answer is correct. A Model-based reward involves a separate neural network (a reward model) that provides a continuous score for a response.

The primary difference between GRPO and Online RFT lies in how they handle these coefficients. In Online RFT, the algorithm lacks a nuanced way to differentiate between the quality of different correct answers. It uniformly reinforces all responses that meet the "Rule" of correctness. On the other hand, GRPO utilizes the magnitude of the reward provided by the reward model to adjust the gradient coefficient. This allows GRPO to perform "differential reinforcement," where it can prioritize high-scoring outputs over merely correct ones.

Furthermore, the research explores the granularity of these coefficients through Outcome Supervision (OS) and Process Supervision (PS). Outcome Supervision provides a reward only at the very end of a complete solution. Process Supervision, however, provides rewards at the end of each individual reasoning step. By using Process Supervision with GRPO, the model receives more fine-grained, step-aware gradient coefficients. This allows the model to receive feedback on each intermediate thought, rather than just the final result, which is particularly beneficial for complex mathematical tasks.

# Empirical Analysis: Why RL works (Top-K robustness vs. fundamental capability)

A critical question in the study of mathematical reasoning is why Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides such a substantial boost to models that have already undergone Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). To investigate this, the researchers analyzed the difference between "Pass@K" (the probability that at least one of the top $K$ outputs is correct) and "Maj@K" (the probability that the most frequent correct answer among the top $K$ is the correct one).

The empirical results show that RL significantly enhances Maj@K accuracy but does not notably improve Pass@K. This finding is crucial because it suggests that RL does not necessarily grant the model "new" fundamental capabilities or a deeper understanding of mathematics that it didn't have before. Instead, RL works by making the model's output distribution more robust. It effectively "boosts" the correct responses within the top $K$ candidates, ensuring that the model is more likely to converge on the correct answer when it generates multiple paths. This aligns with the "misalignment" theory, which suggests that SFT models might already contain the necessary reasoning but need preference alignment to correctly prioritize and output those correct reasoning paths.

# Future Directions: Weak-to-Strong alignment, reward uncertainty, and advanced sampling

The unified paradigm allows for a systematic exploration of how to improve reinforcement learning in the future. The research identifies three key areas for development: the data source, the algorithm, and the reward function.

Regarding the data source, the authors suggest that moving beyond simple nucleus sampling to advanced decoding strategies, such as tree-search methods, could improve the exploration of the policy model. Additionally, efficient inference techniques like speculative decoding could help determine how effectively a model can explore the space of possible answers. 

In terms of algorithms, the researchers propose "weak-to-strong" alignment. This approach is designed to be robust against noisy reward signals. Since even human-annotated reward models can have errors (for instance, a 20% error rate in some datasets), it is vital to develop algorithms that can distinguish between a truly high-quality reasoning path and a high-scoring but incorrect one.

Finally, for the reward function, the focus should be on improving the generalization of reward models to out-of-distribution questions. If a reward model cannot generalize, reinforcement learning may only stabilize the model's current distribution rather than improving its actual reasoning. The authors also highlight the need to reflect the uncertainty of the reward model—using that uncertainty as a bridge to the weak-to-strong learning algorithms—and the development of high-quality process reward models to provide the finest possible granularity for training.
