\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Scaling Mathematical Pre-training: Data Engineering at Scale

# The Data Selection Pipeline: Using fastText classifiers for web data mining

Mathematical reasoning is a complex and highly structured task that poses a significant challenge for large language models (LLMs). To address this, the researchers behind DeepSeekMath sought to harness the vast potential of publicly available web data by creating a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline. The core of this pipeline involves extracting high-quality mathematical content from the Common Crawl (CC) dataset, which is an enormous repository of web pages. To manage this scale, the researchers utilized a fastText-based classifier to distinguish between general web content and mathematically relevant text.

The construction of the classifier begins with a carefully curated "seed corpus." In this specific project, the researchers chose OpenWebMath as the initial seed because it is a collection of high-quality mathematical web texts. To train the fastText model, they selected 500,000 data points from OpenWebMath to serve as positive examples. To provide a contrast, they selected 500,000 web pages from the Common Crawl to serve as negative examples. This binary classification setup allows the model to learn the distinctive linguistic and structural features of mathematical discourse compared to general internet text.

The technical configuration of the fastText model included specific hyperparameters to ensure effective training: a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum word n-gram length of 3, a minimum number of word occurrences of 3, and a total of 3 training epochs. Before running the classifier, the researchers performed URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication on the Common Crawl data, which reduced the pool to 40 billion HTML web pages. The fastText model was then used to score these pages, and only the top-ranking pages—those most likely to contain mathematical content—were preserved for the initial iteration. This methodology ensures that the model is fed a high-density concentration of useful information rather than being overwhelmed by irrelevant "noise" from the general web.

# Iterative Corpus Construction: Seed data, expansion, and human annotation

A single pass of a classifier often fails to capture the full diversity of a domain. The researchers observed that many high-quality mathematical pages remained uncollected after the first iteration because the initial seed corpus (OpenWebMath) lacked sufficient diversity to represent all types of mathematical content. To solve this, they implemented an iterative pipeline designed to expand the seed corpus and, consequently, the reach of the fastText classifier.

The expansion process began by organizing the Common Crawl into disjoint domains, where a domain is defined as a group of web pages sharing the same base URL. The researchers analyzed each domain to see how many of its pages were collected in the first iteration. If more than 10\% of the pages in a domain were collected, that domain was flagged as "math-related" (for example, sites like mathoverflow.net). Within these identified domains, the researchers manually annotated specific URLs that contained mathematical content. Any uncollected pages linked to these specific URLs were then added back into the seed corpus.

This iterative cycle allowed the researchers to "teach" the classifier about new types of mathematical content in each round. By updating the classifier with this enriched and more diverse set of positive examples, the model became increasingly adept at mining mathematical data in subsequent iterations. This process was repeated four times. By the fourth iteration, the researchers observed that nearly 98\% of the available mathematical data had already been collected in the third iteration, signaling that the pipeline had reached a point of saturation. The final result of this multi-stage process was a corpus of 35.5 million mathematical web pages, totaling 120 billion tokens.

# Data Decontamination: Filtering out benchmark questions to ensure integrity

A critical step in training any language model intended for academic evaluation is "decontamination." If a model is trained on the exact questions and answers from a benchmark like GSM8K or MATH, it may "memorize" the answers rather than learning the underlying reasoning. This would lead to artificially inflated scores that do not reflect the model's true capabilities. To prevent this, the researchers implemented a strict filtering protocol to ensure the DeepSeekMath Corpus did not contain data from the benchmarks used for testing.

The primary filtering criterion focused on the length of the text strings. The researchers identified any text segment containing a 10-gram string that matched any sub-string from the evaluation benchmarks—including English benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese benchmarks (CMATH, AGIEval). If a match was found, the entire web page was removed from the training corpus. This high-granularity check ensures that the model cannot rely on simple pattern matching of known benchmark problems.

For shorter benchmark texts, the researchers applied a secondary rule. If a benchmark text was shorter than 10 grams but contained at least 3 grams, the researchers employed exact matching to filter out the contaminated web pages. By applying these rules across both English and Chinese datasets, the researchers ensured that the model's performance on these benchmarks would be a true measure of its mathematical reasoning ability rather than a result of data leakage during the pre-training phase.

# Corpus Comparison: Analyzing DeepSeekMath vs. MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2

To validate the quality of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, the researchers conducted controlled experiments comparing it against three existing mathematical corpora: MathPile, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile-2. These corpora differ significantly in their composition; for instance, MathPile is largely comprised of arXiv papers (over 85\%), while Proof-Pile-2 includes a mixture of OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack (code), and arXiv papers.

The researchers tested these corpora by training a 1.3B parameter model (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B) for 150 billion tokens on each dataset. They used the AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and a weight decay of 0.1. The learning rate followed a multi-step schedule, peaking at $5.3 \times 10^{-4}$ after 2,000 warmup steps, and decreasing as the training progressed. These experiments were conducted using a batch size of 4 million tokens with a 4K context length.

The evaluation results provided three key insights. First, the DeepSeekMath Corpus demonstrated higher average quality; the model trained on it outperformed the model trained on Proof-Pile-2 even when the latter was given a full epoch of training (50B tokens). Second, the DeepSeekMath Corpus showed a "steeper learning curve," meaning the model improved more rapidly and sustained those improvements longer than models trained on the smaller baseline corpora, which tended to reach a performance plateau quickly. Finally, the results confirmed that DeepSeekMath is the largest of the compared corpora, proving that scaling the volume of high-quality data is a viable path toward improving mathematical reasoning.

# Multilingualism in Math Data: Impact on English and Chinese benchmarks

A significant differentiator of the DeepSeekMath Corpus is its multilingual nature. While many previous efforts in mathematical pre-training focused almost exclusively on English-centric data, the DeepSeekMath Corpus was designed to include high-quality mathematical content in multiple languages, most notably English and Chinese. This was a deliberate choice to ensure the model remained robust across different linguistic contexts.

The impact of this multilingual approach was clearly reflected in the evaluation benchmarks. The researchers found that training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus significantly enhanced mathematical reasoning performance in both English and Chinese. In contrast, they observed that existing mathematical corpora, which are primarily English-centric, showed limited improvement in Chinese mathematical reasoning and, in some cases, may even hinder performance in that domain. By including high-quality non-English math data, the DeepSeekMath-Base 7B model was able to achieve superior results on Chinese benchmarks compared to models that followed the previous trend of only collecting English math data. This suggests that mathematical reasoning is a cross-linguistic capability that can be bolstered by high-quality data in the model's native target languages.
