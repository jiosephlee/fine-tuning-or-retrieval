\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does RL improve Maj@K but not Pass@K in this study?
Question:
In the 'Why RL Works?' section, the authors observe that RL enhances Maj@K’s performance but not Pass@K. They conclude that this suggests the improvement is 'boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.' 

Could someone explain what this means in terms of probability distributions? If the 'fundamental capabilities' aren't changing, is the model just becoming more 'confident' in the correct answer, or is the distribution of the output becoming more peaked?
Answer:
To understand the distinction between $\text{Maj@K}$ and $\text{Pass@K}$ in the context of the DeepSeekMath paper, it is helpful to think of these metrics as measuring two different properties of a model's output probability distribution: **concentration** and **coverage**.

$\text{Pass@K}$ measures the model's "coverage" or "reach." It asks: "Among the $K$ samples generated, is at least one of them correct?" If $\text{Pass@K}$ remains unchanged after Reinforcement Learning (RL), it implies that the model's ability to "stumble upon" or "reach" a correct solution has not improved. In terms of probability distributions, the total "volume" of the probability mass assigned to correct answers remains the same. The model is not learning new reasoning paths or "fundamental capabilities" that allow it to solve problems it previously could not solve at all.

$\text{Maj@K}$, on the other hand, measures the "concentration" or "consistency" of the model. It asks: "If we take the $K$ samples, is the most frequent (majority) answer correct?" When RL improves $\text{Maj@K}$ without changing $\text{Pass@K}$, it suggests that the model already had the correct answer within its reachable "knowledge space," but it was perhaps being erratic or "noisy" in its generation. RL acts as a refinement process that sharpens the distribution. It suppresses the probability of incorrect paths and boosts the probability of the correct paths that the model already knew how to produce.

To put this into a visual intuition: imagine the model's output as a landscape of peaks and valleys. $\text{Pass@K}$ tells you if there is a "peak" representing a correct answer anywhere in the visible landscape. $\text{Maj@K}$ tells you how high and sharp that peak is. The authors' observation suggests that RL is not creating new peaks (new capabilities); instead, it is making the existing correct peaks taller and sharper while flattening out the surrounding "noise" of incorrect answers. This is why they describe the improvement as "boosting the correct response from $\text{TopK}$"—the model is becoming more confident and consistent in selecting the correct answer from its existing repertoire, rather than expanding its repertoire to include new types of solutions.
