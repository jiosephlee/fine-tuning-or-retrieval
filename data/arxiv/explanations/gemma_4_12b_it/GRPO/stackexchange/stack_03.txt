\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does the paper claim that arXiv papers are ineffective for math reasoning pre-training?
Question:
Many existing models like Minerva and MathPile include a significant amount of arXiv data. The authors of DeepSeekMath claim that training on arXiv papers 'brings no notable improvements' and even caused 'deterioration.' I'm confused about the logic here—if these papers contain high-level mathematics, why would they be less effective than Common Crawl data? Is it a matter of data quality, the specific types of math covered, or something else in the data selection pipeline?
Answer:
The authors of DeepSeekMath conclude that arXiv papers are ineffective based primarily on empirical results from their ablation studies rather than a theoretical analysis of the data's inherent "difficulty." In their experiments, they tested whether training on arXiv-heavy corpora would improve mathematical reasoning by training two different models—DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B—on two distinct arXiv-based datasets: MathPile (which is over $85\%$ arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama (which is entirely arXiv LaTeX files).

The results of these experiments were consistent across both models and both datasets: training on these arXiv-only corpora resulted in "no notable improvements or even deterioration" across a wide range of benchmarks, including quantitative reasoning (GSM8K and MATH), multiple-choice challenges (MMLU-STEM), and formal mathematics (miniF2F). This suggests that while arXiv papers contain high-level mathematics, the specific distribution of information within them may not align well with the types of reasoning required by standard benchmarks.

To understand why this happened while their Common Crawl-based data succeeded, it is helpful to look at the authors' data selection pipeline. They argue that the "DeepSeekMath Corpus" is high-quality because they used an iterative fastText-based classifier to mine web pages that specifically resemble high-quality mathematical content (like MathOverflow). By contrast, arXiv papers are highly specialized research documents. The paper implies a disconnect between the "research-heavy" nature of arXiv and the "problem-solving" nature of the benchmarks. While arXiv might be excellent for learning about specific niche theories, the web-based data they filtered contains a much broader and perhaps more "grounded" variety of mathematical problems that translate better to general mathematical reasoning.

However, the authors are careful to note that this conclusion should be taken "with a grain of salt." They acknowledge three specific limitations in their findings that could explain why arXiv data might still be useful in other contexts:
1. They did not test the impact of arXiv tokens on specific tasks not included in their study, such as the "informalization of theorems" (converting formal proofs into informal language).
2. They did not study the effect of arXiv tokens when they are mixed with other types of data (e.g., code or natural language), which might provide the necessary context for the model to "digest" the complex math in the papers.
3. They have not explored whether the benefits of arXiv papers might manifest at a much larger model scale, where the model might have the capacity to internalize the high-level abstractions found in academic research.
