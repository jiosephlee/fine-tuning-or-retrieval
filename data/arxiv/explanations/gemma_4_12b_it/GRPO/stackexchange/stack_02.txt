\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How is the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ calculated differently in Outcome Supervision vs. Process Supervision?
Question:
In the 'Process Supervision RL with GRPO' subsection, the paper describes how to calculate the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$. For Outcome Supervision, it says $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \widetilde{r}_i$. However, for Process Supervision, it is defined as $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$. Could someone explain the intuition behind this summation? Why does summing future rewards at specific step indices provide a better signal for the tokens in that step compared to just using the final output reward?
Answer:
The difference between Outcome Supervision (OS) and Process Supervision (PS) in the context of the DeepSeekMath paper lies in how the "credit" for a correct or incorrect answer is distributed across the individual tokens of a reasoning chain. In Outcome Supervision, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is treated as a constant for every token in a given output because the reward is only provided at the very end. This means that if a model produces a long sequence of reasoning steps and arrives at the correct final answer, every single token in that sequence—regardless of whether that specific step was logically sound or even necessary—receives the same positive reinforcement. Conversely, if the model makes a brilliant logical leap in the first half but makes a trivial arithmetic error at the very end, the Outcome Supervision signal will treat the entire sequence as a failure, potentially punishing the correct reasoning in the early steps.

Process Supervision addresses this "blunt" signal by providing rewards at the end of each individual reasoning step. The summation $\hat{A}_{i,t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ is a way of performing credit assignment. By summing all normalized rewards from the current token position $t$ until the end of the output, the model is rewarded based on the success of the specific steps it completes from that point forward. Intuitively, this tells the model: "The value of the token you are producing right now is the sum of all the successful reasoning steps that will follow it."

This approach provides a significantly better signal because it localizes the reinforcement. If a model performs perfectly for three steps and then makes a mistake on the fourth step, the rewards $\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$ for the fourth step and any subsequent steps will likely be low or negative. Because of the summation, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for the tokens in the first three steps will remain high (as they are still part of a "successful" prefix), while the advantage for the tokens in the fourth step will drop sharply. This allows the gradient to more precisely identify which specific part of the reasoning process failed.

From a broader reinforcement learning perspective, this summation is conceptually analogous to the "return" or the "Value" function $V(s_t)$ used in standard Reinforcement Learning. In standard RL, the value of a state is the expected sum of future rewards. In the DeepSeekMath paper's GRPO implementation, since they are not using a separate critic model to estimate these values, they use this suffix sum of step-wise rewards as a proxy for the value of the current state (the current token position). It transforms the reward from a global "pass/fail" grade into a sequence of "progress" markers, allowing the model to learn the underlying logic of the mathematical process rather than just learning to produce an output that happens to match the correct final answer.
