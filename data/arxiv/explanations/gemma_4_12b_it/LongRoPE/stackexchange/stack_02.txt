\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the piecewise function $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$ differ from standard Position Interpolation (PI)?
Question:
In Equation 3, the authors define a rescaling factor $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$ that depends on the token position $n$ and a threshold $\hat{n}$. In standard Position Interpolation (PI), the rescaling factor is a constant $s$ applied to all positions. Could someone explain the intuition behind why keeping the first $\hat{n}$ tokens without interpolation (where the factor is 1) improves performance? Why do these specific initial tokens need to be 'preserved'?
Answer:
The fundamental difference between standard Position Interpolation (PI) and the piecewise function $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$ introduced in the LongRoPE paper lies in the move from **uniform** to **non-uniform** interpolation across the token sequence.

In standard Position Interpolation, every token position $n$ in a sequence is scaled by a constant factor $s$ (where $s$ is the ratio of the new context length to the original length). This means that the positional information is compressed uniformly across the entire window. While this allows the model to "see" more tokens, it leads to the "crowding" problem mentioned in the paper, where the model struggles to distinguish between adjacent tokens because their positional embeddings become too similar.

LongRoPE addresses this by making the rescaling factor a function of the token's position $n$. By defining $\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_{i}, \hat{n})$ as a piecewise function, the authors create a "protected zone" for the first $\hat{n}$ tokens. For any position $n < \hat{n}$, the rescaling factor is exactly $1$, meaning these tokens use their original, pre-trained positional embeddings. For any position $n \geq \hat{n}$, the model applies the searched rescale factor $1/\lambda_{i}$.

The intuition for preserving these specific initial tokens is grounded in the attention dynamics of Large Language Models. As the authors note in Finding 2, initial tokens in a sequence often receive significantly higher attention scores than tokens in the middle or end of a long document. This phenomenon is well-documented in literature such as *StreamingLLM*, which suggests that early tokens act as "anchors" for the model's attention mechanism. These anchors provide the primary structural context for the rest of the sequence.

By keeping the first $\hat{n}$ tokens without interpolation, LongRoPE ensures that these critical "anchor" positions remain identical to the distributions the model encountered during its initial pre-training phase. If these initial positions were also compressed (as they are in standard PI), the model might lose its ability to effectively utilize these high-attention anchors, leading to a degradation in performance. Essentially, the "preservation" of these tokens allows the model to maintain a familiar starting point for its attention mechanism while still leveraging interpolated positions to accommodate much longer contexts.

This approach effectively balances two competing needs: the need to expand the context window (which requires interpolation to avoid out-of-distribution values) and the need to maintain the structural integrity of the most important positional information (which requires preservation of the original embeddings).
