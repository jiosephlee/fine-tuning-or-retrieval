\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How does the 'crowding' effect physically manifest in the attention mechanism?
Question:
The paper mentions that standard Position Interpolation (PI) makes position information 'crowded,' which hinders the model's ability to distinguish between closely positioned tokens. Could someone explain the mechanics of this? If we downscale the rotation angles $\theta_i$ by a large factor $s$, how does that specifically lead to the attention mechanism 'confusing' two adjacent tokens? Is it a matter of the dot-product similarity becoming too uniform?
Answer:
The "crowding" effect mentioned in the paper refers to a loss of spatial resolution in the positional embeddings. To understand how this physically manifests in the attention mechanism, it is helpful to view Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) as a series of rotations in a high-dimensional space.

In RoPE, each token at position $n$ is assigned a rotation angle based on the frequencies $\theta_i$. The difference in the rotation angle between two adjacent tokens (at positions $n$ and $n+1$) for a specific dimension $i$ is $\theta_i$. The attention mechanism relies on these angles to determine relative positions: when the model computes the dot-product similarity between a Query ($Q$) and a Key ($K$), the resulting score is sensitive to the angular distance between their respective positions.

When you apply Position Interpolation (PI) with a large extension ratio $s$, you downscale the rotation frequencies to $\frac{\theta_i}{s}$. Physically, this means that as you move from one token to the next, the "angular velocity" of the embedding decreases. The change in angle between adjacent tokens becomes $\Delta \phi = \frac{\theta_i}{s}$. If $s$ is very large, $\Delta \phi$ becomes extremely small. Consequently, the positional encodings for position $n$ and position $n+1$ become nearly identical, as they are rotated by almost the same amount.

This leads to "crowding" because the model's ability to distinguish between these tokens collapses at a local scale. In the attention calculation, if the rotation difference between position $n$ and $n+1$ is negligible, the dot-product similarity $\text{sim}(Q_n, K_{n+1})$ becomes nearly indistinguishable from $\text{sim}(Q_n, K_{n+2})$ or even $\text{sim}(Q_n, K_{n+3})$. Instead of the attention mechanism being able to "see" a sharp distinction between a neighboring token and a slightly more distant one, the positional information becomes "blurred." The attention weights become less discriminative because the unique "signature" provided by the position index is compressed into a range so small that the model can no longer resolve the individual steps.

The paper addresses this by highlighting that not all RoPE dimensions are equally important for this distinction. High-frequency dimensions (lower indices $i$) provide the fine-grained, local positional information needed to distinguish adjacent tokens, while low-frequency dimensions (higher indices $i$) provide the global structure. Standard PI treats all dimensions equally, downscaling them all by $s$ and thus "crowding" the high-frequency dimensions that are most responsible for local differentiation. LongRoPE's approach of non-uniform interpolation seeks to preserve the original rotation angles in key dimensions and for initial tokens, ensuring that the model retains enough "angular distance" between adjacent tokens to maintain its performance on short-context tasks while still expanding the total available range for long-context tasks.
