\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

Chapter 2: Fundamentals of RoPE and Positional Interpolation

# Mathematical Foundation of RoPE: Rotation Frequencies and Angles

To understand how modern Large Language Models (LLMs) process the order of words in a sentence, we must first examine Rotary Positional Embeddings, commonly known as RoPE. Transformer architectures require explicit positional information to distinguish between the same word appearing at different points in a sequence. RoPE is a widely adopted method for this purpose because it encodes positional information by rotating the token's embedding in a high-dimensional space. Specifically, for a token located at a position index $n$, RoPE generates an encoding based on a set of rotation frequencies.

The mathematical representation of a RoPE encoding for a token at position $n$ within an embedding dimension $d$ can be simplified as a vector of cosine and sine values. The embedding is structured as:

$\left[ cos(n\theta_0), sin(n\theta_0), cos(n\theta_1), \cdot\cdot\cdot, cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), sin(n\theta_{d/2-1}) \right]$

In this expression, $n\theta_i$ represents the rotary angle of the token at position $n$ for the $i^{th}$ dimension. The rotation frequencies $\theta_i$ are determined by the formula $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$. In standard implementations, the base value of $\theta$ is typically set to $10000$. By using these trigonometric functions, the model can maintain a relative sense of distance between tokens because the rotation depends on the index $n$, allowing the attention mechanism to compute relationships based on the relative rotation between different positions.

# Context Window Extension Ratio ($s$) and the Scaling Factor ($\lambda$)

When researchers attempt to extend the context window of a pre-trained LLM—moving from a small limit like 4,096 tokens to a much larger limit—they face the challenge of "out-of-distribution" indices. Because the model was never trained on position indices beyond its original limit, introducing new, large indices can lead to catastrophic values. To solve this, researchers use positional interpolation, which involves downscaling the rotation frequencies so that new, larger positions are mapped back into the range of angles the model already understands.

To formalize this, we define the context window extension ratio $s$ as the ratio of the target extended context length $L'$ to the original length $L$, expressed as $s = \frac{L'}{L}$. To perform the interpolation, we introduce a scaling factor $\lambda$. We can unify various positional interpolation methods into a single mathematical framework where the rotation angles are adjusted by a factor $\lambda(\beta)^i$. The modified RoPE encoding becomes:

$\left[cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), sin \left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^0}\right), cos\left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^1}\right), \cdot\cdot\cdot,  sin \left(\frac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\right) \right]$

Here, $\beta$ is defined as $\beta = \theta^{2/d}$, and $\lambda$ represents the actual rescale factor related to the extension ratio $s$. By adjusting $\lambda$, we effectively "compress" the positions into the existing range of the pre-trained model.

# Linear Positional Interpolation (PI): Mechanics and Limitations

The most straightforward method for extending the context window is Linear Positional Interpolation, or PI. In this approach, the rotation angles of all positions are linearly reduced by the extension ratio $s$ across every dimension of the RoPE embedding. Essentially, $\lambda$ is set directly to $s$. While this is simple to implement, it introduces a significant drawback known as the "crowded-positions" issue.

Because PI applies the same linear reduction to every dimension, it makes the positional information very dense. When the extension ratio is high, the differences between adjacent token positions become extremely small in the embedding space. This makes it difficult for the model to distinguish between closely positioned tokens, which hinders its ability to perform effectively. Consequently, while PI is a foundational technique, it tends to underperform as the target context window becomes significantly larger than the original training length.

# NTK-based Interpolation: Information Encoding and Frequency Distribution

To address the crowding issue of PI, researchers developed interpolation methods based on Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory. These methods view RoPE from an information encoding perspective. Instead of treating all dimensions equally, NTK-based methods distribute the "interpolation pressure" across the different frequencies of the RoPE dimensions. The intuition is that different dimensions carry different types of information; therefore, they should be scaled differently.

In these methods, the scaling factor $\lambda$ is not a constant $s$, but rather varies based on the dimension index $i$, often following the rule $\lambda = s^i$. This results in a configuration where lower dimensions (which have higher frequencies) are scaled less, while higher dimensions (which have lower frequencies) are scaled more. This creates a balance between interpolation and extrapolation. Unlike PI, NTK-aware methods can sometimes extend context windows even in non-fine-tuning scenarios, but they are generally limited to relatively small extension ratios, such as $4\times$.

# YaRN: Group-wise Frequency Interpolation and Empirical Grouping

YaRN (Yet another RoPE extension) provides a further refinement by categorizing the RoPE dimensions into three distinct groups based on their frequencies. Instead of a continuous formula for every dimension, YaRN applies three different strategies: extrapolation, NTK-based interpolation, and linear interpolation (PI). Specifically, high-frequency dimensions undergo extrapolation (where $\lambda=1$), low-frequency dimensions use linear interpolation, and the dimensions in between employ the NTK approach.

The primary strength of YaRN is that it prevents the information loss caused by over-interpolating high-frequency dimensions. However, a significant limitation of YaRN is that its grouping of dimensions is based on human-led empirical experiments. Because these groups are designed by humans rather than being mathematically optimized for a specific model architecture, they may result in sub-optimal performance when applied to new or different Large Language Models.

# Comparative Analysis: Why Human-Designed Rules are Sub-optimal

The core argument presented in this research is that current positional interpolation methods—including PI, NTK, and YaRN—rely too heavily on human-designed rules. While these methods show improvement over simple linear interpolation, they fail to account for the "complex non-uniform information entropy" inherent in the Transformer architecture. Because these rules are static and manually defined, they often fail to leverage the specific non-uniformities of the model's RoPE dimensions and token positions.

The study finds that the optimal solution for $\lambda$ is not a uniform scale, a simple power law, or a human-defined group. Instead, the optimal factors depend heavily on the target extended length and the specific model being used. By recognizing that RoPE dimensions exhibit substantial non-uniformities, we see that human-designed rules are sub-optimal because they cannot capture the precise, multi-dimensional nuances required to preserve information. To achieve extreme context windows, such as those exceeding 2 million tokens, the model needs a customized, non-uniform positional interpolation that is discovered through search rather than prescribed by heuristic rules.
