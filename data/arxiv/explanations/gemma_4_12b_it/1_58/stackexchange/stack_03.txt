\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How is matrix multiplication performed using 'almost no multiplication operations'?
Question:
The paper claims that BitNet b1.58 is more cost-effective because matrix multiplication involves only integer addition. Since the weights $\widetilde{W}$ are restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, I can see that multiplying by $1$ or $-1$ is simple. But if the activations $x$ are still 8-bit integers, how does the operation $\sum \widetilde{W}_{ij} x_j$ avoid multiplications entirely? Does the hardware treat the weight as a sign/zero indicator for the activation value?
Answer:
Your intuition is correct: the hardware essentially treats the ternary weights as instructions for how to handle the activation values, rather than as operands for a traditional multiplication circuit.

In a standard floating-point or full-integer matrix multiplication, the operation $y = Wx$ requires a Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) unit. This unit is hardware-intensive because it must be able to handle any combination of values, requiring complex logic to align bits and perform the multiplication of two multi-bit numbers. However, in BitNet b1.58, the weights $\widetilde{W}$ are restricted to the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. When you substitute these specific values into the summation $\sum \widetilde{W}_{ij} x_j$, the mathematical expression simplifies significantly based on the value of the weight:

1. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 1$, the operation becomes $1 \cdot x_j$, which is simply $x_j$.
2. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1$, the operation becomes $-1 \cdot x_j$, which is equivalent to $-x_j$.
3. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$, the operation becomes $0 \cdot x_j$, which is $0$.

Because of this, the computational "multiplication" step is eliminated. Instead of using a multiplier, the hardware only needs to perform addition and subtraction. From a hardware architecture perspective, a subtraction operation is essentially an addition where the bits of one operand are inverted (via a very simple NOT gate) before being passed into an adder. To handle the $0$ weight, the system simply skips the addition for that specific index or adds zero.

This is why the paper claims that the operation involves "only integer addition." By replacing complex multiplier circuits with simple adders and sign-inverters, the model avoids the most power-hungry part of standard deep learning inference. This structural change is what leads to the 71.4x reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption mentioned in the paper. Essentially, the weights act as a "control signal" that tells the hardware whether to add the activation, subtract it, or ignore it entirely, completely bypassing the need for a dedicated multiplication circuit.
