\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Introduction: The Bottlenecks of Modern Large Language Models

# The rise of LLMs and their capabilities

In recent years, the field of artificial intelligence has experienced an unprecedented surge in the growth and capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). These models have demonstrated remarkable performance across a wide range of natural language processing tasks, becoming the backbone of modern AI applications. This rapid advancement is largely driven by the increasing size of these models, which allows them to capture more complex patterns and information from massive datasets.

However, as these models grow in scale, their ability to perform sophisticated tasks also brings about significant practical challenges. While their performance is impressive, the sheer size of these models creates a series of logistical and technical hurdles. To understand the future of these models, one must first understand the limitations imposed by the current methods of building and running them, particularly as they scale toward even greater complexity.

# Hardware limitations: Memory bandwidth and power constraints

The deployment of large-scale models is heavily constrained by the physical limitations of modern hardware. One of the primary bottlenecks is the power constraint. In many contemporary computing chips, the fundamental limit to how fast a system can compute is dictated by power consumption. Because high-performance computing generates significant heat and requires massive amounts of electricity, engineers must find ways to perform more operations with less energy.

Another critical bottleneck is the movement of data, specifically the transfer of model parameters. During the inference phase of an LLM, model parameters must be moved from the DRAM (Dynamic Random Access Memory) to the memory of an on-chip accelerator, such as SRAM. This process is computationally expensive and slow. While one could theoretically enlarge the SRAM to improve throughput, doing so introduces significantly higher costs than using DRAM. Consequently, the bandwidth—or the speed at which data can be moved from memory to the processor—becomes a major limiting factor in how quickly a model can generate responses.

# The environmental and economic impact of high-precision (FP16/BF16) training and inference

The standard for training and running LLMs currently relies on high-precision floating-point values, specifically $FP16$ or $BF16$. These 16-bit formats allow for a high degree of numerical precision, which is beneficial for the accuracy of the model's weights and activations. However, this precision comes with a heavy price tag in terms of both economics and the environment.

Because these models are so large and require high-precision calculations, they consume massive amounts of energy. This high energy consumption raises serious concerns regarding the environmental impact of AI development. Furthermore, the economic cost of training and running these models is substantial, making it difficult to deploy large-scale models efficiently in many real-world applications. The industry is therefore under pressure to find ways to reduce the precision of these models without sacrificing the performance that makes them useful.

# Current solutions: Post-training quantization (PTQ) and its limitations

To address the high costs of $FP16$ and $BF16$ models, the industry has turned to post-training quantization (PTQ). This technique involves taking a model that has already been trained and reducing the precision of its weights and activations. By moving from 16-bit precision to lower bit-widths, such as 4-bit variants, developers can significantly reduce the memory and computational requirements for inference. Well-known methods for this include techniques like SmoothQuant, GPTQ, and QuIP.

Despite their widespread use in commercial LLMs, post-training quantization is considered sub-optimal. Because the model is quantized after the training process is complete, the weights are not optimized to work effectively in a low-bit format. This results in a compromise where some performance is inevitably lost to achieve the desired gains in efficiency. While PTQ is a useful tool for the current infrastructure, it does not address the underlying inefficiencies of the high-precision architecture.

# The motivation for training-aware low-bit architectures

A more promising direction involves designing models that are low-bit by design, rather than as an afterthought. This is the motivation behind architectures like BitNet. In vanilla LLMs, the bulk of the computation is performed through matrix multiplication. In a standard $FP16$ model, this requires complex floating-point addition and multiplication operations, which are the primary drivers of energy consumption and latency.

By moving toward a 1-bit model paradigm, the computation of matrix multiplication can be reduced to almost entirely integer addition. This shift in the computation paradigm saves orders of magnitude in energy cost because integer addition is far less demanding than floating-point multiplication. Additionally, 1-bit LLMs have a much lower memory footprint from both a capacity and a bandwidth standpoint, which reduces the time and cost of loading weights from DRAM to the accelerator.

The introduction of $1.58$-bit LLMs, specifically BitNet b1.58, takes this further by using ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This inclusion of the $0$ value allows for explicit feature filtering, which strengthens the modeling capability of the model. By training the model from scratch in this low-bit format, it is possible to match the performance of full-precision $FP16$ baselines while being significantly more cost-effective in terms of latency, memory, throughput, and energy. This represents a shift toward a new scaling law where high-performance models can be made both economically and environmentally sustainable.

The 1-bit Paradigm: From Floating-Point to Integer Arithmetic

# The dominance of matrix multiplication in Transformer architectures

To understand the shift toward 1-bit Large Language Models (LLMs), one must first recognize the fundamental architectural building blocks that define modern AI. Current Large Language Models are primarily built upon the Transformer architecture, which relies heavily on linear transformations to process and represent information. When we examine the internal mechanics of these models, it becomes clear that the vast majority of the computational workload—the "bulk" of the model—consists of matrix multiplications.

In a standard Transformer, every time the model processes a sequence of tokens, it performs a series of operations where large matrices of weights are multiplied by matrices of input data (activations). Because these operations are repeated across many layers and heads, the efficiency of a single matrix multiplication determines the overall feasibility of the model. If the matrix multiplication is slow, memory-intensive, or energy-expensive, the entire model becomes difficult to deploy. Consequently, the quest for more efficient LLMs begins with finding ways to optimize this specific operation, as it is the primary bottleneck for both speed and resource consumption.

# Computational costs of floating-point addition vs. multiplication

The standard method for performing these matrix multiplications involves using high-precision floating-point values, such as FP16 or BF16. In these systems, every weight and every activation is represented as a decimal number with a specific degree of precision. While this allows for high modeling accuracy, it comes with a significant computational penalty. The primary cost of these operations stems from the underlying floating-point addition and multiplication.

In the hierarchy of computational complexity, floating-point multiplication is significantly more demanding than addition. In fact, the paper highlights that by moving away from standard floating-point multiplication and toward a paradigm that favors integer addition, we can achieve massive reductions in energy costs. Since the fundamental limit to computing performance in modern chips is often power (energy consumption), reducing the complexity of the math allowed for by the model translates directly into faster computation. By simplifying the arithmetic required to perform a matrix multiplication, we can bypass the energy-intensive floating-point logic that currently governs LLM inference.

# The BitNet concept: Replacing nn.Linear with BitLinear

To address the inefficiencies of floating-point arithmetic, the BitNet architecture introduces a fundamental change to the standard neural network layer. In a conventional model, a linear layer (often referred to as $nn.Linear$) utilizes a weight matrix $W$ that contains high-precision floating-point numbers. BitNet replaces this with a specialized $BitLinear$ layer. In this new paradigm, the weights of the model are constrained to a ternary system, meaning every single parameter takes on one of three possible values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

This specific choice of ternary values is what defines the "1.58-bit" designation, as the inclusion of the value $0$ adds an extra bit of information compared to a strictly binary $\{-1, 1\}$ system. This inclusion of $0$ is not merely a mathematical nuance; it provides the model with explicit support for feature filtering, which significantly improves the performance of 1-bit LLMs. To achieve this, the model employs an \textit{absmean} quantization function. First, the weight matrix $W$ is scaled by its average absolute value, denoted as $\gamma$, where:

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$$

The weight matrix is then transformed into the ternary matrix $\widetilde{W}$ using a rounding and clipping function:

$$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1\right)$$

Where the rounding and clipping function is defined as:

$$\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))$$

By enforcing this structure, the matrix multiplication in BitNet no longer requires complex floating-point multiplications. Instead, because the weights are limited to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the operation is reduced to simple integer addition and subtraction, which is far more efficient for hardware to execute.

# Energy efficiency gains in integer-based computation

One of the most compelling arguments for the 1.58-bit paradigm is the radical improvement in energy efficiency. In standard FP16 LLMs, the matrix multiplication consists of both FP16 addition and FP16 multiplication. In contrast, BitNet b1.58 performs matrix multiplications primarily through INT8 addition. This shift has profound implications for the "energy wall" faced by modern hardware.

The research provides a concrete example of this efficiency: for matrix multiplications on 7nm chips, BitNet b1.58 saves $71.4$ times the arithmetic operations energy consumption compared to the FP16 baseline. This is because integer addition is orders of magnitude cheaper in terms of power than floating-point multiplication. As model sizes scale, this energy advantage becomes even more pronounced. Because the percentage of $nn.Linear$ operations grows as the model gets larger, the relative energy savings of the 1.58-bit approach increase, making it a more sustainable way to train and deploy massive models. This efficiency allows for higher throughput and lower latency, as the chips can perform more operations per second without exceeding their power limits.

# Memory footprint reduction: DRAM-to-SRAM transfer efficiency

Beyond the raw math of the computation, there is a significant logistical cost associated with how data moves within a computer. During inference, model parameters must be transferred from the main memory (DRAM) to the memory of an on-chip accelerator, such as SRAM. Because DRAM has high capacity but lower speed, and SRAM is much faster but more expensive and smaller, the bottleneck is often the bandwidth—how quickly we can move the weights from DRAM to the processor.

1-bit LLMs offer a major advantage here because they have a much lower memory footprint from both a capacity and a bandwidth standpoint. Because the weights are represented using only 1.58 bits instead of 16 bits, the amount of data that needs to be moved is drastically reduced. This leads to faster and more efficient inference because the time and cost associated with loading weights from DRAM are minimized. By reducing the memory footprint, BitNet b1.58 allows for higher throughput and lower latency, as the system spends less time waiting for data to arrive and more time performing the actual computations. This makes it especially promising for edge and mobile devices, which are often severely limited by their memory and computational power.

Technical Deep Dive: The BitNet b1.58 Architecture

# Defining 1.58-bit: The ternary weight system $\{-1, 0, 1\}$

The fundamental innovation of BitNet b1.58 lies in its departure from traditional high-precision floating-point representations for model weights. In standard Large Language Models (LLMs), weights are typically stored and computed using 16-bit floating-point formats, such as $\text{FP16}$ or $\text{BF16}$. While these high-precision values allow for nuanced gradients and representation, they necessitate complex floating-point addition and multiplication operations. Because matrix multiplication is the primary computational bottleneck in Transformer architectures, these floating-point operations consume significant energy and limit the speed of inference.

BitNet b1.58 addresses this by employing a ternary weight system. In this architecture, every single parameter (or weight) in the model is constrained to one of three possible values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This specific choice is what gives the model its name. In information theory, the number of bits required to represent a set of values is given by $\log_2(n)$. Since there are three possible values in the ternary system, the number of bits required is $\log_2(3)$, which is approximately $1.58$. Thus, the model is referred to as a 1.58-bit LLM.

By restricting weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the model fundamentally changes the computation paradigm of matrix multiplication. When weights are restricted to these values, the standard dot product—which involves multiplying elements and summing them—is simplified. Since multiplying any value by $1$ leaves it unchanged, and multiplying by $-1$ simply flips its sign, the operation becomes a series of integer additions and subtractions. This eliminates the need for floating-point multiplications, which are much more expensive in terms of energy and hardware complexity. Furthermore, the inclusion of $0$ as a weight value provides a significant advantage over purely binary models. It allows for explicit "feature filtering," where the model can effectively "turn off" certain connections, thereby improving modeling capabilities while maintaining a highly efficient computational footprint.

# Weight Quantization: The absmean function and RoundClip mechanism

To achieve the ternary weight system while still training a model that performs comparably to full-precision variants, BitNet b1.58 employs a specific quantization function. This function ensures that during the training process, the weights are mapped from their continuous real-valued representations into the discrete set of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. The process involves two main components: a scaling factor based on the "absmean" and a rounding-clipping operation.

First, the model calculates a scaling factor, $\gamma$, which is the average absolute value of all weights in the weight matrix $W$. This is represented mathematically as:
$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$
where $n$ and $m$ represent the dimensions of the weight matrix. By calculating this average, the model establishes a baseline for the magnitude of the weights. The weight matrix $W$ is then scaled by this value plus a small epsilon ($\epsilon$) to ensure numerical stability. This scaling step is crucial because it centers the distribution of the weights, making it easier to map them to the desired ternary values.

Once the weights are scaled, the model applies the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function to produce the final quantized weights, $\widetilde{W}$. The transformation is defined as:
$\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$
The $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function itself is defined as:
$\mathrm{RoundClip}(x, a, b) = \max(a, \min(b, \mathrm{round}(x)))$
In the context of BitNet b1.58, $a$ is set to $-1$ and $b$ is set to $1$. This mechanism works by taking the scaled weight, rounding it to the nearest integer, and then "clipping" that result to ensure it does not exceed the bounds of $-1$ or $1$. This ensures that regardless of the input value, the resulting weight $\widetilde{W}$ is strictly one of the three allowed ternary values. This approach allows the model to maintain the structural benefits of a 1.58-bit system while providing a clear, reproducible pathway for weight quantization during training.

# Activation Quantization: 8-bit scaling and zero-point removal

While the weights in BitNet b1.58 are ternary, the activations—the values that flow through the network during inference—are quantized to 8 bits. This distinction is important because while weights can be restricted to very low precision without losing significant modeling power, activations often require a slightly higher bit-depth to maintain the nuances of the data as it passes through the layers of the Transformer.

The quantization of activations in BitNet b1.58 follows a specific implementation designed for system-level efficiency. Unlike some previous methods that might scale activations before they enter non-linear functions, BitNet b1.58 scales all activations to the range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token. This specific choice is motivated by the desire to remove "zero-point" quantization. In many quantization schemes, a zero-point is required to shift the range of values so they can be represented by integers; however, removing this zero-point makes the implementation and the subsequent system-level optimizations much simpler and more convenient.

By scaling to a symmetric range around zero ($[-Q_b, Q_b]$) rather than a range starting from zero, the model simplifies the underlying hardware logic required to process the 8-bit activations. The authors note that while this simplification is highly beneficial for implementation and hardware optimization, it introduces only negligible effects on the actual performance of the model. This balance allows BitNet b1.58 to maintain high accuracy while being significantly more efficient in terms of memory consumption and throughput compared to full-precision $\text{FP16}$ baselines.

# LLaMA-alike components: RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and Rotary Embedding (RoPE)

To ensure that BitNet b1.58 is not only theoretically sound but also practically useful for the broader research community, the authors designed the architecture to mirror the LLaMA series. LLaMA has become the de-facto backbone for open-source Large Language Models, and by adopting its core components, BitNet b1.58 ensures it can be easily integrated into existing software ecosystems.

The model incorporates several specific "LLaMA-alike" components. First, it utilizes RMSNorm (Root Mean Square Layer Normalization) instead of standard Layer Normalization. RMSNorm is computationally more efficient as it normalizes the activations by their root mean square rather than by their variance, which simplifies the calculation while maintaining similar performance. Second, the model employs the SwiGLU activation function. SwiGLU is a variant of the Gated Linear Unit that has shown superior performance in many Transformer architectures, and its inclusion ensures that BitNet b1.58 remains competitive with state-of-the-art models.

Furthermore, BitNet b1.58 uses Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE). RoPE is a method for encoding positional information into the Transformer's attention mechanism by rotating the query and key vectors in a complex plane. This allows the model to handle relative positions more effectively than standard absolute positional embeddings. By incorporating these specific components—RMSNorm, SwiGLU, and RoPE—the authors ensure that BitNet b1.58 is structurally compatible with the standards of modern LLM research.

# Design choices: Removing biases for software compatibility

A final, but critical, design choice in the BitNet b1.58 architecture involves the removal of all bias terms from the linear layers. In many standard neural networks, a bias term is added to the output of a linear transformation, which can help the model shift its activation patterns. However, for the purposes of BitNet b1.58, the authors chose to omit these biases entirely.

The primary motivation for this choice is software compatibility and ease of integration. By removing the biases, BitNet b1.58 can be integrated into popular open-source software frameworks—such as Huggingface, vLLM, and llama.cpp—with minimal effort. These tools are the standard for deploying and running LLMs in production and research environments. If the model were to include complex bias structures, it might require significant modifications to these libraries to support the unique 1.58-bit weight requirements. 

By choosing a "bias-free" design, the authors ensure that BitNet b1.58 is "plug-and-play" for the community. It allows researchers and developers to take the benefits of the 1.58-bit architecture—such as the massive energy savings and reduced memory footprint—and immediately apply them to existing pipelines without having to rewrite the underlying inference code. This makes the model significantly more accessible for real-world deployment on both large-scale servers and resource-constrained edge devices.

Empirical Evaluation: Performance and Scaling Laws

# Benchmarking methodology: RedPajama dataset and zero-shot task evaluation

To rigorously evaluate the performance of the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ architecture, the researchers established a controlled benchmarking environment designed to provide a fair comparison against the full-precision $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ baseline. The foundation of this comparison is the training data and the subsequent evaluation tasks. Both the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ and the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ were pre-trained on the RedPajama dataset, a massive corpus consisting of $100$ billion tokens. By keeping the training dataset and the number of training tokens constant, the researchers ensured that any observed differences in performance could be attributed to the model architecture and weight precision rather than differences in the data seen during the learning phase.

The evaluation of these models focused on zero-shot performance, which measures a model's ability to perform a task without any specific training or fine-tuning on that specific task. This is a critical metric for Large Language Models as it demonstrates the model's inherent reasoning and generalization capabilities. The researchers selected a diverse range of language tasks to test this, including ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, and BoolQ. These tasks cover various aspects of linguistic understanding, logic, and knowledge retrieval.

In addition to these end-task evaluations, the researchers used standard metrics to assess the models' underlying language modeling capabilities. Specifically, they reported the validation perplexity on the WikiText2 and C4 datasets. Perplexity is a standard measure of how well a probability model predicts a sample; a lower perplexity indicates that the model is more confident and accurate in its predictions of the next token in a sequence. By combining perplexity scores with zero-shot accuracy across multiple benchmarks, the study provides a comprehensive view of how $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ compares to full-precision models across different scales.

# Perplexity and End-task performance parity at the 3B scale

One of the most significant findings in the study is the point at which $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ achieves parity with full-precision models. The results indicate that $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ starts to match the full-precision $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ in terms of perplexity starting at a model size of $3\text{B}$ parameters. This is a crucial observation because it suggests that the transition to 1.58-bit ternary weights ($\{-1, 0, 1\}$) does not necessarily result in a significant loss of modeling capability once the model reaches a certain scale.

The researchers further demonstrated that $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ can actually provide a "Pareto improvement" over the state-of-the-art. For example, the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ model with a $3.9\text{B}$ parameter size was shown to perform significantly better than the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ $3\text{B}$ model. This improvement is notable because the $3.9\text{B}$ $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ model achieves these superior results while simultaneously requiring lower memory and exhibiting lower latency. This proves that the 1.58-bit architecture is not just a compromise for efficiency but can actually outperform traditional models of a smaller size when optimized correctly.

Furthermore, the data shows that the performance gap between $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ and the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ baseline narrows as the model size increases. This suggests that the 1.58-bit quantization is highly scalable. As the number of parameters grows, the benefits of the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ architecture become more pronounced, eventually matching the high-fidelity performance of full-precision models while maintaining its massive advantages in resource consumption.

# Comparative analysis: Latency, GPU memory usage, and throughput

The practical utility of $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ is most evident in its resource efficiency. The researchers conducted a detailed comparative analysis of runtime GPU memory and inference latency using the FasterTransformer codebase, which is highly optimized for GPU devices. For the $3\text{B}$ scale models, $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ was found to be $2.71$ times faster and utilized $3.55$ times less GPU memory than the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ baseline. At the $3.9\text{B}$ size, $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ maintained a similar trajectory, being $2.4$ times faster and consuming $3.32$ times less memory.

As the model size scales up to $7\text{B}$, $13\text{B}$, and $70\text{B}$ parameters, the speed-up advantage of $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ becomes even more dramatic. The study found that the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ $70\text{B}$ model is $4.1$ times faster than the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ baseline. This trend occurs because the computational cost of the $nn.Linear$ layers—which constitute the bulk of the model's operations—grows with the model size, and the efficiencies gained by using 1.58-bit weights scale accordingly. Memory consumption follows a similar trend; while the embedding layer remains full precision, its relative memory proportion decreases as the model grows, making the savings from the 1.58-bit weights even more impactful.

Throughput, which measures how many tokens a model can process in a given amount of time, also sees a massive boost. When tested on two 80GB A100 cards using pipeline parallelism, the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ $70\text{B}$ model could support up to $11$ times the batch size of the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ $70\text{B}$ model. This resulted in a throughput that was $8.9$ times higher. These results highlight that $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ is not only faster for a single user but can also handle much higher demand in production environments.

# The new scaling law: Equivalent model sizes between 1.58-bit and 16-bit models

The study introduces a transformative concept: a new scaling law that defines the relationship between 1.58-bit models and traditional 16-bit (FP16/BF16) models. Because $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ is so much more efficient in terms of latency, memory, and energy, a much larger 1.58-bit model can perform as well as, or better than, a much smaller full-precision model within the same hardware constraints. This creates a new "equivalence" in the field of AI model scaling.

The researchers established several specific points of equivalence based on their experimental data. First, a $13\text{B}$ $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ model is more efficient in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy consumption than a $3\text{B}$ FP16 $\text{LLaMA LLM}$. Second, a $30\text{B}$ $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ model is more efficient than a $7\text{B}$ FP16 $\text{LLaMA LLM}$. Finally, a $70\text{B}$ $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ model is more efficient than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 $\text{LLaMA LLM}$.

The intuition behind this new scaling law is that the radical reduction in computational complexity—moving from floating-point multiplications to integer additions—allows developers to "trade" precision for parameter count. In a resource-constrained environment, instead of deploying a small, high-precision model, one can deploy a significantly larger 1.58-bit model that possesses a much higher capacity for knowledge and reasoning while staying within the same power and memory limits.

# Energy analysis: Savings in arithmetic operations on 7nm chips

To understand the economic and environmental impact of $\text{BitNet b1.58}$, the researchers analyzed the energy consumption of arithmetic operations, specifically focusing on matrix multiplication. Matrix multiplication is the primary computational burden for Large Language Models. In a standard $\text{LLaMA LLM}$, this operation involves both FP16 additions and FP16 multiplications. In contrast, the computation in $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ primarily involves INT8 additions.

The researchers used an energy model to estimate the savings on $7\text{nm}$ chips. They found that $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ saves $71.4$ times the arithmetic operations energy consumption for matrix multiplications compared to the $\text{LLaMA LLM}$ baseline. This is a massive reduction in the energy required to perform the core "thinking" steps of the model. By eliminating the need for complex floating-point multiplications, the 1.58-bit architecture addresses one of the fundamental limits of modern computing: power consumption.

Furthermore, the study observed that the energy efficiency of $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ improves as the model size scales. This is because the $nn.Linear$ components, which benefit from the $71.4\text{x}$ energy savings, make up a larger percentage of the total model as it grows. Conversely, the energy costs of other components (which do not benefit from the 1.58-bit optimization) become a smaller fraction of the total cost. Consequently, the larger the model becomes, the more significant the energy advantages of the $\text{BitNet b1.58}$ architecture become relative to the full-precision baseline.

The Future of 1-bit AI: Edge, MoE, and Hardware

# Enabling 1-bit Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) for reduced inter-chip overhead

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) has emerged as a highly effective architectural strategy for scaling Large Language Models (LLMs). The core intuition behind MoE is to increase the model's capacity by using a sparse activation pattern; instead of passing the input through every single parameter in a dense layer, the model selects a specific subset of "experts" to process the data. This approach allows for a massive increase in the number of total parameters without a proportional increase in the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) required for a forward pass, thereby making it computationally cost-effective.

However, despite its computational efficiency, MoE models face significant deployment hurdles, primarily related to memory consumption and inter-chip communication. Because MoE models contain a vast number of parameters, they often cannot fit on a single accelerator, necessitating their distribution across multiple devices. This distribution creates a bottleneck where activations must be transferred across networks or interconnects, leading to high communication overhead. Furthermore, the sheer size of the weights demands a massive amount of high-speed memory, which can be prohibitively expensive and difficult to scale.

The introduction of 1.58-bit LLMs like BitNet b1.58 provides a direct solution to these MoE-specific challenges. By reducing every weight to a ternary value of $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the memory footprint of the model is drastically minimized. This reduction means that many MoE models that previously required a cluster of devices could potentially be hosted on fewer devices, or even a single chip, eliminating the need for inter-chip communication entirely. Additionally, because the 1.58-bit architecture reduces the precision of the activations, the overhead involved in transferring those activations across a network is also significantly diminished. By addressing the memory and communication bottlenecks, 1.58-bit weights enable the scalability of MoE to new heights.

# Native support for long sequences and KV cache compression

One of the most significant barriers to deploying LLMs for long-context applications is the memory consumption associated with the Key-Value (KV) cache. During inference, the model must store the keys and values of all previous tokens in a sequence to perform attention calculations for the next token. As the sequence length grows, the size of this KV cache expands linearly, eventually consuming all available GPU memory and limiting the maximum context length the model can handle.

BitNet b1.58 offers a path toward native support for longer sequences by addressing the precision of activations. In standard full-precision models, activations are typically stored in 16-bit formats such as $FP16$ or $BF16$. In contrast, the BitNet b1.58 architecture utilizes 8-bit activations. By moving from 16-bit to 8-bit activations, the memory required to store the KV cache is effectively halved for the same amount of hardware resources. This allows the model to support double the context length within the same memory constraints.

Furthermore, the paper suggests that this efficiency can be extended even further. While the current implementation uses 8-bit activations to maintain performance, these activations could potentially be losslessly compressed to 4 bits or even lower in the future. This progression highlights a fundamental shift: by optimizing the bit-width of the internal components of the transformer, we can overcome the physical memory limits that currently restrict how much information a model can "remember" during a single inference pass.

# Deploying LLMs on Edge and Mobile devices: Overcoming memory constraints

The ambition to run high-performance LLMs on edge and mobile devices is often thwarted by the severe limitations of these platforms. Unlike high-end data center GPUs, mobile chips have restricted memory capacities and strict energy budgets. These constraints typically force a compromise, where developers must choose between a very small, less capable model or a large model that cannot run at all due to "Out of Memory" errors.

1.58-bit LLMs provide a breakthrough for edge deployment by simultaneously addressing both memory and energy. Because the weights are ternary ($\{-1, 0, 1\}$), the model's capacity to store parameters is vastly improved compared to $FP16$ baselines. This allows for larger, more capable models to fit into the smaller RAM modules found in smartphones and IoT devices. Simultaneously, the move away from floating-point multiplication toward integer addition significantly reduces the energy consumption of the model.

By lowering the power threshold required to run a large model, BitNet b1.58 enables a new range of applications. High-performance language modeling can move from the cloud to the device, allowing for private, offline, and real-time interactions. This shift is crucial for the adoption of AI in everyday mobile technology, where latency and battery life are just as important as the accuracy of the model's responses.

# CPU-friendly computation and the need for specialized 1-bit hardware (LPUs)

A fundamental shift introduced by BitNet b1.58 is the transition of the core computation paradigm. In traditional LLMs, the primary computational cost stems from floating-point addition and multiplication within matrix multiplications. BitNet b1.58 replaces these with operations that involve almost no multiplications, relying instead on integer addition. This change has profound implications for the types of hardware that can efficiently run these models.

Standard CPUs, which are the primary processors for most edge and mobile devices, are inherently "friendly" to this new paradigm. While GPUs are traditionally favored for their high-throughput floating-point performance, the integer-heavy nature of 1.58-bit models allows CPUs to perform these calculations more efficiently than they would with full-precision weights.
