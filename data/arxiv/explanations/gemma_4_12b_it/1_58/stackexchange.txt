\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Why is the model referred to as '1.58-bit' specifically?
Question:
The paper introduces BitNet b1.58 and states that every parameter takes on ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. However, I am confused by the nomenclature. In standard computing, we usually talk about 1-bit, 2-bit, or 8-bit representations. Where does the specific number '1.58' come from mathematically? Is it a rounded value for some other bit depth?
Answer:
The nomenclature "1.58-bit" refers to the information-theoretic amount of information (entropy) contained in each weight, rather than a standard integer bit-depth used in traditional binary storage.

In computer science, we are accustomed to integer bit depths because hardware is designed to address memory in powers of two. A 1-bit value can represent $2^1 = 2$ distinct states, and a 2-bit value can represent $2^2 = 4$ distinct states. However, the amount of information required to represent a choice from a set of $k$ possibilities is defined mathematically as $\log_2(k)$. 

In the BitNet b1.58 architecture, the authors constrain every weight to a ternary set of values: $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. Because there are exactly $3$ possible states for each parameter, the number of bits required to represent these states is $\log_2(3)$. When you calculate this value, $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58496$. The authors use "1.58-bit" to provide a precise description of the model's information density; it is not a rounded value for a 2-bit representation, but rather a specific mathematical derivation of the ternary constraint.

This distinction is important for the model's computation paradigm. If the model were a true 2-bit model, it would require 4 states, which would increase the complexity of the $\text{BitLinear}$ operations. By using exactly $3$ states, the model stays in a "ternary" regime where weights can be represented as integers, allowing the matrix multiplications to be replaced by integer additions (as the paper notes, "the matrix multiplication of BitNet only involves integer addition"). The 1.58-bit label effectively signals that the model has slightly more expressive power than a binary 1-bit model (which only has $2$ states) but remains significantly more efficient than standard 2-bit or 4-bit quantized models.

### How does the quantization function $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$ ensure the weights don't vanish during training?
Question:
In the Quantization Function section, the paper explains how weights are constrained to $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. I understand that $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of the weight matrix. However, I'm confused about the role of $\gamma$ and $\epsilon$ in the training process. Does $\gamma$ act as a normalization factor to ensure that a significant portion of the weights actually reach the $-1, 0, 1$ boundaries, or does it serve a different purpose in maintaining gradient flow?
Answer:
The quantization function $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$ is designed to ensure that the model maintains a consistent signal despite the extreme constraints of ternary weights. The role of $\gamma$ is indeed to act as a dynamic normalization factor, and its primary purpose is to prevent the weights from "collapsing" into a zero-state during the rounding process.

To understand this intuitively, consider what would happen if we rounded the weights $W$ without the $\gamma$ term. If the weights in a layer were very small—for example, if all $W_{ij}$ were roughly $0.01$—the $\mathrm{Round}$ operation would map every single weight to $0$. Once a weight becomes $0$ and the gradient is also small, the model loses its ability to represent any features, effectively "vanishing" the information in that layer. By dividing by $\gamma$, which is the average absolute value of the weights ($\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$), the formula effectively standardizes the weight matrix. This ensures that the weights are scaled relative to their own magnitude before the rounding operation occurs.

Specifically, $\gamma$ ensures that the weights are pushed toward the boundaries of $\{-1, 0, +1\}$. If the weights are small, $\gamma$ will be small, and dividing by it will "amplify" the weights so that values significantly different from the mean are rounded to $+1$ or $-1$ rather than being collapsed to $0$. Conversely, if the weights are very large, $\gamma$ will be large, and dividing by it "squashes" them into the range where the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function can effectively map them to the ternary values. This creates a self-regulating mechanism where the weights are always expressed in terms of their relative importance.

Regarding your question about gradient flow, $\gamma$ plays a crucial role in maintaining a stable training objective. Because $\gamma$ is calculated based on the current state of the weights, it ensures that the "effective" weights used in the forward pass maintain a consistent scale. While the rounding operation itself is non-differentiable, the scaling factor $\gamma$ prevents the weights from entering a regime where they become so small that they lose their expressive power. In essence, $\gamma$ ensures that the weights remain "large enough" to be represented by the $1.58$-bit system, while the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function ensures they stay within the permitted ternary range. The $\epsilon$ term is a standard numerical stability constant used to prevent division by zero in the event that all weights in a matrix happen to be zero.

### How is matrix multiplication performed using 'almost no multiplication operations'?
Question:
The paper claims that BitNet b1.58 is more cost-effective because matrix multiplication involves only integer addition. Since the weights $\widetilde{W}$ are restricted to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, I can see that multiplying by $1$ or $-1$ is simple. But if the activations $x$ are still 8-bit integers, how does the operation $\sum \widetilde{W}_{ij} x_j$ avoid multiplications entirely? Does the hardware treat the weight as a sign/zero indicator for the activation value?
Answer:
Your intuition is correct: the hardware essentially treats the ternary weights as instructions for how to handle the activation values, rather than as operands for a traditional multiplication circuit.

In a standard floating-point or full-integer matrix multiplication, the operation $y = Wx$ requires a Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) unit. This unit is hardware-intensive because it must be able to handle any combination of values, requiring complex logic to align bits and perform the multiplication of two multi-bit numbers. However, in BitNet b1.58, the weights $\widetilde{W}$ are restricted to the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. When you substitute these specific values into the summation $\sum \widetilde{W}_{ij} x_j$, the mathematical expression simplifies significantly based on the value of the weight:

1. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 1$, the operation becomes $1 \cdot x_j$, which is simply $x_j$.
2. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = -1$, the operation becomes $-1 \cdot x_j$, which is equivalent to $-x_j$.
3. If $\widetilde{W}_{ij} = 0$, the operation becomes $0 \cdot x_j$, which is $0$.

Because of this, the computational "multiplication" step is eliminated. Instead of using a multiplier, the hardware only needs to perform addition and subtraction. From a hardware architecture perspective, a subtraction operation is essentially an addition where the bits of one operand are inverted (via a very simple NOT gate) before being passed into an adder. To handle the $0$ weight, the system simply skips the addition for that specific index or adds zero.

This is why the paper claims that the operation involves "only integer addition." By replacing complex multiplier circuits with simple adders and sign-inverters, the model avoids the most power-hungry part of standard deep learning inference. This structural change is what leads to the 71.4x reduction in arithmetic operations energy consumption mentioned in the paper. Essentially, the weights act as a "control signal" that tells the hardware whether to add the activation, subtract it, or ignore it entirely, completely bypassing the need for a dedicated multiplication circuit.

### What is the specific mechanism of 'feature filtering' provided by the '0' value?
Question:
The authors mention that BitNet b1.58 has stronger modeling capability because of its 'explicit support for feature filtering' due to the inclusion of $0$ in the weights. I'm struggling to understand this conceptually. How does a weight of $0$ fundamentally differ from a weight of $1$ or $-1$ in terms of 'filtering'? Is it analogous to a dropout mechanism, or does it allow the model to learn a sparse representation of important features?
Answer:
The "feature filtering" mechanism in BitNet b1.58 stems from the mathematical property of the zero element in a linear transformation. To understand this, consider the standard operation of a linear layer (or the matrix multiplication within a Transformer), which can be represented as $y = Wx$. In this equation, each element $W_{ij}$ determines how much the $j$-th feature of the input vector $x$ contributes to the $i$-th element of the output vector $y$.

In a strictly 1-bit model (where weights are restricted to $\{-1, 1\}$), every single input feature is forced to have a non-zero impact on every output unit. Because the weight cannot be $0$, the model has no way to "ignore" a specific input dimension; it must find a way to incorporate every piece of information, even if that information is irrelevant noise or redundant data. This forces the model to use the weights purely for orientation (deciding whether a feature should be added or subtracted), but it lacks the ability to "gate" or "mute" features.

By introducing the value $0$ in BitNet b1.58, the weight space expands to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. A weight of $0$ fundamentally differs from $1$ or $-1$ because it represents the total absence of a connection. When $W_{ij} = 0$, the $j$-th input feature is effectively filtered out or "silenced" for that specific output unit. This provides the model with an explicit mechanism to perform feature selection: it can learn to identify which input dimensions are useful for a specific calculation and assign them weights of $1$ or $-1$, while "filtering" out the useless dimensions by assigning them a weight of $0$.

While this might seem similar to dropout, there is a critical conceptual distinction. Dropout is a stochastic regularization technique used during training to prevent co-adaptation by randomly zeroing out activations. In contrast, the $0$ in BitNet b1.58 is a learned, deterministic weight. It is more analogous to "weight pruning" integrated directly into the quantization process. Instead of randomly dropping units, the model's optimization process (via the $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ function) allows it to strategically choose to prune connections that do not contribute significantly to the objective.

From a broader perspective, this allows the model to learn a more efficient internal representation. In high-dimensional spaces, many input features are often noise. By allowing for zero weights, BitNet b1.58 can achieve a better signal-to-noise ratio because it isn't forced to propagate every bit of input signal through every layer. This "filtering" capacity is what the authors identify as a key reason why the 1.58-bit model exhibits stronger modeling capabilities than the original 1-bit version, as it provides the network with the freedom to ignore irrelevant information while maintaining the extreme computational efficiency of ternary arithmetic.

### Why can a 13B BitNet b1.58 model be 'more efficient' than a 3B FP16 LLM?
Question:
This seems counter-intuitive to me. Usually, a 13B parameter model is significantly larger and slower than a 3B parameter model. The paper claims that 13B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient in terms of latency, memory usage, and energy than a 3B FP16 LLM. Can someone explain the 'new scaling law' here? How does the reduction in bit-precision from 16-bit to 1.58-bit overcome the overhead of having over four times as many parameters?
Answer:
The apparent paradox of a 13B parameter BitNet b1.58 model being more efficient than a 3B FP16 model stems from a fundamental shift in the computational and architectural bottlenecks of Large Language Models (LLMs). In standard LLMs, performance is heavily gated by two primary factors: the energy and time cost of floating-point multiplications and the memory bandwidth required to move weights from DRAM to the processor's on-chip memory (SRAM). BitNet b1.58 addresses both of these bottlenecks simultaneously by reducing the bit-precision of the weights to a ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

From a computational perspective, the paper explains that the majority of the cost in vanilla LLMs comes from floating-point addition and multiplication during matrix multiplications. By restricting weights to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, BitNet b1.58 replaces these expensive operations with integer additions. Because integer addition is significantly cheaper in terms of energy and faster in terms of execution than floating-point multiplication, the "cost per parameter" is drastically reduced. This means that even if you have four times as many parameters, the total energy and time required to process those parameters can be lower than a smaller model that still relies on 16-bit floating-point math.

From a memory perspective, the bottleneck is often the speed at which weights can be transferred from DRAM to the on-chip accelerator. Because 1.58-bit weights occupy much less space than 16-bit weights, the memory footprint is significantly smaller from both a capacity and a bandwidth standpoint. This reduction in bit-precision allows the model to "feed" the weights to the processor much more efficiently. Even though a 13B model has more parameters, the fact that each parameter is roughly $10 \times$ smaller in bit-width (1.58 bits vs 16 bits) results in a net reduction in the total amount of data that must be moved during inference.

Finally, the paper notes that these efficiency gains scale favorably with model size. As the model size increases, the proportion of the total computation and memory dedicated to the $\text{nn.Linear}$ layers grows, while the overhead from other components (such as embeddings) remains relatively constant or represents a smaller percentage of the total cost. Since the $\text{nn.Linear}$ layers are the components that benefit most from the ternary weight optimization, the efficiency gap between BitNet and standard FP16 models widens as the models get larger. This creates the "new scaling law" where a larger 1.58-bit model can become more efficient than a significantly smaller full-precision model because the massive reduction in the cost of the operations performed on those parameters completely overshadows the linear increase in the number of parameters.

### Why are activations kept at 8-bit while weights are reduced to 1.58-bit?
Question:
The paper states that BitNet b1.58 is trained with 1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations. If the goal is to maximize efficiency and move toward a 1-bit paradigm, why isn't the activation precision also reduced to 1-bit or 2-bit? Does keeping activations at 8-bit prevent gradient instability during backpropagation, or is there a hardware reason why 8-bit activations are preferred for the 'BitLinear' layers?
Answer:
The distinction between the precision of weights and the precision of activations is a fundamental design choice in BitNet b1.58 that balances the goals of extreme computational efficiency with the requirements of representational capacity and trainability. While the ultimate goal of the 1.58-bit paradigm is to move toward a 1-bit architecture, keeping activations at 8-bit serves several critical purposes related to information theory, hardware optimization, and training dynamics.

From an information-theoretic perspective, activations represent the dynamic "signal" passing through the network. While weights are static parameters that the model learns, activations must capture the nuanced features of the input data. If activations were reduced to 1-bit or 2-bit precision, the network would effectively become a series of hard thresholding operations. This would lead to a massive loss of information at every layer, as the model would struggle to preserve the subtle differences between different tokens or concepts. By maintaining 8-bit activations, the model ensures that the "signal" remains rich enough to be processed by the subsequent layers. The paper notes that the 8-bit activation scheme (specifically scaling them to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token) introduces "negligible effects to the performance," suggesting that 8 bits provide a sufficient "sweet spot" where the model retains its expressive power while still benefiting from reduced memory footprint compared to FP16.

Regarding the hardware and implementation aspects, the authors explicitly mention that 8-bit activations are "convenient and simple for both implementation and system-level optimization." Most modern hardware, such as GPUs and specialized AI accelerators, is already highly optimized for INT8 arithmetic. By using 8-bit activations, the model can leverage existing, highly-tuned kernels for integer math. Moving to a 1-bit or 2-bit activation scheme would require specialized hardware logic or custom kernels that may not yet be as efficient as standard INT8 operations on current-generation hardware. The primary efficiency gain of BitNet b1.58 comes from the fact that 1.58-bit weights allow the matrix multiplication $W x$ to be computed using only integer additions, which is where the massive energy savings occur. Since the weights are the parameters being compressed, reducing them to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ is the primary driver for the new computation paradigm.

Finally, your intuition regarding gradient stability is highly relevant. In many quantization schemes, particularly those trained from scratch, maintaining a higher precision for activations during the forward pass helps ensure that the gradients calculated during backpropagation remain stable and meaningful. If activations were too constrained, the gradient signal could vanish or become extremely noisy, making it difficult for the model to converge. The paper acknowledges this balance by stating that while 8-bit is used currently, further compression of activations to 4 bits or even lower is considered "future work," implying that while it is theoretically possible to lower the activation bit-depth, it requires further investigation into the trade-offs between compression and model convergence.
