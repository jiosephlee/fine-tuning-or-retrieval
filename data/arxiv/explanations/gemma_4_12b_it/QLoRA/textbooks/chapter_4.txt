\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 4: Empirical Analysis: QLoRA vs. Standard Finetuning

# Comparative benchmarks across different architectures

To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the \textsc{QLoRA} method, the researchers conducted extensive comparative benchmarks across a variety of neural network architectures. It is not enough for a new finetuning method to work on a single type of model; it must demonstrate efficacy across different structural designs. Consequently, the study included three primary architectures: Encoder, Encoder-Decoder, and Decoder-only. By testing across these distinct designs, the researchers could confirm that the benefits of 4-bit quantization and Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA) are not architecture-specific but are applicable to the broad range of models used in modern natural language processing.

The experimental setup for these benchmarks involved models scaled up to 3B parameters. To evaluate these models comprehensively, the researchers utilized three well-established benchmarks. For the Encoder architecture, they used RoBERTa-large and evaluated it on the GLUE benchmark. For the Encoder-Decoder architecture, they employed the T5 model and tested it on the Super-NaturalInstructions (TKInstruct) dataset. Finally, for the Decoder-only architecture, they finetuned LLaMA models—which were pre-trained on either Flan v2 or Alpaca—and evaluated them using the 5-shot MMLU (Massively Multitask Language Understanding) benchmark.

These diverse benchmarks allow for a high-fidelity comparison between \textsc{QLoRA} and standard 16-bit finetuning. By spanning different tasks—ranging from general language understanding to specific instruction following—the researchers were able to build a comprehensive evidence base. This multi-architecture approach serves to prove that \textsc{QLoRA} is a versatile tool capable of handling various model types while maintaining high performance.

# The importance of LoRA adapter placement

A critical finding in the study involves the specific placement of LoRA adapters within the neural network. In many standard implementations of LoRA, adapters are only applied to the query and value attention projection matrices. However, the researchers discovered that this standard practice is insufficient for replicating the performance of a fully finetuned 16-bit model when working with large base models. Specifically, they observed that while query and value projections are important, they do not capture enough of the necessary weight updates to match the capacity of full finetuning.

To address this, the researchers analyzed how the number of adapters affects performance. They found that the most critical hyperparameter for matching 16-bit full finetuning performance is the total number of LoRA adapters used. The data revealed that LoRA adapters must be applied to all linear transformer block layers rather than just a select few. By spreading the adapters across all linear layers, the model is better able to approximate the comprehensive updates that occur during full 16-bit finetuning.

Furthermore, the researchers investigated other hyperparameters, such as the projection dimension, denoted as $r$. Interestingly, they found that the specific value of $r$ did not significantly affect the final performance of the model. This highlights a key intuition: it is more important to have adapters in the correct places (all linear layers) than it is to have a very high-dimensional projection. This finding is crucial because it allows users to maintain high performance while keeping the number of trainable parameters relatively low, which is the primary goal of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning (PEFT).

# Hyperparameter sensitivity: Learning rates, batch sizes, and projection dimensions

When comparing \textsc{QLoRA} to full-finetuning baselines, it is essential to ensure that the baselines are not "undertuned." The researchers performed a rigorous hyperparameter search to ensure a fair comparison. They explored a range of learning rates from $1e^{-6}$ to $5e^{-5}$ and batch sizes ranging from $8$ to $128$. This search was necessary because default hyperparameters for fully finetuned models often do not represent their peak potential. By finding robust baselines, the researchers ensured that the comparison between \textsc{QLoRA} and standard finetuning was as accurate as possible.

The study also looked at how these hyperparameters need to scale as the model size increases. While the hyperparameters found for the 7B LLaMA model generally worked well across different scales, the researchers noted specific adjustments required for larger models. For the 33B and 65B parameter models, they found that they needed to halve the learning rate and double the batch size compared to the settings used for the 7B model. 

These observations provide practical guidance for researchers looking to implement \textsc{QLoRA}. It demonstrates that while the method is robust, successful finetuning still requires careful attention to the relationship between model scale and optimization parameters. By identifying these sensitivities, the paper provides a clear roadmap for achieving state-of-the-art results on models that would otherwise be too large to train.

# Evaluating NF4 precision: Comparing NF4 against FP4 and Int4

A core innovation of \textsc{QLoRA} is the 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type. Because NF4 is designed to be information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights, the researchers needed to prove that this theoretical advantage translated into empirical gains. They conducted a series of experiments across various models, including OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM, and Pythia, ranging in size from 125M to 13B parameters. They compared the performance of NF4 against two other common 4-bit types: 4-bit Floating Point (FP4) and 4-bit Integers (Int4).

The results of these evaluations were significant. The researchers found that NF4 consistently improved performance over both FP4 and Int4 in terms of language modeling and zero-shot accuracy. This confirms that the specific way weights are discretized matters immensely; using a data type that aligns with the actual distribution of weights in a pretrained model yields much better results than using a generic floating-point or integer representation.

Additionally, the researchers evaluated the impact of Double Quantization (DQ) on this precision. They found that they could apply Double Quantization to reduce the memory footprint even further without any observed degradation in performance. This is a vital finding for the efficiency of \textsc{QLoRA}, as it shows that the memory savings gained from quantizing the quantization constants do not come at the cost of model accuracy.

# Performance recovery: Demonstrating that 4-bit QLoRA matches 16-bit performance

The ultimate goal of the \textsc{QLoRA} method is to prove that one can train a 4-bit quantized model and achieve the same results as a 16-bit model. To test this, the researchers conducted two major types of experiments. The first involved smaller models (125M to 3B parameters), such as RoBERTa and T5, evaluated on the GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions datasets. In both of these cases, the researchers observed that 16-bit, 8-bit, and 4-bit adapter methods all successfully replicated the performance of the fully finetuned 16-bit baseline.

The second experiment targeted larger models where full 16-bit finetuning is computationally prohibitive. They finetuned LLaMA models ranging from 7B to 65B parameters on the Alpaca and FLAN v2 datasets and evaluated them on the MMLU benchmark. The results were consistent: the 4-bit \textsc{QLoRA} method fully recovered the performance of the 16-bit LoRA baseline. Even at these massive scales, the 4-bit precision was sufficient to capture the necessary information for high-quality finetuning.

These results provide the "compelling evidence" the authors sought. They demonstrate that the performance lost during the initial quantization of the base model can be almost entirely recovered by performing adapter finetuning. This means that \textsc{QLoRA} is not a compromise; it is a high-fidelity alternative that allows researchers to train massive models on consumer-grade hardware while maintaining the predictive power of full 16-bit precision.
