\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why is NF4 'information-theoretically optimal' for normally distributed weights?
Question:
The paper claims that NF4 is an 'information-theoretically optimal' data type for weights that follow a zero-centered normal distribution. I'm trying to understand the underlying math here. Does 'optimal' in this context mean it minimizes the quantization error (mean squared error) for a given bit-width, or does it refer to maximizing the entropy of the distribution? Why does the quantile approach specifically achieve this optimality compared to standard linear quantization?
Answer:
In the context of the \textsc{QLoRA} paper, the term "information-theoretically optimal" refers to the property that each quantization bin in the data type captures an equal amount of probability mass from the source distribution. This is fundamentally related to maximizing the entropy of the discrete distribution created by quantization. When a data type is designed such that every bin has an equal number of values assigned to it from the input tensor, it ensures that no "information" is wasted. For example, if you have a high-density region of data (like the center of a normal distribution) and a low-density region (the tails), a non-optimal data type might allocate many bins to the sparse tails where they are rarely used, while providing insufficient resolution for the dense center. By ensuring each bin has an equal number of values, the \textsc{NF4} data type maximizes the utility of the available bit-width.

The reason the quantile approach specifically achieves this optimality—and why it outperforms standard linear quantization for normally distributed weights—lies in the shape of the distribution. Standard linear quantization uses evenly spaced bins across the range of the data. However, weights in neural networks typically follow a zero-centered normal distribution $N(0, \sigma^2)$. In such a distribution, the majority of the values are clustered very closely around zero, while the values become increasingly sparse as you move toward the extremes. Linear quantization "wastes" bits by providing high-precision bins for the tails where data is rare, while "starving" the center of the distribution where most of the weight values reside. 

Quantile quantization solves this by using the empirical cumulative distribution function to determine the bin boundaries. Instead of equal distances between bins, it uses equal probabilities. This means the bins are packed much more densely near zero and become wider as they move toward the tails. This effectively "stretches" the dense part of the normal distribution to occupy more of the available discrete states.

The \textsc{NF4} data type specifically handles the fact that the standard deviation $\sigma$ of these weights can vary across different layers or tensors. The paper explains that because these weights are approximately normal, they can be transformed into a single fixed distribution by scaling them. By first normalizing the weight tensor into a fixed range of $[-1, 1]$ via absolute maximum rescaling, the authors can then apply a fixed set of quantiles derived from a theoretical $N(0, 1)$ distribution. Specifically, they estimate $2^k$ values $q_i$ as:

$$q_i  = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right)$$

where $Q_X(\cdot)$ is the quantile function of the standard normal distribution. By doing this, the data type's bin structure perfectly mirrors the shape of the weights. While this approach ultimately leads to a lower mean squared error (MSE) because it captures the most important values with higher precision, the "optimality" claim specifically refers to this efficient distribution of probability mass across the available bits.
