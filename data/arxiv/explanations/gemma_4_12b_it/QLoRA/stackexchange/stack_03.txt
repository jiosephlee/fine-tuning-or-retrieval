\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How do gradients flow back to LoRA weights if the base model weights are frozen?
Question:
The paper states that QLoRA backpropagates gradients through a 'frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model' into the Low-Rank Adapters. If the base weights $\mathbf{W}$ are frozen, then by definition, the gradient $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ is not updated. I am struggling to understand how the chain rule allows the gradient to 'pass through' these frozen weights to reach the adapter weights $\mathbf{L}_1$ and $\mathbf{L}_2$. Does the dequantization step play a role in how the derivative is calculated?
Answer:
To understand how gradients flow through frozen weights in QLoRA, it is helpful to distinguish between the **update** of a parameter and the **propagation** of a gradient through that parameter.

In standard backpropagation, the chain rule dictates how the error (loss) $E$ is distributed back through the network. When we compute the gradient of the loss with respect to a specific set of weights $\mathbf{L}_i$, we are essentially asking: "How much would the total loss change if I slightly changed these weights?" To answer this, the chain rule requires us to multiply the incoming gradient from the output by the partial derivative of the current layer's output with respect to its inputs.

Consider the forward pass equation provided in the paper for a single linear layer:
$$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$$

In this context, $\mathbf{W}$ represents the frozen, 4-bit quantized weights of the base model, and $\mathbf{L}_1, \mathbf{L}_2$ are the trainable LoRA adapters. During the backward pass, the goal is to find $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$. By the chain rule, this calculation depends on the gradient of the loss with respect to the input $\mathbf{X}$. Because the output $\mathbf{Y}$ is a result of the transformation $\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2)$, the gradient with respect to the input $\mathbf{X}$ is:
$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{X}} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{Y}} (\mathbf{W} + \mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2)^T$$

Even though $\mathbf{W}$ is "frozen" (meaning we do not perform the update $\mathbf{W} \leftarrow \mathbf{W} - \eta \frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$), the value of $\mathbf{W}$ is still a required mathematical constant in the expression for $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{X}}$. To calculate the gradients for the LoRA adapters in the current layer, we must "pass" the gradient back through the weight matrix $\mathbf{W}$ to reach the previous layers of the network. If we were to treat $\mathbf{W}$ as non-existent during backpropagation, the gradient would effectively "stop" at the current layer and never reach the preceding weights or inputs.

The dequantization step is critical here because of the 4-bit storage. As the paper explains, QLoRA stores the weights in a 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) format to save memory. However, to ensure that the gradient calculation remains accurate and matches the behavior of a 16-bit model, the weights must be dequantized to a higher precision (BFloat16) during the backward pass. Specifically, the paper notes that the calculation of $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ "proceeds via... dequantization from storage $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ to computation data type $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$." 

In summary, the frozen weights act like a fixed physical bridge in a circuit: while the bridge itself isn't changing shape or moving, it is still the structure that allows the current to flow from one side to the other. The dequantization ensures that the "bridge" used during the backward pass has the same shape and precision as the one used during the forward pass, allowing the gradient to reach the LoRA weights accurately.
