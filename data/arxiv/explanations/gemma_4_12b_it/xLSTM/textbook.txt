\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Foundations: The Legacy and Evolution of LSTMs

# The Vanishing Gradient Problem in standard RNNs

To understand the necessity of the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture, one must first understand the fundamental challenges of standard Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). RNNs were designed to process sequential data by maintaining a hidden state that captures information from previous time steps. However, a significant mathematical hurdle emerged during the training of these models: the vanishing gradient problem.

The vanishing gradient problem occurs during the process of backpropagation through time. When an RNN is tasked with learning dependencies over long sequences, the gradients used to update the network's weights are multiplied repeatedly by the weight matrices at each time step. If these weights are small, the gradient can shrink exponentially as it propagates backward toward the earlier parts of the sequence. Consequently, the model "forgets" the beginning of the sequence, making it nearly impossible to learn long-range dependencies or maintain context over extended periods.

This limitation effectively capped the utility of standard RNNs. While they could handle short-term correlations, they struggled with complex tasks where the beginning of a sentence or a long series of actions influenced the outcome much later. This bottleneck necessitated a more robust architecture capable of preserving information across many steps without the gradient signal fading into insignificance.

# The LSTM Breakthrough: Constant Error Carousel and Gating Mechanisms

The LSTM was introduced in the 1990s specifically to overcome the vanishing gradient problem by introducing two central innovations: the constant error carousel and gating mechanisms. The constant error carousel is the conceptual heart of the LSTM. It functions as an additive update of the cell state, denoted as $c_{t-1}$, which is moderated by sigmoid gates. By using addition rather than just repeated multiplication, the network can maintain a "running total" of information that persists through time.

Complementing this is the concept of gating. Gates act as regulators that decide how much information should be allowed to flow into, stay in, or leave the memory cell. The input gate and the forget gate work together to control the update of the cell state. Specifically, they determine which parts of the previous state to discard and which new pieces of information from the current input should be stored. The output gate then decides which parts of the internal memory are relevant to produce the hidden state, which is the actual output of the cell at that moment.

The intuition behind these components is that they allow the network to selectively "write" to and "read" from its memory. The cell state acts as a long-term memory conveyor belt, while the gates provide the mechanism to manipulate that memory at each step. This design ensures that information can travel along the conveyor belt for many time steps without being drastically altered or lost, effectively bypassing the vanishing gradient issue.

# Mathematical formulation of the standard LSTM (Input, Forget, and Output gates)

The standard LSTM architecture can be mathematically described by a set of update rules that govern the evolution of the cell state $c_t$ and the hidden state $h_t$ at each time step $t$. The fundamental equations for the memory cell are:

$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$
$h_t = o_t \psi(c_t)$

In these equations, $c_t$ represents the cell state, $h_t$ is the hidden state, and $f_t$, $i_t$, and $o_t$ are the forget, input, and output gates, respectively. The function $\psi$ is used to normalize or "squash" the cell state, as the cell state could otherwise become unbounded. Typically, $\psi$ and the cell input function $\varphi$ are $\tanh$ activations, while the gates are governed by the sigmoid function, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + \exp(-x))$.

The internal components of these gates and the cell input are calculated as follows:

$z_t = \varphi(\tilde{z}_t), \quad \tilde{z}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_z \mathbf{x}_t + r_z h_{t-1} + b_z$
$i_t = \sigma(\tilde{i}_t), \quad \tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_i \mathbf{x}_t + r_i h_{t-1} + b_i$
$f_t = \sigma(\tilde{f}_t), \quad \tilde{f}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_f \mathbf{x}_t + r_f h_{t-1} + b_f$
$o_t = \sigma(\tilde{o}_t), \quad \tilde{o}_t = \mathbf{w}^\top_o \mathbf{x}_t + r_o h_{t-1} + b_o$

In this formulation, $\mathbf{x}_t$ is the input at time $t$. The terms $\mathbf{w}_z, \mathbf{w}_i, \mathbf{w}_f,$ and $\mathbf{w}_o$ are weight vectors representing the connections between the input and the respective gates and cell input. The terms $r_z, r_i, r_f,$ and $r_o$ are recurrent weights between the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ and the current gates/inputs. Finally, $b_z, b_i, b_f,$ and $b_o$ are the respective bias terms.

# Success stories of LSTMs in sequence modeling and reinforcement learning

Because of their ability to extract semantic information and store it in memory cells, LSTMs have achieved significant success across a wide variety of domains. In the realm of sequence modeling, they have been used to generate text, produce handwritten characters, and perform sequence-to-sequence translation. They have also been applied to evaluating computer programs, generating image captions, and producing source code. Beyond linguistics, LSTMs have been utilized in scientific modeling, such as rainfall-runoff modeling and hydrological models for flood warnings.

In the field of reinforcement learning, LSTMs have emerged as the best-performing sequence models for complex environments. For example, they were core components of the AlphaStar model for StarCraft II and the OpenAI Five model for Dota 2. They have even been used to model the magnetic controller for nuclear fusion. These successes stem from the LSTM's unique ability to learn abstractions; the models are adept at extracting linguistic information, such as "sentiment neurons" or "number and syntax neurons," and storing those abstractions within their memory cells to inform future predictions.

# The Rise of Transformers: Why parallelizable self-attention outpaced LSTMs

Despite their historical dominance, LSTMs eventually faced a significant challenge with the advent of Transformer technology in 2017. The primary reason for this shift was the introduction of parallelizable self-attention. Because LSTMs rely on hidden-hidden connections between states from one time step to the next, they enforce sequential processing, which makes them difficult to scale efficiently on modern hardware. Transformers, by contrast, allow for much higher degrees of parallelization, allowing them to outpace LSTMs as models scaled to billions of parameters.

Furthermore, LSTMs possess three specific limitations that became apparent as the demands of Large Language Models (LLMs) increased. First is the inability to revise storage decisions; for instance, in a nearest neighbor search, an LSTM struggles to update a stored value when a more similar vector appears later in a sequence. Second is a limited storage capacity, as information must be compressed into scalar cell states, which can hinder the prediction of rare tokens. Third is the lack of parallelizability due to the memory mixing provided by the recurrent connections. These three constraints paved the way for Transformers to become the standard for modern language modeling, leading to the current research into architectures like xLSTM that attempt to remediate these specific flaws.

Identifying the Bottlenecks: Why LSTMs Struggle to Scale

# Inability to revise storage decisions: The Nearest Neighbor Search problem

To understand why the original Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) architecture struggles to scale to the level of modern Large Language Models (LLMs), we must first examine its fundamental logic of information storage. The LSTM was designed around the concept of a constant error carousel, where a cell state is updated additively. While this was a breakthrough for preventing the vanishing gradient problem, it created a specific behavioral limitation: the model finds it difficult to revise its internal storage decisions once they have been made. 

A clear way to visualize this limitation is through the Nearest Neighbor Search problem. In this task, the model is provided with a reference vector and must scan a sequence of vectors to identify the one that is most similar to the reference. Once the most similar vector is found, the model must provide the value associated with that specific vector at the end of the sequence. For an LSTM, this is a difficult task because the architecture is designed to accumulate and modulate information over time rather than dynamically "overwriting" a previous entry with a better one.

Because the LSTM processes the sequence linearly and updates its cell state incrementally, it often struggles to "change its mind." If the model encounters a vector that is somewhat similar to the reference early in the sequence, the cell state updates to reflect that finding. When it later encounters a vector that is even more similar, the LSTM lacks a robust mechanism to effectively discard the previous "best" find in favor of the new one. This inability to revise storage decisions limits the model's ability to perform precise retrieval tasks, a bottleneck that the xLSTM architecture seeks to remedy by introducing exponential gating.

# Limited storage capacity: The Rare Token Prediction problem and scalar compression

A second major bottleneck is the physical limitation of the storage unit itself. In a standard LSTM, information must be compressed into a scalar cell state $c_t$. While this scalar representation allows for a continuous flow of information, it acts as a massive information bottleneck. When a model is forced to squeeze a vast amount of semantic information into a limited number of scalar values, the "resolution" of that memory becomes very low.

This limitation is most evident in the Rare Token Prediction problem. In language modeling, different tokens have different frequencies; common words appear often, while rare words appear infrequently. Because the LSTM has such limited storage capacity, it performs significantly worse on rare tokens. This is because the model simply does not have enough "space" in its scalar cell state to store the specific nuances and contexts required to predict a word that it doesn't see very often. The information regarding these rare tokens is essentially "washed out" or overwritten by more frequent information.

The xLSTM addresses this by moving away from the scalar memory cell. By increasing the storage capacity from a scalar $c \in \mathbb{R}$ to a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, the model can store much more complex relationships. In this matrix-based approach, the model can store pairs of keys and values, allowing for a much higher "resolution" of memory. This allows the model to maintain distinct information for rare tokens without them being compressed into the same limited scalar space that characterizes the original LSTM.

# The sequential processing bottleneck: Lack of parallelizability in memory mixing

The third and perhaps most significant barrier to scaling LSTMs to the scale of modern LLMs is the lack of parallelizability. The very mechanism that gives LSTMs their power—memory mixing—is also their greatest computational weakness. Memory mixing refers to the hidden-hidden connections between states at time step $t-1$ and time step $t$. Because the hidden state $h_t$ depends directly on the hidden state $h_{t-1}$, the model is forced to process the sequence one step at a time.

This sequential nature is the primary reason the Transformer architecture, with its parallelizable self-attention, became the standard for Large Language Models. In a Transformer, the relationship between all tokens in a sequence can be computed simultaneously because the model does not rely on a hidden state that must be passed linearly from one step to the next. In contrast, an LSTM must complete the computation for the first word before it can begin the computation for the second word. This creates a "wall" for scaling: as sequences get longer and models get larger, the time required to compute these sequential steps grows linearly, making it much harder to utilize the massive parallel processing power of modern GPUs.

To overcome this, the xLSTM introduces the mLSTM variant. The mLSTM is designed to be fully parallelizable by abandoning the hidden-hidden connections (memory mixing) while still maintaining a matrix memory structure. By removing the requirement that the current state must be derived from the immediate previous state, the mLSTM allows for the same type of parallel computation that made Transformers so successful, while retaining the recurrent advantages of the LSTM family.

# Conceptual comparison: LSTM memory vs. Transformer attention

When comparing these architectures, it is helpful to view the difference between LSTM memory and Transformer attention as a difference in how the "past" is accessed. In the original LSTM, the past is summarized into a compressed, running summary represented by the cell state $c_t$. The update rule $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ shows that the new state is a gated combination of the old state and the new input. This is an additive, "forget-and-update" mechanism. The model's ability to remember something depends on how well the gates $f_t$ and $i_t$ manage to preserve that information through every single time step in the sequence.

Transformer attention, by contrast, does not rely on a running summary. Instead, it allows the model to look back at every previous token in the sequence directly. This is why Transformers have such high memory capacity; they don't have to "compress" the past into a single state; they can "attend" to any part of the past at any time. However, this comes at a quadratic computational cost relative to the sequence length, as every token must be compared to every other token.

The xLSTM attempts to find a middle ground. By using matrix memory in the mLSTM, it mimics the high storage capacity of the Transformer (allowing for retrieval of specific keys and values) but maintains a linear complexity with respect to sequence length. By using exponential gating and memory mixing in the sLSTM, it retains the "state tracking" capabilities of the original LSTM—the ability to track entities and logic over long narratives—which Transformers and some State Space Models struggle with. In essence, the xLSTM is an attempt to combine the high-capacity retrieval of the Transformer with the expressive state-tracking of the LSTM, while solving the sequential bottleneck through parallelizable matrix operations.

# The xLSTM Innovation: Exponential Gating and Stabilization

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network was designed to overcome the vanishing gradient problem by utilizing a "constant error carousel," which is essentially an additive update mechanism for a cell state. In the standard LSTM architecture, the cell state $c_t$ is updated at each time step $t$ based on the previous state $c_{t-1}$, a forget gate $f_t$, an input gate $i_t$, and a cell input $z_t$. This is expressed by the fundamental update rule:
$c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$.
While this mechanism has been highly successful, the original LSTM has several inherent limitations when scaled to the size of modern Large Language Models. One of the most significant limitations is the inability to revise storage decisions. In tasks like Nearest Neighbor Search—where a model must scan a sequence and provide the value associated with the most similar vector found so far—the standard LSTM struggles to overwrite or revise a previously stored value when a better match appears later in the sequence. To address this, the xLSTM introduces exponential gating as a core innovation.

# Introduction to Exponential Gating

In the standard LSTM, the gates $i_t$ and $f_t$ are typically governed by sigmoid activation functions, which squash the values into a range between 0 and 1. While this provides a clear mechanism for "forgetting" or "remembering," it limits the dynamic range and flexibility of the update rule. The xLSTM proposes a modification where the input and forget gates can utilize exponential activation functions. 

By moving to exponential gating, the model changes how it modulates the flow of information into and out of the memory. Specifically, the input gate $i_t$ is defined as $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, where $\tilde{i}_t$ is the pre-activation value. The forget gate $f_t$ can also be defined using an exponential function $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$ or remain as a sigmoid $\sigma(\tilde{f}_t)$. This shift is designed to enrich the LSTM family by providing a more expressive way to handle memory updates. The goal is to allow the network to make more nuanced decisions about how much of the previous state should be retained and how much of the new input should be incorporated, specifically aiming to remediate the "revision" problem identified in standard LSTMs.

# The Normalizer State: Summing products of input and future forget gates

A significant challenge arises when using exponential functions for gating: because the exponential function grows very rapidly, the values in the cell state $c_t$ can quickly become extremely large, potentially leading to numerical instability or overflow. To counteract this, the xLSTM introduces a "normalizer state," denoted as $n_t$.

The normalizer state is updated at each time step using a rule that mirrors the cell state update but focuses on the gate values themselves. The formula for the normalizer state is:
$n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$.
This state effectively keeps a running sum of the product of the input gate and all previous forget gates. By maintaining this history, the model tracks the cumulative "strength" or "gain" applied to the memory. To ensure the hidden state remains within a manageable range, the xLSTM modifies the calculation of the hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$. Instead of simply squashing the cell state with a $\tanh$ function, the model divides the cell state by the normalizer state:
$\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$.
This division serves as a normalization step that balances the exponential growth of the cell state, allowing the model to benefit from the expressive power of exponential gating without suffering from numerical explosion.

# Stabilization Techniques: Preventing overflows using log-space stabilization ($m_t$ state)

Even with a normalizer state, calculating $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ directly can still lead to floating-point overflows if the pre-activation values are large. To ensure the model remains numerically stable during training and inference, the xLSTM employs a stabilization technique using an auxiliary state $m_t$, often referred to as a stabilizer state.

The stabilizer state $m_t$ tracks the maximum log-scale value of the gates to ensure that the arguments passed to the exponential functions stay within a safe range. It is updated as follows:
$m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$.
Using this state, the gates are re-formulated into stabilized versions, $i'_t$ and $f'_t$. The stabilized input gate is calculated as:
$i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$.
The stabilized forget gate is calculated as:
$f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$.
By subtracting $m_t$ (the maximum value) from the log-values before exponentiating, the authors ensure that the resulting values do not exceed 1. This is a common trick in machine learning (similar to the log-sum-exp trick) to maintain numerical precision while preserving the underlying mathematical relationship of the operations.

# How exponential gating enables memory revision

The primary intuition behind using exponential gating is to solve the "storage revision" problem. In a standard LSTM, the update rule $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$ with sigmoid gates acts somewhat like a moving average or a weighted sum where the weights are strictly bounded between 0 and 1. This makes it difficult for the model to "overwrite" a high-confidence memory with a new, even more relevant piece of information because the influence of the old state is always preserved to some degree unless the forget gate is nearly zero.

Exponential gating changes the dynamics of this update. Because the gates can take on a wider range of values, the model gains the ability to more aggressively revise its internal storage. In the context of the Nearest Neighbor Search problem, if the model encounters a vector that is much more similar to the query than the one it currently has stored in its memory, the exponential gating allows it to "re-write" that memory cell more effectively. The combination of exponential gates and the normalizer state allows the xLSTM to behave more like a dynamic memory that can be updated and revised as new, more relevant information arrives in the sequence.

# Mathematical proofs of derivative preservation in stabilized gates

A critical requirement for any architectural modification is ensuring that the change does not inadvertently break the gradient flow during backpropagation. The authors demonstrate that the stabilization technique used for the gates does not change the actual output of the network, nor does it alter the derivatives of the loss function with respect to the model parameters.

The logic follows from the fact that the stabilizer state $m_t$ is essentially a constant with respect to the parameters during a single forward pass calculation of a specific gate. When we replace $i_t$ with $i'_t$ and $f_t$ with $f'_t$, we are essentially performing a transformation that factors out a common term. Since the final hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$ involves a ratio (the cell state divided by the normalizer state), any constant factor introduced by the stabilization (like $\exp(m_t)$) appears in both the numerator and the denominator. Consequently, these factors cancel each other out in the final output. Because the output remains mathematically identical to the non-stabilized version, the gradient of the loss with respect to the weights $\mathbf{w}$ and $r$ remains unchanged, ensuring that the model can be trained using standard optimization techniques without any modification to the gradient calculation.

New Memory Structures: sLSTM and mLSTM

# sLSTM: Scalar memory, scalar updates, and memory mixing

The sLSTM is one of the two primary innovations introduced in the xLSTM architecture to address the inherent limitations of the original Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network. One of the most significant weaknesses of the traditional LSTM is its inability to revise storage decisions. This occurs because the standard architecture struggles to update a stored value when a more similar or relevant piece of information is encountered later in a sequence. The sLSTM addresses this by introducing exponential gating paired with a unique normalization mechanism.

In the sLSTM, the memory cell remains a scalar value $c_t$, but the way this memory is updated and utilized is fundamentally altered. The cell state update follows the rule $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$. Here, $z_t$ represents the cell input, and $i_t$ and $f_t$ are the input and forget gates, respectively. To enable the model to "revise" its storage, the input and forget gates are permitted to use exponential activation functions. Because exponential functions can lead to extremely large values that cause numerical overflows, the sLSTM employs a stabilization state $m_t$. This stabilizer is defined as $m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$, which allows the gates to be reformulated as $i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$ and $f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$. This mathematical transformation ensures that the network remains numerically stable while benefiting from the expressive power of exponential gating.

A critical component of the sLSTM is the introduction of a "normalizer state" $n_t$. In standard LSTMs, the cell state $c_t$ can become unbounded, necessitating a squashing function like $\tanh$. The sLSTM instead tracks the history of the gates through the normalizer state, defined by the update rule $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. The hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$ is then calculated as the ratio of the cell state to the normalizer state: $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$. This normalization ensures that the memory remains scaled correctly regardless of how many times the cell state has been updated. The final hidden state $h_t$ is produced by applying the output gate $o_t$ to this normalized state, such that $h_t = o_t \tilde{h}_t$.

Furthermore, the sLSTM supports "memory mixing," which is the ability to share information across multiple memory cells via recurrent connections. When the sLSTM utilizes multiple cells, the hidden state vector $\mathbf{h}$ influences the inputs for the cell input $\mathbf{z}$ and the gates $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{f}, \mathbf{o}$ through recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{z}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{i}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{f}}, \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{o}}$. This allows the model to learn complex dependencies and track states across different memory units.

# Multi-head mechanisms and memory mixing across cells vs. heads

The xLSTM architecture introduces a sophisticated distinction between how memory cells are organized and how "heads" are utilized. This distinction is vital for understanding how the model balances expressivity with computational structure. In the sLSTM variant, the architecture can be expanded to include multiple memory cells. When these cells are used, the model employs memory mixing, meaning there are hidden-to-hidden connections that allow information to flow between the hidden states of one time step to the next across these cells.

The paper introduces the concept of "heads" for the sLSTM as a way to increase the model's capacity while managing the complexity of memory mixing. Specifically, the sLSTM can be configured to have multiple heads, but with a strict rule: memory mixing occurs across the cells within each individual head, but no memory mixing occurs across the heads themselves. This means that each head operates as a somewhat independent unit of memory, yet each unit is internally interconnected. This setup allows the model to specialize different heads for different types of information while maintaining the state-tracking capabilities provided by the recurrent connections within each head.

By contrast, when moving to the mLSTM variant, the architecture treats multiple heads and multiple cells as equivalent. Because the mLSTM lacks the hidden-hidden connections (memory mixing) that define the sLSTM, there is no internal recurrence to "mix" across cells in the same way. Therefore, the distinction between "cells" and "heads" collapses; they both serve as independent, parallelizable components of the memory structure. This architectural choice is what allows the mLSTM to be fully parallelizable, as it removes the sequential bottleneck inherent in the sLSTM's memory mixing.

# mLSTM: Matrix memory and Query/Key/Value (QKV) projections

To overcome the limited storage capacity of scalar memory—where information must be compressed into a single number—the xLSTM introduces the mLSTM. The mLSTM replaces the scalar memory cell $c$ with a matrix memory $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This shift from a scalar to a matrix allows the model to store and retrieve information using a framework similar to the Query, Key, and Value (QKV) system found in Transformers.

In the mLSTM, at each time step $t$, the model generates three primary components from the input $\mathbf{x}_t$: the query $\mathbf{q}_t$, the key $\mathbf{k}_t$, and the value $\mathbf{v}_t$. These are produced through linear projections:
$\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{W}_q \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_q$
$\mathbf{k}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathbf{W}_k \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_k$
$\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{W}_v \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}_v$

The matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ acts as a repository of these key-value pairs. Instead of simply adding a new value to a scalar sum, the mLSTM performs a matrix update that stores the relationship between the key and the value. The retrieval process is then performed via matrix multiplication. To find the relevant information from the past, the model multiplies the current query $\mathbf{q}_t$ by the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$. To ensure stability, the mLSTM also utilizes a normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$, which is a weighted sum of the key vectors. The retrieved hidden state $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$ is calculated as:
$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max \{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}$

The use of matrix memory significantly boosts the model's ability to handle "Rare Token Prediction," as it provides a much larger space to store distinct pieces of information compared to the compressed scalar cells of the standard LSTM or sLSTM.

# The Covariance Update Rule: Optimal separability and Fast Weight Programmers

The mathematical foundation for updating the mLSTM's matrix memory is the covariance update rule. This rule is designed to store a pair of vectors—a key $\mathbf{k}_t$ and a value $\mathbf{v}_t$—into the matrix $\mathbf{C}$. The update is formulated as:
$\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$

This specific rule is grounded in the theory of Bidirectional Associative Memories (BAMs). It is considered optimal for achieving maximal separability of retrieved binary vectors, which is mathematically equivalent to maximizing the signal-to-noise ratio during retrieval. By using the outer product $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, the model creates a direct mapping between the key and the value within the matrix.

Furthermore, the paper draws a conceptual link between this update rule and "Fast Weight Programmers." In those systems, memory is updated using a constant decay rate and a constant learning rate. The xLSTM integrates these ideas into the LSTM framework by assigning these roles to the gates: the forget gate $f_t$ acts as the decay rate (controlling how much of the previous memory $\mathbf{C}_{t-1}$ is retained), and the input gate $i_t$ acts as the learning rate (controlling how much of the new key-value pair $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ is added). This allows the model to dynamically decide which information is worth "learning" into its matrix memory and which information should be "forgotten."

# Comparison of storage capacities: Scalar vs. Matrix representations

The fundamental difference between the sLSTM and the mLSTM lies in how they manage the trade-off between storage capacity and computational complexity. The sLSTM utilizes a scalar memory cell, which is highly efficient but suffers from "limited storage capacities." Because all information must be compressed into a single scalar value, the sLSTM struggles with tasks like "Rare Token Prediction," where the model must distinguish between many different, infrequent tokens. However, the sLSTM excels at "state tracking" because its memory mixing (hidden-to-hidden connections) allows it to maintain a sequential narrative, making it more expressive than models that lack such mixing.

In contrast, the mLSTM provides a massive increase in storage capacity by utilizing a matrix $\mathbf{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$. This matrix memory allows the model to store pairs of vectors, making it much more capable of memorizing large vocabularies and complex associations. While the mLSTM's matrix operations are computationally more expensive than the sLSTM's scalar additions, they do not require additional parameters—the matrix $\mathbf{C}$ is part of the dynamic state, not the weights of the network.

Ultimately, the xLSTM architecture balances these two structures. The sLSTM provides the "memory mixing" necessary for complex state tracking (like evaluating code or tracking entities in a story), while the mLSTM provides the "matrix memory" necessary for high-capacity information retrieval. By combining these two variants with exponential gating, xLSTM overcomes the traditional limitations of LSTMs while remaining competitive with state-of-the-art Transformers and State Space Models.

Architectural Design: Residual Stacking and Parallelization

# xLSTM Blocks: Post-up-projection vs. Pre-up-projection architectures

To build a functional Large Language Model (LLM) using the xLSTM framework, the individual memory units must be integrated into structured modules known as xLSTM blocks. These blocks are designed to non-linearly summarize the history of a sequence in a high-dimensional space. The primary objective of this summarization is to separate different histories or contexts from one another. Effective separation of these contexts is a fundamental prerequisite for the model to correctly predict the next element in a sequence, such as the next token in a sentence. Depending on the specific type of LSTM variant used within the block—either the sLSTM or the mLSTM—the architecture employs two different structural designs: post-up-projection and pre-up-projection.

The post-up-projection architecture is utilized for blocks containing an sLSTM. In this configuration, the model first non-linearly summarizes the past in the original space. Following this summarization, the block performs a linear map into a high-dimensional space, applies a non-linear activation function, and finally maps the data back to the original space. This structure ensures that the scalar memory of the sLSTM is utilized effectively within the residual framework.

Conversely, the pre-up-projection architecture is used for blocks containing an mLSTM. In this design, the model first performs a linear map into a high-dimensional space before non-linearly summarizing the past within that high-dimensional space. The result is then mapped back to the original space. The motivation for choosing pre-up-projection for the mLSTM is that the matrix memory structure of the mLSTM gains significantly larger capacity when operating in a high-dimensional space. By moving the summary process into a higher dimension first, the model can leverage the expansive capacity of the matrix memory more effectively than it could in the original space.

# Applying Cover’s Theorem to high-dimensional history summarization

The design choices regarding high-dimensional mapping in xLSTM blocks are grounded in a fundamental principle of machine learning known as Cover's Theorem. Cover's Theorem states that patterns that are non-linearly embedded in a lower-dimensional space can be more easily separated linearly when mapped into a higher-dimensional space. In the context of language modeling, the "patterns" are the various histories or contexts the model encounters as it processes a sequence. Because these histories are often complex and non-linear, it can be difficult for a model to distinguish between them in a low-dimensional representation.

By employing up-projection to move the history into a higher-dimensional space, xLSTM creates a framework where these different contexts become more linearly separable. This separation is crucial because, for a model to predict the next token accurately, it must be able to identify which specific history is currently relevant. If the model cannot distinguish between two different contexts (for example, two different narrative threads in a long story), it will struggle to produce the correct next token. Thus, the high-dimensional summarization acts as a mechanism to "disentangle" the past, providing a clearer signal for the model's predictive capabilities.

# Residual stacking and pre-LayerNorm backbones

Once the individual xLSTM blocks are defined, they must be organized into a deep architecture capable of learning the complex nuances of human language. To achieve this, xLSTM architectures are constructed by residually stacking these blocks. Residual stacking is a standard technique in modern deep learning, where the output of one block is added to its input (or a transformed version of its input), allowing gradients to flow more easily through very deep networks. This technique has been foundational to the success of both deep convolutional networks and the Transformer architectures that power current Large Language Models.

Specifically, the xLSTM architecture adopts the pre-LayerNorm residual backbone, which is the most common configuration in contemporary Large Language Models. In this setup, Layer Normalization is applied before the processing of the block, and the residual connection facilitates the stacking of many blocks. By following this established backbone, the xLSTM architecture inherits the stability and scaling properties that have made modern LLMs successful. This structural choice allows the xLSTM to be scaled to billions of parameters while maintaining the ability to train effectively.

# Computational Complexity: Linear vs. Quadratic scaling with sequence length

One of the primary advantages of the xLSTM architecture over the standard Transformer is its computational and memory complexity. In a standard Transformer, the self-attention mechanism results in quadratic complexity with respect to the sequence length. This means that as the input sequence becomes longer, the amount of computation and memory required increases by the square of the length, which eventually creates a bottleneck for processing very long contexts.

In contrast, xLSTM networks maintain a linear computation complexity and a constant memory complexity with respect to the sequence length. Because the xLSTM memory is compressive, it does not need to store a growing history of all previous tokens in a way that scales quadratically. This makes xLSTM particularly well-suited for industrial applications and deployment on edge devices where memory resources might be limited. While the mLSTM uses a $d \times d$ matrix memory, which involves a specific computational cost for updates and retrieval, these operations are still independent of the sequence length $L$. Furthermore, because these matrix operations can be performed in parallel on GPUs, the wall-clock time overhead of the complex matrix memory remains minor.

# Hardware Optimization: Parallelizing mLSTM and CUDA kernels for sLSTM

A significant hurdle for traditional LSTMs has been their lack of parallelizability due to hidden-hidden connections, which enforce sequential processing. xLSTM addresses this by offering two different types of memory structures with different hardware characteristics. The mLSTM is designed to be fully parallelizable. Because it abandons the memory mixing (the recurrent connections between hidden states from one time step to the next), its operations can be reformulated into a parallel version. This allows the mLSTM to be computed efficiently on GPUs, similar to how FlashAttention or Gated Linear Attention (GLA) operate.

The sLSTM, however, remains inherently non-parallelizable because it retains memory mixing through hidden-hidden connections. To mitigate the performance impact of this sequential nature, the researchers developed a fast CUDA implementation specifically for the sLSTM. This implementation includes GPU memory optimizations down to the register level. While the sLSTM is still sequential, these optimizations ensure that it is typically less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM implementation. While the current mLSTM kernels are approximately four times slower than FlashAttention or the scan used in Mamba, the research suggests that further optimization in the vein of FlashAttention could continue to improve these speeds.

Empirical Evaluation: Benchmarking xLSTM against State-of-the-Art

# Synthetic Tasks: Formal language (state tracking) and Associative Recall (memory capacity)

To validate the theoretical improvements introduced by the xLSTM architecture, the researchers first conducted evaluations on synthetic tasks designed to isolate specific capabilities: state tracking and memory capacity. These tasks provide a controlled environment to observe how exponential gating and modified memory structures behave compared to established models like Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs).

The first synthetic test involves formal language tasks, which are specifically used to evaluate a model's ability to perform state tracking. State tracking is the ability of a model to maintain and update information about a sequence over time, which is a prerequisite for solving tasks like regular grammars (e.g., the parity task). The study reveals a fundamental limitation in models like Transformers and certain State Space Models: because they lack memory mixing (the hidden-hidden recurrent connections), they are fundamentally less powerful than Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) in these specific domains. In contrast, the sLSTM component of the xLSTM architecture, which utilizes exponential gating combined with memory mixing, successfully solves these formal language tasks. By comparing a 2-block architecture across various models, the researchers demonstrated that xLSTM's ability to mix memory allows it to handle complex state tracking that other models cannot achieve.

The second synthetic test focuses on memory capacity using the Multi-Query Associative Recall task. In this setting, a model is presented with a series of key-value pairs from a large vocabulary and must recall the correct value for a given key later in the sequence. To increase the difficulty, the researchers scaled the number of key-value pairs up to 256 and extended the context length to 2048. This task serves as a benchmark for storage capacity, where Transformers are typically considered the gold standard because their memory capacity scales exponentially with the coding dimension. 

The results of this experiment highlight the effectiveness of the mLSTM's matrix memory. When comparing non-Transformer models, the xLSTM[1:1] configuration—which includes both sLSTM and mLSTM blocks—performed best, even at small model scales. A significant finding from this ablation was that the inclusion of an sLSTM block did not diminish the memory capacity of the system; rather, it leveraged the capacity provided by the mLSTM. Furthermore, the analysis indicated that xLSTM's enhanced memory capacity allows it to extrapolate successfully to context lengths longer than those encountered during the training phase.

# Long Range Arena: Testing long-context capabilities

Beyond synthetic tasks, it is crucial to understand how a model handles long-range dependencies in more complex, diverse environments. To assess this, the researchers utilized the Long Range Arena, a benchmark designed to evaluate a model's performance on sequences with significant length and variety.

The evaluation of xLSTM on the Long Range Arena demonstrates its efficiency and robustness. The xLSTM architecture showed consistent, strong performance across all tasks within the arena. This suggests that the architecture is remarkably adept at handling various aspects of long-context problems, from maintaining coherence to identifying specific information buried deep within a sequence. By successfully navigating these tasks, xLSTM proves that the modifications to the LSTM—specifically the transition to a more expressive memory structure and improved gating—enable it to compete with, and in some cases exceed, the performance of models specifically designed for long-context processing.

# Language Modeling: Perplexity results on SlimPajama (15B and 300B tokens)

The primary goal of the research is to determine how xLSTM performs as a Large Language Model (LLM). To answer this, the researchers conducted extensive language modeling experiments using the SlimPajama dataset. The core metric for these evaluations is perplexity, which measures how well the model predicts the next token in a sequence.

In the initial large-scale experiment, models were trained on 15B tokens. The researchers compared xLSTM against a wide array of architectures, including Transformers (GPT-3, Llama), State Space Models (H3, Mamba), and various Recurrent Neural Networks (RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, HGRN, and HGRN2). The results were definitive: the xLSTM model outperformed all existing methods in validation perplexity. This was further supported by ablation studies, which showed that the combination of exponential gating and matrix memory was the primary driver of this superior performance.

To further test the potential of xLSTM as a true LLM, the researchers scaled up the training to 300B tokens and tested various model sizes, ranging from 125M to 1.3B parameters. In these experiments, xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] emerged as the best-performing models across all tested sizes. The researchers also evaluated these models on downstream tasks measuring common sense reasoning; xLSTM was the superior method in the vast majority of these tasks, with the sole exception of the ARC task, where Mamba occasionally performed better. Additionally, on the PALOMA benchmark—which consists of 571 different text domains—xLSTM[1:0] demonstrated exceptional versatility, achieving lower perplexity than Mamba in 568 out of 571 domains, lower than Llama in 486 out of 571 domains, and lower than RWKV-4 in 570 out of 571 domains.

# Scaling Laws: Comparing xLSTM performance growth against Transformers and SSMs

A critical component of modern AI research is understanding how models improve as they are scaled up in size and data. The researchers analyzed the power-law scaling behavior of xLSTM to see if it follows the same trajectories as current state-of-the-art models.

The scaling experiments revealed that xLSTM shares a similar power-law scaling behavior with Transformers and State Space Models. However, xLSTM demonstrates a superior offset in its scaling curve. Specifically, the researchers observed that xLSTM performs better than Mamba, and Mamba performs better than Llama. This indicates that while the growth rate of performance relative to model size remains comparable to existing technologies, xLSTM starts from a higher baseline of efficiency. This finding is significant because it suggests that as xLSTM is scaled to even larger parameter counts, it will continue to perform favorably compared to the Transformer and SSM architectures currently dominating the field.

# Inference Efficiency: Generation times, maximal throughput, and constant memory benefits

One of the most practical advantages of xLSTM over the Transformer architecture lies in its computational and memory complexity. While Transformers suffer from quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, xLSTM maintains linear computation and constant memory complexity. This makes it particularly well-suited for industrial applications and edge computing.

The researchers quantified these benefits by measuring text generation times and maximal throughput at the 1.3B parameter scale. In the generation time experiments, xLSTM exhibited linear scaling, whereas the Llama Transformer model exhibited quadratic scaling. Because xLSTM's memory is constant, it can handle much larger batch sizes than Llama. This leads to a significant advantage in maximal throughput: xLSTM achieved the highest throughput among all tested models because its architecture allows for more efficient parallel processing of larger batches without the memory bottlenecks inherent in the Transformer's KV cache.

# Limitations and Future Work

Despite its strong performance, the researchers identified several limitations and areas for future optimization regarding the xLSTM architecture. One primary limitation involves the sLSTM component; because it utilizes memory mixing (hidden-hidden connections), it cannot be fully parallelized. While the researchers developed a fast CUDA kernel that is less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM, it remains a bottleneck compared to fully parallel designs.

The mLSTM component, while fully parallelizable, currently faces optimization challenges. The researchers noted that their current CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not fully optimized, making them about 4 times slower than the FlashAttention or Mamba scan implementations. Furthermore, the matrix memory of the mLSTM introduces high computational complexity due to the processing of $d \times d$ matrices. Although these operations are parallelizable and have a minor impact on wall-clock time, they represent a significant computational load.

Other considerations include the need for careful initialization of the forget gates and the potential for memory overload at extremely long sequence lengths, as the matrix memory is independent of sequence length. Finally, the researchers acknowledge that because the experiments were computationally expensive, the architecture and hyperparameters were not fully optimized, suggesting that extensive fine-tuning is required to reach the full potential of the xLSTM framework.
