\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

Chapter 6: Empirical Evaluation: Benchmarking xLSTM against State-of-the-Art

# Synthetic Tasks: Formal language (state tracking) and Associative Recall (memory capacity)

To validate the theoretical improvements introduced by the xLSTM architecture, the researchers first conducted evaluations on synthetic tasks designed to isolate specific capabilities: state tracking and memory capacity. These tasks provide a controlled environment to observe how exponential gating and modified memory structures behave compared to established models like Transformers and State Space Models (SSMs).

The first synthetic test involves formal language tasks, which are specifically used to evaluate a model's ability to perform state tracking. State tracking is the ability of a model to maintain and update information about a sequence over time, which is a prerequisite for solving tasks like regular grammars (e.g., the parity task). The study reveals a fundamental limitation in models like Transformers and certain State Space Models: because they lack memory mixing (the hidden-hidden recurrent connections), they are fundamentally less powerful than Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) in these specific domains. In contrast, the sLSTM component of the xLSTM architecture, which utilizes exponential gating combined with memory mixing, successfully solves these formal language tasks. By comparing a 2-block architecture across various models, the researchers demonstrated that xLSTM's ability to mix memory allows it to handle complex state tracking that other models cannot achieve.

The second synthetic test focuses on memory capacity using the Multi-Query Associative Recall task. In this setting, a model is presented with a series of key-value pairs from a large vocabulary and must recall the correct value for a given key later in the sequence. To increase the difficulty, the researchers scaled the number of key-value pairs up to 256 and extended the context length to 2048. This task serves as a benchmark for storage capacity, where Transformers are typically considered the gold standard because their memory capacity scales exponentially with the coding dimension. 

The results of this experiment highlight the effectiveness of the mLSTM's matrix memory. When comparing non-Transformer models, the xLSTM[1:1] configuration—which includes both sLSTM and mLSTM blocks—performed best, even at small model scales. A significant finding from this ablation was that the inclusion of an sLSTM block did not diminish the memory capacity of the system; rather, it leveraged the capacity provided by the mLSTM. Furthermore, the analysis indicated that xLSTM's enhanced memory capacity allows it to extrapolate successfully to context lengths longer than those encountered during the training phase.

# Long Range Arena: Testing long-context capabilities

Beyond synthetic tasks, it is crucial to understand how a model handles long-range dependencies in more complex, diverse environments. To assess this, the researchers utilized the Long Range Arena, a benchmark designed to evaluate a model's performance on sequences with significant length and variety.

The evaluation of xLSTM on the Long Range Arena demonstrates its efficiency and robustness. The xLSTM architecture showed consistent, strong performance across all tasks within the arena. This suggests that the architecture is remarkably adept at handling various aspects of long-context problems, from maintaining coherence to identifying specific information buried deep within a sequence. By successfully navigating these tasks, xLSTM proves that the modifications to the LSTM—specifically the transition to a more expressive memory structure and improved gating—enable it to compete with, and in some cases exceed, the performance of models specifically designed for long-context processing.

# Language Modeling: Perplexity results on SlimPajama (15B and 300B tokens)

The primary goal of the research is to determine how xLSTM performs as a Large Language Model (LLM). To answer this, the researchers conducted extensive language modeling experiments using the SlimPajama dataset. The core metric for these evaluations is perplexity, which measures how well the model predicts the next token in a sequence.

In the initial large-scale experiment, models were trained on 15B tokens. The researchers compared xLSTM against a wide array of architectures, including Transformers (GPT-3, Llama), State Space Models (H3, Mamba), and various Recurrent Neural Networks (RWKV-4, RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, HGRN, and HGRN2). The results were definitive: the xLSTM model outperformed all existing methods in validation perplexity. This was further supported by ablation studies, which showed that the combination of exponential gating and matrix memory was the primary driver of this superior performance.

To further test the potential of xLSTM as a true LLM, the researchers scaled up the training to 300B tokens and tested various model sizes, ranging from 125M to 1.3B parameters. In these experiments, xLSTM[1:0] and xLSTM[7:1] emerged as the best-performing models across all tested sizes. The researchers also evaluated these models on downstream tasks measuring common sense reasoning; xLSTM was the superior method in the vast majority of these tasks, with the sole exception of the ARC task, where Mamba occasionally performed better. Additionally, on the PALOMA benchmark—which consists of 571 different text domains—xLSTM[1:0] demonstrated exceptional versatility, achieving lower perplexity than Mamba in 568 out of 571 domains, lower than Llama in 486 out of 571 domains, and lower than RWKV-4 in 570 out of 571 domains.

# Scaling Laws: Comparing xLSTM performance growth against Transformers and SSMs

A critical component of modern AI research is understanding how models improve as they are scaled up in size and data. The researchers analyzed the power-law scaling behavior of xLSTM to see if it follows the same trajectories as current state-of-the-art models.

The scaling experiments revealed that xLSTM shares a similar power-law scaling behavior with Transformers and State Space Models. However, xLSTM demonstrates a superior offset in its scaling curve. Specifically, the researchers observed that xLSTM performs better than Mamba, and Mamba performs better than Llama. This indicates that while the growth rate of performance relative to model size remains comparable to existing technologies, xLSTM starts from a higher baseline of efficiency. This finding is significant because it suggests that as xLSTM is scaled to even larger parameter counts, it will continue to perform favorably compared to the Transformer and SSM architectures currently dominating the field.

# Inference Efficiency: Generation times, maximal throughput, and constant memory benefits

One of the most practical advantages of xLSTM over the Transformer architecture lies in its computational and memory complexity. While Transformers suffer from quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, xLSTM maintains linear computation and constant memory complexity. This makes it particularly well-suited for industrial applications and edge computing.

The researchers quantified these benefits by measuring text generation times and maximal throughput at the 1.3B parameter scale. In the generation time experiments, xLSTM exhibited linear scaling, whereas the Llama Transformer model exhibited quadratic scaling. Because xLSTM's memory is constant, it can handle much larger batch sizes than Llama. This leads to a significant advantage in maximal throughput: xLSTM achieved the highest throughput among all tested models because its architecture allows for more efficient parallel processing of larger batches without the memory bottlenecks inherent in the Transformer's KV cache.

# Limitations and Future Work

Despite its strong performance, the researchers identified several limitations and areas for future optimization regarding the xLSTM architecture. One primary limitation involves the sLSTM component; because it utilizes memory mixing (hidden-hidden connections), it cannot be fully parallelized. While the researchers developed a fast CUDA kernel that is less than two times slower than the parallel mLSTM, it remains a bottleneck compared to fully parallel designs.

The mLSTM component, while fully parallelizable, currently faces optimization challenges. The researchers noted that their current CUDA kernels for mLSTM are not fully optimized, making them about 4 times slower than the FlashAttention or Mamba scan implementations. Furthermore, the matrix memory of the mLSTM introduces high computational complexity due to the processing of $d \times d$ matrices. Although these operations are parallelizable and have a minor impact on wall-clock time, they represent a significant computational load.

Other considerations include the need for careful initialization of the forget gates and the potential for memory overload at extremely long sequence lengths, as the matrix memory is independent of sequence length. Finally, the researchers acknowledge that because the experiments were computationally expensive, the architecture and hyperparameters were not fully optimized, suggesting that extensive fine-tuning is required to reach the full potential of the xLSTM framework.
