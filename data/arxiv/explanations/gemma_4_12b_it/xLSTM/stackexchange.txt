\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Doesn't the original LSTM already use a 'constant error carousel' to prevent vanishing gradients? Why do we need exponential gating to 'revise' storage decisions?
Question:
I'm reading the xLSTM paper and I'm a bit confused about the motivation. The introduction states that LSTMs were designed to solve the vanishing gradient problem using the constant error carousel (Equation 1). However, the authors then say that LSTMs have a limitation in 'revising storage decisions' and propose exponential gating to fix this. 

Could someone explain the difference between the 'constant error' flow of a standard LSTM and the 'revision' capability the authors are trying to add? Is the standard LSTM's forget gate $\sigma(f_t)$ mathematically insufficient to 'overwrite' old information effectively?
Answer:
The confusion you're experiencing is very common because it touches on the distinction between **gradient dynamics** (how the model learns) and **state dynamics** (how the model "thinks" or "remembers" during inference). 

To clarify, the "Constant Error Carousel" (CEC) and the "Revision" capability address two different problems. The CEC, as you noted, was designed to solve the vanishing gradient problem. By using the additive update $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, the LSTM ensures that the gradient can flow backward through time without being repeatedly multiplied by small values, because the derivative $\frac{\partial c_t}{\partial c_{t-1}}$ is $f_t$, which can be close to $1$. This creates a "highway" for the gradient.

However, the limitation regarding "revising storage decisions" is a problem of state management. Even if the gradient flows perfectly, the model might still struggle to update the content of the memory cell $c_t$ effectively. In a standard LSTM, the gates $f_t$ and $i_t$ are bounded by the sigmoid function $\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$, which means they are strictly confined to the interval $(0, 1)$. 

Mathematically, if you want to "overwrite" a piece of information in the cell state—for example, replacing an old, less relevant vector with a new, more relevant one—you ideally want $f_t \approx 0$ (to "erase" the old) and $i_t \approx 1$ (to "write" the new). Because the sigmoid function is "squashed," the model requires very large pre-activation values to get the gates close to these extremes. This creates a "soft" update behavior where the old information persists as a "ghost" in the memory, even when the model encounters a much better piece of information.

The xLSTM paper uses the *Nearest Neighbor Search* problem to illustrate this. Imagine the model is scanning a sequence to find the vector most similar to a query. If the model finds a "good" vector early on and stores it in $c_t$, but later finds a "much better" vector, it needs to be able to aggressively "revise" its storage. In a standard LSTM, the bounded nature of $\sigma(f_t)$ makes it difficult to completely "forget" the first vector, leading to a mixture of both in the cell state. This "noise" from the discarded information degrades the retrieval accuracy.

By introducing exponential gating, $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$, the authors allow the input gate to be unbounded. This allows for much sharper, more decisive updates. When combined with the new normalization techniques (the $n_t$ state), the model gains a mechanism that behaves more like an "overwrite" operation than a "weighted average" operation. Essentially, while the original LSTM was designed to keep the gradient from disappearing, the xLSTM is designed to give the model the "agency" to decide exactly when to clear out old information and replace it with something more relevant. 

In short: the CEC keeps the "pipes" open for learning, but exponential gating gives the model a sharper "eraser" for managing its memory.

### How does the stabilizer state $m_t$ mathematically ensure that the gradients remain unchanged?
Question:
In Section 3.1, the authors introduce a stabilizer state $m_t$ to handle the potential overflows of exponential gates. They provide the following equations:

$m_t = \max(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1}, \log(i_t))$ 

$i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t) - m_t) = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t)$

$f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t) + m_{t-1} - m_t)$


The paper claims that replacing $f_t$ and $i_t$ with $f'_t$ and $i'_t$ does not change the output of the network nor the derivatives of the loss. I can see that the $m_t$ term cancels out in the product $i'_t \times f'_t$ (or similar combinations), but I'm struggling to see why the gradient $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}}$ remains identical. Could someone provide a brief derivation or intuition for why this stabilization is 'lossless'?
Answer:
The reason this stabilization is "lossless" is that the stabilizer state $m_t$ acts as a dynamic shift in the log-domain that factorizes out of the linear recurrence. Mathematically, while the individual values of the stabilized gates $f'_t$ and $i'_t$ are different from the original gates $f_t$ and $i_t$, the ratio of the components in the cell state update remains invariant.

To see this, let's define the original (un-stabilized) gates as $i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$. The stabilizer state is defined as $m_t = \max(\tilde{f}_t + m_{t-1}, \tilde{i}_t)$. When we substitute the definitions of the stabilized gates into the cell state recurrence, we get:

$c_t = f'_t c_{t-1} + i'_t z_t$
$c_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t + m_{t-1} - m_t) c_{t-1} + \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t) z_t$

Because the term $\exp(-m_t)$ is common to both additions, we can factor it out:

$c_t = \exp(-m_t) \left[ \exp(\tilde{f}_t + m_{t-1}) c_{t-1} + \exp(\tilde{i}_t) z_t \right]$
$c_t = \exp(-m_t) \left[ f_t \exp(m_{t-1}) c_{t-1} + i_t z_t \right]$

Now, let's define an auxiliary variable $C_t = c_t \exp(m_t)$. If we substitute $c_{t-1} = C_{t-1} \exp(-m_{t-1})$ into the equation above, the terms simplify significantly:

$C_t \exp(-m_t) = \exp(-m_t) \left[ f_t \exp(m_{t-1}) (C_{t-1} \exp(-m_{t-1})) + i_t z_t \right]$
$C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t z_t$

This shows that $C_t$ follows the exact same recurrence as the original cell state $c_t$ would have followed in a non-stabilized system. The same logic applies to the normalizer state $n_t$: by defining $N_t = n_t \exp(m_t)$, you can show that $N_t = f_t N_{t-1} + i_t$, which is the original normalizer update.

The final hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$ is calculated as the ratio of these two states. In the stabilized version, we have:

$\tilde{h}_t = \frac{c_t}{n_t} = \frac{C_t \exp(-m_t)}{N_t \exp(-m_t)} = \frac{C_t}{N_t}$

Because the $\exp(-m_t)$ terms cancel out perfectly, the value of the hidden state $\tilde{h}_t$ is algebraically identical in both the stabilized and non-stabilized versions of the network. Since the hidden state is the only output of the memory cell that feeds into the rest of the network (and subsequently into the loss function $\mathcal{L}$), any operation performed after this point—including the calculation of the loss and the backpropagation of gradients—is operating on the exact same values. 

In terms of intuition, this is very similar to the "Log-Sum-Exp" trick used in Softmax. In Softmax, $\frac{\exp(x_i)}{\sum \exp(x_j)} = \frac{\exp(x_i - \max(x))}{\sum \exp(x_j - \max(x))}$. The stabilizer $m_t$ performs a similar operation but does so dynamically at every time step. It shifts the magnitude of the gates to prevent numerical overflow while ensuring that the relative proportions of the input and forget operations remain unchanged. Because the forward pass is mathematically invariant to this shift, the Chain Rule ensures that the gradients $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}}$ remain identical.

### Why is mLSTM parallelizable while sLSTM is not, despite both being recurrent?
Question:
I'm trying to understand the parallelization difference between the two new variants. The paper says sLSTM is not parallelizable due to 'memory mixing' (hidden-hidden connections), while mLSTM is fully parallelizable because it abandons those connections.

If mLSTM is parallelizable, how does it still function as a recurrent model? Does it use a 'scan' operation similar to State Space Models (SSMs), or is the recurrence handled in a different way that allows for parallel computation on a GPU?
Answer:
The reason for the difference in parallelizability between sLSTM and mLSTM lies in the presence or absence of "memory mixing," which the paper defines as the hidden-hidden connections between time steps. In a standard LSTM or the proposed sLSTM, the gates (input, forget, and output) are computed using both the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$. Specifically, as shown in the original LSTM equations in Section 2.1, the gate inputs $\tilde{z}_t, \tilde{i}_t, \tilde{f}_t,$ and $\tilde{o}_t$ all include a recurrent weight term multiplied by $h_{t-1}$ (e.g., $r_z h_{t-1}$). This creates a strictly sequential dependency: you cannot compute the gates for time step $t$ until you have finished the computation for $t-1$. Because the hidden state $h_t$ is the output of these gates, this dependency chains throughout the entire sequence, preventing the GPU from processing multiple time steps in parallel.

In contrast, the mLSTM is designed to be fully parallelizable by explicitly abandoning these hidden-hidden connections. If you look at the mLSTM forward pass equations in Section 2.3, the gate inputs $\tilde{i}_t$, $\tilde{f}_t$, and $\tilde{\mathbf{o}}_t$ are functions only of the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$ and a bias term. For example, $\tilde{i}_t = \mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_t + b_i$. Because the gates no longer depend on $h_{t-1}$, the only remaining recurrence in the model is the update of the cell state $\mathbf{C}_t$ and the normalizer $\mathbf{n}_t$. 

This brings us to your question about how it still functions as a recurrent model while being parallelizable. The mLSTM's state updates are linear recurrences. The cell state update is defined as $\mathbf{C}_t = f_t \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. Since the gates $f_t$ and $i_t$ are now independent of the previous hidden state and only depend on the current input $\mathbf{x}_t$, the entire sequence of cell states can be viewed as a linear recurrence of the form $S_t = \alpha_t S_{t-1} + \beta_t$. In mathematics and computer science, such linear recurrences can be computed in parallel using a "scan" operation (also known as a prefix sum). 

Instead of iterating through the sequence step-by-step, the scan operation allows the model to compute all states $\mathbf{C}_1, \mathbf{C}_2, \dots, \mathbf{C}_T$ in $O(\log T)$ time on a GPU by breaking the sequence into a tree-like structure of associative operations. This is the same principle that allows State Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba to be parallelized during training. By removing the "memory mixing" (the $h_{t-1}$ dependency in the gates), the authors have transformed the LSTM from a non-linear recurrent system into a system where the recurrence is isolated in a linear update, making it compatible with efficient parallel hardware kernels.

### How does the covariance update rule in mLSTM relate to the attention mechanism in Transformers?
Question:
The paper describes the mLSTM memory as $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, which it calls a covariance update rule. This looks very similar to how the Key-Value (KV) cache is updated in a Transformer, but without the Softmax operation.

Specifically, how does the mLSTM's retrieval $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \}$ approximate the attention mechanism? Is the normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ acting as a substitute for the partition function $Z$ in Softmax attention?
Answer:
The mLSTM memory structure and its retrieval mechanism can be understood as a specific implementation of **Linear Attention**. While the standard Transformer uses a Softmax-based attention mechanism, which requires $O(T^2)$ complexity (or $O(T)$ memory with a KV-cache), the mLSTM approximates this by linearizing the interaction between queries, keys, and values, allowing for $O(1)$ memory complexity.

To see how the mLSTM retrieval approximates the attention mechanism, we can expand the recursive definition of the memory matrix $\mathbf{C}_t$. If we ignore the gating terms $f_t$ and $i_t$ for a moment to see the underlying structure, the memory $\mathbf{C}_t$ is essentially a sum of outer products: $\mathbf{C}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{t} \mathbf{v}_j \mathbf{k}_j^\top$. When we perform the retrieval $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$, we use the associativity of matrix multiplication:
$$\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t = \left( \sum_{j=1}^{t} \mathbf{v}_j \mathbf{k}_j^\top \right) \mathbf{q}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{t} \mathbf{v}_j (\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$$
In this expression, the term $(\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$ acts as a scalar weight for each value vector $\mathbf{v}_j$. This is exactly the form of the attention mechanism, where the output is a weighted sum of values. The difference is that in a Transformer, these weights are passed through a Softmax function: $w_j = \frac{\exp(\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t)}{\sum \exp(\mathbf{k}_i^\top \mathbf{q}_i)}$. By removing the exponential, the mLSTM effectively treats the dot product as the weight directly.

Your intuition regarding the normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ is correct: it serves as the linear substitute for the Softmax partition function $Z$. In the Transformer, the partition function $Z = \sum \exp(\mathbf{k}_i^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$ ensures that the weights sum to one. In the mLSTM, the normalizer state is updated as $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$, which means $\mathbf{n}_t$ is a weighted sum of the keys: $\mathbf{n}_t \approx \sum \mathbf{k}_j$. When we compute the denominator $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$, we get:
$$\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t = \left( \sum_{j=1}^{t} \mathbf{k}_j \right)^\top \mathbf{q}_t = \sum_{j=1}^{t} \mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t$$
By dividing the weighted sum of values by this sum of weights, the retrieval becomes:
$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \frac{\sum \mathbf{v}_j (\mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t)}{\sum \mathbf{k}_j^\top \mathbf{q}_t}$$
This is the standard formula for Linear Attention. It approximates the Transformer's behavior by ensuring that the output $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t$ is a convex combination of the stored values $\mathbf{v}_j$, where the "importance" of each value is determined by the similarity between the current query $\mathbf{q}_t$ and the past keys $\mathbf{k}_j$.

From a broader perspective, the paper highlights a fundamental trade-off between **separability** and **complexity**. The Softmax operation provides "higher separability" (the ability to distinguish between different context patterns clearly), which is why Transformers excel at complex tasks. However, this comes at a quadratic cost in memory. The mLSTM's covariance update rule chooses to trade off some of that separability for a linear computation and constant memory complexity, making it significantly more efficient for long sequences and edge applications while still retaining the ability to store and retrieve information from the past.

### Why does mLSTM use 'pre up-projection' while sLSTM uses 'post up-projection' based on Cover's Theorem?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors explain their choice of residual block architectures. They use post up-projection for sLSTM and pre up-projection for mLSTM. They mention Cover's Theorem, which suggests that non-linearly embedded patterns are more likely to be linearly separated in higher dimensions.

I don't quite see the connection. Why does the mLSTM benefit specifically from the high-dimensional space *before* the memory update $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$, whereas the sLSTM doesn't require that same ordering? Does the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}$ have some property that makes it more sensitive to the dimensionality of the input $\mathbf{x}_t$?
Answer:
The distinction between the "pre up-projection" and "post up-projection" architectures in xLSTM boils down to where the model performs its "summarization" of the past and how much "room" it has to store those summaries.

To understand this, we have to look at how each variant handles its memory and how Cover's Theorem applies to those specific mechanisms.

### Cover's Theorem and the Goal of xLSTM
The authors state that the goal of the residual block is to "non-linearly summarize the past in a high-dimensional space to better separate different histories." Cover's Theorem provides the mathematical justification for this: if you have data that is not linearly separable in a low-dimensional space, projecting it into a higher-dimensional space makes it much more likely that a linear classifier (or a linear update rule) can separate those patterns. In the context of an LSTM, "separating histories" means the model's memory should be able to distinguish between different contexts (e.g., the "past" of a story about a cat vs. a story about a spaceship) so that it can retrieve the correct information for the current token.

### Why mLSTM needs Pre Up-Projection
The mLSTM uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}$ updated via a covariance rule: $\mathbf{C}_t = \mathbf{C}_{t-1} + \mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$. 

This is the critical technical detail. The memory $\mathbf{C}$ is essentially a sum of outer products. If the vectors $\mathbf{v}_t$ and $\mathbf{k}_t$ are in a dimension $d$, the memory $\mathbf{C}$ is a $d \times d$ matrix. If we use **pre up-projection**, we project the input $\mathbf{x}_t$ into a higher dimension $D$ (where $D > d$) *before* computing the outer product. This means:
1. The vectors $\mathbf{v}_t$ and $\mathbf{k}_t$ now exist in $\mathbb{R}^D$.
2. The update $\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ happens in a much larger space, resulting in a $D \times D$ matrix.

By performing the update in the high-dimensional space, the "memory capacity" (the number of distinct, linearly separable patterns the matrix can store) increases significantly. Because the update is additive, the matrix $\mathbf{C}$ acts as a repository of these high-dimensional patterns. If we had used post up-projection, the update would happen in the smaller $d \times d$ space first, and we would only project the *result* of the summation. This would severely bottleneck the storage capacity, as the model would be trying to "cram" all past information into a smaller matrix before ever seeing the high-dimensional space.

### Why sLSTM uses Post Up-Projection
In contrast, the sLSTM uses a scalar memory $c_t$. The update rule is $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$. 

In this architecture, the "bottleneck" is the scalar nature of $c_t$ itself. Because the update is a simple addition of scalars (or a small vector of scalars if there are multiple cells), projecting to a high-dimensional space *before* the update doesn't "expand" the storage capacity in the same way an outer product does. The information is already being compressed into the scalar state.

For sLSTM, the high-dimensional "separation" is achieved by the residual block's ability to non-linearly transform the hidden state $h_t$ *after* it has been retrieved from the memory. This is why post up-projection (like in Transformers) is sufficient: the model summarizes the past through the recurrence, and then uses the high-dimensional projection to perform the complex non-linear reasoning required to predict the next token.

### Summary Intuition
You can think of the memory $\mathbf{C}$ in mLSTM as a physical warehouse. 
*   **Pre up-projection** for mLSTM is like expanding the size of the warehouse *before* you start moving boxes (the $\mathbf{v} \mathbf{k}^\top$ products) into it. This gives you more room to store distinct items.
*   **Post up-projection** for sLSTM is like having a small box (the scalar $c_t$) that you move around, but having a very large room (the high-dimensional projection) to unpack and organize those items once you take them out of the box.

Because the mLSTM's "storage" is the matrix itself, it needs that room *during* the storage process. Because the sLSTM's "storage" is a compressed scalar, it only needs the room *after* the storage process to perform the final computation.
