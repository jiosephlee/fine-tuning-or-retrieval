# Beyond Tokens: Why the Next Generation of LLMs Might Forget Tokenizers Entirely

Current large language models (LLMs) are trained almost entirely end-to-end, with one notable exception: tokenization. Before a model ever sees a string of text, a tokenizer groups individual bytes into "tokens" based on heuristic rules. While this has been the standard for years, it introduces significant limitations. Tokenization creates biases in how strings are compressed, leads to domain-specific sensitivities, struggles with input noise, and often results in multilingual inequity because the vocabulary is rarely balanced across all languages.

Furthermore, tokenization creates a rigid trade-off between vocabulary size and compute. Increasing the vocabulary size allows for larger tokens (which reduces the number of steps the model must take), but it also increases the size of the output projection layer, consuming more memory and parameters. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) proposes a way to break free from this fixed-vocabulary paradigm by processing raw bytes directly through a dynamic patching system.

## From Tokens to Patches

The core innovation of BLT is the shift from tokens to "patches." Unlike tokens, which are drawn from a fixed vocabulary determined before training, patches in BLT are dynamically grouped sequences of bytes. These patches serve as the primary units of computation.

A significant advantage of this approach is the ability to dynamically allocate compute. In standard tokenization-based models, the same amount of compute is spent on every token, regardless of its complexity. For example, predicting the final letter of a common word is computationally "easy" and low-entropy, while choosing the first word of a new sentence is "hard" and high-entropy. 

BLT addresses this by segmenting data into patches based on the entropy of the next-byte prediction. By using a small, lightweight byte-level model to estimate entropy, the system identifies where information density is high and creates patch boundaries accordingly. The entropy $H(x_i)$ is calculated as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

This allows the model to group bytes into patches with relatively uniform information density, ensuring that the "heavy" part of the model—the Global Latent Transformer—is invoked only when necessary.

## The BLT Architecture

To manage the transition from raw bytes to high-level representations without a fixed vocabulary, BLT utilizes a three-part architecture:

*   **Local Encoder:** This lightweight module takes a sequence of raw bytes and maps them into latent patch representations. It uses a series of transformer layers and cross-attention modules to pool byte representations into patches. To improve robustness, it incorporates hash n-gram embeddings ($e_i$), which help the model understand sub-word structures:
    $$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n = 3,...,8}E_{n}^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$
*   **Latent Global Transformer:** This is the "brain" of the model. It is a large autoregressive transformer that operates exclusively on the latent patch representations produced by the encoder. Because it processes patches rather than individual bytes, it significantly reduces the number of steps required during inference.
*   **Local Decoder:** This module performs the inverse of the encoder, taking the global model's patch representations and decoding them back into the original raw bytes.

## Efficiency and Scaling Laws

One of the most compelling results of the BLT research is how patches scale compared to tokens. Because patch sizes are dynamic and not bound by a fixed vocabulary, BLT offers a new dimension for scaling. In a fixed inference-flop budget, a model can simultaneously increase its patch size and its global model size. 

When the model identifies a predictable sequence of bytes, it can group them into a longer patch. Because the Global Latent Transformer is run less frequently for longer patches, those saved flops can be "reallocated" to grow the size of the global transformer itself. The researchers demonstrated that BLT can match the performance of Llama 3 at scales up to 8B parameters while offering up to 50% fewer flops at inference.

## Improved Robustness and Generalization

By moving away from the "black box" of tokenization and working directly with bytes, BLT demonstrates superior robustness to input noise. In experiments using strategies like random character deletion or casing changes, BLT outperformed tokenizer-based models significantly.

Furthermore, BLT shows a much deeper understanding of orthographic and sub-word knowledge. It excelled on the CUTE benchmark, which tests a model's ability to manipulate text at the character level—a task where many tokenizer-based models struggle because they lack direct access to the underlying bytes. BLT also showed improved performance in low-resource machine translation, proving that byte-level modeling is a more equitable foundation for handling the "long tail" of human language.

By moving beyond the constraints of fixed vocabularies, the Byte Latent Transformer provides a blueprint for a more efficient, robust, and linguistically fair generation of large language models.



# Smart Scaling: How 'Entropy Patching' Gives LLMs a Human-Like Brain

Current large language models (LLMs) operate on a somewhat inefficient principle: they allocate the same amount of "brain power" to every token, regardless of how difficult that token is to predict. For example, predicting the "s" at the end of a common plural word requires very little cognitive effort compared to predicting the first word of a complex sentence. In standard architectures, however, these two tasks consume the same amount of computational resources.

The **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** paper introduces a way to change this. By moving away from fixed-vocabulary tokenization and toward a dynamic "Entropy Patching" mechanism, BLT allows models to focus their computational capacity where it matters most—on high-uncertainty data—while skipping the heavy lifting for predictable patterns.

## The Problem with Fixed Tokenization

Most modern LLMs rely on tokenization, a heuristic pre-processing step that groups bytes into a static set of tokens. While effective for scaling, tokenization introduces several drawbacks. It creates a bias in how strings are compressed, can be sensitive to input noise, and often struggles with "long-tail" data like rare words or low-resource languages. 

Furthermore, tokenization fixes the "unit" of computation. Because the model processes each token through the same number of transformer layers, it wastes FLOPs (floating-point operations) on easy predictions and may lack the capacity to handle complex ones.

## What is Entropy Patching?

To solve this, BLT replaces fixed tokens with **patches**. Instead of a static vocabulary, BLT encodes raw bytes into dynamically sized groups. The size of these patches is determined by **entropy patching**, a data-driven approach that identifies boundaries based on prediction uncertainty.

The researchers train a small, dedicated byte-level language model to estimate the entropy $H(x_i)$ of the next byte in a sequence. Entropy, in this context, measures the "surprise" or uncertainty of a prediction:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

By analyzing these entropy values, the model can identify where the information density changes. BLT identifies patch boundaries using two main criteria:
1. **Global Constraint:** A boundary is created when the entropy $H(x_t)$ exceeds a global threshold $\theta_g$.
2. **Approximate Monotonicity Constraint:** A boundary is identified if there is a significant relative jump in entropy, defined as $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$.

This allows the model to group easy-to-predict bytes (like the middle of a common word) into long patches, while breaking down complex, high-entropy segments into smaller patches.

## The BLT Architecture

The BLT architecture is designed to manage this dynamic patching through a three-part system:

*   **Local Encoder ($\mathcal{E}$):** A lightweight transformer that takes raw bytes and maps them into latent patch representations. It uses a unique cross-attention layer to pool byte representations into the patches defined by the entropy model.
*   **Latent Global Transformer ($\mathcal{G}$):** This is the "heavy lifter" of the architecture. It operates on the patch representations rather than individual bytes. Because it only runs on patches, the model can save significant compute by processing longer patches more efficiently.
*   **Local Decoder ($\mathcal{D}$):** Another lightweight transformer that takes the processed patch representations and decodes them back into the original raw bytes.

By separating the "heavy" global computation from the "light" byte-level encoding and decoding, BLT can dynamically allocate compute. When the data is predictable, the patches are long, and the global transformer is invoked less frequently.

## Efficiency and Scaling Gains

The results of this approach are significant. The researchers conducted a flop-controlled scaling study up to 8B parameters and found that BLT matches the performance of tokenization-based models like Llama 3. However, because BLT can use longer patches for predictable data, it can achieve up to **50% fewer flops at inference** while maintaining similar performance.

Perhaps the most exciting finding is a new dimension for scaling. In traditional models, increasing the model size usually increases the inference cost. With BLT, because the global transformer is run less often as patches get longer, we can **simultaneously increase the model size and the patch size** while keeping the inference flop budget constant. This allows for much larger, more capable models that stay within the same hardware constraints.

## Robustness and Character Awareness

Beyond efficiency, working directly with raw bytes gives BLT a "deeper" understanding of text. The paper demonstrates that BLT is significantly more robust to input noise (such as random character deletions or case changes) than token-based models.

Furthermore, BLT excels at tasks that require character-level manipulation—things that often baffle token-based LLMs. On the CUTE benchmark, which tests orthographic similarity and sequence manipulation, BLT-Entropy outperformed Llama 3 by more than 25 points. It showed near-perfect accuracy (99.9%) on spelling tasks, proving that by looking at the "atoms" of language—the bytes—the model gains a more fundamental understanding of how words are constructed.



# The Scaling Hack: How BLT Lets You Build Bigger Models for the Same Cost

In the current landscape of Large Language Models (LLMs), developers face a constant tug-of-war between two competing goals: increasing model capacity to improve intelligence and maintaining inference speed to keep costs manageable. Traditionally, scaling a model's parameters automatically increases the computational burden during inference. However, a new architecture called the Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}) introduces a "scaling hack" that breaks this linear relationship. By moving away from traditional tokenization in favor of dynamic "patches," {\textsc BLT} allows for the simultaneous growth of model size and patch size while keeping inference costs under control.

## The Problem with Tokens

Most modern LLMs rely on tokenization—a pre-processing step that groups raw bytes into a fixed vocabulary of subword units. While this has been the industry standard, it introduces several limitations. Tokenization is a heuristic; it doesn't always group data in a way that reflects the underlying complexity of the information. This leads to "multilingual inequity," where some languages are represented less efficiently than others, and a lack of "orthographic knowledge," where models struggle with character-level manipulations like spelling or phonology.

Furthermore, tokenization creates a bottleneck for scaling. In standard models, if you want to increase the average size of a token to save on computation, you must also increase the vocabulary size. This expands the output dimension of the model's final projection layer, creating a direct trade-off between how much "chunk" the model processes at once and how large the model can actually be.

## The BLT Architecture: From Bytes to Patches

Instead of using a fixed vocabulary, {\textsc BLT} operates directly on raw bytes but groups them into "patches." These patches serve as the primary units of computation. The architecture is split into three distinct components:

*   **The Local Encoder:** A lightweight model that takes raw bytes and maps them into expressive latent patch representations.
*   **The Global Latent Transformer:** The "heavy lifter" of the architecture, which processes these patch representations. This is where the bulk of the model's parameters reside.
*   **The Local Decoder:** A lightweight model that takes the global model's output and decodes the patch representations back into raw bytes.

By separating the byte-level processing (Encoder/Decoder) from the high-level reasoning (Global Transformer), {\textsc BLT} can allocate compute more intelligently.

## Dynamic Entropy Patching

One of the most innovative aspects of {\textsc BLT} is how it decides where a patch begins and ends. Rather than using a fixed stride (like every $k$ bytes) or a simple rule (like spaces), {\textsc BLT} uses **entropy patching**.

The model uses a small, pre-trained byte-level language model to calculate the entropy of the next byte prediction. The entropy $H(x_i)$ is calculated as follows:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

By identifying points of high uncertainty or breaks in monotonic entropy, the model segments the data into patches with relatively uniform information density. This means the model naturally spends more "attention" and compute on complex, high-entropy data (like the start of a new sentence) and uses larger, more efficient patches for predictable, low-entropy data (like the middle of common words).

## The Scaling Hack: Reallocating Compute

The core finding of the paper is that {\textsc BLT} unlocks a new dimension for scaling. In a token-based model, if you want to make the model bigger, you generally have to accept a higher inference cost. In {\textsc BLT}, however, because the Global Latent Transformer only runs on *patches* rather than *tokens*, you can increase the average patch size to save on compute.

Because larger patches result in fewer steps for the Global Latent Transformer, you can "reallocate" those saved flops to grow the size of the Global Latent Transformer itself. This allows for a model that is simultaneously larger (more capable) and produces a similar inference cost to a smaller, token-based model. 

The researchers demonstrated this by showing that {\textsc BLT} can achieve better scaling trends than BPE-based models (like Llama 3) in "inference-flop controlled" settings. For example, by using a patch size of 8 bytes, {\textsc BLT} can achieve nearly 50% savings in inference flops compared to traditional architectures, while still maintaining competitive performance.

## Robustness and Character Awareness

Beyond the efficiency gains, the "byte-first" approach provides significant qualitative improvements. Because {\textsc BLT} sees every byte rather than a pre-compressed token, it is much more robust to input noise (such as random character deletions or case changes). 

Furthermore, {\textsc BLT} excels at character-level tasks that often baffle token-based models. In the CUTE benchmark—which tests a model's ability to understand and manipulate orthographic similarity—{\textsc BLT} outperformed Llama 3 by more than 25 points. It demonstrated near-perfect scores on spelling tasks, proving that by thinking in bytes and patches rather than arbitrary tokens, we can build models that are both more efficient and more fundamentally aware of the text they process.



# Spelling Bees and Typos: Why Byte-Level Models are More Robust

When we interact with Large Language Models (LLMs), we often assume they "read" text the same way humans do—by processing characters and words. In reality, most modern LLMs (like Llama 3) rely on a process called tokenization. Tokenization is a heuristic pre-processing step that groups raw bytes into a static set of "tokens" before the model ever sees them. While this is efficient, it creates a significant structural weakness: if the model encounters a typo, an obscure spelling, or a rare character sequence that wasn't in its fixed vocabulary, it can stumble.

The Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}) changes this paradigm by moving away from fixed vocabularies entirely. By training on raw bytes and using a dynamic "patching" system, {\textsc BLT}} develops a "superpower" for character-level understanding, making it significantly more robust to the messy, noisy data of the real world.

## The Tokenization Trap

The problem with tokenization is that it creates a rigid boundary for what the model "knows." Because tokens are induced by compression heuristics rather than linguistic logic, they can be highly sensitive to input noise. If a user makes a simple typo, the tokenizer might break a word into completely different, nonsensical tokens, leading the model to lose the intended meaning. Furthermore, tokenization often leads to "multilingual inequity," where low-resource languages suffer because they aren't represented well in the fixed vocabulary.

{\textsc BLT}} bypasses this by treating the input as a sequence of raw bytes. Instead of a static dictionary, it uses a learnable method to group these bytes into "patches." These patches serve as the primary units of computation. To manage efficiency, the model segments data based on the entropy of the next-byte prediction. In simple terms, it allocates more model capacity to complex, high-information parts of the text and groups predictable, low-entropy bytes into longer patches to save on compute.

## A Superpower for Character-Level Understanding

By operating at the byte level, {\textsc BLT}} maintains a much deeper awareness of the underlying orthography (the conventional spelling system of a language). This leads to several key improvements in robustness:

### 1. Resilience to Noisy Data
The researchers tested {\textsc BLT}} against five different noising strategies, including "AntSpeak" (converting text to uppercase, space-separated characters), random character deletion, and random casing. The results were clear: {\textsc BLT}} outperformed token-based models across the board, showing an average advantage of 8 points in robustness. Because it understands the "atoms" of the text (the bytes), it can still piece together the meaning even when the structure is disrupted.

### 2. Mastery of Phonology and Spelling
One of the most striking results comes from the CUTE benchmark, which evaluates a model's ability to understand composition, orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation. Standard token-based models often struggle here because they "know" the word but don't "know" how it's spelled. {\textsc BLT}} outperformed the Llama 3 baseline by more than 25 points on this benchmark. Specifically, it showed exceptional proficiency in character manipulation tasks, achieving near-perfect scores (99.9%) on spelling tasks. It also demonstrated superior performance in Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) tasks, successfully mapping written characters to their phonetic pronunciations.

### 3. Generalization to the "Long Tail"
Because {\textsc BLT}} isn't limited by a fixed vocabulary, it is much better at handling the "long tail" of data—those rare words, obscure dialects, and low-resource languages that are often ignored by standard tokenizers. In evaluations using the FLORES-101 benchmark, {\textsc BLT}} showed a distinct advantage in translating into and out of lower-resource language families. By modeling bytes directly, the model can generalize to unseen sequences more effectively than a model trying to map them to a predefined list of tokens.

## Conclusion

The transition from tokens to patches represents a shift toward a more fundamental understanding of language. By removing the "middleman" of tokenization, {\textsc BLT}} allows the model to see the text as it truly is: a sequence of bytes where meaning can be derived from the smallest units of information. This byte-level granularity doesn't just make the model more robust to the occasional typo; it provides a more equitable and flexible framework for handling the full diversity of human communication.



# Giving Llama a Byte-Level Upgrade: The 'Byte-ifying' Workflow

Most modern Large Language Models (LLMs) rely on a pre-processing step called tokenization. This heuristic groups bytes into static units (tokens) before the model ever sees them. While effective, tokenization introduces several limitations: it can be sensitive to input noise, creates domain-specific biases, and often leads to multilingual inequities. To address these issues, researchers have proposed training models directly on raw bytes. However, training on bytes is computationally expensive because it significantly increases the sequence length.

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture offers a way to achieve the benefits of byte-level modeling while maintaining the efficiency of token-based models. By grouping bytes into dynamically sized "patches," BLT allocates compute resources where they are needed most. A particularly exciting development in this research is the "byte-ifying" workflow—a method to upgrade existing, pre-trained token-based models into byte-level architectures without starting from scratch.

## The Architecture: From Bytes to Latent Patches

The core innovation of BLT is how it handles data. Instead of a fixed vocabulary of tokens, BLT uses a three-part architecture to process raw bytes:

*   **Local Encoder:** A lightweight model that maps a sequence of input bytes into latent patch representations.
*   **Latent Global Transformer:** The "heavy lifter" of the model, which processes these patch representations.
*   **Local Decoder:** A lightweight model that converts the processed patch representations back into raw bytes.

Rather than using a fixed number of bytes for every patch, BLT uses **entropy patching**. It calculates the entropy of the next byte using a small auxiliary model:

$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$

By identifying high-uncertainty points, the model can group bytes into patches of varying sizes. This allows the model to skip expensive computations for predictable data (like spaces or common suffixes) and dedicate more parameters to complex, high-entropy sequences.

## The 'Byte-ifying' Workflow

One of the most practical contributions of this paper is the ability to "byte-ify" an existing model. Training a large byte-level model from zero is a massive undertaking. The researchers explored a workflow where they could leverage the massive knowledge already stored in a pre-trained model, like Llama 3.1, and "translate" that knowledge into a byte-level framework.

The workflow follows these specific steps:

1.  **Weight Initialization:** The parameters of the Global Latent Transformer in the BLT architecture are initialized using the weights of a pre-trained, token-based model (e.g., Llama 3.1).
2.  **Partial Training:** The model is then trained to adapt these weights to the new byte-patch format.
3.  **Differential Learning Rates:** To preserve the knowledge inherited from the original model while allowing the new local components to learn, the researchers use a specific learning rate schedule. They update the weights of the global transformer using only one-tenth the learning rate employed for the local encoder and local decoder.

## Why This Matters

This approach provides a shortcut to creating more robust models. By "byte-ifying" a model like Llama 3.1, the resulting architecture inherits the massive amount of world knowledge and reasoning capabilities from the original pre-training. At the same time, it gains the inherent advantages of byte-level modeling, such as:

*   **Improved Robustness:** Better handling of noisy inputs (like typos or random character insertions).
*   **Character-Level Understanding:** Enhanced ability to perform tasks involving orthographic similarity and phonology, which often trip up standard tokenizers.
*   **Efficiency:** The potential to reduce inference FLOPs by up to 50% by utilizing larger patches for predictable data.

The results are compelling. The researchers found that a BLT model "byte-ified" from Llama 3.1 significantly outperformed both the original Llama 3 and a BLT model trained from scratch with the same FLOP budget. This suggests that we don't always have to choose between the established power of token-based models and the superior robustness of byte-level architectures; we can potentially have both.
