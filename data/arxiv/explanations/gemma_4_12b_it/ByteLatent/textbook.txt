\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

The Limitations of Tokenization in Large Language Models

# The Mechanism of Subword Tokenization (e.g., Byte-Pair Encoding)

In the current landscape of natural language processing, Large Language Models (LLMs) are almost entirely trained end-to-end, with one notable exception: the preprocessing step known as tokenization. Tokenization serves as a heuristic method for grouping raw bytes into a static set of discrete units called tokens. The most prevalent method for achieving this is Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). In a BPE system, a fixed vocabulary is determined before the training process begins. This vocabulary defines the "atoms" of the model, where the model learns to predict the next token in a sequence rather than the next individual byte of data.

Because tokenization is a pre-processing step, it imposes a specific structure on how a string is compressed and represented. The model does not interact with the raw bytes directly; instead, it sees a sequence of integers corresponding to the entries in the predefined vocabulary. While this simplifies the task for the model by providing larger semantic chunks, it creates a fundamental disconnect between the model's internal computations and the underlying orthographic reality of the text. By forcing the data into a fixed vocabulary, the model is restricted by the choices made during the tokenizer's construction.

Furthermore, a critical consequence of this mechanism is that the model loses direct access to the underlying byte features. Because the tokens are "pre-baked" groupings, the model's ability to understand the internal structure of a word or the relationship between adjacent characters is mediated—and often obscured—by the token boundaries. This creates a ceiling on the model's ability to perform tasks that require a granular understanding of text, as the model is essentially looking at the data through a filter that prioritizes compression heuristics over linguistic or structural nuance.

# Bias and Inequities: Domain sensitivity, modality sensitivity, and multilingual inequity

The reliance on a fixed set of tokens introduces significant biases into how an LLM perceives and processes information. Because tokens are induced using specific compression heuristics, these heuristics do not always correlate with the actual complexity of the predictions the model needs to make. This leads to "domain sensitivity," where a model may perform significantly differently depending on the subject matter, simply because the tokenizer was optimized for one type of data (such as general web text) and performs poorly on another (such as specialized code or scientific literature). Similarly, "modality sensitivity" occurs when the tokenizer fails to represent different types of data inputs in a consistent or efficient manner.

One of the most pressing issues caused by tokenization is "multilingual inequity." Because the vocabulary is finite, certain languages—particularly those with less representation in the training data used to build the tokenizer—are often represented by much smaller or less efficient tokens. This means that for the same amount of computational effort, the model may be able to process much more information in a dominant language (like English) than in a lower-resource language. This disparity creates a systemic inequality in the performance and accessibility of LLMs across different global cultures.

Ultimately, tokenization biases the model's internal "worldview" by pre-determining which patterns are considered important enough to be grouped together. Instead of the model learning to identify these patterns dynamically based on the complexity of the data it encounters, it is forced to operate within the confines of a human-designed (or heuristically-designed) compression scheme. This lack of flexibility means that the model cannot adapt its focus to the specific information density of a given context, leading to inefficiencies and performance bottlenecks in diverse applications.

# The Orthographic Knowledge Gap: Why tokens fail at character-level understanding

A major shortcoming of token-based LLMs is their lack of "orthographic knowledge," which refers to the model's awareness of the spelling, structure, and constituent parts of words. Because a tokenizer groups bytes into subword units, the model often treats these units as monolithic blocks. It may know that a specific token represents a word, but it may not truly "understand" how that word is constructed from its individual characters. This gap is particularly evident in tasks that require the manipulation of text at a granular level, such as spelling, character deletion, or orthographic similarity.

Research has shown that while token-based models might seem to "know" how to spell because they have seen the tokens before, they struggle significantly when asked to manipulate those spellings. For instance, in tasks involving character manipulation—such as inserting or deleting specific characters or identifying words with similar spellings—token-based models often fail because they cannot "see" the internal components of the token. They lack the ability to reason about the sub-word aspects of the input data, which are essential for tasks like phonology or precise text editing.

This gap is further highlighted by benchmarks like the CUTE benchmark, which tests a model's ability to understand composition, orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation. Token-based models frequently struggle with these tasks because they cannot effectively utilize the knowledge of a token's spelling to perform operations on it. In contrast, a model that works directly with bytes or characters can maintain a continuous understanding of the text's structure, allowing it to perform these manipulations with much higher accuracy. The inability to bridge this gap means that token-based models remain fundamentally limited in their ability to handle tasks that require a deep, character-level mastery of language.

# Sensitivity to Input Noise and Out-of-Vocabulary issues

Tokenization makes LLMs remarkably brittle when faced with "noisy" inputs. Because the model relies on a fixed vocabulary, any input that deviates slightly from the expected format can cause the tokenizer to break the text into fragments that the model is not well-equipped to handle. If a sequence of bytes does not match a known token exactly, the model may be forced to process it as a series of much smaller, less meaningful units. This sensitivity to noise can lead to a significant degradation in performance, as the model's learned representations of "meaning" are tied to specific, clean token sequences.

The paper identifies several specific ways that noise can disrupt token-based models, such as "AntSpeak" (where text is converted to uppercase, space-separated characters), "Drop" (where random characters are removed), "RandomCase" (where capitalization is randomized), "Repeat" (where characters are repeated), and simple "UpperCase" transformations. For a token-based model, these variations can fundamentally change how the input is tokenized, effectively "breaking" the model's understanding of the prompt or the completion. Because the model lacks a direct connection to the underlying bytes, it cannot easily reconstruct the intended meaning from these distorted versions of the text.

Furthermore, tokenization creates a perpetual struggle with "out-of-vocabulary" (OOV) issues. Whenever the model encounters a term, name, or technical jargon that was not included in the fixed vocabulary during the pre-processing stage, it must resort to fallback mechanisms. These fallbacks often result in highly fragmented representations that lack the semantic richness of a single token. By contrast, a byte-level model avoids the OOV problem entirely; since it operates on the raw bytes that compose all text, it can technically represent any possible string of characters, making it inherently more robust to the "long-tail" of data and more resilient to unexpected input variations.

# The Computational Trade-off: Why we don't simply train on raw bytes (Sequence length vs. Transformer costs)

If byte-level modeling offers such clear advantages in robustness, orthographic knowledge, and equity, a natural question arises: why do we use tokenization at all? The answer lies in the computational cost of the Transformer architecture. The primary reason we do not simply train LLMs on raw bytes is that the resulting sequence lengths would be prohibitively long. For example, a sentence that might be represented by 20 tokens could consist of 100 or more individual bytes. Since the computational cost of a Transformer is heavily influenced by the number of steps (sequence length) it must process, training on raw bytes at scale becomes extremely expensive.

In a standard Transformer, the computational cost is dominated by the large feed-forward network layers that run on every unit in the sequence. When the units are bytes, these expensive layers must be executed for every single byte of the input. This makes training small models on bytes somewhat feasible, but it becomes a massive barrier to scaling. Tokenization was introduced as a way to "compress" the sequence length, allowing the model to process larger amounts of information in fewer steps. It essentially trades the richness of byte-level detail for the sake of computational efficiency.

However, this trade-off has a hidden cost: tokenization allocates the same amount of compute to every token, regardless of its complexity. For instance, a large Transformer does not need to expend significant effort to predict the final letter of a common word (a low-entropy, easy decision), yet in a token-based model, that word's token consumes the same amount of "compute" as a complex, high-entropy decision, such as choosing the first word of a new sentence. This leads to an inefficient allocation of resources. The goal of new architectures like the Byte Latent Transformer is to break this trade-off by grouping bytes into dynamic patches, allowing the model to maintain the benefits of byte-level data while achieving the efficiency of, or even surpassing, token-based models.

From Bytes to Patches: Dynamic Data Segmentation

# Defining Patching: The mapping of bytes to latent representations

In the standard paradigm of Large Language Models (LLMs), text is typically processed as tokens—discrete units from a fixed vocabulary. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) introduces a fundamental shift by moving away from these fixed vocabularies toward a system of dynamic "patches." A patch is essentially a group of bytes that serves as the primary unit of computation within the model. Rather than selecting a token from a pre-defined list, the model processes a sequence of bytes and groups them into patches based on the complexity and context of the data.

Formally, this process is described by a patching function, denoted as $f_p$. This function takes a sequence of bytes $\pmb{x}=\{x_i,|i=1,\ldots n\}$ of length $n$ and segments them into a sequence of $m$ patches $\pmb{p}=\{p_j|j=1,\ldots,m\}$, where $m < n$. The function $f_p$ maps each individual byte $x_i$ to a binary value $\{0,1\}$, where a value of 1 signifies the start of a new patch. Because BLT does not rely on a fixed vocabulary, these patches are not restricted to specific strings; instead, any arbitrary group of bytes can be mapped to a latent patch representation.

The primary motivation for this shift lies in the allocation of computational resources. In a Transformer architecture, the computational cost is largely determined by the number of steps executed by the main model. In the context of BLT, each step corresponds to a patch. By grouping bytes into patches, the model can decide how to distribute its "attention" and processing power. If a sequence of bytes is predictable and simple, it can be grouped into a larger patch, requiring fewer transformer steps. Conversely, if the bytes are complex and high-entropy, they can be grouped into smaller patches, allowing the model to dedicate more parameters and more computation to those specific sections.

# Static Patching: Strided Patching (K-bytes) and its limitations

The most basic way to implement the grouping of bytes into patches is through strided patching. In this scheme, bytes are grouped into patches of a fixed size, $k$. This is a straightforward method because it is easy to implement during both training and inference. Because the stride is constant, it provides a simple mechanism for researchers to control the average patch size and, by extension, the total floating-point operations (FLOPs) required to process the data.

However, strided patching suffers from significant architectural limitations, primarily because it fails to allocate compute dynamically. In a strided system, the model spends the same amount of computational effort on every $k$ bytes, regardless of the information density of those bytes. This leads to inefficiencies: for instance, the model might waste a transformer step on a sequence of whitespace or simple punctuation, while simultaneously failing to allocate sufficient compute to complex segments like mathematical equations or dense technical descriptions.

Furthermore, strided patching leads to inconsistent and non-contextual segmenting. Because the boundaries are fixed by a rigid count of $k$, the same word or phrase might be split differently depending on where it appears in a sequence. This lack of linguistic awareness can hinder the model's ability to form coherent representations of common units of meaning, as the boundaries of the patches do not align with the actual structure of the language.

# Heuristic Patching: Space Patching and linguistic boundaries

To address the limitations of strided patching, researchers have explored heuristic methods that use linguistic markers to define boundaries. One such method is space patching. In this approach, new patches are created after any "space-like" bytes. Space-like bytes are defined as any byte that is not a Latin character, a digit, or a UTF-8 continuation byte. Additionally, the patching rules ensure that each patch contains at least one non-space-like byte.

The intuition behind space patching is that spaces serve as natural boundaries for linguistic units in many languages. By creating a new patch at every space, the model effectively allocates a latent transformer step—and thus more compute—to model every word. This is particularly useful because the first bytes of a word are typically the most difficult to predict. For example, predicting the first letter of a response to a question is computationally harder than predicting the subsequent letters of that same word. Once the first letter is established, the completion of the word becomes a comparatively easy, low-entropy decision.

While space patching is an improvement over strided patching because it ensures words are patched consistently across different sequences, it still has drawbacks. It cannot gracefully handle every language or technical domain where spaces might not be the primary delimiter. Most importantly, space patching remains a rigid heuristic; it does not provide a mechanism to vary the patch size based on the actual complexity of the information, which is a key goal of the BLT architecture.

# Entropy Patching: Using a small Byte LM to identify high-uncertainty boundaries

The Byte Latent Transformer introduces a more sophisticated, data-driven method called entropy patching. Instead of relying on hard-coded rules like whitespace, entropy patching uses a small, byte-level auto-regressive language model to identify points of high uncertainty in the data. The goal is to identify boundaries where the next byte is difficult to predict, as these are the points where the model requires more computational capacity.

To do this, a small byte-level language model is trained on the training data. This model provides an entropy estimate $H(x_i)$ for each byte $x_i$ under the distribution $p_{e}$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. The entropy is calculated as:
$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$

The architecture uses these entropy values to identify patch boundaries through two different methods. The first method uses a global entropy threshold, where a new patch begins whenever $H(x_t) > \theta_g$. The second method looks for relative changes in uncertainty, identifying points where the entropy is significantly higher than the previous byte: $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$. This second approach is useful for identifying points that break a trend of monotonically decreasing entropy within a patch. This process is performed during a lightweight preprocessing step as data is loaded, allowing the model to dynamically adjust its grouping based on the actual information density of the input.

# The Property of Incremental Patching vs. BPE

A critical distinction between the patching method used in BLT and the Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) used in models like Llama 3 is the property of incremental patching. For a patching scheme $f_p$ to be considered incremental, it must satisfy the following mathematical property:
$f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}$
This means that the decision of where to place a patch boundary at position $i$ must depend only on the bytes that have appeared before $i$, and not on any future bytes in the sequence.

BPE tokenization is not an incremental patching scheme. In BPE, the same prefix of a string can be tokenized in multiple different ways depending on what characters follow it. This is because BPE looks for the longest possible matches from a fixed vocabulary, and the "best" match can change depending on the remainder of the string. Because BPE is not incremental, the model cannot decide whether it is at a patch boundary without knowing the entire sequence. 

In contrast, because BLT uses an incremental patching scheme (like entropy patching), the model can decide whether to invoke the expensive Latent Transformer at any given step based solely on the history of the sequence. This allows for a more fluid and dynamic allocation of compute during the generation process, as the model can determine the necessity of a transformer step independently of the yet-to-be-generated future.

# The Patch Size vs. Computation relationship: How patch size controls FLOPs

The fundamental advantage of the patch-based architecture over token-based architectures lies in the relationship between patch size and computational cost (FLOPs). In standard LLMs using BPE, there is a strict trade-off between vocabulary size and compute. If you want to use larger tokens (which reduces the number of steps), you must increase the size of the output projection layer and the embedding table. This limits how much you can increase the average token size without significantly increasing the model's parameter count.

BLT breaks this trade-off. Because patches are not pulled from a fixed vocabulary, BLT can increase the average patch size without increasing the output dimension of the model. This leads to a direct relationship where inference FLOPs are inversely proportional to the average patch size. Longer patches save compute because the expensive global latent transformer is run less frequently.

This creates a new dimension for scaling models. Because longer patches save compute, those "saved" FLOPs can be reallocated to grow the size of the global latent transformer itself. Effectively, for a fixed inference budget, BLT can simultaneously grow both the size of the patches and the size of the model. This allows the model to become more powerful (larger parameters) while remaining efficient (fewer steps due to larger patches), a scaling path that is not available to traditional token-based architectures.

# The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) Architecture

# The High-Level Architecture Overview

The Byte Latent Transformer, or BLT, represents a fundamental shift in how large language models process information. While traditional models rely on a fixed vocabulary of tokens—a heuristic preprocessing step that groups bytes into static units—BLT operates directly on raw bytes. However, processing every single byte through a massive Transformer layer is computationally expensive. To solve this, the BLT architecture introduces a three-part system designed to dynamically allocate computational resources: a local encoder, a global latent transformer, and a local decoder.

The overarching philosophy of this architecture is the separation of concerns between fine-grained byte processing and high-level sequence modeling. The local encoder takes the raw byte sequence and compresses it into a series of "patches." These patches are latent representations—compact, mathematical summaries of groups of bytes. Once the data is in this patch format, it is passed to the global latent transformer. This global model is the "brain" of the system; it performs the heavy lifting of reasoning and prediction by operating on the patches rather than the individual bytes. Finally, the local decoder takes the output of the global transformer and reconstructs those latent patches back into a sequence of raw bytes.

By using this sandwich-like structure, BLT achieves a significant efficiency gain. It avoids running the largest, most expensive layers of the model on every single byte. Instead, it only invokes the global transformer when moving from one patch to the next. This allows the model to be more efficient than token-based models because it can dynamically adjust the size of these patches based on the complexity of the data, essentially deciding where the most "thinking" is required.

# The Latent Global Transformer: Processing high-level latent representations

At the core of the BLT architecture lies the Latent Global Transformer, denoted as $\mathcal{G}$. This component is an autoregressive transformer model consisting of $l_{\mathcal{G}}$ layers. Its primary responsibility is to map a sequence of latent input patch representations, $p_j$, into a sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$. The subscript $j$ is used specifically to denote patches, distinguishing them from the subscript $i$, which denotes individual bytes.

The Latent Global Transformer is designed to be the most computationally intensive part of the architecture. Because it operates on patches—which are groups of multiple bytes—it processes fewer "steps" than a model that operates on individual bytes. This allows the model to be scaled to very large sizes (such as 8 billion parameters) while remaining efficient. To ensure the model maintains the correct temporal flow of information, it utilizes a block-causal attention mask. This mask restricts the model's attention so that it can only look at the current patch and previous patches within the same document, preventing it from "looking ahead" into future data.

Because $\mathcal{G}$ consumes the bulk of the floating-point operations (flops) during both training and inference, it is the primary lever for controlling the model's compute budget. By determining the boundaries of the patches, the architecture determines how many times the Latent Global Transformer is invoked. This provides a mechanism to allocate more compute to complex sequences (where patches are small) and less compute to predictable sequences (where patches are large), a feat that is difficult to achieve with fixed-vocabulary tokenization.

# The Local Encoder: Byte-to-Patch mapping

The transition from raw bytes to latent patches is handled by the Local Encoder model, denoted as $\mathcal{E}$. Unlike the global transformer, the local encoder is designed to be lightweight, consisting of a much smaller number of layers ($l_{\mathcal{E}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$). Its role is to transform a sequence of input bytes, $b_i$, into the expressive patch representations, $p_j$, that the global transformer requires as input.

The encoding process begins by embedding the input bytes. Each byte $b_i$ is mapped to a vector $x_i$ using an embedding matrix of size $\mathbb{R}^{256 \times h_{\mathcal{E}}}$. These initial embeddings are then augmented with extra contextual information, specifically hash n-gram embeddings, to ensure the model understands the local neighborhood of each byte. These augmented embeddings are then fed into a series of alternating transformer layers and cross-attention layers.

A defining feature of the local encoder is the use of a "local block causal" attention mask. This means that each byte $b_i$ can only attend to a fixed window of $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ preceding bytes. Crucially, this window is allowed to cross the dynamic boundaries of the patches being formed, but it cannot cross the boundaries of different documents. This ensures that the encoder captures sufficient local context to create a meaningful representation for each patch before passing that representation to the global model.

# Encoder Hash n-gram Embeddings: Capturing local context with RollPolyHash

To make the local encoder robust and expressive, BLT incorporates information about the bytes immediately surrounding any given position. It does this by modeling each byte $b_i$ not just in isolation, but as part of a byte n-gram. For every byte position $i$, the model constructs n-grams $g_{i,n}$ of various sizes, specifically for $n$ ranging from 3 to 8. 

To handle these n-grams efficiently without creating a massive and unmanageable vocabulary, BLT uses a hashing mechanism. Each n-gram $g_{i,n}$ is passed through a hash function, $\text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n})$, and then mapped to an index in a fixed-size embedding table $E_{n}^{hash}$. The resulting embedding is added to the original byte embedding $x_i$. The final augmented embedding, $e_i$, is calculated as:

$$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n = 3,...,8}E_{n}^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$

where the hash is computed as:

$$\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_{n}^{hash}|$$

After summing these hash embeddings, the final vector $e_i$ is normalized by the number of n-gram sizes plus one (in this case, 8). This method allows the model to maintain a rich understanding of local character patterns—such as common prefixes, suffixes, or sub-word structures—while keeping the memory footprint of the embedding layer constant.

# Multi-Headed Cross-Attention: Pooling byte representations into patches

The most critical architectural step in the encoder is the conversion of many byte representations into a single patch representation. This is achieved through a multi-headed cross-attention module, which draws inspiration from the Perceiver architecture. In this module, the goal is to "pool" the information from all the bytes that constitute a specific patch $p_j$ into a single latent vector.

To start this process, a query vector is created for each patch. This query is initialized by pooling the byte representations that make up patch $p_j$ (denoted as $f_{\text{bytes}}(p_j)$) and then passing them through a linear projection $\mathcal{E}_{C} \in \mathbb{R}^{h_{\mathcal{E}} \times (h_{\mathcal{E}} \times U_{\mathcal{E}})}$, where $U_{\mathcal{E}}$ is the number of cross-attention heads. These queries then attend to the keys and values derived from the byte representations $h_{l-1,i}$ from the previous layer.

The update rule for the patch representations $P$ across layers is:

$$P_l = P_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$$

In this equation, $Q_j$ is the query for the patch, while $K_i$ and $V_i$ are the keys and values for the individual bytes. A specific masking strategy is applied here: each query $Q_j$ is only allowed to attend to the keys and values that correspond to the bytes within its specific patch $j$. This ensures that the patch representation $p_j$ is a compressed summary of its constituent bytes. The hidden states of the encoder, $h_l$, are updated simultaneously using a standard transformer layer.

# The Local Decoder: Patch-to-Byte reconstruction

Once the global transformer $\mathcal{G}$ has finished its computation, it produces a sequence of output patch representations, $o_j$. These are high-level concepts that need to be translated back into human-readable (or machine-readable) bytes. This is the role of the local decoder, $\mathcal{D}$. Like the encoder, the decoder is a lightweight transformer-based model with a small number of layers ($l_{\mathcal{D}} \ll l_{\mathcal{G}}$).

The decoder's job is to predict the sequence of raw bytes $y_i$ as a function of the previously decoded bytes and the latent information provided by the global model. The architecture of the decoder is designed to be the inverse of the encoder. Instead of compressing bytes into patches, it expands patch representations back into a sequence of bytes.

A key structural difference in the decoder is the ordering of its operations. In the decoder, the cross-attention layer is applied *before* the transformer layer. This is because the cross-attention layer is used to "re-inflate" the patch representations into byte-level representations. Once these byte-level representations are established, they are passed into the decoder's transformer layers to perform the final autoregressive prediction of the next byte in the sequence.

# Decoder Cross-Attention: Reversing the query/key roles for byte generation

To perform the reconstruction, the decoder employs a cross-attention module that essentially reverses the roles of the queries and keys used in the encoder. In the encoder, the patches were the queries and the bytes were the keys/values. In the decoder, the bytes are the queries, and the patches are the keys and values.

The process begins with an initial byte representation $D_0$, which is set to the hidden representations from the last layer of the encoder, $h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$. For each subsequent layer $l$ in the decoder, the model calculates a new set of byte representations $B_l$. These are computed using the following logic:

$$D_0 = h_{l_{\mathcal{E}}}$$
$$B_l = D_{l-1} + W_o\left(\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\right)$$
$$D_l = \text{Decoder-Transformer-layer}_l(B_l)$$

In this setup, the query $Q_i$ is derived from the byte representation $d_{l-1,i}$ from the previous decoder layer. The keys $K_i$ and values $V_i$ are derived from the patch representations $o_j$ produced by the global model, after they have been processed by a linear transformation and split operation $\mathcal{D}_C$. 

By setting the bytes as the queries, the model asks: "Given the current byte and the sequence of bytes predicted so far, which parts of the global patch representations are most relevant for determining the next byte?" The cross-attention mechanism allows the model to attend to the global "concepts" while maintaining the fine-grained precision required to output individual bytes. The resulting representation $D_l$ is then processed by a standard decoder transformer layer to produce the final output.

# Scaling Laws and Performance Benchmarks

# Defining Training FLOPs for Byte-Level Models

To evaluate the performance of large language models (LLMs) fairly, researchers must establish a standardized way to measure the amount of "work" a model performs during training. This is typically measured in Floating Point Operations (FLOPs), which represent the number of elementary arithmetic operations the hardware must execute. In the context of the Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}), calculating these FLOPs is slightly more complex than in standard token-based models because {\textsc BLT} operates on a mixture of individual bytes and dynamically sized patches.

The researchers base their FLOPs estimation on the standard equations used for transformer models, specifically those derived from the Chinchilla scaling laws. These calculations include the operations required for the feed-forward layers, the query-key-value-output (QKVO) projections in the self-attention layers, and the operations for the attention mechanism itself and the final output projection. However, a specific adjustment is made for {\textsc BLT}: the input embedding layer is treated as an efficient lookup rather than a dense matrix multiplication, meaning it is counted as a 0-flop operation. Furthermore, the authors account for the fact that the backward pass during training typically requires twice as many FLOPs as the forward pass.

Because {\textsc BLT}} uses a multi-part architecture, the total FLOPs per byte ($\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}$) is the sum of the computations for three distinct components: the local encoder, the global latent transformer, and the local decoder, along with the cross-attention blocks that connect them. The formula for this calculation is:

$\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)$

In this equation, $n_{ctx}$ represents the sequence length in bytes, while $n_p$ is the patch size. The variables $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, $h_{\mathcal{E}}$, and $h_{\mathcal{D}}$ refer to the hidden dimensions of the global, encoder, and decoder models respectively, and $l$ represents their number of layers. The variable $w$ denotes the window size for the local models. The term $V$ represents the vocabulary size, which is only relevant for the final projection in the local decoder. The ratio $r$ is the ratio of queries to key/values, and $k$ represents the ratio of the patch dimension to the byte dimension (the number of local model splits concatenated to form a global representation). By accounting for these variables, the researchers can precisely compare the computational cost of {\textsc BLT} against token-based models on a per-byte basis.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB): A tokenizer-independent metric for perplexity

A standard way to measure how well a language model predicts a sequence is through perplexity. However, perplexity is inherently tied to the specific tokenizer used; a model using a large vocabulary of complex tokens will naturally have different perplexity numbers than a model using a small vocabulary of simple bytes. Because {\textsc BLT}} aims to eliminate the dependency on a fixed vocabulary, the researchers introduce Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a tokenizer-independent alternative to perplexity.

BPB provides a way to compare the uncertainty of a byte-level model like {\textsc BLT}} with a token-based model like Llama 3. It measures the uncertainty over the data as captured by the cross-entropy loss ($\mathcal{L}_{CE}$), but it normalizes this loss by the total number of bytes in the sequence rather than the number of tokens. The formula for BPB is:

$\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$

By using this metric, the researchers can demonstrate how well {\textsc BLT}} models are learning the underlying structure of the data regardless of how that data is "chunked" during the prediction process. This allows for a head-to-head comparison of the "raw" predictive power of the two different architectures.

# Compute-Optimal Scaling: Comparing BLT with Llama 3

To determine if {\textsc BLT}} is a viable architecture for large-scale use, the researchers conducted a study on compute-optimal scaling. A "compute-optimal" regime is one where the ratio of model parameters to the amount of training data is tuned to achieve the best possible performance within a fixed total computational budget. The researchers used the Llama 2 dataset—comprising 2 trillion tokens—to train various sizes of both standard BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding) models and {\textsc BLT}} models, ranging from 1B to 8B parameters.

The results showed that {\textsc BLT}} models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across all scales when trained in a compute-optimal regime. This was a significant finding, as it was the first time a byte-level transformer architecture achieved matching scaling trends with state-of-the-art BPE models. The authors noted that while simple patching methods (like space-patching) show improvement over basic byte-level models, they still fall short of Llama 3's performance. The success of {\textsc BLT}} is attributed to the combination of its unique architecture—specifically the use of cross-attention and n-gram hash embeddings—and its dynamic entropy-based patching scheme.

Furthermore, the study highlighted how different tokenizers affect performance for BPE models; for instance, models using the Llama 3 tokenizer outperformed those using the Llama 2 tokenizer on the same data. Interestingly, {\textsc BLT}}'s performance trends between the Llama 2 and Llama 3 BPE models when using larger patch sizes (e.g., 8 bytes). This suggests that as patch sizes increase, the model begins to behave more like a token-based model, but with the added benefits of a dynamic, learnable boundary system.

# The 'Patches Scale Better Than Tokens' Hypothesis

A central hypothesis of this research is that "patches scale better than tokens." This stems from a fundamental limitation in token-based models: the tradeoff between vocabulary size and compute. In a standard LLM, if you want larger tokens (which means fewer steps and lower inference costs), you must increase the vocabulary size. This leads to a much larger output dimension for the final projection layer, which limits how much you can grow the tokens without incurring massive computational overhead.

{\textsc BLT}} breaks this tradeoff because it does not use a fixed vocabulary for its patches. Because patches are dynamically grouped based on the entropy of the next byte, the model can choose to use very long patches for predictable data. Longer patches mean fewer steps are required by the global latent transformer. This creates a unique opportunity for scaling: because the global transformer is run less frequently, the computational "savings" from longer patches can be reallocated to grow the size of the global transformer itself. This allows {\textsc BLT}} to simultaneously increase both the model size and the patch size while staying within the same inference flop budget.

# Trading Inference Flops for Model Size: Simultaneous scaling of patches and parameters

To test the "Patches Scale Better Than Tokens" hypothesis, the researchers conducted a fixed-inference scaling study. In this experiment, they kept the inference flop budget constant and compared models of different sizes and patch lengths. They compared BPE models using the Llama 2 and Llama 3 tokenizers against {\textsc BLT}} models using entropy-based patching with average patch sizes of 6 and 8 bytes.

The results demonstrated that {\textsc BLT}} models achieve superior scaling trends compared to token-based architectures. In both small and large inference flop classes, the BPE models performed better only at very small training budgets. As the training budget increased, the {\textsc BLT}} models quickly surpassed the BPE models. The researchers observed that this advantage becomes more pronounced at larger model scales. Specifically, as the total parameters grow from 400M to 8B, the number of parameters in the local byte-level modules only roughly doubles, while the global latent transformer grows much more significantly. 

Because the local modules' parameters scale more slowly than the global transformer, the efficiency gains from larger patch sizes (which only affect the global transformer's frequency) are preserved as the model grows. This means that for a fixed inference budget, a {\textsc BLT}} model can be significantly larger than a token-based model, leading to better performance without increasing the cost of generating text.

# Experimental Setup: Data mixtures (Llama 2 and BLT-1T datasets)

The experimental framework for this study relied on two primary datasets to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the scaling laws. The first is the Llama 2 dataset, which contains 2 trillion tokens collected from a diverse variety of public sources. This dataset was used primarily to establish the compute-optimal training regimes and to determine the best architectural choices for {\textsc BLT}} by comparing its scaling trends against established BPE baselines.

The second dataset is the {\textsc BLT}}-1T dataset, a new collection of 1 trillion tokens. This dataset includes a subset of the pre-training data released by Datacomp-LM and was used for complete pre-training runs to compare {\textsc BLT}} against Llama 3 on downstream tasks. Notably, neither of these datasets included data from Meta products or services, ensuring a neutral evaluation environment. For the baseline comparisons involving token-based models, the researchers utilized the Llama 3 tokenizer with a 128K vocabulary, as it provided stronger baseline performance than the older Llama 2 tokenizer. This setup allowed for a rigorous, head-to-head comparison of the byte-level and token-level approaches across different scales and data mixtures.

Robustness and Long-Tail Generalization

The transition from token-based language modeling to byte-level modeling represents a fundamental shift in how models perceive and process human language. While traditional Large Language Models (LLMs) rely on a fixed vocabulary of subword tokens, the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture operates directly on the raw bytes that constitute text. This shift is not merely a technical implementation detail; it carries profound implications for the model's ability to handle "noisy" data, perform character-level tasks, and generalize to low-resource languages. Because tokenizers are heuristic pre-processing steps, they often create a bottleneck where the model loses access to the underlying orthographic knowledge of the data. By operating at the byte level, BLT retains this granular information, allowing it to excel in areas where token-based models typically struggle.

# Robustness to Input Noise

One of the primary advantages of byte-level modeling is its inherent robustness to input noise. In standard tokenization-based models, a single character change or a minor perturbation in the input string can lead to a completely different token or even an "out-of-vocabulary" error. This sensitivity makes such models brittle when faced with real-world data that may contain typos, formatting inconsistencies, or deliberate noise. BLT, by contrast, processes the individual bytes, making it much more resilient to variations at the character level.

To quantify this robustness, the researchers evaluated the models using five distinct noising strategies applied to benchmark classification tasks. The first strategy, AntSpeak, transforms the entire text into uppercase, space-separated characters. The second, Drop, involves the random removal of $10\%$ of the characters. The third, RandomCase, randomly alternates $50\%$ of the characters between uppercase and lowercase. The fourth, Repeat, repeats $20\%$ of the characters up to a maximum of four times. Finally, the UpperCase strategy transforms all characters into uppercase. 

The results demonstrate a significant advantage for the BLT architecture. When tested on the HellaSwag benchmark under these various noise conditions, BLT consistently outperformed the token-based baseline. On average, BLT showed an 8-point advantage over the tokenizer-based model trained on the same data. Remarkably, BLT even outperformed the Llama 3.1 model, which had been trained on a significantly larger and more diverse dataset. This suggests that the architectural choice to model bytes directly provides a structural defense against noise that even massive scale in token-based models cannot fully compensate for.

# Phonology: Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) Task Performance

Beyond simple robustness to noise, byte-level modeling provides the model with a deeper "awareness" of how characters combine to form linguistic units. A key way to measure this is through phonology, specifically the Grapheme-to-Phoneme (G2P) task. In this task, the model must map a sequence of graphemes—the letters or characters that represent a word—into a transcription of how that word is actually pronounced (phonemes). 

For a tokenizer-based model, a word like "knight" is often represented as a single token ID. The model might "know" the word is associated with a certain meaning, but it lacks the internal representation of the individual letters and how they interact to produce a specific sound. Because BLT processes bytes, it can learn the underlying patterns of how specific character sequences correlate with sounds. 

The researchers tested BLT on the Phonology Bench in a 5-shot setting. The results showed that the BLT model outperformed the Llama 3 1T tokenizer-based model. This ability to perform well on G2P tasks highlights a crucial qualitative advantage: by not "abstracting away" the characters into tokens too early, the BLT architecture preserves the sub-word information necessary for tasks that require an understanding of the internal structure of words.

# The CUTE Benchmark: Character Manipulation and Orthographic Similarity

The limitations of tokenization are perhaps most visible in tasks involving character manipulation and orthographic similarity. The CUTE benchmark was designed specifically to challenge models on these aspects. It includes tasks that require the model to understand the composition of words, recognize words that are orthographically similar (looking alike), and manipulate sequences of characters.

Tokenizer-based models often fail these tasks because they possess knowledge of a token's meaning but struggle to utilize the spelling of that token. For example, while a model might know the word "apple," it may struggle to perform a task that requires deleting or inserting a specific letter because the letters themselves are not the primary units of computation. BLT-Entropy, however, demonstrated exceptional proficiency in these areas. 

The BLT model outperformed the BPE Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on the CUTE benchmark. In particular, the BLT model achieved a near-perfect score of $99.9\%$ on spelling tasks, which involve the direct manipulation of characters. This massive improvement, achieved despite BLT being trained on 16 times less data than Llama 3.1, underscores that character-level information is a fundamental piece of knowledge that token-based models find difficult to learn.

# Low-Resource Machine Translation: Generalizing to Long-Tail Byte Sequences

Tokenization introduces a significant bias toward the languages and domains represented most frequently in the training data. This is because the vocabulary of a tokenizer is constructed based on the most common subword patterns. Consequently, "long-tail" data—such as low-resource languages, rare dialects, or specialized technical scripts—suffers from multilingual inequity. In these cases, the tokenizer may break words into very small, fragmented pieces, making it harder for the model to learn meaningful representations.

BLT addresses this by modeling raw bytes, which allows it to generalize much more effectively to the long-tail of byte sequences. To test this, the researchers evaluated the model's performance on the FLORES-101 benchmark, which includes translations into and out of twenty-one lower-resource languages across six different language families. 

The results showed that BLT outperformed the Llama 3 model, achieving a 2-point advantage when translating into English and a 0.5-point advantage when translating from English. While the performance was comparable to Llama 3 on popular language pairs, the consistent advantage on numerous lower-resource language pairs highlights the effectiveness of byte modeling. By removing the fixed-vocabulary bottleneck, BLT can learn the patterns of any script or language with the same underlying mechanism.

# Byte-ifying Pre-trained Models: Distilling Knowledge from Llama 3.1

A final contribution of the research is a method for "byte-ifying" existing pre-trained tokenizer-based models. This process allows the benefits of the BLT architecture to be combined with the vast knowledge already stored in state-of-the-art models like Llama 3.1. The researchers developed a workflow where they initialized the global transformer parameters of a BLT model with the weights of a pre-trained Llama 3.1 model. 

In this setup, the local encoder and local decoder models are trained from scratch, while the global transformer is updated using a significantly lower learning rate—specifically, one-tenth the learning rate used for the local models. This effectively "re-trains" the pre-trained knowledge of Llama 3.1 to work on a byte-level representation rather than a token-level one.

The results of this distillation were striking. The "BLT from Llama 3.1" significantly outperformed both the original Llama 3 and the baseline BLT models (which were trained with the same flop budget). Notably, even though it was not trained on the same massive amount of data as Llama 3.1, this "byte-ified" version achieved superior performance on the MMLU task compared to the BLT-Entropy model trained on 1T tokens. This suggests that converting a pre-trained tokenizer-based model into a tokenizer-free BLT framework is an effective way to retain high-level reasoning capabilities while gaining the robustness and flexibility of byte-level processing.

Ablation Studies and Future Horizons

# Ablating Patching Schemes

To understand the effectiveness of the Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}), it is necessary to examine how different methods of grouping raw bytes into patches influence the model's performance. The primary goal of patching is to reduce the number of steps the global transformer must take, thereby controlling the computational cost. If the patching scheme is inefficient, the model may waste computation on simple, predictable sequences or fail to allocate enough capacity to complex, high-information segments. The researchers compared several strategies to determine the optimal way to segment these data units.

The most basic method discussed is strided patching, where bytes are grouped into patches of a fixed size $k$. While this is easy to implement and provides a consistent way to control the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) by simply changing $k$, it is fundamentally limited. Because it is a fixed rule, it does not account for the actual complexity of the data. For instance, a strided patch might fall on a sequence of whitespace, which is computationally "easy" to predict, yet the model still spends a full transformer step on it. Conversely, a sequence of complex mathematical notation might be split across two patches, depriving the model of the dense compute needed to process it. This lack of dynamic allocation makes strided patching inferior to more context-aware methods.

Space patching offers a significant improvement by using linguistic boundaries as a heuristic. In this scheme, new patches are created after "space-like" bytes—any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte. The intuition here is that spaces often represent the ends of words, which are natural units of meaning. By ensuring that words are generally contained within patches, the model maintains consistency across different sequences. This prevents the same word from being split differently in different contexts. However, while space patching is more effective than strided patching, it still relies on a human-defined rule and cannot dynamically adjust the size of the patches based on the difficulty of the information.

The most sophisticated method is entropy patching, which uses a data-driven approach to identify boundaries. This method involves training a small, auxiliary byte-level language model to estimate the entropy $H(x_i)$ of the next byte. The entropy is calculated using the formula:
$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$
where $p_{e}$ is the probability distribution over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. By identifying bytes with high entropy, the model can detect points of high uncertainty and complexity. Patch boundaries are then placed at these high-entropy points. This allows {\textsc BLT} to group bytes into patches of relatively uniform information density. Experimental results show that entropy-based patching consistently outperforms both space and strided patching because it allows for the dynamic allocation of compute exactly where the data complexity demands it.

# The Necessity of Cross-Attention and Hash n-gram Embeddings

Beyond the patching strategy, the specific architectural components of {\textsc BLT}—namely hash n-gram embeddings and cross-attention layers—are vital for matching the performance of traditional tokenization-based models. Because {\textsc BLT} operates on raw bytes rather than pre-defined tokens, it must find ways to "see" the sub-word structures that tokenizers normally provide. Hash n-gram embeddings solve this by allowing the model to incorporate information about surrounding bytes into the representation of each individual byte.

For every byte position $i$, the model constructs byte-grams $g_{i,n}=\{b_{i-n + 1},\ldots, b_i\}$ for various sizes $n$ ranging from three to eight. These n-grams are passed through a hash function to map them to an index in a fixed-size embedding table $E_{n}^{hash}$. The resulting embedding is added to the initial byte embedding $x_i$ to create an augmented embedding $e_i$:
$$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n = 3,...,8}E_{n}^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$
where $\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_{n}^{hash}|$. This mechanism provides the model with a sense of orthographic knowledge, helping it understand how characters cluster into words and phonemes. The ablation studies show that these hash embeddings are critical for bringing the performance of {\textsc BLT} up to the level of BPE-based models, particularly on datasets like Wikipedia and GitHub where structural character patterns are prevalent.

While n-gram embeddings help the model understand the bytes, cross-attention modules are required to bridge the gap between individual bytes and the global latent patches. The local encoder uses cross-attention to pool the representations of multiple bytes into a single patch representation $p_j$. This involves a query vector $Q_j$ (derived from the patch) attending to the keys $K_i$ and values $V_i$ of the bytes that comprise that patch. Conversely, the local decoder uses cross-attention to expand the global patch representations $o_j$ back into a sequence of raw bytes. In the decoder, the roles are reversed: the byte representations act as the queries, and the patch representations act as the keys and values. Ablation results indicate that cross-attention in the decoder is particularly effective, as it helps the model reconstruct the fine-grained byte sequence from the high-level latent information.

# Impact of Entropy Model Hyperparameters (Size and Context Window)

The success of entropy-based patching depends heavily on the quality of the entropy estimates provided by the auxiliary model. Since this model determines where the "hard" parts of the data are, its capacity and its "field of vision" (context window) are crucial hyperparameters. The researchers conducted ablations to see how the size of this entropy model (ranging from 1 million to 100 million parameters) and its context window (ranging from 64 to 512 bytes) affected the final {\textsc BLT} performance.

The results indicate that the performance of the final model is positively correlated with both the size and the context window of the entropy model. This is intuitive: a larger model with a wider window can more accurately identify complex transitions and patterns in the data, leading to more precise patch boundaries. However, the researchers also observed a point of diminishing returns. Once the entropy model exceeds 50 million parameters, the improvements in the final {\textsc BLT} model's performance begin to level off. This suggests that while a sufficiently capable entropy model is necessary to capture the nuances of the data, there is a limit to how much extra capacity in the auxiliary model will actually benefit the main transformer.

# Current Limitations: Wall-clock time vs. FLOP parity

One of the important distinctions made in the research is the difference between theoretical computational cost (FLOPs) and actual execution time (wall-clock time). When comparing {\textsc BLT} to Llama 3, the researchers primarily use FLOP-controlled scaling. This means they compare models that require the same number of total floating-point operations to train or run. Under this metric, {\textsc BLT} is highly competitive, matching the performance of BPE-based models while offering the possibility of significant inference efficiency gains by using larger patches.

However, a remaining challenge is that current transformer libraries and hardware optimizations are heavily tuned for standard tokenization-based architectures. Because {\textsc BLT} uses a non-standard architecture—specifically its local encoder/decoder and dynamic patching masks—it may not yet achieve the same wall-clock speed as a vanilla Transformer on the same hardware. To mitigate this, the researchers employ FlexAttention to produce fused implementations for their dynamic masks, but the paper acknowledges that further optimizations may be needed to reach parity with the highly optimized software stacks used by token-based models. This distinction is important for practical deployment, where the time it takes to finish a forward pass is often as critical as the theoretical number of operations performed.

# Future Directions: End-to-end learnable patching and larger-scale byte models

Looking forward, the researchers identify several promising avenues for improving the Byte Latent Transformer. One primary goal is to move from a two-stage patching process to an end-to-end learnable system. Currently, {\textsc BLT} uses a separately trained entropy model to decide where patches begin. A more seamless approach would be to integrate the patching mechanism directly into the transformer's training objective, allowing the model to learn the optimal grouping of bytes as part of its global optimization process. This would potentially eliminate the need for an auxiliary model and allow for even more fluid data compression.

Another significant area for exploration involves the scaling laws of {\textsc BLT}. Because the current study used the scaling laws of Llama 3 (which is a BPE model) to determine the optimal number of training steps for {\textsc BLT}, there is a possibility that the current training regime is suboptimal. Future work will aim to establish dedicated scaling laws for byte-level models, which may reveal even more favorable trends for the {\textsc BLT} architecture. Additionally, the researchers are interested in "byte-ifying" existing pre-trained models. By initializing the global transformer of a {\textsc BLT} model with the weights of a pre-trained model like Llama 3.1, they hope to create a way to transform established token-based models into robust, byte-aware models without the need to start training from scratch.
