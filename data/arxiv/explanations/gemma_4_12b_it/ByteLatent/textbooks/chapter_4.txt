\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

Chapter 4: # Scaling Laws and Performance Benchmarks

# Defining Training FLOPs for Byte-Level Models

To evaluate the performance of large language models (LLMs) fairly, researchers must establish a standardized way to measure the amount of "work" a model performs during training. This is typically measured in Floating Point Operations (FLOPs), which represent the number of elementary arithmetic operations the hardware must execute. In the context of the Byte Latent Transformer ({\textsc BLT}), calculating these FLOPs is slightly more complex than in standard token-based models because {\textsc BLT} operates on a mixture of individual bytes and dynamically sized patches.

The researchers base their FLOPs estimation on the standard equations used for transformer models, specifically those derived from the Chinchilla scaling laws. These calculations include the operations required for the feed-forward layers, the query-key-value-output (QKVO) projections in the self-attention layers, and the operations for the attention mechanism itself and the final output projection. However, a specific adjustment is made for {\textsc BLT}: the input embedding layer is treated as an efficient lookup rather than a dense matrix multiplication, meaning it is counted as a 0-flop operation. Furthermore, the authors account for the fact that the backward pass during training typically requires twice as many FLOPs as the forward pass.

Because {\textsc BLT}} uses a multi-part architecture, the total FLOPs per byte ($\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}}$) is the sum of the computations for three distinct components: the local encoder, the global latent transformer, and the local decoder, along with the cross-attention blocks that connect them. The formula for this calculation is:

$\text{FL}_{\text{BLT}} = \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{G}}, l_{\mathcal{G}}, m=n_{ctx}/n_p, V=0) / n_p + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=w_{\mathcal{E}}, V=0) + \text{Transf. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=w_{\mathcal{D}}, V=256) + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{E}}, l_{\mathcal{E}}, m=n_p, r=n_p/k) \times k/n_p + \text{Cross Attn. FL}(h_{\mathcal{D}}, l_{\mathcal{D}}, m=k, r=k/n_p)$

In this equation, $n_{ctx}$ represents the sequence length in bytes, while $n_p$ is the patch size. The variables $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, $h_{\mathcal{E}}$, and $h_{\mathcal{D}}$ refer to the hidden dimensions of the global, encoder, and decoder models respectively, and $l$ represents their number of layers. The variable $w$ denotes the window size for the local models. The term $V$ represents the vocabulary size, which is only relevant for the final projection in the local decoder. The ratio $r$ is the ratio of queries to key/values, and $k$ represents the ratio of the patch dimension to the byte dimension (the number of local model splits concatenated to form a global representation). By accounting for these variables, the researchers can precisely compare the computational cost of {\textsc BLT} against token-based models on a per-byte basis.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB): A tokenizer-independent metric for perplexity

A standard way to measure how well a language model predicts a sequence is through perplexity. However, perplexity is inherently tied to the specific tokenizer used; a model using a large vocabulary of complex tokens will naturally have different perplexity numbers than a model using a small vocabulary of simple bytes. Because {\textsc BLT}} aims to eliminate the dependency on a fixed vocabulary, the researchers introduce Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a tokenizer-independent alternative to perplexity.

BPB provides a way to compare the uncertainty of a byte-level model like {\textsc BLT}} with a token-based model like Llama 3. It measures the uncertainty over the data as captured by the cross-entropy loss ($\mathcal{L}_{CE}$), but it normalizes this loss by the total number of bytes in the sequence rather than the number of tokens. The formula for BPB is:

$\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2) \cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$

By using this metric, the researchers can demonstrate how well {\textsc BLT}} models are learning the underlying structure of the data regardless of how that data is "chunked" during the prediction process. This allows for a head-to-head comparison of the "raw" predictive power of the two different architectures.

# Compute-Optimal Scaling: Comparing BLT with Llama 3

To determine if {\textsc BLT}} is a viable architecture for large-scale use, the researchers conducted a study on compute-optimal scaling. A "compute-optimal" regime is one where the ratio of model parameters to the amount of training data is tuned to achieve the best possible performance within a fixed total computational budget. The researchers used the Llama 2 dataset—comprising 2 trillion tokens—to train various sizes of both standard BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding) models and {\textsc BLT}} models, ranging from 1B to 8B parameters.

The results showed that {\textsc BLT}} models either match or outperform their BPE counterparts across all scales when trained in a compute-optimal regime. This was a significant finding, as it was the first time a byte-level transformer architecture achieved matching scaling trends with state-of-the-art BPE models. The authors noted that while simple patching methods (like space-patching) show improvement over basic byte-level models, they still fall short of Llama 3's performance. The success of {\textsc BLT}} is attributed to the combination of its unique architecture—specifically the use of cross-attention and n-gram hash embeddings—and its dynamic entropy-based patching scheme.

Furthermore, the study highlighted how different tokenizers affect performance for BPE models; for instance, models using the Llama 3 tokenizer outperformed those using the Llama 2 tokenizer on the same data. Interestingly, {\textsc BLT}}'s performance trends between the Llama 2 and Llama 3 BPE models when using larger patch sizes (e.g., 8 bytes). This suggests that as patch sizes increase, the model begins to behave more like a token-based model, but with the added benefits of a dynamic, learnable boundary system.

# The 'Patches Scale Better Than Tokens' Hypothesis

A central hypothesis of this research is that "patches scale better than tokens." This stems from a fundamental limitation in token-based models: the tradeoff between vocabulary size and compute. In a standard LLM, if you want larger tokens (which means fewer steps and lower inference costs), you must increase the vocabulary size. This leads to a much larger output dimension for the final projection layer, which limits how much you can grow the tokens without incurring massive computational overhead.

{\textsc BLT}} breaks this tradeoff because it does not use a fixed vocabulary for its patches. Because patches are dynamically grouped based on the entropy of the next byte, the model can choose to use very long patches for predictable data. Longer patches mean fewer steps are required by the global latent transformer. This creates a unique opportunity for scaling: because the global transformer is run less frequently, the computational "savings" from longer patches can be reallocated to grow the size of the global transformer itself. This allows {\textsc BLT}} to simultaneously increase both the model size and the patch size while staying within the same inference flop budget.

# Trading Inference Flops for Model Size: Simultaneous scaling of patches and parameters

To test the "Patches Scale Better Than Tokens" hypothesis, the researchers conducted a fixed-inference scaling study. In this experiment, they kept the inference flop budget constant and compared models of different sizes and patch lengths. They compared BPE models using the Llama 2 and Llama 3 tokenizers against {\textsc BLT}} models using entropy-based patching with average patch sizes of 6 and 8 bytes.

The results demonstrated that {\textsc BLT}} models achieve superior scaling trends compared to token-based architectures. In both small and large inference flop classes, the BPE models performed better only at very small training budgets. As the training budget increased, the {\textsc BLT}} models quickly surpassed the BPE models. The researchers observed that this advantage becomes more pronounced at larger model scales. Specifically, as the total parameters grow from 400M to 8B, the number of parameters in the local byte-level modules only roughly doubles, while the global latent transformer grows much more significantly. 

Because the local modules' parameters scale more slowly than the global transformer, the efficiency gains from larger patch sizes (which only affect the global transformer's frequency) are preserved as the model grows. This means that for a fixed inference budget, a {\textsc BLT}} model can be significantly larger than a token-based model, leading to better performance without increasing the cost of generating text.

# Experimental Setup: Data mixtures (Llama 2 and BLT-1T datasets)

The experimental framework for this study relied on two primary datasets to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the scaling laws. The first is the Llama 2 dataset, which contains 2 trillion tokens collected from a diverse variety of public sources. This dataset was used primarily to establish the compute-optimal training regimes and to determine the best architectural choices for {\textsc BLT}} by comparing its scaling trends against established BPE baselines.

The second dataset is the {\textsc BLT}}-1T dataset, a new collection of 1 trillion tokens. This dataset includes a subset of the pre-training data released by Datacomp-LM and was used for complete pre-training runs to compare {\textsc BLT}} against Llama 3 on downstream tasks. Notably, neither of these datasets included data from Meta products or services, ensuring a neutral evaluation environment. For the baseline comparisons involving token-based models, the researchers utilized the Llama 3 tokenizer with a 128K vocabulary, as it provided stronger baseline performance than the older Llama 2 tokenizer. This setup allowed for a rigorous, head-to-head comparison of the byte-level and token-level approaches across different scales and data mixtures.
