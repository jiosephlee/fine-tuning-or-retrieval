\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Byte Latent Transformer: Patches Scale Better Than Tokens"}

# Beyond Tokens: Why the Next Generation of LLMs Might Forget Tokenizers Entirely

Current large language models (LLMs) are trained almost entirely end-to-end, with one notable exception: tokenization. Before a model ever sees a string of text, a tokenizer groups individual bytes into "tokens" based on heuristic rules. While this has been the standard for years, it introduces significant limitations. Tokenization creates biases in how strings are compressed, leads to domain-specific sensitivities, struggles with input noise, and often results in multilingual inequity because the vocabulary is rarely balanced across all languages.

Furthermore, tokenization creates a rigid trade-off between vocabulary size and compute. Increasing the vocabulary size allows for larger tokens (which reduces the number of steps the model must take), but it also increases the size of the output projection layer, consuming more memory and parameters. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) proposes a way to break free from this fixed-vocabulary paradigm by processing raw bytes directly through a dynamic patching system.

## From Tokens to Patches

The core innovation of BLT is the shift from tokens to "patches." Unlike tokens, which are drawn from a fixed vocabulary determined before training, patches in BLT are dynamically grouped sequences of bytes. These patches serve as the primary units of computation.

A significant advantage of this approach is the ability to dynamically allocate compute. In standard tokenization-based models, the same amount of compute is spent on every token, regardless of its complexity. For example, predicting the final letter of a common word is computationally "easy" and low-entropy, while choosing the first word of a new sentence is "hard" and high-entropy. 

BLT addresses this by segmenting data into patches based on the entropy of the next-byte prediction. By using a small, lightweight byte-level model to estimate entropy, the system identifies where information density is high and creates patch boundaries accordingly. The entropy $H(x_i)$ is calculated as:

$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$

This allows the model to group bytes into patches with relatively uniform information density, ensuring that the "heavy" part of the model—the Global Latent Transformer—is invoked only when necessary.

## The BLT Architecture

To manage the transition from raw bytes to high-level representations without a fixed vocabulary, BLT utilizes a three-part architecture:

*   **Local Encoder:** This lightweight module takes a sequence of raw bytes and maps them into latent patch representations. It uses a series of transformer layers and cross-attention modules to pool byte representations into patches. To improve robustness, it incorporates hash n-gram embeddings ($e_i$), which help the model understand sub-word structures:
    $$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n = 3,...,8}E_{n}^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$
*   **Latent Global Transformer:** This is the "brain" of the model. It is a large autoregressive transformer that operates exclusively on the latent patch representations produced by the encoder. Because it processes patches rather than individual bytes, it significantly reduces the number of steps required during inference.
*   **Local Decoder:** This module performs the inverse of the encoder, taking the global model's patch representations and decoding them back into the original raw bytes.

## Efficiency and Scaling Laws

One of the most compelling results of the BLT research is how patches scale compared to tokens. Because patch sizes are dynamic and not bound by a fixed vocabulary, BLT offers a new dimension for scaling. In a fixed inference-flop budget, a model can simultaneously increase its patch size and its global model size. 

When the model identifies a predictable sequence of bytes, it can group them into a longer patch. Because the Global Latent Transformer is run less frequently for longer patches, those saved flops can be "reallocated" to grow the size of the global transformer itself. The researchers demonstrated that BLT can match the performance of Llama 3 at scales up to 8B parameters while offering up to 50% fewer flops at inference.

## Improved Robustness and Generalization

By moving away from the "black box" of tokenization and working directly with bytes, BLT demonstrates superior robustness to input noise. In experiments using strategies like random character deletion or casing changes, BLT outperformed tokenizer-based models significantly.

Furthermore, BLT shows a much deeper understanding of orthographic and sub-word knowledge. It excelled on the CUTE benchmark, which tests a model's ability to manipulate text at the character level—a task where many tokenizer-based models struggle because they lack direct access to the underlying bytes. BLT also showed improved performance in low-resource machine translation, proving that byte-level modeling is a more equitable foundation for handling the "long tail" of human language.

By moving beyond the constraints of fixed vocabularies, the Byte Latent Transformer provides a blueprint for a more efficient, robust, and linguistically fair generation of large language models.
