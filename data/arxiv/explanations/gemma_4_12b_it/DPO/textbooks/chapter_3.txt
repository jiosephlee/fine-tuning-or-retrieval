\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 3: The Bottlenecks of Reinforcement Learning in Alignment

# Complexity of the RLHF Pipeline

To understand the motivation behind Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), one must first understand the standard procedure for aligning large language models, known as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). While RLHF has been highly successful in producing models that are helpful and safe, the pipeline required to achieve these results is remarkably complex. The standard RLHF framework is not a single step but a multi-stage process that involves training and maintaining several different versions of large language models simultaneously.

The process typically begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where a pre-trained model is trained on high-quality, human-written demonstrations. Once an SFT model, denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, is established, the next phase involves "Reward Modeling." In this stage, the model is prompted to generate multiple outputs, which are then ranked by humans. These rankings are used to train a separate reward model, $r_\phi$, which acts as a proxy for human preferences. This reward model is essentially a regressor that learns to output a scalar score for any given completion.

The final and most difficult stage is the RL optimization phase. Here, the model being trained (the policy, $\pi_\theta$) is updated to maximize the scores provided by the reward model $r_\phi$. However, because the language model is being optimized via reinforcement learning, it cannot be updated in a straightforward supervised manner. Instead, it must interact with the reward model in a loop. This multi-stage architecture—moving from a base model to an SFT model, then to a reward model, and finally to an RL-optimized policy—creates a massive engineering overhead. It requires managing different model weights, ensuring consistency between the reward model and the policy, and coordinating a pipeline where errors in one stage (such as a poorly calibrated reward model) can cascade into the final RL training.

# Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms

Even if the RLHF pipeline is perfectly engineered, the actual process of reinforcement learning is notoriously unstable. Most modern RLHF implementations rely on Actor-Critic algorithms, most notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). These algorithms are designed to solve the problem of training a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize a reward while ensuring the model does not drift too far from its original behavior. This is managed by adding a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to the objective function:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

The instability stems from the nature of the policy gradient. In many actor-critic settings, the policy gradient can suffer from extremely high variance. To mitigate this, researchers often use a "baseline" or a "value function" to center the rewards. However, as the paper highlights, without an accurate normalization term or baseline, the gradients can become erratic, making the training process difficult to converge.

The paper provides a theoretical diagnosis of this instability by linking it to the "control as inference" framework. It shows that standard RLHF objectives can be viewed as optimizing a specific reward class. In these traditional methods, the policy gradient often lacks a necessary normalization term—specifically the partition function $Z(x)$, which represents the "soft value function" of the reference policy. Because this term is difficult to estimate accurately during the RL loop, the training becomes prone to fluctuations that can prevent the model from learning smoothly.

# The 'Value Function' Problem and the Need for Reward Normalization

A significant hurdle in RLHF is the variance of the reward model's outputs. Because reward models $r_\phi$ are trained on human preferences, their raw scores can vary wildly or exhibit high variance across different prompts. To make training manageable, prior works often had to impose manual normalization constraints, such as ensuring that the expected reward over a dataset is zero.

The theoretical core of the DPO paper addresses this by looking at the relationship between the reward function and the optimal policy. The optimal policy $\pi_r$ for a given reward $r$ can be expressed as:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

Here, $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. In traditional RLHF, $Z(x)$ is treated as a "soft value function." The problem is that $Z(x)$ is an expensive sum over all possible outputs $y$, which is computationally impossible to calculate directly for a language model. 

Because we cannot easily compute $Z(x)$, traditional RLHF methods have to rely on "baselines"—often just a single sample estimate of the reward—to stabilize the gradients. DPO solves this by using a change of variables. It identifies that the reward function can be reparameterized in a way that makes the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out when comparing two different completions. By mathematically eliminating the need to compute $Z(x)$, DPO removes the "Value Function" problem entirely, allowing for a stable objective that does not require reward normalization or complex baselines.

# Computational Costs of Sampling from the LM Policy

One of the most significant practical bottlenecks of RLHF is the computational cost associated with sampling. In a standard supervised learning setup, you feed a prompt into a model, and it produces a completion. You calculate the loss, and you update the weights. This is a "one-pass" operation.

In contrast, RLHF (specifically PPO) requires the model to sample completions *during* the training loop. For every prompt $x$ in a training batch, the policy $\pi_\theta$ must generate multiple completions $y$ so that the reward model can score them. These samples are then used to calculate the policy gradient. Because generating text is an autoregressive process (where each token depends on the previous ones), this sampling is extremely slow and computationally expensive. 

This creates a "sampling bottleneck": the training speed of the model is limited by how fast it can generate text. As models get larger (e.g., 6B parameters and beyond), the cost of generating these samples for every training step becomes prohibitive. DPO eliminates this bottleneck by converting the RL objective into a simple binary cross-entropy loss. Instead of sampling completions to see how "good" they are, DPO looks at a fixed dataset of preferred and dispreferred completions ($y_w$ and $y_l$) and directly optimizes the probabilities of those specific completions. This turns a generative sampling problem into a standard classification-style optimization.

# Hyperparameter Sensitivity in Traditional RLHF

Finally, the RLHF pipeline is notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters, making it difficult to replicate results or scale the method to new tasks. Because RLHF involves an interplay between the reward model's scale, the KL coefficient $\beta$, the learning rate of the policy, and the specific RL algorithm's parameters (like those in PPO), even small changes can lead to "model degeneration." Model degeneration occurs when the policy finds a way to "cheat" the reward model—generating gibberish or repetitive text that happens to receive a high score from $r_\phi$ without actually being useful.

To prevent this degeneration, researchers must carefully tune the KL penalty to ensure the model stays close to the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$. However, finding the "sweet spot" for this penalty is often a matter of trial and error, requiring many expensive training runs. 

The DPO method is designed to be much more robust. By simplifying the objective to a binary cross-entropy loss, DPO reduces the number of moving parts. The authors note that DPO requires "virtually no tuning of hyperparameters" compared to the complex sweeps required for PPO. By providing a stable, mathematically grounded objective that avoids the pitfalls of high-variance policy gradients and expensive sampling, DPO offers a much more accessible path to aligning language models with human preferences.
