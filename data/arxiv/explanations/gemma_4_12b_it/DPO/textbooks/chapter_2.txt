\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: Foundations of RLHF and Preference Modeling

# Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): The first step in alignment

Before a language model can be effectively aligned with human preferences, it must first be capable of performing the specific tasks desired by the user. While large-scale unsupervised language models are trained on massive datasets to acquire broad world knowledge and reasoning capabilities, they are inherently unsupervised. This means they learn to imitate the data they were fed, which includes a vast variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets—some of which may be undesirable, incorrect, or unsafe. For instance, a model might understand a common misconception because it appeared frequently in its training data, but we do not want the model to repeat that misconception as a fact.

To address this, the first step in the alignment pipeline is Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). In this phase, the pre-trained language model is fine-tuned using a curated set of high-quality human demonstrations for specific downstream tasks, such as dialogue, summarization, or coding. By training on these high-quality examples, the model begins to learn the desired format and style of the responses. The resulting model is denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This model serves as the foundational policy for the subsequent stages of alignment, providing a starting point that is already biased toward helpful and high-quality outputs.

# Human preference data collection: Pairwise comparisons

While SFT is a powerful tool for teaching a model how to behave, it relies on high-quality human demonstrations, which can be difficult and expensive to collect at scale. A more scalable and often more accessible method for obtaining human feedback is to collect relative preferences rather than absolute demonstrations. In this approach, humans are presented with two different completions for the same prompt and asked to indicate which one is better.

To collect this data, the model $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is prompted with a set of inputs $x$. For each prompt, the model generates a pair of answers, $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then presented to human labelers, who choose a preferred completion, denoted as $y_w$ (the winning/preferred response) and a dispreferred completion, $y_l$. This process yields a dataset of pairwise comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$.

The underlying intuition here is that there exists some latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$ that represents the human preference. We do not have direct access to this function $r^*$, but we can observe its behavior through the human choices. The goal of the preference learning stage is to approximate this hidden reward function as accurately as possible using the collected comparisons.

# The Bradley-Terry (BT) Model: Modeling human choice mathematically

To turn these qualitative human preferences into a quantitative mathematical framework, we utilize a preference model. A widely used choice in the literature is the Bradley-Terry (BT) model. The BT model provides a way to calculate the probability that a human will prefer one response over another based on their respective reward values.

The BT model stipulates that the probability $p^*$ of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ given a prompt $x$ is given by:

\begin{equation}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

This formula shows that the probability of preference is determined by the difference in rewards between the two completions. If $r^*(x, y_1)$ is significantly higher than $r^*(x, y_2)$, the probability of preferring $y_1$ approaches 1. Conversely, if the rewards are equal, the probability is 0.5. This model is essential because it allows us to frame the problem of learning from preferences as a supervised learning problem: we want to find a model that maximizes the likelihood of the observed human preferences.

# Reward Model Training: Parameterizing and optimizing $r(x, y)$

Once we have the Bradley-Terry framework, we can train a neural network to act as our reward model. We parametrize this reward model as $r_{\phi}(x, y)$, where $\phi$ represents the learnable parameters of the network. In the context of language models, this network is often initialized using the $\pi^\text{SFT}$ model, with a linear layer added on top of the final transformer layer to produce a single scalar value representing the reward.

To train the reward model, we use the dataset of human preferences $\mathcal{D}$. We treat this as a binary classification task where the "correct" class is the preferred response. The objective is to minimize the negative log-likelihood loss:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

Here, $\sigma$ represents the logistic function. By minimizing this loss, we are essentially training the model to ensure that the predicted reward for the human-preferred completion $y_w$ is consistently higher than the reward for the dispreferred completion $y_l$. To ensure the reward function remains stable and has lower variance, prior works often normalize the rewards such that the expected reward across the dataset is zero: $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

# The RLHF objective: Maximizing reward with KL-divergence constraints

After training the reward model $r_\phi$, the final stage of the traditional RLHF pipeline is to fine-tune the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize this reward. However, simply maximizing the reward can lead to several issues, such as the model "drifting" too far from the original distribution, losing its generation diversity, or collapsing into a few "safe" but repetitive answers (mode-collapse).

To prevent this, the optimization objective includes a penalty based on the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. The objective is formulated as:

\begin{equation}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]
\end{equation}

In this equation, $\beta$ is a hyperparameter that controls how much we want to restrict the model from deviating from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (which is typically the initial $\pi^\text{SFT}$ model). The KL-divergence term ensures that the new policy $\pi_\theta$ remains close to the distribution of the original model while still attempting to maximize the reward $r_\phi$.

Because language generation is a discrete process, this objective is not directly differentiable. Therefore, it is typically optimized using reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In practice, this is achieved by constructing a modified reward function that incorporates the KL penalty directly:

\begin{equation}
    r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))
\end{equation}

The goal of RLHF is to maximize this modified reward, which balances the desire for high-quality outputs with the need to maintain the linguistic properties and diversity of the original model.
