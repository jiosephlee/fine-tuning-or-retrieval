\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Language Model Alignment

# Capabilities and limitations of unsupervised pre-training

Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) have demonstrated an impressive ability to acquire broad world knowledge and sophisticated reasoning skills. These models are typically trained on massive datasets containing vast amounts of human-generated text. Through this self-supervised process, the models learn to predict the next token in a sequence, effectively internalizing the patterns, facts, and linguistic structures present in the training data. While this allows them to perform a wide variety of tasks—from answering general knowledge questions to generating coherent prose—it also introduces a fundamental limitation regarding the model's behavior.

The primary issue stems from the fact that the training data is generated by humans with a diverse array of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Because the training is completely unsupervised, the model essentially learns to imitate the entire distribution of human output, which includes behaviors that may not be desirable in an AI system. For instance, while we want an AI coding assistant to understand common programming mistakes so that it can correct them, we do not necessarily want it to reproduce those same mistakes when generating new code. We want the model to be biased toward the high-quality coding abilities present in the data, even if those high-quality examples are relatively rare compared to the more common errors.

Similarly, a model might learn a common misconception that is believed by a large portion of the population. While it is useful for the model to be aware of such misconceptions to understand human context, it is undesirable for the model to claim those misconceptions as truths in a large percentage of its responses. Consequently, the challenge of unsupervised pre-training is that it provides a model with a vast reservoir of "knowledge and abilities," but it does not provide a mechanism to select which of those abilities should be expressed in a given context.

# The challenge of steerability: selecting desired behaviors from internal knowledge

Because unsupervised models contain a wide spectrum of behaviors, the goal of researchers is to achieve "steerability." Steerability refers to the ability to precisely control a model's behavior so that it aligns with specific human values, such as safety, helpfulness, and accuracy. This is a critical step in building AI systems that are performant and safe. Without steerability, a model's output is essentially a reflection of the average of its training data, which may include biases, hallucinations, or incorrect reasoning.

The central difficulty in steerability is the need to selectively extract desired responses from the model's internal knowledge. We want the model to operate within a specific "behavioral guardrail" while still utilizing its vast capabilities. For example, in a dialogue setting, we want the model to be helpful and engaging, but we also want to ensure it does not provide harmful information. This requires a method of "aligning" the model's vast internal weights to produce outputs that match human preferences. 

To solve this, researchers look for methods that can steer the model toward high-quality, safe, and useful responses. This involves shifting the model from a state where it simply predicts the most likely next token based on a broad dataset to a state where it predicts the most appropriate token based on a specific set of human-defined criteria.

# Overview of the alignment pipeline: from pre-training to instruction-tuning

The standard pipeline for creating a well-behaved and useful language model typically involves multiple stages of refinement. The first stage is the large-scale unsupervised pre-training described previously. This provides the model with its foundational knowledge. Once this foundation is established, the next stage is often "instruction-tuning." This is a form of supervised fine-tuning where the model is trained on a curated dataset of instructions and high-quality human-written completions. Instruction-tuning helps the model generalize to specific tasks and increases its overall usability by teaching it how to follow directions.

However, supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations is not always the most efficient way to achieve perfect alignment. Collecting high-quality expert demonstrations for every possible task can be difficult and expensive. A more scalable approach is to collect human labels regarding the relative quality of different model-generated responses. This leads to the third and most advanced stage of the alignment pipeline: preference learning. In this stage, the model is trained to prefer one output over another, mimicking the way humans judge the quality of different answers.

# Introduction to the Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) paradigm

The most successful class of methods for preference learning is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This process is typically more complex than supervised learning because it involves training multiple models and performing reinforcement learning in a loop. The RLHF pipeline generally consists of two main sub-phases: reward modeling and RL optimization.

In the reward modeling phase, a dataset of human preferences is used to train a reward model $r_{\phi}$. Humans are shown pairs of answers—a preferred completion $y_w$ and a dispreferred completion $y_l$—given a prompt $x$. These preferences are assumed to be generated by some latent reward model $r^*(y, x)$. A common way to model these preferences is the Bradley-Terry model, which states that the probability of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ is:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

A reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is then trained using a binary cross-entropy loss to minimize the difference between the model's predicted preferences and the human labels. 

In the RL optimization phase, the learned reward model is used to provide feedback to the language model policy $\pi_{\theta}$. The objective is to maximize the expected reward while ensuring the model does not drift too far from the original reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (usually the supervised fine-tuned model). This is formulated as:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

Here, $\beta$ is a parameter that controls how much the model is allowed to deviate from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. The KL-divergence constraint is vital because it prevents the model from "mode-collapsing" into a single high-reward answer and ensures it remains within the distribution where the reward model is accurate. However, because language generation is discrete, this objective is not differentiable and usually requires complex algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which are computationally expensive and can be unstable.

# The core motivation for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

The core motivation for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is to simplify the RLHF pipeline by eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning. The authors observe that the RL objective used in RLHF can actually be solved in closed form if we choose a specific parameterization for the reward model. By using a "change of variables," we can derive a mathematical relationship that maps the reward function directly to the optimal policy.

The optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem takes the form:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

where $Z(x)$ is a partition function. By rearranging this equation, we can express the reward function as:

$$r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

Because the Bradley-Terry model only depends on the difference between rewards for two different completions, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out. This allows us to express the human preference probability directly in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}$$

With this derivation, the authors show that they can optimize the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ directly using a simple binary cross-entropy loss, which is the same loss used to train a reward model:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

DPO eliminates the need to train a separate reward model, the need to perform reinforcement learning, and the need to sample from the model during the fine-tuning loop. It is stable, computationally lightweight, and achieves results that are as good as or better than PPO-based RLHF. Essentially, DPO treats the language model as an implicit reward model, allowing for a much simpler and more direct path to aligning the model with human preferences.
