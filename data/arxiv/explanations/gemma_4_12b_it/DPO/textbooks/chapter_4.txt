\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 4: Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

# The optimal policy of the KL-constrained reward maximization problem

To understand the derivation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), we must first establish the standard framework for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). In the traditional RLHF pipeline, after a language model has undergone supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to produce a base model $\pi^\text{SFT}$, the goal is to align the model's behavior with human preferences. This is typically achieved by first training a reward model $r_\phi$ that predicts a scalar reward for a given prompt $x$ and a completion $y$. Once this reward model is learned, the language model is fine-tuned using reinforcement learning to maximize this reward.

However, maximizing the reward in isolation can lead to "reward hacking," where the model finds ways to achieve high scores from the reward model without producing high-quality text, or it may drift too far from the original distribution of the language. To prevent this, the optimization objective includes a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence constraint. The standard RLHF objective is formulated as finding a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected reward while staying close to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (usually the initial SFT model):

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

In this equation, $\beta$ is a hyperparameter that controls the strength of the KL penalty. The KL divergence ensures that the fine-tuned model $\pi_\theta$ does not deviate excessively from $\pi_\text{ref}$, which maintains the diversity of the generation and keeps the model within the distribution of data for which the reward model is reliable.

A critical theoretical result in this area is that the optimal solution to this KL-constrained reward maximization problem has a specific closed-form expression. It can be shown that the optimal policy $\pi_r(y\mid x)$ takes the following form:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

Here, $Z(x)$ represents the partition function, which is a normalization constant ensuring that the probabilities sum to one: $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. This equation reveals a fundamental relationship: the optimal policy is essentially the reference policy weighted by the exponentiated reward, scaled by the inverse of the KL coefficient $\beta$. While this expression is mathematically elegant, it is difficult to use directly in practice because calculating $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible completions $y$ in the vocabulary, which is computationally prohibitive for large language models.

# The change-of-variables: mapping rewards to policy probabilities

The core innovation of DPO lies in a "change-of-variables" approach. Instead of trying to solve the RL problem by first estimating a reward model and then performing RL to find the policy, the authors suggest that we can express the reward function itself in terms of the optimal policy. By rearranging the closed-form expression for the optimal policy derived in the previous section, we can isolate the reward function $r(x, y)$.

Starting with the equation for the optimal policy:
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

We take the logarithm of both sides:
$$\log \pi_r(y\mid x) = \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y) - \log Z(x)$$

By algebraically rearranging these terms to solve for $r(x, y)$, we obtain:
$$r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

This reparameterization is a profound shift in perspective. It shows that the reward function $r(x, y)$ can be decomposed into two parts: a term that represents the relative log-probability of the policy $\pi_r$ compared to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and a term $\beta \log Z(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$ and not on the specific completion $y$. This means that the policy $\pi_r$ implicitly contains all the information necessary to represent the reward function. Because the reward model's primary job in the Bradley-Terry framework is to rank different completions $y$ for a fixed prompt $x$, any term that is constant across all $y$ (like the partition function $Z(x)$) becomes irrelevant to the ranking.

# Canceling the partition function in the Bradley-Terry model

To move from a theoretical reward expression to a practical optimization objective, we must consider how human preferences are modeled. The paper utilizes the Bradley-Terry (BT) model, which is a standard framework for predicting the probability that a human prefers one completion over another. Under the BT model, the probability that a human prefers completion $y_1$ over $y_2$ given prompt $x$ is:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

This can be simplified using the logistic function $\sigma(\cdot)$:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$$

The authors then substitute the reparameterized reward function into this preference model. By replacing $r^*(x, y)$ with the expression derived in the previous section—$\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$—we can see what happens to the difference $r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)$:

$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right) - \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)\right)$$

The partition functions $\beta \log Z(x)$ are identical for both $y_1$ and $y_2$ because they only depend on the prompt $x$. Therefore, they cancel out completely. This leaves us with:
$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)}$$

Substituting this back into the Bradley-Terry probability formula, we get a new expression for the human preference probability that depends only on the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}$$

This is the pivotal step of the DPO derivation. It proves that we can model human preferences directly using the policy parameters, bypassing the need to ever explicitly define or train a separate reward model $r_\phi$.

# Defining the DPO loss function: A binary cross-entropy objective

With the preference probability expressed entirely in terms of the policy, we can now formulate a training objective. In standard RLHF, one would minimize the negative log-likelihood of the preference data to train the reward model. Following that same logic, we can now minimize the negative log-likelihood of the preference data to train the policy $\pi_\theta$ directly.

Given a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ containing triples of prompts $x$, preferred completions $y_w$, and dispreferred completions $y_l$, we define the DPO loss function as:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$

This is a binary cross-entropy objective. It essentially asks the model to maximize the log-probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ relative to the reference model, while simultaneously minimizing the log-probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$ relative to the reference model. The $\beta$ parameter scales the importance of this difference, effectively acting as the KL-divergence coefficient from the original RLHF objective.

Because this objective is differentiable with respect to the parameters $\theta$ of the language model, we can optimize it using standard gradient descent. This eliminates the need for the complex RL training loop (like PPO), the need to sample completions from the model during training, and the need to manage a separate reward model. The policy network now serves a dual purpose: it is the language model being trained, and it implicitly defines the reward function.

# Mechanistic understanding: Analyzing the DPO gradient

To understand how DPO actually updates the model's weights, it is helpful to analyze the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to the parameters $\theta$. By applying the chain rule to the loss function, the gradient can be expressed as:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{higher weight when reward estimate is wrong}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{increase likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{decrease likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg]$$

In this expression, $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ represents the implicit reward assigned by the current model $\pi_\theta$.

The gradient tells us two things. First, the inner term $[\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)]$ shows that the update seeks to increase the log-probability of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decrease the log-probability of the dispreferred completion $y_l$. This is the standard goal of preference learning.

Second, the outer term $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$ acts as a weighting mechanism. This weight is determined by the current "mistake" of the model: if the model currently assigns a much higher reward to the dispreferred completion $y_l$ than to the preferred completion $y_w$, the term inside the sigmoid will be large, making the weight closer to 1. This means the gradient update will be stronger for examples where the model is significantly misaligned with human preferences.

# Weighting mechanism: Preventing model degeneration during updates

The weighting mechanism described above is not just a theoretical artifact; it is essential for the stability of the training process. The paper highlights that if one were to use a "naive" version of this objective—one that simply increases the probability of $y_w$ and decreases the probability of $y_l$ without the $\sigma(\cdot)$ weighting—the language model would quickly degenerate.

Model degeneration in this context refers to the model collapsing into generating nonsensical or repetitive tokens that happen to have high probability but low quality. Without the weighting mechanism, the model might aggressively over-optimize for certain completions, causing the probabilities to become extreme and the model to lose its ability to generate coherent language.

The sigmoid weighting $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$ ensures that the model focuses its learning on the samples that it currently finds most confusing or incorrect. As the model improves and begins to correctly rank $y_w$ over $y_l$, the value of $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w)$ becomes smaller (more negative), the weight $\sigma(\cdot)$ decreases, and the updates become more subtle. This provides a natural "cooling" effect on the optimization, allowing the model to converge smoothly toward the human preference distribution without drifting into degenerate states. This mechanism allows DPO to achieve the same results as complex RL algorithms while remaining computationally lightweight and stable.
