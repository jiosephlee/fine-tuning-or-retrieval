\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does initializing $\pi_{ref}$ via maximum likelihood mitigate distribution shift in DPO?
Question:
In the DPO outline, the authors suggest that if $\pi^{SFT}$ is unavailable, $\pi_{ref}$ should be initialized by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions $(x, y_w)$. The paper claims this helps 'mitigate the distribution shift.' Could someone explain why maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions is a better way to handle distribution shift than simply using a generic pre-trained language model as the reference?
Answer:
To understand why maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions $(x, y_w)$ is a superior way to initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ compared to using a generic pre-trained model, we need to look at the role $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ plays in the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective.

In the DPO framework, the objective function is derived from a KL-constrained reward maximization problem. The final loss function, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\text{ref}})$, implicitly penalizes the model $\pi_{\theta}$ for drifting too far from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ via a KL-divergence term: $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta} \mid \mid \pi_{\text{ref}}]$. This term serves as a vital regularizer; it ensures that while the model learns to prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$, it remains "anchored" to a baseline distribution that maintains linguistic coherence, diversity, and the desired behaviors established during the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) phase.

The problem arises when the original $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ (the model used to generate the preference pairs in the dataset $\mathcal{D}$) is unavailable. If you were to use a generic, raw pre-trained language model as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, you introduce a significant distribution shift. The preference data $\mathcal{D}$ was likely collected from a model that had already undergone SFT to follow instructions or adopt a specific style. A generic pre-trained model, however, has a completely different output distribution. If $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is too far removed from the distribution that produced the training data, the KL-divergence penalty becomes poorly calibrated. The model $\pi_{\theta}$ might be penalized for moving toward the "correct" preferred behaviors simply because those behaviors are far from the generic pre-trained distribution, or it might be allowed to drift into regions of the distribution that are irrelevant to the specific task (like summarization or dialogue).

By initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing the likelihood of preferred completions—$\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]$—you are essentially creating a reference model that is "task-aligned." Instead of picking an arbitrary starting point, you are identifying a model that produces outputs most similar to the "high-quality" examples in your training set. This makes $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ a much more accurate proxy for the missing $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ because it shares the same underlying distribution of preferred responses.

Intuitively, think of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as a "safety rail." If the safety rail is placed in the wrong location (a generic model), the model might be forced to steer in ways that conflict with the preferences, or it might not be held back enough from generating nonsensical text. By fitting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to the preferred completions $y_w$, you ensure the safety rail is placed exactly where the high-quality data resides, thereby mitigating the distribution shift between the unknown source of the data and the reference model used to regularize the training.
