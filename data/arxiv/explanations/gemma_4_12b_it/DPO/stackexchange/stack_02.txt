\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out in the derivation of the DPO objective?
Question:
In Section 4, the paper derives the optimal policy $\pi_r(y|x)$ in Equation 5, which includes a partition function $Z(x)$. When they move to Equation 6, they claim that $Z(x)$ cancels out because the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the difference of rewards between two completions. Can someone explain the algebraic steps or the intuition behind why a term that depends on the prefix $x$ but not the completion $y$ disappears when we look at the difference $r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)$?
Answer:
To understand why the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out, it is helpful to look at both the algebraic derivation and the underlying intuition regarding how reward models are evaluated in the context of human preferences.

From Equation 5, the optimal policy $\pi_r(y|x)$ is defined as:
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$
By taking the natural logarithm of both sides, we get:
$$\log \pi_r(y\mid x) = \log \frac{1}{Z(x)} + \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)$$
Rearranging this to solve for the reward $r(x, y)$ yields Equation 6:
$$r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

The crucial observation here is the dependency of each term. The term $\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$ depends on the specific completion $y$, while the partition function $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is a summation over all possible completions $y$ for a fixed prefix $x$. Therefore, $Z(x)$ is a constant with respect to $y$.

When we apply the Bradley-Terry model (Equation 1), we are interested in the probability $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$, which is determined by the difference in rewards between two specific completions, $y_1$ and $y_2$:
$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$$
If we substitute the reparameterized version of $r^*(x, y)$ into this difference, we see:
$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right) - \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right)$$
Because $Z(x)$ depends only on the prompt $x$ and is identical for both $y_1$ and $y_2$, the $\beta \log Z(x)$ terms subtract to zero. This leaves us with:
$$r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}$$

Intuitively, this happens because the Bradley-Terry model is a relative preference model. It does not care about the absolute "score" of a completion; it only cares about the margin by which one completion is preferred over another. The partition function $Z(x)$ acts as a global normalization constant for a specific prompt $x$. In the reward space, adding or subtracting a constant that depends only on $x$ (like $\beta \log Z(x)$) shifts the rewards of all possible completions $y$ by the same amount. Since the relative ranking of the completions remains unchanged by a uniform shift, the resulting preference probabilities remain identical. This property allows DPO to bypass the need to calculate the computationally expensive partition function $Z(x)$ during training.
