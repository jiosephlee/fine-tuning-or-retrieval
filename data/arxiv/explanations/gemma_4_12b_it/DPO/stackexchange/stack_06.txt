\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Does the $\beta$ parameter in DPO represent the same concept as the $\beta$ in the KL-constrained RL objective?
Question:
In Equation 3, $\beta$ is the coefficient for the KL-divergence constraint. In the DPO objective (Equation 8), $\beta$ appears inside the log terms. Are these mathematically the same $\beta$? If I change $\beta$ in the DPO loss, is it behaving identically to how $\beta$ constrains the policy in the PPO-based RLHF pipeline, or does the change-of-variables introduce a different behavior?
Answer:
Yes, the $\beta$ parameter in the DPO objective represents the same conceptual and mathematical constraint as the $\beta$ used in the KL-constrained reinforcement learning (RL) objective. While the "change of variables" in DPO makes $\beta$ appear in a different position within the formula (as a multiplier for the log-ratios rather than a coefficient for a KL term), the underlying behavior of the constraint on the policy remains identical.

To understand why, we can trace the derivation provided in the paper. In the standard RLHF pipeline, the goal is to maximize the reward while keeping the new policy $\pi_{\theta}$ close to a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This is expressed in Equation 3 as:
$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_{\theta}} [r_{\phi}(x, y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)]$$
In this context, $\beta$ acts as a "leash" or a penalty weight: a larger $\beta$ imposes a stricter penalty on any deviation from the reference model, forcing $\pi_{\theta}$ to remain more similar to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

The paper then shows that the optimal solution to this specific problem (Equation 5) takes the form:
$$\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$
Notice that here, $\beta$ has moved to the denominator of the reward term. This is mathematically consistent with Equation 3; if $\beta$ is very large, the term $\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)$ becomes very small, meaning the reward has very little "power" to push the policy away from the reference distribution.

The "change of variables" happens when we solve for the reward $r(x,y)$ in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$. By taking the logarithm of Equation 5, we obtain Equation 6:
$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$
This equation is the bridge between RLHF and DPO. It shows that the reward $r$ is proportional to $\beta$ times the log-ratio of the policies. When we substitute this reparameterized reward into the Bradley-Terry preference model (which only depends on the difference between rewards of two completions), the partition functions $Z(x)$ cancel out. 

This leads directly to the DPO objective in Equation 8:
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\mathbb{E} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$
In this final form, $\beta$ appears as a multiplier for the log-ratios. Because we substituted $r$ with $\beta \log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$, the $\beta$ here is the exact same $\beta$ from the RL objective. 

Intuitively, the behavior is preserved because of how $\beta$ scales the relationship between "reward" and "policy shift." In the RL objective, a large $\beta$ means you need a massive reward to justify a small move away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In the DPO objective, a large $\beta$ means that even a tiny change in the log-ratio $\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ is magnified into a large reward difference inside the sigmoid function. Consequently, to minimize the loss, the model is forced to keep the log-ratios very small, which effectively keeps the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ very close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Thus, changing $\beta$ in DPO behaves identically to how it constrains the policy in a PPO-based RLHF pipeline.
