\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the equivalence relation between reward functions justify the DPO reparameterization?
Question:
Section 5.1 introduces an equivalence relation where two rewards are equivalent if they differ only by a function of the prefix $f(x)$. The paper uses this to prove Theorem 1, which says DPO doesn't lose any generality. I'm struggling to understand the connection: why is it sufficient to only recover an 'arbitrary' reward function from the optimal class? If we are missing the specific $f(x)$ part of the reward, does that actually matter for the final policy $\pi_\theta$?
Answer:
To understand why the equivalence relation justifies the DPO reparameterization, we need to look at how the reward function interacts with both the preference model (how humans choose) and the optimal policy (how the model acts). The short answer is that any reward component $f(x)$ that depends only on the prefix $x$ has no effect on the relative ranking of completions or the final probability distribution of the policy, which are the only two things we actually care about.

In the Bradley-Terry preference model, the probability of preferring $y_w$ over $y_l$ is given by the logistic function of the difference in rewards: $p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$. If we have two reward functions, $r$ and $r'$, that differ only by a function of the prefix $f(x)$, such that $r(x, y) = r'(x, y) + f(x)$, the difference becomes $(r'(x, y_w) + f(x)) - (r'(x, y_l) + f(x))$. The $f(x)$ terms cancel out completely. This means that from the perspective of the human labeler (or the preference data $\mathcal{D}$), these two reward functions are indistinguishable; they induce the exact same preference distribution.

This logic extends directly to the derivation of the optimal policy. The paper shows that the optimal policy $\pi_r$ is proportional to $\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y))$. When we write the full expression for the policy, we include the partition function $Z(x)$ in the denominator:
$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$
If we substitute $r(x, y) = r'(x, y) + f(x)$ into this equation, the exponential term becomes $\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}r'(x, y)) \cdot \exp(\frac{1}{\beta}f(x))$. Since $f(x)$ does not depend on the completion $y$, the term $\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}f(x))$ can be factored out of the summation in the partition function $Z(x)$. Specifically, the new partition function $Z'(x)$ would be $\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}f(x)) \cdot Z(x)$. When you divide the numerator by the denominator, the $\exp(\frac{1}{\beta}f(x))$ terms cancel out again, leaving $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \pi_{r'}(y\mid x)$.

Because all reward functions within the same equivalence class yield the exact same optimal policy, the "goal" of the optimization is not to find a unique, absolute reward value for every pair $(x, y)$. Instead, the goal is to find any reward function that belongs to the class of functions consistent with the human preferences. The DPO reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ is simply a way to pick one specific representative from each equivalence class—specifically, the one where the partition function $Z(x)$ is normalized to $1$.

In summary, we don't "lose" any generality because the $f(x)$ part of the reward is mathematically redundant. It doesn't change the win rates in the Bradley-Terry model, and it doesn't change the resulting probabilities in the language model's policy. DPO successfully captures the "useful" part of the reward function while ignoring the "arbitrary" part that only depends on the prompt.
