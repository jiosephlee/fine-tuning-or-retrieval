\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does DPO optimize for human preferences without an explicit reward model?
Question:
I am trying to understand the fundamental difference between RLHF and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). The paper states that DPO eliminates the need for explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning. However, if there is no reward model being trained, what is the objective function actually 'rewarding' during the optimization process? Is it simply looking at the labels of the preferred completions, or is there still an underlying reward representation being updated?
Answer:
The core insight of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is that the optimal policy for a given reward function can be expressed in a closed form. In standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), the reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ and the policy $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ are treated as two distinct entities: you train a separate reward model to mimic human preferences and then use reinforcement learning (like PPO) to train the policy to maximize that reward. DPO simplifies this by showing that these two entities are mathematically linked through a change of variables.

To understand how DPO avoids an explicit reward model, we first look at the standard RLHF objective, which aims to maximize the expected reward while staying close to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ via a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence constraint. The paper shows that the optimal solution to this constrained maximization problem takes the form $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$, where $Z(x)$ is a partition function. By rearranging this equation, we can express the reward function $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. 

This reparameterization is the "secret" mentioned in the paper's title. It demonstrates that the reward function is implicitly represented by the log-ratio of the current policy's probability to the reference policy's probability. When we apply this to the Bradley-Terry model—which defines the probability of a human preferring $y_w$ over $y_l$ based on the difference in their rewards—the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out because it is independent of $y$. This allows the authors to substitute the reward model entirely with the policy's own distribution. 

Consequently, when DPO optimizes the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$, the objective is still "rewarding" a conceptual reward, but that reward is now defined as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. Instead of training a separate network to learn what humans like, DPO trains the language model weights so that the model's own probability distribution shifts in a way that mimics a reward function optimized for those preferences. 

In essence, the "underlying reward representation" is not being updated as a separate set of weights; rather, the policy weights themselves are being updated to simultaneously represent the language model and the reward function. The gradient of the DPO loss effectively increases the likelihood of the preferred completion $y_w$ and decreases the likelihood of the dispreferred completion $y_l$, weighted by how incorrectly the current model (acting as an implicit reward model) ranks them. This allows DPO to achieve the same goal as RLHF—aligning the model with human preferences—without the computational complexity of maintaining a separate reward model or running a reinforcement learning loop.
