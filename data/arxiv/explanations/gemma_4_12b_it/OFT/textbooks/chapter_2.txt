\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations: Hyperspherical Energy and Angular Semantics

# Empirical Evidence: Angular vs. Magnitude Information in Neural Networks

To understand the theoretical motivation behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), one must first understand the role that neuron directions play in representing information within a neural network. A core observation in the study of deep learning is that the semantic information processed by a model is often encoded in the angular relationships between neurons rather than the raw magnitude of their activations. This is supported by empirical observations where angular feature differences are shown to characterize semantic gaps more effectively than other metrics.

The paper provides a compelling toy experiment to illustrate this concept. By training a standard convolutional autoencoder on a set of flower images, the researchers compared three different methods of generating feature maps during the testing phase: using the standard inner product (the method used during training), using only the magnitude of the neurons, and using only the angular information of the neurons. The results of this experiment were striking. It was found that the angular information of the neurons could almost perfectly reconstruct the original input images. In contrast, the magnitude information of the neurons contained no useful information for reconstruction.

This experiment is significant because the researchers did not apply a cosine activation during the training phase. This means the model learned to represent the images primarily through the inner products of its kernels. The fact that the angular information alone can recover the semantics implies that the directions (angles) of the neurons are the primary carriers of the knowledge the network has acquired. Therefore, if the goal of finetuning a text-to-image diffusion model is to modify the semantic content of a generated image—such as changing a subject or adding a specific control signal—the most effective way to achieve this is by modifying the directions of the neurons.

# Understanding Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

Building on the idea that angles are the primary carriers of semantic information, the paper introduces a formal mathematical measure to characterize these relationships: Hyperspherical Energy (HE). Hyperspherical energy is a metric used to quantify the pairwise relational structure among neurons within a specific layer of a neural network. Specifically, it measures the level of neuron uniformity on a unit hypersphere.

The hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ is defined by considering each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ in the weight matrix. First, each neuron is normalized to lie on a unit hypersphere, represented as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. Once normalized, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is calculated as the sum of the inverse distances between all unique pairs of these normalized neurons:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

By summing the inverse of the Euclidean distances between all pairs of neurons on the hypersphere, this metric captures how "spread out" or "clumped" the neurons are. If neurons are very close to one another, the distance $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is small, making the inverse value large and increasing the total hyperspherical energy. Conversely, if the neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere, the distances will be larger, resulting in a lower hyperspherical energy. This energy serves as a proxy for the internal structural organization of the network's knowledge.

# Properties of Neurons on the Unit Hypersphere

The concept of hyperspherical energy relies on the behavior of neurons when projected onto a unit hypersphere. This perspective suggests that the capacity of a neural network can be maintained, or even improved, by ensuring that neurons are normalized. Research has shown that training networks with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields comparable capacity to standard training but often provides better generalizability. This is because the direction of a neuron on the hypersphere captures the most critical information from the data.

In the context of text-to-image diffusion models, these hyperspherical properties are vital. Since the goal of these models is to map text prompts to high-fidelity images, the "knowledge" of the model is embedded in the geometry of these neuron directions. If we view the neurons as points on a hypersphere, the semantic meaning is encoded in the angular distances between these points. Consequently, any modification to the model's output that preserves the underlying semantic integrity should, in theory, maintain the structural relationships (the angles) between these neurons.

# The Invariance Property of Orthogonal Transformations

A pivotal mathematical insight in the paper is the invariance property of hyperspherical energy under orthogonal transformations. The researchers argue that if we want to modify the model while preserving its pretraining generative performance, we need a way to change the neurons without destroying their relative angular relationships. They identify orthogonal transformations as the ideal mechanism for this task.

An orthogonal transformation is a linear transformation represented by a matrix $\bm{R}$ that satisfies the property $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. When we apply the same orthogonal transformation to every neuron in a layer, the pairwise hyperspherical similarity—and thus the hyperspherical energy—is provably preserved. Mathematically, if we transform a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ into a new matrix $\bm{W}$ using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the relative angles between the neurons remain unchanged.

This can be interpreted as adjusting the canonical coordinate system for the neurons. Just as rotating a 3D object does not change the relative distances between its points, applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the neurons in a layer rotates the "semantic space" without distorting the underlying knowledge. This provides a principled way to finetune: we can learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix to adapt the model to a downstream task while guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy remains exactly the same as it was in the pretrained model.

# Hypothesis: Preservation of Pairwise Neuron Angles as a Metric for Semantic Integrity

The central hypothesis of the paper is that a high-quality finetuned model should exhibit a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the original pretrained model. Because hyperspherical energy characterizes the pairwise relational structure of neurons, preserving this energy ensures that the intrinsic "knowledge" of the pretrained model is not destroyed during the finetuning process. 

The authors argue that the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks. While standard finetuning methods implicitly assume that a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained weights indicates better preservation of ability, the authors argue that this is insufficient. A more structural measure—the preservation of pairwise angles—is a more robust way to ensure that the model retains its ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes advantage of this by seeking to optimize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ for each layer. By only learning $\bm{R}$, the model is constrained to explore transformations that preserve the hyperspherical energy. This creates a "sweet spot" between flexibility and regularity. It provides enough flexibility to move the neurons into new directions required by the downstream task (like a specific subject or a Canny edge signal) while providing enough regularity to ensure that the relative semantic structure of the pretraining remains intact.

# Comparison: Minimizing Energy vs. Preserving Energy in Finetuning

Finally, it is important to distinguish the goals of OFT from other research involving hyperspherical energy. Some existing methods, such as those used for training classification networks from scratch, aim to *minimize* the hyperspherical energy. In those cases, the goal is to ensure that neurons are as uniformly spaced as possible, which has been shown to improve generalization in classification tasks.

However, the authors argue that minimizing hyperspherical energy is counterproductive for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. In a pretrained diffusion model, the current hyperspherical energy represents a complex, high-quality semantic structure. If one were to minimize the energy during finetuning, they would essentially be "flattening" or "smoothing out" the existing knowledge, which would destroy the model's ability to generate photorealistic images.

Instead, OFT aims to *preserve* the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. By maintaining the same energy level as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$, the model ensures that the pretrained structure is preserved. To further increase stability, the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds a secondary constraint: the finetuned model must stay within a small radius $\epsilon$ of the pretrained model. This explicit constraint on the deviation from the identity matrix (via the Cayley parameterization) ensures that the model remains close to the original weights while still benefiting from the angle-preserving properties of the orthogonal transformation.
