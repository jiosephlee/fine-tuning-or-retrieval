\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Text-to-Image Diffusion and Finetuning

# Overview of Diffusion Models in Image Generation

The field of generative artificial intelligence has been revolutionized by the development of text-to-image diffusion models. These models are designed to generate high-fidelity, photorealistic images based on natural language prompts. The underlying mechanism of these models involves a diffusion process where noise is gradually added to an image, and the model learns to reverse this process to reconstruct a clean image from a noisy input. This capability allows for impressive performance in text-guided control, where a user can specify the contents, style, and composition of an image through a textual description.

Current state-of-the-art models can be categorized based on the space in which they operate. Some models, such as GLIDE and Imagen, are trained directly in the pixel space. GLIDE trains a text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, while Imagen utilizes a frozen, pretrained text encoder. In contrast, other prominent models like Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 operate in a latent space. These models utilize a latent embedding space to represent images and text more efficiently. Stable Diffusion specifically employs a VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook that serves as its latent space, while DALL-E2 adopts the CLIP framework to construct a joint embedding space for both modalities. Regardless of the specific architecture or space, these models form the foundation for modern generative tasks, providing a powerful starting point for various downstream applications.

# The Challenge of Downstream Adaptation

While large-scale text-to-image diffusion models possess remarkable generative capabilities, they often face limitations when required to perform specific, fine-grained tasks. Text prompts alone can be ambiguous or insufficient for providing precise control over every detail of a generated image. Therefore, a significant area of research focuses on how to effectively guide or adapt these powerful models to perform specific downstream tasks without sacrificing their original generative power. The core challenge lies in "finetuning" the model: modifying it enough to learn new behaviors while ensuring it does not lose the high-fidelity and diverse performance it acquired during its initial pretraining phase.

Effective adaptation requires navigating a complex balance between the new task requirements and the preservation of the model's existing knowledge. If a model is modified too aggressively, it may suffer from "catastrophic forgetting," where it loses its ability to generate high-quality images from general prompts. Conversely, if the modification is too subtle, the model may fail to achieve the desired level of control for the new task. Identifying a principled method to achieve this balance is a primary objective in current research, as it requires a deep understanding of how weights and neurons in these massive architectures represent semantic information.

# Subject-Driven Generation: Identity Preservation

One of the primary applications of finetuning is subject-driven generation. In this task, the goal is to generate images of a specific subject—such as a particular person, pet, or object—in various contexts and poses, given only a few reference images of that subject and a text prompt. The primary difficulty here is maintaining "identity preservation," meaning the generated images must retain the unique, defining characteristics of the subject while allowing the background and actions to change according to the prompt.

Previous approaches to subject-driven generation have included using user-provided masks to protect specific areas, employing inversion methods to modify context, or using local and global editing techniques. Some methods, like Pivotal Tuning, finetune a generator around an initial inverted latent code with specific regularization to preserve identity. Others, such as MyStyle, learn a personalized generative face prior from a collection of images. A well-known framework for this is DreamBooth, which uses a customized token and a few subject images to finetune the model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss. However, these methods often rely on naive finetuning strategies, such as updating weights with a very small learning rate, which may not always guarantee the best preservation of the model's original semantic capabilities.

# Controllable Generation: Guided Synthesis (Canny, Segmentation, Landmarks)

Another critical area of adaptation is controllable generation, where the model is required to follow specific structural control signals in addition to the text prompt. These control signals can take various forms, such as Canny edges (which outline the shapes in an image), segmentation maps (which define different regions of an image like "sky," "tree," or "car"), or facial landmarks (which pinpoint specific features of a face). The objective is to produce an image that is both semantically aligned with the text and structurally aligned with the provided signal.

This task can be viewed as a specialized form of image-to-image translation. Recent methods like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter address this by finetuning a pretrained diffusion model to accept and interpret these additional control signals. For example, in a landmark-to-face (L2F) task, the model must generate a face that perfectly matches a set of specified coordinates. While these adapter-based methods are effective, research seeks to find ways to achieve even stronger controllability with fewer trainable parameters and faster convergence speeds, ultimately seeking a more efficient way to inject these external constraints into the diffusion process.

# Analysis of Existing Finetuning Strategies (DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet)

Several strategies have emerged to handle the finetuning of large models for the tasks described above. DreamBooth serves as a foundational framework for subject-driven tasks by incorporating reconstruction and prior preservation losses. However, its standard implementation involves updating the neuron weights directly with a small learning rate, which may lack structural guarantees for preserving pretraining performance.

Another popular approach is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), which is frequently used in natural language processing and has been adapted for image generation. LoRA assumes that the weight updates during finetuning can be represented by a low-rank structure. Specifically, it adds a small component with re-parameterized weights to the existing model, where the rank of the update, $r'$, is kept small. While LoRA is parameter-efficient, it does not explicitly guarantee the preservation of the original model's hyperspherical energy—a measure of the pairwise relationships between neurons.

Finally, adapter-based methods like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are designed to add new pathways for control signals. These methods often involve finetuning additional components or adapting the model to incorporate external signals like Canny edges or segmentation maps. While these methods are highly effective for controllable generation, they can introduce additional computational overhead during the inference stage. The goal of newer research is to find methods that provide the stability and control of these adapters but with the efficiency of direct finetuning and no extra inference costs.

# The Trade-off between Training Efficiency and Generative Performance

When designing a finetuning method, there are two primary "desiderata" or requirements that must be balanced: training efficiency and generalizability preservation. Training efficiency refers to minimizing the resources required for the adaptation process. This includes having fewer trainable parameters (to reduce memory usage and storage) and requiring fewer training epochs (to reduce the time spent on the GPU). Efficient methods are highly desirable because they allow for faster iteration and make it easier to adapt models to a wide variety of niche tasks.

On the other hand, generalizability preservation is the requirement that the finetuned model must retain the high-fidelity and diverse generative performance of the original pretrained model. A model that learns to generate a specific subject perfectly but loses its ability to generate a "sunset" or a "forest" is not a successful adaptation. Many existing methods prioritize one over the other or use simple heuristics to balance them. For instance, using a very small learning rate is a common way to favor preservation, but it can make training slow and difficult to tune. The challenge is to find a principled framework that optimizes both—achieving rapid, stable convergence on a downstream task while ensuring the model's broader generative "knowledge" remains intact.

# Limitations of Euclidean-based Preservation Measures

A significant problem in current finetuning research is the lack of a principled measure to quantify how well a model's pretraining performance is being preserved. Most current finetuning methods implicitly assume that a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights ($\bm{M}$) and the pretrained model weights ($\bm{M}^0$) indicates better preservation of the original ability. Mathematically, this is often represented as trying to minimize:

$\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$

Because of this assumption, researchers typically use very small learning rates to keep the weights as close as possible to the original values. However, this paper argues that Euclidean distance alone is insufficient to capture the degree of semantic preservation. Two weights can be close in Euclidean distance but have very different orientations, or they can be far apart in Euclidean distance but maintain the same relative angular relationships.

The paper suggests that a more structural measure is needed to characterize the difference between the finetuned and pretrained models. Specifically, it highlights that hyperspherical similarity (such as cosine similarity) encodes semantic information much better than simple Euclidean distance. By focusing on the pairwise relational structure among neurons—specifically their angles on a unit hypersphere—rather than just their positions in Euclidean space, we can develop more stable and effective finetuning strategies. This leads to the concept of "hyperspherical energy," which captures the uniformity of neurons on the hypersphere and serves as a more robust metric for semantic preservation.
