\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### Could you explain the mathematical derivation for the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint in COFT?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the paper introduces Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) where $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. To make this differentiable with the Cayley transform, the authors use a Neumann series approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$. Can someone walk me through how the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ is mathematically transformed into $\|\bm{Q} - \bm{0}\| \leq \epsilon'$? I want to understand why the factor of 2 appears and if this approximation holds for large $\bm{Q}$.
Answer:
To understand the mathematical transition from the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint on the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$, we need to look at the properties of the Cayley transform and the behavior of the Neumann series.

In the paper, the orthogonal matrix is parameterized as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$). The goal of Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) is to ensure that the learned transformation $\bm{R}$ does not deviate too far from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$, expressed by the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. To make this optimization tractable, the authors want to express this constraint directly in terms of the learnable parameters in $\bm{Q}$.

The transformation relies on the Neumann series expansion. For a matrix $\bm{A}$ where the operator norm $\|\bm{A}\| < 1$, the inverse can be expressed as $(\bm{I} - \bm{A})^{-1} = \bm{I} + \bm{A} + \bm{A}^2 + \dots$. Applying this to the Cayley transform where $\bm{A} = \bm{Q}$, we get:
$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q}^2 + \dots)$$
When we perform the matrix multiplication and keep only the first-order terms, we obtain:
$$\bm{R} = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$$
This is the derivation that explains the appearance of the factor of 2. Because $\bm{R}$ is approximately $\bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$, the term $(\bm{R} - \bm{I})$ simplifies to approximately $2\bm{Q}$. Substituting this back into the original constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$ gives us $\|2\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon$, which is equivalent to $\|\bm{Q}\| \leq \epsilon/2$. Thus, the new hyperparameter $\epsilon'$ is effectively $\epsilon/2$.

Regarding your question about whether this approximation holds for large $\bm{Q}$, the short answer is no. The Neumann series only converges if the spectral radius of $\bm{Q}$ is strictly less than 1. Furthermore, the linear approximation $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q}$ is only valid when the higher-order terms $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ are negligible, which occurs when $\bm{Q}$ is "small." 

However, in the context of this specific paper, the approximation is valid because COFT is explicitly designed to keep the model in a "small neighborhood" of the pretrained weights. Since the purpose of the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint is to prevent the model from drifting too far from its original state, $\bm{R}$ will remain very close to $\bm{I}$ during training. Consequently, $\bm{Q}$ will remain small, ensuring that the $\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$ terms remain insignificant and the linear approximation remains a highly accurate representation of the constraint.
