\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# The Fourier Transform of AI: Preserving the 'Soul' of Diffusion Models

When we finetune a massive text-to-image diffusion model—perhaps to recognize a specific person (subject-driven generation) or to follow a precise structural map (controllable generation)—we face a fundamental tension. If we change the model's weights too much to learn the new task, we risk "breaking" the model's pre-trained knowledge. We might lose the high-fidelity textures, the artistic diversity, and the general semantic understanding that made the model impressive in the first place. The goal is to teach the AI a new skill without destroying its "soul."

In their paper, *Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning*, the researchers propose a solution rooted in a fascinating analogy: the Fourier Transform.

## The Phase vs. The Magnitude

To understand the paper's core contribution, we first have to look at how we perceive information. In signal processing, the Fourier Transform allows us to decompose a signal into magnitude and phase. A well-known phenomenon in image processing is that the phase spectrum carries the vast majority of the structural and semantic information. You can actually reconstruct a recognizable image from its phase alone, even if the magnitude spectrum is completely random.

The researchers draw a direct parallel between this and the internal structure of a neural network. They argue that the "semantic information" of a diffusion model is stored primarily in the **angles** (the directions) of the neurons, rather than their **magnitudes**. 

They tested this with a toy experiment using a standard convolutional autoencoder. They found that while the magnitude of the neurons contained almost no useful information, the angular information could almost perfectly recover the input images. This suggests that if we want to modify what an AI generates, we should be focusing on the directions of the neurons. However, if we change those directions haphazardly, we destroy the model's pre-trained capabilities.

## Hyperspherical Energy: Measuring the 'Soul'

To quantify this "soul" or semantic structure, the authors use a concept called **hyperspherical energy (HE)**. Imagine every neuron in a layer as a point on a unit hypersphere. Hyperspherical energy characterizes the pairwise relationships—specifically the angles—between all these neurons. 

The hypothesis is simple: a good finetuned model should have a hyperspherical energy that remains as close as possible to the original pretrained model. If the energy changes significantly, the "relational structure" of the neurons has been warped, and the model’s generative ability will degrade.

## Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

Existing methods like LoRA or standard finetuning try to keep the model stable by ensuring the change in weights is small in terms of Euclidean distance. The authors argue that Euclidean distance isn't enough to capture the preservation of semantics. Instead, they propose **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**.

OFT works by applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to the neurons. Instead of updating the weights $\bm{W}^0$ directly, they learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that the new weights are:

$$\bm{W} = \bm{R} \cdot \bm{W}^0$$

Because $\bm{R}$ is an orthogonal matrix (meaning it represents a rotation or reflection), it is provably guaranteed to preserve the pairwise angles between neurons. In other words, OFT changes the "coordinate system" of the neurons to suit the new task while keeping the hyperspherical energy—and thus the semantic "soul"—of the model perfectly intact.

## Adding Constraints: COFT

While OFT provides a principled way to preserve semantics, it can still offer too much freedom, potentially leading to instability during training. To solve this, the authors introduced **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)**. 

COFT adds a radius constraint to the transformation. It ensures that the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ does not stray too far from the identity matrix $\bm{I}$. By enforcing the constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$, the model is anchored to its original state. This provides a "sweet spot" between the flexibility needed to learn a new task and the regularity needed to keep the model stable.

## Why This Matters

The results of this approach are significant across two major tasks:

*   **Subject-Driven Generation:** OFT and COFT were able to preserve the identity of a specific subject much better than LoRA, which often lost the subject's unique details.
*   **Controllable Generation:** When following control signals like Canny edges or segmentation maps, OFT converged significantly faster and produced higher-quality images with fewer parameters than traditional methods like ControlNet.

Perhaps most importantly for deployment, OFT introduces **zero additional inference overhead**. Because the transformation is a simple matrix multiplication, the learned $\bm{R}$ can be baked into the weights during the final step. The resulting model performs just as fast as the original pretrained model, but with the added power of specific, controlled generation.
