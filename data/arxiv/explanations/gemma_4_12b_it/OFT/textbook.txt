\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Introduction to Text-to-Image Diffusion and Finetuning

# Overview of Diffusion Models in Image Generation

The field of generative artificial intelligence has been revolutionized by the development of text-to-image diffusion models. These models are designed to generate high-fidelity, photorealistic images based on natural language prompts. The underlying mechanism of these models involves a diffusion process where noise is gradually added to an image, and the model learns to reverse this process to reconstruct a clean image from a noisy input. This capability allows for impressive performance in text-guided control, where a user can specify the contents, style, and composition of an image through a textual description.

Current state-of-the-art models can be categorized based on the space in which they operate. Some models, such as GLIDE and Imagen, are trained directly in the pixel space. GLIDE trains a text encoder jointly with a diffusion prior using paired text-image data, while Imagen utilizes a frozen, pretrained text encoder. In contrast, other prominent models like Stable Diffusion and DALL-E2 operate in a latent space. These models utilize a latent embedding space to represent images and text more efficiently. Stable Diffusion specifically employs a VQ-GAN to learn a visual codebook that serves as its latent space, while DALL-E2 adopts the CLIP framework to construct a joint embedding space for both modalities. Regardless of the specific architecture or space, these models form the foundation for modern generative tasks, providing a powerful starting point for various downstream applications.

# The Challenge of Downstream Adaptation

While large-scale text-to-image diffusion models possess remarkable generative capabilities, they often face limitations when required to perform specific, fine-grained tasks. Text prompts alone can be ambiguous or insufficient for providing precise control over every detail of a generated image. Therefore, a significant area of research focuses on how to effectively guide or adapt these powerful models to perform specific downstream tasks without sacrificing their original generative power. The core challenge lies in "finetuning" the model: modifying it enough to learn new behaviors while ensuring it does not lose the high-fidelity and diverse performance it acquired during its initial pretraining phase.

Effective adaptation requires navigating a complex balance between the new task requirements and the preservation of the model's existing knowledge. If a model is modified too aggressively, it may suffer from "catastrophic forgetting," where it loses its ability to generate high-quality images from general prompts. Conversely, if the modification is too subtle, the model may fail to achieve the desired level of control for the new task. Identifying a principled method to achieve this balance is a primary objective in current research, as it requires a deep understanding of how weights and neurons in these massive architectures represent semantic information.

# Subject-Driven Generation: Identity Preservation

One of the primary applications of finetuning is subject-driven generation. In this task, the goal is to generate images of a specific subject—such as a particular person, pet, or object—in various contexts and poses, given only a few reference images of that subject and a text prompt. The primary difficulty here is maintaining "identity preservation," meaning the generated images must retain the unique, defining characteristics of the subject while allowing the background and actions to change according to the prompt.

Previous approaches to subject-driven generation have included using user-provided masks to protect specific areas, employing inversion methods to modify context, or using local and global editing techniques. Some methods, like Pivotal Tuning, finetune a generator around an initial inverted latent code with specific regularization to preserve identity. Others, such as MyStyle, learn a personalized generative face prior from a collection of images. A well-known framework for this is DreamBooth, which uses a customized token and a few subject images to finetune the model using a reconstruction loss and a class-specific prior preservation loss. However, these methods often rely on naive finetuning strategies, such as updating weights with a very small learning rate, which may not always guarantee the best preservation of the model's original semantic capabilities.

# Controllable Generation: Guided Synthesis (Canny, Segmentation, Landmarks)

Another critical area of adaptation is controllable generation, where the model is required to follow specific structural control signals in addition to the text prompt. These control signals can take various forms, such as Canny edges (which outline the shapes in an image), segmentation maps (which define different regions of an image like "sky," "tree," or "car"), or facial landmarks (which pinpoint specific features of a face). The objective is to produce an image that is both semantically aligned with the text and structurally aligned with the provided signal.

This task can be viewed as a specialized form of image-to-image translation. Recent methods like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter address this by finetuning a pretrained diffusion model to accept and interpret these additional control signals. For example, in a landmark-to-face (L2F) task, the model must generate a face that perfectly matches a set of specified coordinates. While these adapter-based methods are effective, research seeks to find ways to achieve even stronger controllability with fewer trainable parameters and faster convergence speeds, ultimately seeking a more efficient way to inject these external constraints into the diffusion process.

# Analysis of Existing Finetuning Strategies (DreamBooth, LoRA, ControlNet)

Several strategies have emerged to handle the finetuning of large models for the tasks described above. DreamBooth serves as a foundational framework for subject-driven tasks by incorporating reconstruction and prior preservation losses. However, its standard implementation involves updating the neuron weights directly with a small learning rate, which may lack structural guarantees for preserving pretraining performance.

Another popular approach is LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), which is frequently used in natural language processing and has been adapted for image generation. LoRA assumes that the weight updates during finetuning can be represented by a low-rank structure. Specifically, it adds a small component with re-parameterized weights to the existing model, where the rank of the update, $r'$, is kept small. While LoRA is parameter-efficient, it does not explicitly guarantee the preservation of the original model's hyperspherical energy—a measure of the pairwise relationships between neurons.

Finally, adapter-based methods like ControlNet and T2I-Adapter are designed to add new pathways for control signals. These methods often involve finetuning additional components or adapting the model to incorporate external signals like Canny edges or segmentation maps. While these methods are highly effective for controllable generation, they can introduce additional computational overhead during the inference stage. The goal of newer research is to find methods that provide the stability and control of these adapters but with the efficiency of direct finetuning and no extra inference costs.

# The Trade-off between Training Efficiency and Generative Performance

When designing a finetuning method, there are two primary "desiderata" or requirements that must be balanced: training efficiency and generalizability preservation. Training efficiency refers to minimizing the resources required for the adaptation process. This includes having fewer trainable parameters (to reduce memory usage and storage) and requiring fewer training epochs (to reduce the time spent on the GPU). Efficient methods are highly desirable because they allow for faster iteration and make it easier to adapt models to a wide variety of niche tasks.

On the other hand, generalizability preservation is the requirement that the finetuned model must retain the high-fidelity and diverse generative performance of the original pretrained model. A model that learns to generate a specific subject perfectly but loses its ability to generate a "sunset" or a "forest" is not a successful adaptation. Many existing methods prioritize one over the other or use simple heuristics to balance them. For instance, using a very small learning rate is a common way to favor preservation, but it can make training slow and difficult to tune. The challenge is to find a principled framework that optimizes both—achieving rapid, stable convergence on a downstream task while ensuring the model's broader generative "knowledge" remains intact.

# Limitations of Euclidean-based Preservation Measures

A significant problem in current finetuning research is the lack of a principled measure to quantify how well a model's pretraining performance is being preserved. Most current finetuning methods implicitly assume that a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned model weights ($\bm{M}$) and the pretrained model weights ($\bm{M}^0$) indicates better preservation of the original ability. Mathematically, this is often represented as trying to minimize:

$\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$

Because of this assumption, researchers typically use very small learning rates to keep the weights as close as possible to the original values. However, this paper argues that Euclidean distance alone is insufficient to capture the degree of semantic preservation. Two weights can be close in Euclidean distance but have very different orientations, or they can be far apart in Euclidean distance but maintain the same relative angular relationships.

The paper suggests that a more structural measure is needed to characterize the difference between the finetuned and pretrained models. Specifically, it highlights that hyperspherical similarity (such as cosine similarity) encodes semantic information much better than simple Euclidean distance. By focusing on the pairwise relational structure among neurons—specifically their angles on a unit hypersphere—rather than just their positions in Euclidean space, we can develop more stable and effective finetuning strategies. This leads to the concept of "hyperspherical energy," which captures the uniformity of neurons on the hypersphere and serves as a more robust metric for semantic preservation.

Theoretical Foundations: Hyperspherical Energy and Angular Semantics

# Empirical Evidence: Angular vs. Magnitude Information in Neural Networks

To understand the theoretical motivation behind Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), one must first understand the role that neuron directions play in representing information within a neural network. A core observation in the study of deep learning is that the semantic information processed by a model is often encoded in the angular relationships between neurons rather than the raw magnitude of their activations. This is supported by empirical observations where angular feature differences are shown to characterize semantic gaps more effectively than other metrics.

The paper provides a compelling toy experiment to illustrate this concept. By training a standard convolutional autoencoder on a set of flower images, the researchers compared three different methods of generating feature maps during the testing phase: using the standard inner product (the method used during training), using only the magnitude of the neurons, and using only the angular information of the neurons. The results of this experiment were striking. It was found that the angular information of the neurons could almost perfectly reconstruct the original input images. In contrast, the magnitude information of the neurons contained no useful information for reconstruction.

This experiment is significant because the researchers did not apply a cosine activation during the training phase. This means the model learned to represent the images primarily through the inner products of its kernels. The fact that the angular information alone can recover the semantics implies that the directions (angles) of the neurons are the primary carriers of the knowledge the network has acquired. Therefore, if the goal of finetuning a text-to-image diffusion model is to modify the semantic content of a generated image—such as changing a subject or adding a specific control signal—the most effective way to achieve this is by modifying the directions of the neurons.

# Understanding Hyperspherical Energy (HE)

Building on the idea that angles are the primary carriers of semantic information, the paper introduces a formal mathematical measure to characterize these relationships: Hyperspherical Energy (HE). Hyperspherical energy is a metric used to quantify the pairwise relational structure among neurons within a specific layer of a neural network. Specifically, it measures the level of neuron uniformity on a unit hypersphere.

The hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ is defined by considering each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ in the weight matrix. First, each neuron is normalized to lie on a unit hypersphere, represented as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$. Once normalized, the hyperspherical energy $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$ is calculated as the sum of the inverse distances between all unique pairs of these normalized neurons:

$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$

By summing the inverse of the Euclidean distances between all pairs of neurons on the hypersphere, this metric captures how "spread out" or "clumped" the neurons are. If neurons are very close to one another, the distance $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ is small, making the inverse value large and increasing the total hyperspherical energy. Conversely, if the neurons are uniformly distributed across the hypersphere, the distances will be larger, resulting in a lower hyperspherical energy. This energy serves as a proxy for the internal structural organization of the network's knowledge.

# Properties of Neurons on the Unit Hypersphere

The concept of hyperspherical energy relies on the behavior of neurons when projected onto a unit hypersphere. This perspective suggests that the capacity of a neural network can be maintained, or even improved, by ensuring that neurons are normalized. Research has shown that training networks with neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere yields comparable capacity to standard training but often provides better generalizability. This is because the direction of a neuron on the hypersphere captures the most critical information from the data.

In the context of text-to-image diffusion models, these hyperspherical properties are vital. Since the goal of these models is to map text prompts to high-fidelity images, the "knowledge" of the model is embedded in the geometry of these neuron directions. If we view the neurons as points on a hypersphere, the semantic meaning is encoded in the angular distances between these points. Consequently, any modification to the model's output that preserves the underlying semantic integrity should, in theory, maintain the structural relationships (the angles) between these neurons.

# The Invariance Property of Orthogonal Transformations

A pivotal mathematical insight in the paper is the invariance property of hyperspherical energy under orthogonal transformations. The researchers argue that if we want to modify the model while preserving its pretraining generative performance, we need a way to change the neurons without destroying their relative angular relationships. They identify orthogonal transformations as the ideal mechanism for this task.

An orthogonal transformation is a linear transformation represented by a matrix $\bm{R}$ that satisfies the property $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. When we apply the same orthogonal transformation to every neuron in a layer, the pairwise hyperspherical similarity—and thus the hyperspherical energy—is provably preserved. Mathematically, if we transform a weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ into a new matrix $\bm{W}$ using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ such that $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, the relative angles between the neurons remain unchanged.

This can be interpreted as adjusting the canonical coordinate system for the neurons. Just as rotating a 3D object does not change the relative distances between its points, applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the neurons in a layer rotates the "semantic space" without distorting the underlying knowledge. This provides a principled way to finetune: we can learn a layer-shared orthogonal matrix to adapt the model to a downstream task while guaranteeing that the hyperspherical energy remains exactly the same as it was in the pretrained model.

# Hypothesis: Preservation of Pairwise Neuron Angles as a Metric for Semantic Integrity

The central hypothesis of the paper is that a high-quality finetuned model should exhibit a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to the original pretrained model. Because hyperspherical energy characterizes the pairwise relational structure of neurons, preserving this energy ensures that the intrinsic "knowledge" of the pretrained model is not destroyed during the finetuning process. 

The authors argue that the angles between neurons are crucial for representing the knowledge of neural networks. While standard finetuning methods implicitly assume that a small Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained weights indicates better preservation of ability, the authors argue that this is insufficient. A more structural measure—the preservation of pairwise angles—is a more robust way to ensure that the model retains its ability to generate high-fidelity, diverse images.

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes advantage of this by seeking to optimize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ for each layer. By only learning $\bm{R}$, the model is constrained to explore transformations that preserve the hyperspherical energy. This creates a "sweet spot" between flexibility and regularity. It provides enough flexibility to move the neurons into new directions required by the downstream task (like a specific subject or a Canny edge signal) while providing enough regularity to ensure that the relative semantic structure of the pretraining remains intact.

# Comparison: Minimizing Energy vs. Preserving Energy in Finetuning

Finally, it is important to distinguish the goals of OFT from other research involving hyperspherical energy. Some existing methods, such as those used for training classification networks from scratch, aim to *minimize* the hyperspherical energy. In those cases, the goal is to ensure that neurons are as uniformly spaced as possible, which has been shown to improve generalization in classification tasks.

However, the authors argue that minimizing hyperspherical energy is counterproductive for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. In a pretrained diffusion model, the current hyperspherical energy represents a complex, high-quality semantic structure. If one were to minimize the energy during finetuning, they would essentially be "flattening" or "smoothing out" the existing knowledge, which would destroy the model's ability to generate photorealistic images.

Instead, OFT aims to *preserve* the hyperspherical energy of the pretrained model. By maintaining the same energy level as $\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)$, the model ensures that the pretrained structure is preserved. To further increase stability, the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which adds a secondary constraint: the finetuned model must stay within a small radius $\epsilon$ of the pretrained model. This explicit constraint on the deviation from the identity matrix (via the Cayley parameterization) ensures that the model remains close to the original weights while still benefiting from the angle-preserving properties of the orthogonal transformation.

# The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Framework

## Mathematical Formulation of OFT: Layer-shared Orthogonal Matrices

To understand Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), one must first consider the challenge of adapting large, pre-trained text-to-image diffusion models to specific downstream tasks, such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation. Standard finetuning typically involves updating the weights of a model using gradient descent with a small learning rate. This approach implicitly attempts to keep the new weights $\bm{M}$ close to the original weights $\bm{M}^0$ by minimizing the Euclidean distance $\|\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$. However, this Euclidean distance does not necessarily capture the preservation of the semantic information inherent in the model's pre-trained structure.

OFT proposes a different mathematical approach. Instead of simply moving the weights toward a new goal, OFT seeks to transform the neurons in a layer such that their internal relational structure remains unchanged. This is achieved by learning a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ that acts upon the pre-trained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. For a fully connected layer where the output vector $\bm{z}$ is computed as $\bm{z} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, OFT modifies this operation to $\bm{z} = \bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$, where the new weight matrix is defined as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. 

The crucial constraint in this formulation is that $\bm{R}$ must be an orthogonal matrix, meaning it satisfies the property $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. By applying the same orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to all neurons within a specific layer, the model is adjusted to perform the new task while the pairwise angles between the neurons are preserved. This "layer-shared" approach ensures that the transformation is consistent across the neurons of a single layer, providing a structured way to modify the model's behavior without destroying its foundational knowledge.

## The Principle of Preserving Hyperspherical Energy

The motivation behind using orthogonal transformations lies in the concept of hyperspherical energy. Research suggests that the semantic information in a neural network is encoded primarily in the directions (angles) of the neurons rather than their magnitudes. To illustrate this, the authors describe a toy experiment involving a convolutional autoencoder. When they compared different ways of reconstructing images from feature maps, they found that the angular information of the neurons could almost perfectly recover the input images, whereas the magnitude information contained no useful data. This implies that if we want to modify the semantics of a generated image, we must focus on changing the directions of the neurons.

Hyperspherical energy, denoted as $\text{HE}(\bm{W})$, is a formal measure used to characterize the pairwise relational structure among neurons. For a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ containing neurons $\{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\}$, the hyperspherical energy is defined as the sum of the inverse distances between all pairs of normalized neurons:
$$\text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$$
where $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$ represents the normalized direction of the $i$-th neuron. A key property of hyperspherical energy is its invariance under orthogonal transformations. If a weight matrix $\bm{W}$ is multiplied by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the pairwise angles between the neurons remain identical, and thus the hyperspherical energy remains constant.

OFT leverages this property by aiming to minimize the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned model and the pretrained model. Mathematically, this is expressed as minimizing $\|\text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\|$. Because orthogonal transformations provably preserve these angles, the minimum of this objective is zero. By constraining the finetuning process to only allow for orthogonal transformations, OFT ensures that the intrinsic semantic structure of the pretrained model—represented by its hyperspherical energy—is preserved while the model is adapted to a new task.

## Cayley Parameterization for Differentiable Orthogonality

While the goal of OFT is to learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, maintaining the strict requirement $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ during gradient descent can be computationally expensive. Standard methods like the Gram-Schmidt process are differentiable but often too slow for practical use in large-scale models. To solve this, the authors adopt the Cayley parameterization to generate the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in a more efficient and differentiable manner.

The Cayley transform defines the orthogonal matrix as:
$$\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$$
In this equation, $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix, which means it satisfies the property $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. By parameterizing $\bm{R}$ in terms of $\bm{Q}$, the model can optimize the values of $\bm{Q}$ using standard gradient descent. Any matrix $\bm{Q}$ that is skew-symmetric will automatically result in an $\bm{R}$ that is orthogonal. This approach provides a computationally efficient way to ensure the orthogonality constraint is met at every step of the training process.

One minor limitation of the Cayley parameterization is that it only produces orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $1$, which belongs to the special orthogonal group. However, the authors note that this does not negatively impact the performance of the model in practice. By initializing $\bm{Q}$ as an all-zero matrix, the resulting $\bm{R}$ starts as an identity matrix, ensuring that the finetuned model begins with the exact weights of the pretrained model before optimization begins.

## Block-Diagonal Structure for Parameter Efficiency

A significant concern when dealing with large models is the number of trainable parameters. A full orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ of size $d \times d$ (where $d$ is the dimension of the neurons) contains a large number of parameters, specifically $d(d-1)/2$. As $d$ grows, this could lead to high memory usage and slow training. To address this, the authors propose representing $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix.

Instead of a dense matrix, $\bm{R}$ is structured as:
$$\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$$
where each $\bm{R}_i$ is an orthogonal matrix of size $\frac{d}{r} \times \frac{d}{r}$ and $r$ is the number of blocks. This structure significantly reduces the number of parameters. For a standard unconstrained orthogonal matrix, the complexity is $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. With the $r$-block diagonal structure, the parameter count is reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Furthermore, if the blocks are shared—meaning $\bm{R}_i = \bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$—the complexity can be further reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$.

This block-diagonal structure is particularly interesting for certain architectures. For example, in convolution layers, if the number of blocks is set equal to the number of input channels, each block transforms a unique neuron channel. This is conceptually similar to learning depth-wise convolution kernels. Importantly, despite these reductions in parameters to improve efficiency, the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, meaning the fundamental goal of preserving hyperspherical energy is still satisfied.

## Complexity Analysis: OFT vs. LoRA

To evaluate the practical utility of OFT, it is useful to compare it to Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which is currently one of the most popular finetuning methods. LoRA functions by adding a low-rank update to the weights, where the update is represented as the product of two low-rank matrices. For a weight matrix of size $d \times n$ and a rank $r'$, the number of trainable parameters in LoRA is $r'(d + n)$. 

If we assume that the rank $r'$ scales linearly with the neuron dimension $d$ (e.g., $r' = \alpha d$ for some constant $\alpha$), the complexity of LoRA becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$. In contrast, the complexity of OFT, especially when using the block-diagonal structure, is much lower. Because OFT exploits the sparsity structure of the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix rather than a low-rank structure, its parameter complexity is $\mathcal{O}(d)$ or $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ depending on the configuration.

The paper provides a concrete example to highlight this difference. Consider a weight matrix with a size of $128 \times 128$. If we use a rank of $r' = 8$ for LoRA, the model requires $2,048$ trainable parameters. However, if we use $r = 8$ blocks for OFT without block sharing, the model requires only $960$ trainable parameters. This demonstrates that OFT can achieve its goals with fewer parameters than LoRA while maintaining a more structured approach to preserving the pre-trained weights.

## Zero Inference Overhead: Folding Transformations into Pre-trained Weights

One of the primary advantages of the OFT framework over other controllable generation methods, such as ControlNet or T2I-Adapter, is its efficiency during the inference stage. Methods like ControlNet work by adding additional trainable branches to the model, which increases the computational cost during generation because the model must process both the original path and the new control path.

OFT avoids this overhead entirely. Because the finetuned weight matrix $\bm{W}$ is defined as the product of an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and the pre-trained matrix $\bm{W}^0$ ($\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$), the transformation can be "folded" into the weights. Once the training is complete and $\bm{R}$ has been learned, we can simply perform a one-time matrix multiplication to obtain the final weights $\bm{W}$. 

At test time, the model then uses these folded weights $\bm{W}$ just like the original pre-trained model. This means that the inference speed remains identical to that of the original model, making OFT an exceptionally efficient and deployable solution for tasks requiring high-fidelity, controllable image generation.

Constrained and Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning

# Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT): The Radius Constraint

While Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) provides a principled way to adapt text-to-image diffusion models by preserving the hyperspherical energy of the neurons, it still possesses a significant degree of freedom. The core idea of OFT is to learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to transform the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ into new weights $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. However, even with the constraint of orthogonality, the model might deviate too far from the original pretrained weights, potentially leading to instability or the loss of some essential pretraining knowledge. To address this, the authors propose Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT).

COFT introduces an explicit radius constraint to the hypersphere. The goal is to ensure that the finetuned model stays within a small neighborhood of the pretrained model. Mathematically, this is expressed by adding a deviation constraint to the orthogonal transformation. If $\bm{I}$ represents the identity matrix—which corresponds to the original pretrained model since $\bm{W}^0 = \bm{I}\bm{W}^0$—the constraint is defined as $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}} \leq \epsilon$. Here, $\epsilon$ is a hyperparameter that determines the maximum allowable distance between the finetuned weights and the original weights.

By incorporating this constraint, COFT combines two distinct types of regularization. First, it maintains the orthogonality constraint (preserving hyperspherical energy), and second, it explicitly limits the Euclidean deviation from the starting point. This dual approach aims to find a better balance between the flexibility needed to learn a new downstream task (like a specific subject or a control signal) and the stability required to preserve the high-fidelity generative performance of the original model.

# Approximating the Constraint via the Neumann Series

Implementing the constraint $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}} \leq \epsilon$ is non-trivial because the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is parameterized using the Cayley transform. In the previous section, we established that $\bm{R}$ is constructed as $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix ($\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$). Because $\bm{R}$ is a complex function of $\bm{Q}$, applying a direct radius constraint on $\bm{R}$ makes the optimization process difficult to manage directly.

To solve this, the authors utilize the Neumann series to approximate the relationship between $\bm{R}$ and $\bm{Q}$. Under the assumption that the series converges in the operator norm, the Cayley transform can be approximated as $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I}+2\bm{Q}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{Q}^2)$. This approximation is highly useful because it allows the authors to "move" the constraint from the output of the transformation ($\bm{R}$) back to the input parameters ($\bm{Q}$).

By using this approximation, the constraint $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}} \leq \epsilon$ can be reformulated as an equivalent constraint on the skew-symmetric matrix: $\norm{\bm{Q}-\bm{0}} \leq \epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ represents an all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a hyperparameter adjusted to correspond with $\epsilon$. This simplification is crucial for practical implementation, as it allows the training process to operate directly on the matrix $\bm{Q}$ while still ensuring that the resulting orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ remains within the desired radius of the identity matrix.

# Projected Gradient Descent for Enforcing Constraints

Once the constraint has been reformulated to apply to the matrix $\bm{Q}$, the next challenge is to ensure that the model strictly obeys this constraint during the training process. Standard gradient descent updates the weights in the direction of the steepest descent, which might naturally push the values of $\bm{Q}$ outside the allowed radius defined by $\epsilon'$. To prevent this, the authors employ Projected Gradient Descent (PGD).

Projected Gradient Descent works by performing a standard gradient update and then "projecting" the result back onto the feasible set—in this case, the region where $\norm{\bm{Q}-\bm{0}} \leq \epsilon'$. If an update moves $\bm{Q}$ outside the allowed boundary, PGD mathematically pulls it back to the nearest point on the boundary of the constraint. This ensures that at every iteration of the training, the orthogonality and the radius constraints are simultaneously satisfied.

To ensure the model starts exactly at the pretrained weights, the matrix $\bm{Q}$ is initialized as an all-zero matrix. Since $\bm{Q}=\bm{0}$ results in $\bm{R}=\bm{I}$, the finetuned model begins with the exact weights of the pretrained model. As training progresses, PGD allows $\bm{Q}$ to grow just enough to capture the new task information while ensuring it never deviates beyond the safety buffer defined by $\epsilon'$.

# The Impact of the Epsilon Parameter on Finetuning Flexibility

The hyperparameter $\epsilon$ serves as a critical "knob" for the researcher, controlling the trade-off between the preservation of the original model and the adaptation to the new task. The authors observe that the value of $\epsilon$ directly dictates the flexibility of the finetuning process. 

When $\epsilon$ is set to a large value, the constraint becomes very loose. In this regime, the COFT-finetuned model behaves very similarly to the original OFT model. It has a high degree of freedom to change the neuron directions, which allows it to learn complex new behaviors but potentially risks higher instability or a faster drift from the pretraining knowledge.

Conversely, as $\epsilon$ is decreased, the model becomes increasingly constrained. With a small $\epsilon$, the finetuned model is forced to remain very close to the original pretrained text-to-image model. This increases stability and ensures that the model retains much of its original behavior, but it may limit the model's ability to learn highly specific or drastic changes required by the downstream task. The authors find that COFT provides better stability than standard OFT because this explicit deviation constraint prevents the training dynamics from becoming too erratic.

# Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning: Introducing Magnitude Scaling

In addition to COFT, the authors propose an extension called Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning. This variant is motivated by a key property of hyperspherical energy: the energy is determined by the angles between neurons, not their magnitudes. Because the hyperspherical energy involves normalized neurons ($\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$), any change in the magnitude of a neuron is "normalized out" and does not affect the pairwise relational structure that the authors seek to preserve.

Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable diagonal matrix $\bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1, \dots, s_n)$ into the forward pass. The operation becomes $\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$, where each $s_i$ is a scalar greater than zero. By learning these scaling coefficients, the model gains an additional layer of flexibility. It can adjust the "strength" or magnitude of individual neurons independently of their directions.

This approach is highly efficient because it adds a negligible number of parameters to the model. While the original OFT focuses on preserving the "angular" knowledge of the network, Re-scaled OFT allows the model to fine-tune the "magnitude" of that knowledge. The authors note that while they primarily use the standard OFT to demonstrate the power of orthogonal transformations, Re-scaled OFT generally yields even better results by providing the model with more ways to adapt to the downstream data.

# Trade-offs between Flexibility, Regularity, and Stability

A central theme of this research is the inherent trade-off between flexibility and regularity in neural network finetuning. Standard finetuning is the most flexible approach because it allows every weight to change freely. However, this high flexibility often leads to poor stability, where the model may suffer from "model collapse" or lose its generative capabilities very quickly.

OFT and COFT provide a solution to this problem by introducing regularity through the preservation of hyperspherical energy. By constraining the model to maintain the pairwise angles between neurons, the authors ensure that the fundamental "semantic structure" of the pretrained model is not destroyed. They argue that while changing neuron directions (angles) is the primary way to modify the semantics of a generated image, doing so with too much freedom leads to instability.

The OFT framework achieves a "sweet spot" between these extremes. It provides enough flexibility to learn new tasks (like following a Canny edge or generating a specific subject) by transforming the neuron directions, but it imposes enough regularity (through orthogonality) to keep the training stable. COFT further refines this by adding an explicit stability layer via the radius constraint. This hierarchy of constraints—orthogonality for structural preservation, and radius limits for proximity preservation—allows for a finetuning process that is both fast-converging and remarkably stable compared to traditional methods.

Experimental Evaluation and Empirical Insights

# Experimental Setup: Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the Base Model

To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), the researchers utilized Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the foundational text-to-image diffusion model. This choice provides a standardized benchmark for comparing different finetuning methodologies across various downstream tasks. By starting with a well-established pretrained model, the study ensures that any improvements in generation quality or controllability can be attributed to the finetuning strategy rather than variations in the underlying architecture.

The experimental framework was designed to ensure a fair comparison against existing state-of-the-art methods. For subject-driven generation, the researchers compared OFT and COFT against DreamBooth and LoRA. In the realm of controllable generation, the baselines included ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. To maintain a level playing field with LoRA—which typically targets specific layers—the researchers applied OFT and COFT to the exact same layers where LoRA was implemented.

Furthermore, the evaluation process emphasized reliability by employing a randomized selection of generated images for each method. This prevents the results from being skewed by "cherry-picked" high-quality samples and ensures that the reported improvements in fidelity and controllability are representative of the models' general performance.

# Subject-Driven Generation Results: Fidelity, Diversity, and Stability

In the task of subject-driven generation, the goal is to produce images of a specific subject in diverse contexts while maintaining the subject's unique identity. The researchers evaluated the performance of OFT and COFT using a suite of quantitative metrics: DINO and CLIP-I for subject fidelity, CLIP-T for text prompt fidelity, and LPIPS for sample diversity. The results demonstrated that both OFT and COFT outperformed the DreamBooth and LoRA baselines by a considerable margin in the subject fidelity metrics (DINO and CLIP-I).

The superiority of OFT and COFT in subject fidelity is particularly significant because it suggests that the orthogonal transformation effectively preserves the intrinsic semantic structure of the subject. While DreamBooth is a standard for this task, it often struggles to balance identity preservation with prompt adherence. Conversely, LoRA frequently fails to capture the specific details of a subject, sometimes losing the identity entirely. OFT and COFT, however, provide a robust framework that maintains high fidelity to the subject without sacrificing the model's ability to follow the user's text instructions.

In terms of prompt fidelity and sample diversity, OFT and COFT performed comparably to or slightly better than the baseline methods. To ensure the statistical significance of these findings, the researchers repeated the finetuning process using 30 different random seeds for each method. This rigorous approach minimized the impact of randomness and confirmed that the benefits of the OFT framework are consistent and reproducible across different initializations.

# Controllable Generation Results: Canny, Segmentation, and Landmarks

The research extended the evaluation to controllable generation, where the model must adhere to specific external signals such as Canny edges, segmentation maps, or facial landmarks. The experiments were conducted on three distinct and challenging tasks: Canny edge to image (C2I) using the COCO dataset, segmentation map to image (S2I) using the ADE20K dataset, and landmark to face (L2F) using the CelebA-HQ dataset.

The results indicated that OFT and COFT yield much stronger and more accurate control compared to existing adapter-based approaches like T2I-Adapter and ControlNet. A notable finding was the efficiency of the OFT framework; it achieved high-quality results with significantly fewer trainable parameters than ControlNet. Furthermore, OFT demonstrated remarkable data efficiency, showing the ability to converge well using only 5\% of the ADE20K dataset. This suggests that the orthogonal constraints allow the model to learn the necessary transformations much more effectively than standard weight updates.

When comparing OFT to LoRA in these tasks, the researchers found that LoRA's performance ceiling was relatively low. Even with careful hyperparameter tuning, LoRA struggled to produce images that accurately followed the input segmentation maps or face landmarks. In contrast, OFT and COFT produced images with high fidelity, vivid details, and reasonable geometric structures, successfully navigating the complexities of various control signals.

# Fast and Stable Convergence: Comparison of Training Dynamics

One of the most compelling advantages of the OFT framework is its training stability and convergence speed. In the subject-driven generation task, the researchers observed a stark contrast in training dynamics. Standard DreamBooth began to generate "collapsed" images after 2000 iterations, while LoRA failed to preserve the subject's identity even as training progressed. In contrast, both OFT and COFT maintained stable and consistent control over the generated images, showing an impressive insensitivity to the number of finetuning iterations.

This stability is a crucial practical benefit. Because OFT does not suffer from the rapid degradation seen in DreamBooth or the identity loss seen in LoRA, users do not need to meticulously tune the number of training iterations for every new subject. For COFT, when paired with a proper $\epsilon$ hyperparameter, both the learning rate and the iteration number become nearly effortless to set, simplifying the workflow for end-users.

The speed of convergence was further validated in the L2F task. The researchers plotted the face landmark error over time and found that OFT and COFT reached high performance much faster than LoRA. Specifically, it took LoRA 20 epochs to reach the level of performance that OFT achieved in just 8 epochs. Furthermore, the training dynamics of OFT were characterized as "smooth and predictable," unlike ControlNet, which exhibited a sudden emergence of controllability only after the 8th epoch. This smooth progression makes it significantly easier to find optimal hyperparameters for OFT.

# Control Consistency Metric: A New Way to Evaluate Controllability

To provide a more objective and rigorous assessment of controllability, the researchers introduced a novel "control consistency metric." The core intuition behind this metric is to evaluate how well the model actually follows the provided control signal by attempting to "reverse" the process. Specifically, the metric estimates the control signal from the generated image and then compares this estimated signal with the original input signal.

The application of this metric varied depending on the specific task. For the C2I task, the researchers used IoU (Intersection over Union) and F1 scores to measure the overlap between the edges in the generated image and the original Canny edges. For the S2I task, they utilized mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy to compare the predicted segmentation against the original map. Finally, for the L2F task, the metric calculated the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control face landmarks and the landmarks predicted from the generated face.

The results of these tests consistently showed that OFT and COFT yielded stronger and more accurate control than all other compared methods. This quantitative evaluation serves as a powerful validation of the OFT framework, as it proves that the model is not just producing "pleasing" images, but is actually adhering to the geometric and semantic constraints provided by the user with a high degree of precision.

# Qualitative Analysis of Subject Preservation and Geometric Control

The qualitative results provide a visual narrative that complements the quantitative data, highlighting the practical superiority of OFT and COFT. In subject-driven generation, the researchers highlighted a specific "bowl" example to illustrate the trade-offs between different methods. They observed that while DreamBooth could preserve a subject's identity, it often failed to follow the text prompt (e.g., placing the subject in the wrong context). LoRA, conversely, often completely lost the subject's identity. OFT and COFT succeeded in both dimensions, delivering excellent semantic subject preservation while accurately following the text prompt.

In the controllable generation tasks, the qualitative analysis further distinguished OFT from its competitors. In the S2I task, LoRA completely failed to generate images that followed the input segmentation maps, whereas OFT and COFT successfully integrated the map into the image structure. In the C2I task, OFT and COFT demonstrated the ability to "hallucinate" semantically similar and realistic images based on even rough Canny edges, a feat where T2I-Adapter and LoRA performed poorly.

Finally, in the L2F task, OFT and COFT produced the most accurate pose control for generated faces, even under challenging and extreme poses. Across all three tasks, the qualitative evidence showed that OFT and COFT produce images with higher fidelity and more vivid details than the state-of-the-art baselines, all while using far fewer model parameters and avoiding the inference overhead typically associated with adapter-based models.

Comparative Analysis and Future Directions

# OFT vs. LoRA: Sparsity vs. Low-Rank Adaptations

To understand the significance of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), it is essential to compare it with one of the most prevalent modern finetuning techniques: Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). While both methods aim to adapt large pretrained models to downstream tasks with minimal trainable parameters, they rely on fundamentally different mathematical principles and structural assumptions. LoRA operates on the principle of low-rank decomposition. It assumes that the weight update required for a downstream task can be represented by a low-rank matrix, leading to an additive update to the weights. Specifically, if $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights, LoRA approximates the new weights as $\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \bm{A}\bm{B}$, where the product $\bm{A}\bm{B}$ is constrained to have a small rank $r'$.

In contrast, OFT adopts a multiplicative approach based on orthogonal transformations. Instead of adding a low-rank component, OFT learns a layer-shared orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to transform the existing neurons. The finetuned weights are expressed as $\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix satisfying $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{R}\bm{R}^\top = \bm{I}$. The core distinction lies in the structural exploitation: LoRA exploits the low-rank structure of the weight update, while OFT exploits the sparsity structure of the orthogonal matrix. By using a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$, OFT achieves parameter efficiency similar to or even better than LoRA while maintaining a full-rank transformation that provably preserves the hyperspherical energy of the neurons.

The practical implications of these two strategies are evident in their parameter complexity. For a weight matrix of size $d \times n$, the number of trainable parameters in LoRA is proportional to $r'(d + n)$. If we assume the rank $r'$ scales linearly with the dimension $d$ (e.g., $r' = \alpha d$), the complexity becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$. Conversely, by utilizing an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix for OFT, the complexity is reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. When block sharing is applied across these matrices, the complexity can be further reduced to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. For example, in a $128 \times 128$ weight matrix, a LoRA setup with rank $r' = 8$ requires 2,048 parameters, whereas an OFT setup with $r = 8$ blocks requires only 960 parameters. Thus, OFT provides a different path to parameter efficiency by leveraging orthogonal constraints rather than rank restrictions.

# Optimization Landscape: Finetuning on Smooth Hyperspherical Manifolds

A primary motivation for OFT is the observation that the geometric arrangement of neurons—specifically the angles between them—is the primary carrier of semantic information in neural networks. This is supported by empirical evidence showing that while the magnitude of neuron outputs may contain little useful information, the angular information can almost perfectly reconstruct input data. Because the goal of finetuning is to modify the semantic content of the generated images, it is logical to target the directions of the neurons. However, arbitrary changes to these directions can lead to instability and the destruction of the model's pretrained generative abilities.

OFT addresses this by ensuring that the transformation of these neuron directions is restricted to an orthogonal manifold. By learning an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, the method ensures that the pairwise angles between neurons are preserved. This preservation of hyperspherical energy provides a structural regularity that is missing in standard finetuning. From an optimization perspective, this can be viewed as finetuning the model on a smooth hyperspherical manifold. This constraint provides an effective regularization that stabilizes the training process.

The stability of this optimization landscape is a key reason why OFT converges faster and more reliably than other methods. In standard finetuning, the model might undergo drastic weight shifts that cause "model collapse" or a sudden loss of pretrained performance. Because OFT is constrained to move along a manifold that preserves the relative relational structure of the neurons, the optimization remains smooth and predictable. This allows practitioners to use larger iteration counts without the risk of the model's output quality degrading over time, a common issue in methods like DreamBooth or standard LoRA.

# Architecture Agnosticism: Applying OFT to CNNs and Transformers

One of the most compelling attributes of the OFT framework is its architecture-agnostic nature. While many adaptation methods are specifically tailored for certain architectures—such as LoRA being primarily associated with the attention weights of Transformers—OFT can be applied to a wide variety of neural network types, including Transformers and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs).

When applied to Transformers, OFT can be used to finetune the attention weights, providing a stable alternative to LoRA. However, its application to CNNs is particularly interesting due to the block-diagonal structure of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. In a convolutional layer, if the number of blocks in $\bm{R}$ is set to equal the number of input channels, each block essentially transforms a unique neuron channel. This creates a mathematical structure similar to depth-wise convolution kernels, where a unique transformation is learned for each channel. Alternatively, if the blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared across all channels, OFT applies a uniform orthogonal transformation across the entire channel dimension.

This flexibility ensures that OFT is not limited by the specific connectivity of a layer. Whether the layer is a fully connected layer, an attention mechanism, or a convolutional kernel, the principle of applying a layer-shared orthogonal transformation remains valid. This universality makes OFT a robust candidate for general-purpose finetuning across different modalities and architectures within the diffusion modeling pipeline.

# Potential for Compositional Knowledge Transfer

A unique mathematical property of the OFT framework is its potential for compositionality, which offers an intriguing path for future research. Because OFT uses orthogonal matrices $\bm{R}$ to adapt the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$, the total transformation for a task can be viewed as a multiplication. If a model is finetuned for two different downstream tasks, say Task A and Task B, it would produce two different orthogonal matrices, $\bm{R}_A$ and $\bm{R}_B$. 

Because the product of two orthogonal matrices is itself an orthogonal matrix, these transformations can be multiplied together. This means that the knowledge acquired from Task A and Task B could theoretically be composed by applying the product $\bm{R}_B\bm{R}_A$ to the original weights. In many other finetuning paradigms, such as additive updates, combining multiple tasks often leads to interference or "catastrophic forgetting," where the learning of a new task erodes the progress made on a previous one.

The fact that OFT remains within the orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$ means that these transformations are geometrically compatible. This opens the door to investigating whether these composed matrices can preserve the knowledge of all downstream tasks simultaneously. While this is currently an open area of study, the inherent properties of orthogonal matrices provide a theoretical foundation for a more modular and composable approach to model adaptation than currently exists in the field.

# Open Problems: Scalability of Matrix Inversion and Parameter Efficiency Biases

Despite its advantages, the OFT framework presents several open research questions that need to be addressed for broader adoption. The first major challenge concerns the scalability of the Cayley parameterization. To ensure that the learned matrix $\bm{R}$ remains strictly orthogonal during training, the Cayley transform is used, which involves a matrix inversion: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$. While this is computationally feasible for smaller dimensions, the cost of performing a differentiable matrix inversion can become a bottleneck as the dimensionality $d$ of the neurons increases. Finding a way to speed up this inversion or developing a more scalable differentiable orthogonalization strategy is a key area for future work.

The second challenge involves the trade-off between parameter efficiency and model flexibility. To keep the number of trainable parameters low, OFT relies on a block-diagonal structure for the matrix $\bm{R}$. While this significantly reduces the complexity from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ or $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$, it introduces a structural bias. By forcing $\bm{R}$ to be block-diagonal, we limit the model's ability to learn transformations that involve complex cross-channel correlations. Future research must explore how to improve parameter efficiency in a more expressive way—perhaps by finding a middle ground between the rigid block-diagonal structure and the full-rank orthogonal matrix—to ensure that the model retains the maximum possible flexibility without sacrificing the stability provided by the orthogonality constraint.
