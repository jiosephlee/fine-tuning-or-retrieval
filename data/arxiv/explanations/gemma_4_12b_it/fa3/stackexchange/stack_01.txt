\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### If FlashAttention-2 is already fast, why does it only achieve 35% utilization on the H100 GPU?
Question:
I am reading the FlashAttention-3 paper and it mentions that while FlashAttention-2 was a significant improvement over standard attention, it only achieves about 35% utilization on the Hopper H100 GPU. The authors suggest this is because FlashAttention-2 follows a 'simplified synchronous model.' Can someone explain what 'synchronous' means in the context of GPU kernels? Does it mean the hardware is sitting idle while waiting for memory, or is there something else going on with the instruction scheduling?
Answer:
The $35\%$ utilization figure mentioned in the paper highlights a gap between the theoretical peak performance of the Hopper H100 GPU and the actual throughput achieved by the FlashAttention-2 algorithm. This discrepancy arises primarily because FlashAttention-2 is designed around a "simplified synchronous model," which essentially means the algorithm's execution flow is constrained by sequential dependencies that prevent the hardware from running its most powerful units simultaneously.

In the context of GPU kernels, "synchronous" refers to a pattern where the execution of one operation must complete before the next can begin. In FlashAttention-2, the core bottleneck is the relationship between the Matrix Multiplication (GEMM) and the Softmax operation. Because the Softmax operation depends on the output of a GEMM, and the subsequent GEMM depends on the output of that Softmax, the algorithm follows a linear "compute-then-compute" sequence. This creates a synchronization point where the hardware is forced to wait for the "slow" part of the operation (the Softmax) to finish before it can restart the "fast" part (the GEMM).

To understand why this leads to low utilization, we have to look at the specialization of the H100 hardware. Modern GPUs have separate hardware units for different tasks: Tensor Cores are dedicated to massive matrix multiplications, while other units (like multi-function units) handle the floating-point arithmetic and transcendental functions required for Softmax (such as exponentials). The paper provides a striking technical detail to illustrate this: the H100 SXM5 GPU has a peak of $989$ TFLOPS for FP16 matmuls, but only $3.9$ TFLOPS for special functions like exponential. 

Because the Tensor Cores are so much faster than the units processing the Softmax, a synchronous model causes the Tensor Cores to sit idle for a significant portion of the cycle. Even though the GPU is "working" on the Softmax, the most powerful part of the chip—the Tensor Cores—is doing nothing because the software logic says "don't start the next matmul until the current softmax is done." This is why FlashAttention-2 achieves much lower utilization than optimized GEMM kernels; a standard GEMM kernel keeps the Tensor Cores saturated, whereas FlashAttention-2's synchronous dependencies "starve" the Tensor Cores.

FlashAttention-3 solves this by moving toward an "asynchronous" model. Instead of waiting for a Softmax to finish, it uses techniques like warp-specialization and "pingpong" scheduling to overlap these operations. By splitting the warps into producers (handling data movement via the Tensor Memory Accelerator, or TMA) and consumers (handling computation), and by pipelining the GEMMs so that the next block's multiplication happens while the previous block's Softmax is still being processed, the algorithm "hides" the latency of the slower operations. In simpler terms, it ensures that the Tensor Cores are almost always busy by starting the next task before the previous one has fully cleared the "synchronous" bottleneck.
