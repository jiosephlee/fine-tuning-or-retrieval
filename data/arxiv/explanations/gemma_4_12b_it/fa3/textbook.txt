Introduction: The Attention Bottleneck in Large Language Models

# The Transformer Architecture and the Multi-Head Attention (MHA) Mechanism

To understand the modern landscape of artificial intelligence, one must first understand the Transformer architecture, which has become the ubiquitous foundation for large language models. At the heart of this architecture lies the attention mechanism, a process that allows the model to weigh the importance of different parts of an input sequence relative to one another. In a standard multi-head attention (MHA) setup, the model takes three primary inputs: the query, the key, and the value matrices. Let $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ represent these input sequences for a single attention head, where $N$ denotes the sequence length and $d$ represents the head dimension. 

The computation of the attention output, denoted as $\mathbf{O}$, follows a specific sequence of mathematical operations. First, the model computes the attention scores $\mathbf{S}$ by taking the matrix product of the query and the transpose of the key. This is expressed as $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$. Here, $\alpha$ is a scaling factor, typically set to $1/\sqrt{d}$ to ensure that the values within the matrix do not grow too large during computation. Once these scores are obtained, the model applies a row-wise softmax function to produce the probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$, such that $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S}) \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$. This softmax operation ensures that the weights assigned to different parts of the sequence sum to one. Finally, the output $\mathbf{O}$ is produced by multiplying this probability matrix by the value matrix: $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$.

In practical implementations, maintaining numerical stability is a significant concern. Because the exponential function used in the softmax calculation can lead to extremely large numbers, the model typically subtracts the maximum value of each row from the scores $\mathbf{S}$ before applying the softmax. Furthermore, the multi-head attention mechanism allows this process to happen in parallel across several different heads. Each head has its own set of projections, allowing the model to attend to different types of information simultaneously. When training these models, the backward pass is also defined by the chain rule, where gradients for $\mathbf{V}$, $\mathbf{P}$, $\mathbf{S}$, $\mathbf{Q}$, and $\mathbf{K}$ are calculated to update the model's weights.

# Complexity Analysis: The Quadratic Scaling Problem with Sequence Length

While the attention mechanism is highly effective for capturing relationships between words or tokens, it possesses a fundamental computational flaw: it scales quadratically with the sequence length. When we look at the dimensions of the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$, we see they are both $N \times N$. This means that as the length of the input sequence $N$ increases, the amount of memory required to store these matrices and the number of floating-point operations required to compute them grow by the square of $N$.

This quadratic scaling creates a massive bottleneck for "long-context" applications. In modern AI, there is a strong push to process much larger amounts of data at once—such as entire books, long documents, or extensive codebases. However, because of the $N^2$ complexity, doubling the length of a document does not merely double the computational cost; it quadruples it. This makes it increasingly expensive and eventually impossible to process very long sequences using standard attention implementations, as the memory requirements quickly exceed the capacity of even the most powerful hardware.

# Applications of Long-Context Modeling (Codebases, High-Resolution Images, Video)

The demand for faster and more efficient attention is driven by the wide variety of applications that require long-context modeling. One of the primary goals is to enable models to reason over multiple long documents or perform complex reasoning across massive files. For example, in software engineering, it is vital for a model to understand and navigate large codebases where a single function might depend on logic spread across dozens of different files. Similarly, in the field of computer vision, processing high-resolution images requires the model to maintain context over a large number of pixels, while video processing requires the model to maintain consistency across hundreds or thousands of consecutive frames.

Beyond text and images, long-context modeling is essential for audio processing and for facilitating user interactions that involve a long history of conversation. It is also a critical component in agent workflows, where an AI agent must maintain a long horizon of actions and observations to complete a complex task. Because these applications rely on the model's ability to "remember" and relate distant pieces of information, overcoming the quadratic bottleneck of the attention mechanism is one of the most important challenges in current machine learning research.

# Evolution of Attention Optimization: From Approximation to Software Optimization

Because the quadratic scaling of attention is such a significant hurdle, researchers have explored several different strategies to mitigate the problem. One common approach is through approximation. These methods attempt to reduce the complexity of the attention mechanism by only looking at a subset of the tokens, such as using sparse attention or other techniques that limit the number of interactions between distant tokens. While these can be very fast, they may sacrifice the model's ability to capture every nuance of the input.

Another path of research involves exploring alternative architectures entirely. Some models attempt to replace the standard Transformer attention with different mechanisms, such as RWKV, Retentive Networks, or Mamba architectures. These are designed to provide different scaling properties while attempting to achieve similar performance to the original Transformer.

Finally, there is the path of software optimization, which is the focus of the FlashAttention research. Instead of changing the math of the attention mechanism or approximating the result, software optimization seeks to make the "exact" attention calculation much faster by making it more hardware-aware. This involves designing algorithms that better suit the way GPUs actually process data, moving away from theoretical complexity and toward practical hardware efficiency.

# Introduction to FlashAttention: The Core Philosophy of Memory-Efficient Tiling

The FlashAttention family of algorithms represents a breakthrough in software optimization by focusing on the GPU's memory hierarchy. To understand this, one must understand how a GPU works. A GPU has a hierarchy of memory: Global Memory (GMEM), which is large but slow; and Shared Memory (SMEM), which is small but very fast. Standard attention implementations are often bottlenecked because they frequently read from and write to the slow Global Memory to store the large $N \times N$ intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$.

The core philosophy of FlashAttention is to eliminate these expensive intermediate reads and writes. It achieves this through a technique called "tiling." Instead of computing the entire $N \times N$ matrix at once, FlashAttention breaks the matrices into smaller blocks (or tiles) that can fit into the fast Shared Memory. By fusing all the attention operations—the matrix multiplications, the softmax, and the final value multiplication—into a single GPU kernel, FlashAttention performs the entire calculation for a block of data without ever needing to store the massive intermediate scores in the slow Global Memory.

This approach allows for "exact" attention, meaning the results are mathematically identical to standard attention, but it runs significantly faster because it minimizes the time the GPU spends waiting for data to move between memory levels. Subsequent versions of this research, such as FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3, have continued to refine this by parallelizing the workload across more dimensions of the data and exploiting advanced hardware features like asynchrony and low-precision arithmetic to further push the limits of GPU performance.

Foundations: GPU Hardware and Execution Models

# GPU Memory Hierarchy

To understand the optimization techniques presented in FlashAttention-3, one must first grasp the physical architecture of the GPU, specifically how it stores and accesses data. The GPU organizes its memory into a hierarchy of data locales where capacity and bandwidth are inversely related. This means that as you move closer to the execution units, the amount of data you can store decreases, but the speed at which you can access that data increases significantly.

At the bottom of this hierarchy is Global Memory (GMEM), also frequently referred to as High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). This is the off-chip DRAM that is accessible to all streaming multiprocessors (SMs) on the GPU. While it has a massive capacity, it is the slowest memory level in the hierarchy. Data resides in GMEM until it is requested by a kernel. To mitigate some of the latency of GMEM, the hardware includes an on-chip L2 cache, which transparently caches data from the HBM to provide faster access.

Moving inward toward the processing units, we find Shared Memory (SMEM). Shared memory is an on-chip, programmer-managed highly banked cache located within each SM. Unlike the L2 cache, which is managed by the hardware, shared memory is explicitly managed by the developer. This is a critical distinction for algorithms like FlashAttention, which rely on "tiling"—loading small blocks of data into shared memory to perform multiple computations before writing the results back to global memory. Finally, at the highest level of the hierarchy are the registers (RMEM). These are private to each individual thread and represent the fastest possible storage on the chip. While registers provide the highest bandwidth, they are extremely limited in quantity, and each thread can typically have at most 256 registers available to it.

# Thread Hierarchy

The GPU's execution model is built upon a multi-layered thread hierarchy that maps software instructions to physical hardware. At the most basic level is the thread, the smallest unit of execution. Threads are grouped into warps, which consist of 32 threads. Warps are the fundamental unit of scheduling; the GPU executes the same instruction across all threads in a warp simultaneously.

These warps are further organized into warpgroups, which consist of 4 contiguous warps. Higher-level groupings include threadblocks, also known as cooperative thread arrays (CTAs), and threadblock clusters. In the context of the NVIDIA Hopper architecture, CTAs in the same cluster are co-scheduled on the same Graphics Processing Cluster (GPC). The largest unit of organization is the grid, which encompasses all blocks and clusters submitted for a single kernel execution.

The relationship between these software abstractions and the hardware is vital for performance. Threads within the same CTA are co-scheduled on the same SM. This spatial proximity allows all threads in a CTA to address the same shared memory (SMEM) locale. Conversely, the registers are strictly private, meaning each thread in a warp or CTA has its own dedicated register space. Understanding this hierarchy allows developers to design algorithms that maximize occupancy—keeping as many warps as possible active on an SM to hide the latency of individual operations.

# Hardware Acceleration Units

Modern GPUs, particularly the Hopper architecture, feature specialized hardware units designed to accelerate the specific types of math prevalent in machine learning. One of the most significant of these is the Tensor Core. Tensor Cores are dedicated hardware units designed to perform large matrix multiplications much faster than general-purpose CUDA cores. In FlashAttention-3, these are utilized via the warpgroup-wide matrix multiply-accumulate (WGMMA) instruction.

A second critical hardware component introduced in the Hopper architecture is the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA). The TMA is a dedicated hardware unit specifically designed to handle the movement of data between global memory and shared memory. By offloading the logic of data movement to a dedicated unit, the GPU can move data more efficiently without consuming the processing cycles of the standard CUDA cores. 

The synergy between the TMA and the Tensor Cores is a cornerstone of the FlashAttention-3 design. The TMA handles the "producer" role of moving data into shared memory, while the Tensor Cores handle the "consumer" role of performing the matrix multiplications. Because these units are specialized and separate from the cores that handle general logic and floating-point math, the hardware allows for a high degree of concurrency.

# The Concept of Asynchrony

Asynchrony is the principle of performing multiple operations simultaneously such that one operation does not have to wait for another to complete before it begins. In high-throughput computing, asynchrony is the primary mechanism used to hide the "latency" of slow operations, such as moving data from HBM to an SM. If a processor had to wait for every piece of data to arrive before performing a calculation, the hardware would sit idle for the majority of its cycles.

Hopper GPUs exploit this by making the TMA and the Tensor Cores asynchronous. Because the TMA is a separate unit from the Tensor Cores, the GPU can issue a command to move a block of data from global memory to shared memory and then immediately issue a command to perform a matrix multiplication on a different block of data already present in shared memory. This allows the hardware to overlap data movement and computation.

This hardware capability enables a software technique called warp-specialization. In a warp-specialized kernel, the warps within a CTA are divided into distinct roles. Some warps are designated as "producers," whose only job is to issue TMA instructions to move data. Other warps are "consumers," which only perform the computations on that data. By separating these roles, the compiler can generate more optimal instruction schedules, and the GPU can better hide the time it takes to issue instructions and move data, leading to much higher utilization of the hardware's theoretical peak performance.

# Low-Precision Arithmetic: The Shift from FP16 to FP8

As machine learning models have grown in scale, there has been a continuous trend toward using lower-precision numerical formats to increase throughput. This began with the adoption of FP16 and BF16 and has recently moved toward FP8. Using lower precision like FP8 allows for significantly higher throughput—nearly doubling the performance of the Tensor Cores compared to FP16—while using the same amount of power and chip area.

However, moving to lower precision introduces hardware and algorithmic challenges. One major hardware constraint in the Hopper architecture is the layout requirement for FP8 WGMMA instructions. For FP16, the WGMMA instruction is flexible regarding the memory layout of its operands, but for FP8, it requires the operands to be in a "k-major" format, meaning they must be contiguous in the inner dimension. In many attention mechanisms, the matrices are naturally stored in a different format, necessitating in-kernel transposes or pre-processing steps to satisfy these hardware constraints.

Furthermore, lower precision reduces the range and accuracy of the numbers that can be represented. For example, the FP8 (e4m3) format only uses 3 bits for the mantissa and 4 bits for the exponent. This makes it particularly difficult to represent "outlier" features—values that are much larger than the rest of the distribution—which are common in large language models. To combat this, FlashAttention-3 employs techniques like block quantization, which uses a separate scaling factor for different blocks of the tensor, and incoherent processing, which uses a random orthogonal matrix to "spread out" these outliers before they are quantized.

The Evolution of FlashAttention: From Version 1 to 2

# FlashAttention-1: Tiling and Fusing to Minimize Memory Reads/Writes

To understand the evolution of FlashAttention, one must first understand the fundamental bottleneck of the standard attention mechanism within the Transformer architecture. For a single head of attention, given a query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$, a key matrix $\mathbf{K}$, and a value matrix $\mathbf{V}$—all of which have dimensions $N \times d$, where $N$ represents the sequence length and $d$ is the head dimension—the output $\mathbf{O}$ is computed through a series of transformations. Specifically, the attention scores $\mathbf{S}$ are computed as $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, where $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{d}$ is a scaling factor. From these scores, a probability matrix $\mathbf{P}$ is derived using a row-wise softmax operation, $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$. Finally, the output is computed as $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$.

The primary computational challenge in this process is that the intermediate matrices $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{P}$ have a quadratic scaling in relation to the sequence length $N$. In a standard attention implementation, these massive intermediate matrices are materialized in the GPU's global memory (GMEM), also known as High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). Because HBM has significantly lower bandwidth compared to the speed at which the GPU can perform arithmetic operations, the constant reading from and writing to this memory becomes the primary bottleneck. The GPU spends more time waiting for data to move through the memory hierarchy than it does performing the actual mathematical computations.

FlashAttention-1 was designed to solve this memory-bound problem by introducing a novel tiling strategy. Instead of materializing the full $N \times N$ matrices in HBM, FlashAttention-1 breaks the matrices into smaller blocks or tiles. By doing so, the algorithm can perform all the operations—multiplication, softmax, and value weighting—within the GPU's fast, on-chip shared memory (SMEM) or registers. This approach effectively fuses the attention operations into a single GPU kernel. By using a local version of the softmax reduction, FlashAttention-1 eliminates the need for expensive intermediate reads and writes to the slow global memory. The core intuition is to trade off a small amount of extra computation (recomputing certain values) to significantly reduce the amount of data that must be moved across the memory hierarchy.

# FlashAttention-2: Parallelizing over Sequence Length and Block-wise Matrix Multiplication

While FlashAttention-1 successfully addressed the memory bottleneck by using tiling and fusion, it left room for further optimization regarding how work was distributed across the GPU's hardware. FlashAttention-2 was developed to restructure the algorithm to improve occupancy and the distribution of work. One of the primary improvements in FlashAttention-2 is the parallelization over the sequence length dimension. In the initial version, the parallelization strategy was less optimized for the way modern GPUs distribute tasks across their many processing units.

FlashAttention-2 improves upon this by restructuring the inner loop of the forward pass to operate over blocks of the key and value matrices. By parallelizing over the sequence length, the algorithm ensures that the workload is more evenly distributed across the GPU's streaming multiprocessors (SMs). This leads to higher occupancy, meaning more of the GPU's processing units are active and working simultaneously rather than waiting for a single thread or warp to finish a large block of work.

By focusing on these improvements, FlashAttention-2 refined the execution model to be more efficient for standard training and inference. It maintains the core philosophy of FlashAttention-1—minimizing HBM access through tiling—but optimizes the "macro" organization of those tiles. This ensures that as the sequence length increases, the GPU can utilize its parallel architecture more effectively to process the attention scores and output values.

# The Utilization Gap: Why FlashAttention-2 Underperforms on Hopper H100 GPUs

Despite the advancements made in FlashAttention-2, a significant performance gap emerges when running the algorithm on the newest generation of hardware, specifically the NVIDIA Hopper H100 GPU. While FlashAttention-2 is a major improvement over standard attention, it achieves only about 35% utilization on the H100 GPU. In contrast, highly optimized matrix-multiplication (GEMM) kernels, which are the building blocks of many neural network layers, can reach 80-90% utilization on the same hardware.

This discrepancy exists because FlashAttention-2 was designed for a different generation of hardware and does not fully exploit the specific features of the Hopper architecture. One contributing factor is implementation-level: FlashAttention-2 may not use the specific Hopper-specific instructions that are available to replace the older Ampere-era instructions for Tensor Core operations. However, the authors argue that there is a more fundamental algorithmic reason for this gap.

The underlying issue is that FlashAttention-2 adheres to a "simplified synchronous model." It does not explicitly design the algorithm to take advantage of the hardware's inherent ability to perform tasks asynchronously. While the hardware has become much more specialized—with dedicated units for different types of tasks—the software algorithm still treats these operations as sequential, synchronous steps. To reach higher utilization, the algorithm must be redesigned to embrace the way modern hardware actually processes instructions and moves data.

# Synchronous vs. Asynchronous Execution Models in Kernel Design

To understand how to bridge the utilization gap, one must understand the difference between synchronous and asynchronous execution in the context of GPU hardware. Modern GPUs are throughput processors that rely on concurrency to hide the latencies involved in memory access and complex computation. In an asynchronous execution model, different parts of the hardware can work independently at the same time. For example, a GPU has specialized hardware units: Tensor Cores are dedicated to high-speed matrix multiplication, and the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) is dedicated to moving data between global memory and shared memory.

In a synchronous model, the processor must wait for one operation to complete before starting the next. If a kernel is written synchronously, the CUDA cores might sit idle while the TMA is moving data, or the TMA might sit idle while the Tensor Cores are performing a multiplication. This creates a "stop-and-go" flow that prevents the GPU from reaching its maximum theoretical throughput.

Asynchrony allows for "warp-specialization," where different groups of threads (warps) are assigned specific roles. For instance, some warps can be dedicated solely to being "producers," whose only job is to issue memory loads via the TMA. Other warps act as "consumers," which focus entirely on the mathematical computations performed by the Tensor Cores. Because the TMA and Tensor Cores operate independently, the producer warps can start fetching the data for the *next* block of the matrix while the consumer warps are still processing the *current* block. By overlapping these data movement and computation tasks, the kernel can hide the latency of the memory system, ensuring that the Tensor Cores are fed with data as quickly as possible. FlashAttention-2's reliance on a synchronous model fails to exploit this parallelism, leading to the observed utilization gap.

FlashAttention-3: Exploiting Asynchrony via Warp-Specialization

# Warp-Specialization: Dividing Warps into Producers (Data Movement) and Consumers (Computation)

In the development of high-performance GPU kernels, particularly for the Transformer architecture, a significant challenge lies in managing the parallel execution of tasks to maximize hardware utilization. FlashAttention-3 addresses this by employing a strategy known as warp-specialization. To understand this, one must first consider the organization of a GPU's execution units. A Cooperative Thread Array (CTA), often referred to as a threadblock, consists of several warps, where each warp is a group of 32 threads executing the same instruction in lockstep. 

Warp-specialization involves a fundamental architectural shift in how these warps are assigned tasks within a CTA. Instead of having every warp perform a mix of data movement and arithmetic computation, the warps are divided into two distinct roles: producers and consumers. The producer warps are dedicated exclusively to data movement, such as fetching blocks of the query, key, and value matrices from the global memory (GMEM) into the shared memory (SMEM). Conversely, the consumer warps are dedicated to the heavy lifting of arithmetic computation, specifically the matrix multiplications and the subsequent softmax operations required by the attention mechanism.

The intuition behind this specialization is rooted in the way modern compilers and hardware schedules instructions. When warps are specialized, the compiler can generate much more optimal instruction schedules. Because the producer warps are only issuing memory instructions and the consumer warps are only issuing computational instructions, the hardware can more efficiently hide the latencies inherent in each type of operation. This separation prevents the "stalling" of computational units while waiting for data, and conversely, ensures that data movement units are not held up by complex arithmetic logic. By decoupling these responsibilities, FlashAttention-3 achieves a more streamlined execution flow that better utilizes the specialized hardware units available on modern GPUs.

# The Producer-Consumer Pipeline with Circular Shared Memory Buffers

To facilitate the communication between the specialized producer and consumer warps, FlashAttention-3 implements a producer-consumer pipeline supported by a circular shared memory buffer. In this model, the shared memory is treated as a multi-stage staging area. Specifically, the algorithm defines a buffer with $s$ stages. The producer warps are responsible for filling these stages with blocks of the key ($\mathbf{K}$) and value ($\mathbf{V}$) matrices, while the consumer warps process these blocks to compute the output.

The producer mainloop iterates through the blocks of the key and value matrices. For each iteration $j$, the producer issues loads of $\mathbf{K}_j$ and $\mathbf{V}_j$ from the global memory into the $(j \pmod s)$-th stage of the circular buffer. To ensure that data is not overwritten before it is used, the producer must wait for the specific stage of the buffer to be marked as "consumed" before it can begin the next load. Once a load is completed, the producer issues a commit to notify the consumers that the data is ready for use.

On the other side of the pipeline, the consumer warps operate on a tile of the query matrix, denoted as $\mathbf{Q}_i$. The consumer waits for the $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ blocks to be loaded into the shared memory before initiating the attention computation. After the consumer finishes its work on a block—calculating the scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$, the softmax weights $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$, and updating the output $\mathbf{O}_i$—it releases that specific stage of the buffer back to the producer. This circular structure allows the producer to stay ahead of the consumer, pre-fetching data into the shared memory so that it is available as soon as the consumer finishes its current computation. This pipeline effectively masks the latency of global memory access by overlapping it with the arithmetic work performed by the consumers.

# Asynchronous Data Movement with TMA and WGMMA Instructions

The effectiveness of the producer-consumer model in FlashAttention-3 is heavily predicated on the specific hardware capabilities of the NVIDIA Hopper architecture. Specifically, the algorithm exploits the asynchrony of the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) and the Warpgroup-wide Matrix Multiply-Accumulate (WGMMA) instructions. In prior architectures, memory loads and matrix multiplications were often more synchronous, meaning the processor had to wait for a memory operation to finish before the next instruction could proceed.

The TMA is a dedicated hardware unit designed to handle the movement of data between global memory and shared memory. Because TMA is asynchronous, the producer warps can issue multiple load requests for different blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ without stalling. The hardware handles the movement in the background, and the warps only need to wait for the final completion of a specific block. Furthermore, the WGMMA instruction—which is used by the consumer warps to perform matrix multiplications—is also asynchronous. WGMMA can source its input operands directly from shared memory, allowing the consumer warps to "commit" a matrix multiplication to the Tensor Cores and immediately move on to other tasks while the hardware processes the multiplication.

This dual asynchrony is critical for the efficiency of FlashAttention-3. It allows the software to overlap the overall computation and data movement. While the consumer warps are busy performing the $ \mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^T $ matrix multiplications via WGMMA, the producer warps can be concurrently issuing TMA loads for subsequent blocks of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$. By leveraging these hardware-level asynchronous features, the algorithm minimizes the time that either the Tensor Cores or the memory units sit idle, leading to significantly higher hardware utilization compared to previous iterations of FlashAttention.

# Pingpong Scheduling: Overlapping Softmax Computation with Matrix Multiplication

A nuanced bottleneck in the attention mechanism is the disparity in throughput between matrix multiplications (GEMMs) and "special" functions like the exponential function required for softmax. On the Hopper H100 GPU, the throughput for FP16 matrix multiplications is approximately 989 TFLOPS, whereas the throughput for special functions is a mere 3.9 TFLOPS. This means that even though the number of operations for softmax might be smaller, the extremely low throughput of the multi-function unit can cause it to become a primary bottleneck, potentially taking up 50% of the cycles compared to a matrix multiplication.

To mitigate this, FlashAttention-3 introduces a "pingpong" scheduling scheme. This technique aims to hide the low-throughput softmax operations by overlapping them with the high-throughput matrix multiplications of a different warpgroup. The algorithm utilizes synchronization barriers (the `bar.sync` instruction) to orchestrate this. In this scheme, the GEMMs of warpgroup 1 are scheduled to occur before the GEMMs of warpgroup 2. This creates a window where the softmax calculation of warpgroup 1 can be executed simultaneously while warpgroup 2 is busy performing its own GEMMs. 

Once warpgroup 2 finishes its GEMMs, the roles swap: warpgroup 2 performs its softmax while warpgroup 1 performs its next set of GEMMs. By "pingponging" these two sets of operations, the algorithm ensures that the special function units are active during the periods when the Tensor Cores are already occupied with matrix multiplications. This cooperative scheduling between warpgroups maximizes the utilization of different hardware units (the multi-function units and the Tensor Cores) at the same time, further reducing the overall time required for the attention forward pass.

# Register Management: Using 'setmaxnreg' for Dynamic Allocation

High-performance GPU kernels are often limited by "register pressure," which occurs when a kernel requires more registers than the hardware can provide per thread. Registers are the fastest form of memory on the GPU, and managing their allocation is a delicate balance. In the context of FlashAttention-3, the producer and consumer warps have very different requirements. The producer warps, which primarily issue TMA loads, require very few registers. In contrast, the consumer warps require a large number of registers to store the intermediate results of the WGMMA operations and the circular buffer's state.

To solve this, FlashAttention-3 utilizes the Hopper-specific `setmaxnreg` instruction. This allows the kernel to dynamically reallocate registers between warpgroups during execution. When the CTA is initialized, the algorithm can deallocate a predetermined number of registers from the producer warpgroups. These "freed" registers are then made available to the consumer warpgroups. By using `setmaxnreg`, the consumer warps can obtain a larger share of the register file (RMEM) than they would otherwise be allowed to have.

This dynamic allocation is crucial because it allows the consumer warps to handle larger block sizes for the matrix multiplications without suffering from register spilling (where data is forced out of registers and into slower memory). Because the producer warps only need a single thread to issue a TMA load, they can operate with a minimal register footprint. By shifting these resources to the consumer warps, FlashAttention-3 ensures that the computational part of the pipeline has the resources it needs to maintain high throughput, while the producer part remains lean and efficient.

Advanced Pipelining: Overlapping GEMMs and Softmax

# Identifying Sequential Dependencies in the Attention Inner Loop

To understand the optimizations introduced in \textsc{FlashAttention-3}, one must first understand the fundamental limitations of the \textsc{FlashAttention-2} algorithm. In the standard attention mechanism, the operation is characterized by a sequence of matrix multiplications and non-linear activations. Specifically, for a given head, we compute the scores $\mathbf{S} = \alpha \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{K}^\top$, apply a row-wise softmax to obtain probabilities $\mathbf{P} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{S})$, and finally multiply by the values to get the output $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$.

When these operations are tiled to fit into the limited memory of a GPU's streaming multiprocessor (SM), they are executed in an inner loop. In \textsc{FlashAttention-2}, this loop processes blocks of the key and value matrices. However, there is a strict sequential dependency within each iteration of this loop. The local softmax operation requires the output of the first matrix multiplication (the scores $\mathbf{S}_i^{(j)}$) as its input. Subsequently, the second matrix multiplication requires the result of the softmax operation (the probabilities $\widetilde{\mathbf{P}}_i^{(j)}$) as its primary operand.

In a typical implementation, these dependencies are managed using synchronization points. The GPU must wait for the first matrix multiplication to finish before it can begin the softmax computation, and it must wait for the softmax to complete before it can start the second matrix multiplication. These "wait" instructions create a serialized execution flow. Because the hardware units responsible for matrix multiplication (Tensor Cores) and the units responsible for special functions like exponentials (multi-function units) are separate, this serialization prevents the GPU from fully utilizing its hardware. While the Tensor Cores are working on a GEMM, the special function units sit idle, and vice versa.

# The Two-Stage Pipelining Algorithm: Hiding Softmax under Asynchronous WGMMA

The core innovation of \textsc{FlashAttention-3} is the breaking of these sequential dependencies through a two-stage pipelining scheme. This is made possible by the asynchronous nature of the Hopper architecture's Tensor Cores, specifically the warpgroup-wide matrix multiply-accumulate (WGMMA) instructions. Unlike previous architectures, WGMMA allows the GPU to "commit" a matrix multiplication to the hardware and continue executing other instructions without waiting for the result to be finalized.

To exploit this asynchrony, \textsc{FlashAttention-3} reworks the consumer path of the attention algorithm to overlap non-matrix multiplication operations (like softmax) with the matrix multiplications of neighboring iterations. The algorithm maintains intermediate results in registers to act as buffers. Instead of waiting for the first GEMM to complete before starting the softmax, the algorithm initiates the first GEMM of the next iteration (computing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$) and "commits but does not wait." 

While that next iteration's score matrix is being calculated by the Tensor Cores, the algorithm performs the softmax computation and the second GEMM ($\mathbf{O}_{i} = \mathbf{O}_{i} + \tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$) for the current iteration. By "pipelining" across iterations, the algorithm ensures that the relatively low-throughput special function units (used for exponentials in softmax) are active at the same time the high-throughput Tensor Cores are busy with the GEMMs. This is achieved by storing $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ in registers, allowing the consumer warpgroup to transition smoothly between different stages of the calculation without stalling the hardware.

# Register Pressure Trade-offs: Balancing Pipeline Depth and Tile Size

While pipelining offers significant theoretical gains in throughput, it introduces a physical constraint on the GPU hardware: register pressure. Registers are the fastest memory available to the GPU, but they are a strictly limited resource within each streaming multiprocessor. Every thread in a threadblock requires a certain number of registers to store its local variables and intermediate states. If an algorithm requires too many registers, the GPU is forced to "spill" those variables into slower memory, which severely degrades performance.

The two-stage pipelining scheme in \textsc{FlashAttention-3} specifically increases register demand. To maintain the pipeline, the algorithm must store the result of the next iteration's score matrix, $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, in registers while the current iteration's softmax and second GEMM are still being processed. This adds an extra requirement of $B_r \times B_c \times \text{sizeof}(\text{float})$ registers per threadblock, where $B_r$ is the query block size and $B_c$ is the key block size.

This creates a critical engineering trade-off. A larger block size ($B_r$ or $B_c$) generally improves the efficiency of the matrix multiplications by increasing the amount of work done per instruction. However, because the pipeline also demands registers for $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$, increasing the block size simultaneously increases the register pressure. Developers must therefore find an optimal balance: a pipeline that is too deep might require so many registers that it forces a reduction in block size, while a block size that is too large might cause register spilling.

# Theoretical vs. Practical Performance: Compiler Reordering and SASS Analysis

A significant challenge in designing pipelined kernels is the gap between high-level pseudocode and the actual instructions executed by the hardware. When writing CUDA code, the programmer provides a sequence of operations, but the compiler (NVCC) is designed to optimize that code. Often, the compiler may reorder instructions to improve efficiency, which can inadvertently disrupt the carefully orchestrated timing of a pipelined algorithm. For example, if the compiler moves a "wait" instruction earlier or a "commit" instruction later than intended, the overlap between the softmax and the WGMMA might be lost.

To ensure that \textsc{FlashAttention-3} actually achieves the intended overlaps, the authors analyzed the SASS (Streaming Assembler) code. SASS is the low-level assembly language that the GPU actually executes. By inspecting the SASS, they were able to verify that the compiler preserved the intended execution flow. They confirmed that the NVCC compiler successfully generated code where the WGMMA instructions and the non-WGMMA softmax operations were interleaved as designed. This validation is crucial because it proves that the performance gains are a result of the algorithmic design rather than a lucky artifact of the compiler's optimization choices.

# Introduction to 3-Stage Pipelining for Higher Tensor Core Utilization

To push the limits of hardware utilization even further, the authors propose an extension to the two-stage pipeline: a three-stage pipelining variant. The goal of this extension is to provide even greater overlap between the second WGMMA and the subsequent softmax operations, thereby increasing the percentage of time the Tensor Cores are active. By adding another stage to the pipeline, the algorithm can theoretically hide more of the latency associated with the multi-function units.

However, as with the two-stage version, the three-stage variant comes with an increased cost in register usage. Every additional stage in the pipeline requires another buffer to hold intermediate results, further increasing the register pressure on the SM. This makes the balance between pipeline depth and tile size even more delicate. While the three-stage pipeline offers the potential for higher Tensor Core utilization, the engineering challenge lies in determining the maximum depth that the hardware can support before the reduction in tile size outweighs the benefits of the extra overlap. These considerations highlight the complexity of optimizing modern attention mechanisms for cutting-edge hardware.

High-Performance Low-Precision: Mastery of FP8

# FP8 Hardware Constraints: Layout Conformance and k-major Requirements

To understand the design choices in FlashAttention-3, one must first understand the hardware-level incentives for using low-precision formats like FP8. Modern GPUs, specifically the NVIDIA Hopper architecture, include specialized hardware units designed to accelerate matrix multiplications using low-precision numbers. For instance, the WGMMA (Warpgroup Matrix Multiply-Accumulate) instruction can target FP8 Tensor Cores, providing approximately double the throughput per streaming multiprocessor (SM) compared to FP16 or BF16 formats. This jump in throughput is a primary reason why researchers are moving toward FP8 for large-scale training and inference.

However, this performance boost comes with strict hardware constraints regarding how data must be organized in memory. In the context of a general matrix multiplication $\mathbf{A} \times \mathbf{B}^\top$ where $\mathbf{A}$ is an $M \times K$ matrix and $\mathbf{B}$ is an $N \times K$ matrix, the hardware distinguishes between different memory layouts. An operand is considered "mn-major" if it is contiguous in the outer dimension ($M$ or $N$) and "k-major" if it is contiguous in the inner dimension ($K$). While the FP16 WGMMA instruction is flexible enough to accept both mn-major and k-major formats from shared memory, the FP8 version is much more restrictive.

For FP8 WGMMA, the hardware requires the input operands to be in k-major format. This creates a significant hurdle for the attention mechanism. In standard attention implementations, the input tensors $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K},$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are typically stored as contiguous in the head dimension. While this is convenient for many operations, it does not align with the k-major requirement for the second GEMM in the attention calculation. Specifically, to compute the final output, the tiles of the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$ must be contiguous in the sequence length dimension. Because the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) hardware unit—which handles data movement—cannot change the contiguous dimension of a load, the algorithm must find a way to reorient the data to satisfy these hardware requirements.

# In-Kernel Transpositions using LDSM and STSM Instructions

Because of the layout constraints mentioned above, the developers of FlashAttention-3 had to choose between two primary strategies for handling the value matrix $\mathbf{V}$. The first option was to perform a pre-processing transpose on the $\mathbf{V}$ matrix in global memory (HBM) before the attention kernel even begins. While this is a standard approach, it is often inefficient in memory-bound scenarios such as inference, as it requires an extra pass over the data. The second option, which the authors adopted, involves performing an in-kernel transpose of the $\mathbf{V}$ tiles after they have been loaded into shared memory.

To achieve this efficiently, the algorithm utilizes the LDSM (Load Matrix) and STSM (Store Matrix) instructions. These instructions allow a warp of threads to collectively load or store data from shared memory to registers (RMEM) and back at a granularity of 128 bytes. These instructions are highly register-efficient, which is crucial because the GPU's register file is a limited resource. By using LDSM and STSM, the producer warpgroup can move the data into the correct orientation.

There is a technical nuance in using these instructions for 8-bit precision. While LDSM and STSM are typically described as moving 16-bit entries, the authors can pack two 8-bit entries into a single 16-bit slot to move FP8 data. However, because the transpose versions of these instructions cannot split these packed entries, the implementation requires specific register movements between the load and store steps to finalize the tile-wise transpose. By performing this transposition in the shadow of the computations for preceding tiles, the algorithm ensures that the overhead of the transpose does not bottleneck the overall throughput.

# Register Permutation: Bridging Accumulator and Operand Layouts

Even after solving the memory layout problem, a new challenge arises regarding how the GPU hardware handles the registers themselves. In the FP8 WGMMA instruction, there is a mismatch between the memory layout of the FP32 accumulator and the layout assumed for the FP8 operand matrix when they are held in registers. This means that the result of the first matrix multiplication (the intermediate score matrix $\mathbf{P}$) is not automatically in the correct register order to be used as an input for the second matrix multiplication (the final output $\mathbf{O}$).

To bridge this gap, the algorithm employs a register permutation technique. After the first WGMMA operation, the entries in the accumulator registers must be reordered. Specifically, the order of the registers is permuted from their original sequence to $\{ d0, d1, d4, d5, d2, d3, d6, d7 \}$. This permutation is replicated across every 8 bytes of the register file. This specific movement effectively permutes the columns of the logical $\mathbf{P}$ tile.

To ensure the final output is mathematically correct, this register permutation must be balanced by a corresponding row permutation. This is achieved by arranging the in-kernel transpose of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile to write out a matching row permutation. By coordinating the register permutation of the accumulator with the spatial transpose of the $\mathbf{V}$ tile, FlashAttention-3 allows the hardware to execute back-to-back FP8 GEMMs without needing to use expensive shuffle instructions to move data ownership between different threads.

# The Challenge of Outliers in Large Language Models

The move to FP8 is not without significant risks to the model's accuracy. The FP8 (e4m3) format is extremely compact, using only 3 bits for the mantissa and 4 bits for the exponent. Compared to FP16 or BF16, this limited precision makes it much harder to represent a wide range of values accurately. This is particularly problematic in the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), which are known to exhibit "outlier features."

Outliers are specific activations or features that have much larger magnitudes than the majority of other values in the same tensor. In a standard per-tensor quantization scheme, a single scaling factor is used to map the entire tensor to the FP8 range. If a tensor contains a few extreme outliers, the scaling factor must be set to accommodate them, which effectively "squashes" the remaining smaller values into a very narrow range of representable numbers. This loss of precision for the majority of the data leads to significant numerical errors during the attention calculation.

FlashAttention-3 recognizes that simply using a single scaling factor per tensor is insufficient for maintaining the integrity of the model's outputs. To achieve high performance without sacrificing the accuracy required for LLM reasoning, the algorithm must implement more sophisticated quantization and processing techniques that can handle these non-uniform distributions of values.

# Accuracy Preservation Techniques: Block Quantization and Incoherent Processing

To combat the errors introduced by outliers and low precision, FlashAttention-3 introduces two primary techniques: block quantization and incoherent processing. The first, block quantization, moves away from the "one-scale-per-tensor" approach. Instead, the algorithm splits the $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K},$ and $\mathbf{V}$ tensors into smaller blocks of size $B_r \times d$ or $B_c \times d$. Each block is then quantized with its own independent scalar.

This approach is highly effective because the FlashAttention algorithm naturally operates on blocks. By keeping a scalar for each block, the algorithm can account for local variations in magnitude without being forced to accommodate a global outlier. Furthermore, because the input tensors are often processed through an operation like rotary embedding just before the attention kernel, this block quantization can be fused into that preceding step. This ensures that the scaling is handled with no additional computational overhead. Once the blocks are inside the attention kernel, the algorithm can scale the scores $\mathbf{S}$ to account for the block quantization at virtually no cost.

The second technique, incoherent processing, is designed to "even out" the outliers before they even reach the quantization stage. The core idea is to apply a transformation that redistributes the magnitude of the features. By multiplying the query and key matrices by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$, the algorithm ensures that any individual outlier value is mathematically "spread out" across multiple entries in the transformed matrix. Since the outlier is no longer concentrated in a single position, the quantization error is distributed more evenly, preventing the squashing effect that occurs with per-tensor scaling.

# Orthogonal Matrix Multiplication: Spreading Outliers without Changing Output

The elegance of incoherent processing lies in the mathematical properties of orthogonal matrices. An orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ satisfies the property $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = \mathbf{I}$, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix. When we multiply both the query matrix $\mathbf{Q}$ and the key matrix $\mathbf{K}$ by this same orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$, the resulting attention scores remain mathematically identical. This can be shown through the following derivation:

$(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{K}^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$

Because the final product $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ is unchanged, the attention output remains the same, but the internal representations of $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ are modified. Because $\mathbf{M}$ is random, each entry in the transformed matrices $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M}$ becomes a random sum of the original entries. This transformation effectively breaks up the concentration of outliers.

In practice, the authors use a specific type of orthogonal matrix to maintain efficiency. They choose $\mathbf{M}$ to be the product of random diagonal matrices of $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix. This choice is significant because a Hadamard matrix can be multiplied in $O(d \log d)$ time, which is much faster than the $O(d^2)$ time required for a general matrix multiplication. Additionally, this transformation can be fused with the rotary embedding operation, allowing the "spreading" of outliers to occur with no extra computational cost to the overall system. Through these combined techniques, FlashAttention-3 achieves a 2.6$\times$ reduction in numerical error compared to standard FP8 attention.

Empirical Validation and Performance Analysis

# Benchmarking FlashAttention-3 vs. FlashAttention-2 and cuDNN

To evaluate the effectiveness of the FlashAttention-3 algorithm, the researchers conducted an extensive benchmarking suite on an NVIDIA H100 80GB SXM5 GPU. This evaluation aimed to compare FlashAttention-3 against several baselines, including the standard attention implementation in PyTorch, the previous FlashAttention-2 algorithm, and a vendor-optimized version of FlashAttention-2 from the cuDNN library. The experiments were conducted across a variety of sequence lengths, ranging from 512 to 16,384 tokens, while maintaining a constant total number of tokens across batches. The hidden dimension was set to 2,048, and the head dimensions were varied among 64, 128, and 256 to observe how the algorithm scales with different model architectures.

The results of these benchmarks demonstrate that FlashAttention-3 provides a substantial performance boost over its predecessor. In the forward pass, FlashAttention-3 is approximately 1.5 to 2.0 times faster than FlashAttention-2. This speedup is also evident in the backward pass, where FlashAttention-3 achieves a 1.5 to 1.75 times improvement. When compared to the standard attention implementation provided by PyTorch, the gains are even more dramatic, with FlashAttention-3 being between 3 and 16 times faster. This highlights the inefficiency of the standard attention mechanism, which materializes large intermediate matrices in high-bandwidth memory.

A significant milestone in the empirical validation is the performance of FlashAttention-3 relative to the industry-standard cuDNN library. While cuDNN is a highly optimized vendor library, the researchers found that for medium and long sequences—specifically those 1,000 tokens or longer—FlashAttention-3 actually surpasses the speed of the cuDNN implementation. This is a critical finding for the development of long-context applications, as it proves that the proposed optimizations for asynchrony and low-precision provide a superior path for scaling attention than even the most heavily optimized standard libraries.

# TFLOPs/s Analysis: Reaching 75% Utilization on H100 GPUs

A primary goal of the FlashAttention-3 research was to address the poor hardware utilization observed in FlashAttention-2. On the NVIDIA Hopper H100 GPU, FlashAttention-2 achieved only 35% utilization compared to the 80-90% typically seen in optimized matrix-multiplication (GEMM) kernels. FlashAttention-3 addresses this by specifically designing the algorithm to exploit the asynchronous capabilities of the Tensor Cores and the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA). By doing so, the researchers were able to push the hardware much closer to its theoretical limits.

The performance gains are quantified using TFLOPs/s (Teraflops per second), which measures the number of floating-point operations the GPU can perform every second. For the FP16 precision format, FlashAttention-3 reached up to 740 TFLOPs/s. This represents approximately 75% of the theoretical maximum throughput of the H100 GPU. This is a massive improvement over the 35% utilization seen previously, demonstrating that the new techniques for overlapping computation and data movement effectively hide the latencies that previously bottlenecked the attention mechanism.

Furthermore, the researchers explored the benefits of even lower precision. By targeting the FP8 Tensor Cores on the Hopper architecture, FlashAttention-3 achieved a throughput close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s (Petaflops per second). This demonstrates that the combination of hardware-accelerated low-precision GEMM and the proposed layout transformations allows the model to achieve nearly double the throughput of FP16 while maintaining a high level of efficiency. These results validate that the primary bottleneck in attention—computational throughput and memory access—can be significantly mitigated through architectural awareness.

# Ablation Studies: Quantifying the Gains from Asynchrony and Pipelining

To understand which specific components of FlashAttention-3 contribute most to its superior performance, the researchers performed a series of ablation studies. These studies involve isolating specific algorithmic improvements to measure their individual impacts on total throughput. For these experiments, they used a fixed set of parameters for a non-causal FP16 attention operation, including a batch size of 4, a sequence length of 8,448, 16 attention heads, and a head dimension of 128.

The ablation study focused on two key innovations: warp-specialization (which enables producer-consumer asynchrony) and the 2-stage GEMM-softmax pipelining. By comparing a baseline version of the algorithm against the full FlashAttention-3 implementation, the researchers were able to quantify the jump in performance. Specifically, the inclusion of these asynchrony-based improvements increased the throughput from 570 TFLOPs/s to 661 TFLOPs/s. 

This data confirms that the movement toward a "producer-consumer" model—where separate warps handle data movement and computation—combined with the ability to overlap the softmax calculation of one iteration with the GEMM of another, provides a tangible and significant speedup. It proves that the "pingpong" scheduling and the intra-warpgroup overlapping are not just theoretical improvements but are essential for maximizing the throughput of modern GPU architectures like Hopper.

# Numerical Error Analysis: Comparing RMSE across FP16 and FP8

While speed is a primary objective, maintaining numerical accuracy is equally important, especially when moving to low-precision formats like FP8. To analyze this, the researchers compared the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of FlashAttention-2, FlashAttention-3, and a standard attention implementation against a high-precision FP64 reference. To ensure the results were relevant to modern Large Language Models (LLMs), they simulated the presence of outlier features. They modeled these outliers by generating entries using the distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1) + \mathcal{N}(0, 100) \cdot \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.001)$. This distribution creates a scenario where 0.1% of the entries are significantly larger than the rest, a common characteristic in LLM activations.

In the FP16 precision tests, both FlashAttention-2 and FlashAttention-3 achieved 1.7 times lower RMSE compared to the standard attention implementation. The researchers attribute this success to the fact that both FlashAttention versions keep intermediate results, such as the softmax rescaling, in FP32 precision. This prevents the accumulation of rounding errors that occur when performing these sensitive operations in lower precision.

The most significant results, however, come from the FP8 analysis. A standard attention implementation using per-tensor quantization struggled with the accuracy of the FP8 format. In contrast, FlashAttention-3—utilizing block quantization and incoherent processing—achieved a 2.6 times reduction in numerical error compared to the standard FP8 baseline. This validates the authors' claim that "spreading out" outliers through random orthogonal matrices and quantizing in smaller blocks are effective strategies for making low-precision attention viable for high-performance model training and inference.

# Conclusion: The Future of Efficient Long-Context Attention

The empirical validation provided by FlashAttention-3 confirms that rethinking the attention algorithm to align with modern GPU hardware characteristics yields massive dividends in both speed and accuracy. By transitioning from a synchronous model to one that embraces asynchrony, and by providing a robust framework for FP8 low-precision, the researchers have created a primitive that significantly outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods. The achievement of 75% utilization on the H100 GPU marks a major milestone in software optimization for deep learning.

Looking forward, the researchers identify several avenues for further development. These include optimizing the algorithm specifically for LLM inference, developing persistent kernel designs to improve the performance of the FP8 kernels for smaller sequences, and investigating the long-term effects of low-precision attention on large-scale training stability. While this work focused on the Hopper architecture, the underlying principles—exploiting asynchrony, utilizing low-precision hardware, and employing block-based quantization—are expected to be applicable to future hardware accelerators. Ultimately, the goal is to provide a faster and more accurate attention primitive that will enable the next generation of long-context applications, from reasoning over massive codebases to interacting with long-term user histories.
