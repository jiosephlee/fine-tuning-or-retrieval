\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a central technique for extending the capabilities of large language models (LLMs) beyond the limits of pure supervised pre‑training.  By treating a language model as a stochastic policy that maps a user query (or **prompt**) \(x\) to a generated response \(y\), the RL framework enables the model to be guided by an external **reward signal** that reflects desired behaviours such as factual correctness, helpfulness, or coding proficiency.  This chapter explains why RL is indispensable for scaling LLMs, describes the standard RL pipeline used in practice, and discusses the stability and scalability challenges that arise when applying RL to models of billions of parameters.  We conclude by reviewing the shortcomings of prevailing algorithms—most notably Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—and outline the desiderata for a robust RL algorithm capable of training today’s gigantic LLMs.

### RL as a Tool for Language Model Alignment and Capability Scaling  

When an autoregressive language model with parameters \(\theta\) is viewed as a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\), the probability of generating a response \(y = (y_{1},\dots ,y_{|y|})\) conditioned on a query \(x\) can be written as  

\[
\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}\bigl(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t}\bigr),
\]

where \(|y|\) denotes the length of the response and \(y_{<t}=(y_{1},\dots ,y_{t-1})\).  In an RL setting we attach to each generated pair \((x,y)\) a **reward** \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\) computed by a verifier such as a reward model, a rubric, or an external judge.  The objective of RL is to adjust \(\theta\) so that high‑reward responses become more likely under \(\pi_{\theta}\).  Concretely, we aim to maximize the expected reward  

\[
\underset{x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[ r(x,y)\bigr],
\]

where \(\mathcal D\) is the distribution of prompts used during training.  By optimizing this expectation, we *align* the language model’s behaviour with human preferences and unlock advanced capabilities such as multi‑step reasoning, code synthesis, or competition‑level problem solving—tasks that are rarely covered by static supervised data alone.

### Typical RL Pipeline for LLMs  

The practical RL loop for an LLM consists of three stages that repeat over many iterations:

1. **Prompt Sampling** – A batch of queries \(\{x^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{B}\) is drawn from a large prompt pool \(\mathcal D\).  The prompts may be diverse (e.g., math questions, coding tasks, dialogue turns).

2. **Response Generation** – For each prompt we generate multiple candidate completions \(\{y^{(k)}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\) using the current **old policy** \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).  The *group size* \(G\) is often larger than one because relative ranking among several responses yields more stable advantage estimates than relying on absolute rewards.

3. **Reward Evaluation and Policy Update** – A verifier assigns a scalar reward \(r(x^{(k)},y^{(k)}_{i})\) to each response.  From these rewards we compute advantage estimates that measure how much better (or worse) each response is compared to its peers within the same group.  Finally we update the policy parameters from \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) to \(\theta\) using an RL objective that incorporates importance sampling ratios and clipping mechanisms to keep the update within a *proximal* region of the old policy.

The loop repeats: after updating the parameters we designate the newly trained model as the next \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), generate fresh rollouts, and continue optimizing.

### Stability Issues in Large‑Scale RL  

When scaling this pipeline to models with tens or hundreds of billions of parameters—and especially to sparse architectures such as Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)—the training dynamics become fragile.  Empirically, researchers observe **catastrophic model collapse**, a phenomenon where after just a few gradient steps the policy’s output distribution degenerates: likelihoods shift dramatically, generations become incoherent, and performance on downstream benchmarks plummets.  Several intertwined factors contribute to this instability:

- **Off‑policy drift**: Because rollouts are reused over multiple mini‑batches, the distribution from which samples were drawn (\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\)) diverges from the current policy (\(\pi_{\theta}\)).  If not properly corrected, gradients are computed on stale data, leading to uncontrolled updates.

- **High variance importance weights**: Importance sampling ratios attempt to re‑weight off‑policy samples back to the target distribution.  When these ratios vary wildly—particularly across long token sequences—the resulting gradient estimate becomes noisy and can amplify small modelling errors.

- **Clipping sensitivity**: Proximal algorithms employ clipping of importance ratios to bound how far an update can move away from the old policy.  In practice, with token‑level clipping, an overwhelming fraction of tokens may be clipped, effectively discarding useful learning signal while retaining noisy contributions from un‑clipped tokens.

- **Expert activation volatility in MoE models**: In sparse models only a subset of experts (sub‑networks) are active per token.  Between successive updates these routing decisions can change substantially; consequently token‑level likelihoods under \(\pi_{\theta}\) can differ dramatically from those under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), breaking assumptions behind tokenwise importance weights.

These issues compound as response lengths increase (the variance scales roughly with sequence length), making traditional proximal methods ill‑suited for training state‑of‑the‑art LLMs.

### Limitations of Existing Algorithms  

#### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

PPO introduces a clipped surrogate objective that constrains policy updates:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\; y\sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{|y|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min
\Bigl(
w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_t
\Bigr)
\Bigr],
\]

where  

\[
w_t(\theta)=
\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})},
\qquad
\widehat A_t=\text{advantage at token }t.
\]

Although PPO works well for moderate‑size policies in robotics or gaming domains, it demands a separate value model to estimate per‑token advantages.  For LLMs this extra model is comparable in size to the policy itself, incurring prohibitive memory and computation costs.  Moreover, learning accurate token‑level value estimates for long sequences is extremely challenging; any bias directly harms stability.

#### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO was introduced to avoid an explicit value model by using *relative* advantages within each group of responses:

\[
\widehat A_i=
\dfrac{
r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]
}{
\operatorname{std}_{j}[r(x,y_j)]
}.
\]

All tokens in response \(y_i\) share this same advantage.  GRPO’s objective mirrors PPO but aggregates across groups:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\;\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G}
\Bigl[
\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G
\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
%
\min
(
w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
%
\operatorname{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
)
%
\Bigr],
%
\]
with tokenwise importance ratios  

\(w_{i,t}(\theta)=
\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$

At first glance GRPO eliminates the value network; however, it still relies on token‑level importance ratios derived from single samples of each next‑token distribution.  This practice violates the fundamental premise of importance sampling: accurate correction requires averaging over many samples from the behavior distribution.  Consequently GRPO introduces high‑variance noise that accumulates with sequence length and is magnified by clipping, often precipitating irreversible collapse on large models or long responses.

### Goals of a Robust RL Algorithm for LLMs  

A practical algorithm for training today’s massive LLMs must satisfy several stringent criteria:

1. **Sequence‑level consistency** – The unit of optimization should align with how rewards are defined (entire responses).  Importance sampling must therefore be performed on whole sequences rather than individual tokens.

2. **Low variance weighting** – Importance ratios should be stable across long generations; length normalisation or other variance‑reduction techniques are essential.

3. **Minimal reliance on auxiliary networks** – To keep memory footprints manageable, an algorithm should avoid separate value models or other large auxiliary components.

4. **Stability for sparse architectures** – The method must remain robust when expert routing changes between updates, eliminating the need for complex tricks such as routing replay.

5. **Infrastructure friendliness** – Computing only sequence‑level likelihoods permits reuse of inference engine outputs and reduces precision sensitivity between training and serving pipelines.

6. **Sample efficiency** – Even when rollouts are split into many mini‑batches (common in large GPU clusters), off‑policy correction must remain effective without discarding most data through aggressive clipping.

Meeting these goals paves the way for reliably scaling reinforcement learning from modest models up to multi‑billion parameter LLMs while preserving training stability and delivering superior performance on downstream benchmarks. The remainder of this textbook will develop such an algorithm—Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)—and examine its theoretical foundations, gradient behaviour, and empirical advantages over prior approaches.

**Foundations: Policy Gradient Methods and Importance Sampling**

# Markov Decision Processes and Policy Gradients  

Reinforcement learning (RL) is formalized as a **Markov Decision Process (MDP)**, a tuple \((\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)\).  
- \(\mathcal{X}\) denotes the set of possible *states* (in the language‑model setting a state corresponds to a query \(x\) together with the tokens generated so far).  
- \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of *actions*; for an autoregressive language model each action is the choice of the next token \(y_t\).  
- \(P(s'|s,a)\) defines the transition dynamics, which in text generation are deterministic: after emitting token \(y_t\) we move to the next partial sequence.  
- The *reward* function \(R(s,a)\) assigns a scalar value to a completed response; in the paper we denote it by a verifier \(r(x,y)\in[0,1]\).  
- \(\gamma\in[0,1]\) is the discount factor; for finite‑horizon generation it is typically set to 1.

A **policy** \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) parameterized by \(\theta\) maps each state to a distribution over actions. In language modelling \(\pi_\theta\) is exactly the autoregressive model, and the probability of an entire response \(y=(y_1,\dots ,y_{|y|})\) given a query \(x\) is the product of token‑level conditionals  

\[
\pi_\theta (y|x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_\theta (y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}),
\]

where we write \(y_{<t}=(y_1,\dots ,y_{t-1})\).

The **policy‑gradient theorem** provides an unbiased estimator of the gradient of the expected return  

\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\,y\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[\,r(x,y)\,],
\]

as  

\[
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\bigl[
r(x,y)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_\theta(y|x)
\bigr].
\]

In practice we replace the raw reward with an **advantage** estimator \(\widehat A(x,y)\), which measures how much better a particular response is relative to a baseline. Substituting the advantage reduces variance while preserving unbiasedness:

\[
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\bigl[
\widehat A(x,y)\,\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_\theta(y|x)
\bigr].
\]

When dealing with very large language models (LLMs), directly sampling from the current policy at each optimization step becomes prohibitively expensive. Instead we generate a batch of responses using an *old* policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and perform **off‑policy** updates. This necessitates correcting for the distribution mismatch between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_\theta\), which leads us to importance sampling and clipping mechanisms.

# Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): Objective and Clipping  

**Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** is a practical algorithm that stabilizes off‑policy updates by constraining how far the new policy may deviate from the old one. The core component is the *importance ratio* for each token  

\[
w_t(\theta)=
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}.
\]

Given an advantage estimate \(\widehat A_t\), PPO optimizes

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
=
\E_{x,y}
\Bigg[
    \frac{1}{|y|}
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
    \min
    \Bigl(
        w_t(\theta)\,\widehat A_t,
        \,
        \operatorname{clip}\!\bigl(w_t(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_t
    \Bigr)
\Bigg],
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\varepsilon>0\) defines a symmetric clipping interval around 1. The operator  

\[
\operatorname{clip}(z, a, b)=
   \begin{cases}
      a & z<a\\[2pt]
      z & a\le z \le b\\[2pt]
      b & z>b
   \end{cases}
\]

ensures that if an importance ratio strays too far from 1—i.e., if the sample is overly “off‑policy”—its contribution is limited to the clipped value. The min operation in (1) preserves the sign of the advantage while applying this bound.

By construction, PPO’s objective reduces variance relative to naïve importance sampling and prevents overly aggressive policy updates that could destabilize training. However, PPO requires **value functions** to compute per‑token advantages. In large‐scale LLM RL this becomes a bottleneck: training a separate value head that matches the size of billions‑parameter models incurs substantial memory and compute overhead, and obtaining reliable value predictions for long generated texts remains difficult.

# Importance Sampling: Definition and Variance Considerations  

Importance sampling provides a principled way to estimate expectations under one distribution using samples drawn from another. If we wish to compute  

\[
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)],
\]

but only have access to samples from a *behavior* distribution \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), we rewrite

\[
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)]
=
\E_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\Bigl[
     \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
   \Bigr].
\tag{2}
\]

The multiplicative factor  

\[
\rho(z)=\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}
\]

is called the **importance weight**. Equation (2) is unbiased provided that every outcome with non‑zero probability under \(\pi_{\text{tar}}\) also has non‑zero probability under \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\). 

A crucial practical point is **variance**: if only few samples are drawn from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), large fluctuations in (\ref{2}) can arise because some importance weights may be extremely large or tiny. The variance diminishes as we increase the number of samples \(N\); indeed, importance sampling assumes averaging over many independent draws to faithfully correct distributional mismatch.

In language‑model RL, one could treat each *token* as an individual random variable and apply (\ref{2}) token‑wise, as done in GRPO. However, token‑level importance weights involve ratios of single‑token probabilities sampled from distinct categorical distributions (the next‑token distributions at each timestep). Those ratios are based on one observation per distribution, violating the averaging assumption required for low variance. Consequently, they introduce high‑variance noise that compounds with sequence length.

# Token‑level vs. Sequence‑level Estimators  

The paper identifies two distinct granularities for importance weighting:

1. **Token‑level estimator** – used by PPO and GRPO: each token receives its own ratio  

   \[
   w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
   \]

   While mathematically valid for on‑policy updates, when applied off‑policy with single samples per next‑token distribution it fails to approximate (\ref{2}) adequately; variance grows linearly with sequence length because each term introduces independent noise.

2. **Sequence‑level estimator** – introduced in GSPO: define an importance weight for an entire response  

   \[
   s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(
      {\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)} /
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
   \Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   = 
   \exp%
    {\Bigl(
       {1}/{|y_i|}
       \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \log
       {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}}
     \Bigr)} .
   \tag{3}
   \]

   Equation (3) aggregates all token probabilities into a single scalar before taking any expectation, thereby satisfying the requirement that importance sampling operate over many samples—the *samples* here are full sequences generated from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Moreover, normalizing by sequence length (the exponent $1/|y_i|$) controls scale across responses of different lengths and reduces variance further.

Because rewards are defined at the sequence level—\(r(x,y_i)\)—the sequence estimator aligns directly with the objective: each response receives one advantage value  

\[
\widehat A_i=
  {\bigl(r(x,y_i)- 
      {\rm mean}_j r(x,y_j)\bigr)}
  /{\rm std}_j r(x,y_j)},
\tag{4}
\]

where all members of a *group* share this same advantage (a group consists of $G$ responses sampled for identical query $x$). The GSPO objective then mirrors PPO’s clipped formulation but replaces tokenwise ratios with $s_i(\theta)$:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{x,\{y_i}\!}
  [\, 
     {1}/{G}
     \sum_{i=1}^{G}
     {\min}\bigl(
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
        {\rm clip}(s_i(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
     )
  ] .
%
% No tag number needed
\]

The clipping now operates on whole responses rather than individual tokens; if an entire trajectory is too unlikely under $\pi_\theta$ relative to $\pi_{\rm old}$ it will be down‑weighted as a unit.

# Why Value Functions Are Costly for Massive LLMs  

Traditional PPO relies on **value functions** to estimate $\widehat A_t$, typically via another neural network trained to predict expected future returns given a state (or partial sequence). For modest RL agents this overhead is acceptable, but in modern LLM RL there are two decisive impediments:

- **Parameter Scale:** State‑of‑the‑art LLMs contain tens or hundreds of billions of parameters. Adding an equally sized value network doubles memory consumption during training—a prohibitive requirement on current hardware configurations.
  
- **Computation Duplication:** Computing both policy logits and value predictions per token multiplies forward passes, roughly doubling FLOPs per training step. When generating long responses or many rollouts per query (large groups $G$), this cost becomes dominant.
  
- **Reliability at Length:** Value estimators must remain accurate across extremely long horizons (hundreds or thousands of tokens). Errors compound over steps; inaccurate advantages introduce bias into policy gradients, undermining learning stability.
  
Because GSPO replaces tokenwise advantages with *grouped, normalized rewards* (\ref{4}), it eliminates any need for an auxiliary value head. The algorithm thus sidesteps both memory and compute burdens while preserving an unbiased gradient estimator—provided that importance sampling is performed at sequence level as argued above.

In summary, this chapter has traced RL foundations from MDP formalism through classic policy gradients to modern proximal methods. By dissecting importance sampling’s variance behavior we have clarified why token‑level corrections become fragile when scaling LLMs, paving the way for sequence‑level approaches such as Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). This theoretical grounding explains both GSPO’s empirical stability and its practical advantages—particularly in contexts where value networks and massive expert routing would otherwise make reinforcement learning prohibitively complex.
