# Why Clipping More Tokens Can Actually Speed Up RL Training  

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models has become a key driver of recent breakthroughs, but the underlying algorithms are still evolving. A surprising finding from the latest **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** work is that **clipping a far larger fraction of tokens can actually improve sample efficiency** compared to the older **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** method. In this post we unpack the intuition behind this counter‑intuitive result, walk through the math that explains it, and show how noisy token‑level gradients in GRPO waste learning signal while GSPO’s sequence‑level approach extracts more useful information from each rollout.

---

## 1. The Role of Clipping in Policy‑Gradient RL  

Both PPO‑style algorithms (the family that includes GRPO and GSPO) rely on *importance ratios*—the probability of an action under the current policy divided by its probability under the behavior (old) policy. When training with off‑policy data, these ratios can become very large or very small, leading to high‑variance gradient estimates.  

To keep updates stable, PPO introduces **clipping**:  
\[
\text{clip}(w,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)
\]
where \(w\) is an importance ratio and \(\varepsilon\) is a small constant. If \(w\) falls outside the interval \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\), it is truncated to the nearest bound, effectively discarding that sample from the gradient computation.

In practice we apply clipping at two different granularities:

| Algorithm | Clipping Unit | Importance Ratio |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **GRPO**  | Token level   | \(w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\) |
| **GSPO**  | Whole response (sequence) | \(s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}\) |

Both algorithms prune “off‑policy” samples, but **GSPO does it on entire sequences**, while GRPO does it on each token individually.

---

## 2. What the Experiments Reveal  

During RL training of a 30‑B parameter mixture‑of‑experts model, researchers measured **the proportion of clipped tokens**:

* **GRPO** – only about 0.5 % of tokens are clipped (thanks to a relatively large \(\varepsilon\)).  
* **GSPO** – roughly 30 % of tokens end up clipped (the clipping range is tiny: \(\varepsilon\approx3\times10^{-4}\)).

At first glance this seems disastrous for GSPO: “If you discard most of your data, you ought to learn slower”. Yet the *training curves* and downstream benchmark scores tell a different story—GSPO consistently outperforms GRPO in both speed and final performance.

Why? The answer lies in what each algorithm *actually* does with the remaining (unclipped) data.

---

## 3. Token‑Level vs. Sequence‑Level Gradients  

### 3.1 Gradient Formulas

For GRPO the gradient (ignoring clipping for simplicity) looks like:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}} = 
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\widehat{A}_i
\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right].
\tag{1}
\]

Every token receives its own importance weight \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). These weights fluctuate wildly because each is based on a *single* sample from a next‑token distribution.

For GSPO the gradient takes a very different shape:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}} = 
\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat{A}_i\, 
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\right],
\tag{2}
\]
where
\[
s_i(\theta)=
\exp\!\Bigl(
\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log w_{i,t}(\theta)
\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

Notice that **all tokens in a response share the same scalar factor \(s_i(\theta)\)**. The individual token probabilities appear only inside the log‑average that defines \(s_i(\theta)\); they no longer modulate each token’s contribution separately.

### 3.2 Variance Implications

Because GRPO multiplies every token gradient by its own noisy ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), two undesirable effects arise:

1. **High variance across tokens:** Some tokens receive very large weights (when the new policy assigns them much higher probability), while others get values near zero. This imbalance makes the overall gradient direction unstable.
2. **Accumulation over long sequences:** The variance scales with sequence length \(|y|\). For long responses typical of LLM RL, the noise can dominate the signal, eventually causing *model collapse*.

In contrast, GSPO’s sequence‑level factor \(s_i(\theta)\) averages those per‑token ratios on a log scale and then exponentiates. By construction:
* The factor is bounded around 1 (thanks to length normalization).  
* Extreme token‑level fluctuations are smoothed out before influencing any gradient component.

Thus, even if *many* tokens are clipped (because their *sequence* importance ratio falls outside \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\)), the remaining unclipped sequences provide a **cleaner, lower‑variance learning signal**.

## 4. Visualizing the Effect  

### 4.1 Toy Example  

Imagine three sampled responses with advantages \(\widehat{A} = [+0.8, -0.2, +0.5]\). Their token‑level importance ratios under GRPO might be:

| Token | Ratio |
|-------|-------|
| t₁    | 2.0 |
| t₂    | 0.5 |
| t₃    | 3.5 |
| …     | …   |

After clipping at \(\varepsilon=0.2\), only tokens with ratios between 0.8 and 1.2 survive; everything else is zeroed out, leaving a sparse gradient that highly depends on which lucky tokens happen to sit in the safe band.

Now look at GSPO’s sequence ratios:

| Response | Likelihood old | Likelihood new | \(s_i = (\frac{\text{new}}{\text{old}})^{1/|y|}\) |
|----------|----------------|----------------|-----------------------------------|
| y₁       | 10⁻⁶           | 2·10⁻⁶         | 1.14                              |
| y₂       | 5·10⁻⁷         | 4·10⁻⁷         | 0.92                              |
| y₃       | 8·10⁻⁶         | 9·10⁻⁶         | 1.03                              |

All three responses fall comfortably within a tiny clipping window (\(±4\times10^{-4}\)). Because we clip at the *response* level, every token in each response either contributes fully or not at all—there’s no token‑by‑token lottery.

### 4.2 Empirical Distribution  

Figure below (reproduced from the paper) shows two histograms:

* **GRPO:** A narrow peak near zero clipped fraction; most tokens survive clipping but exhibit a heavy tail of extreme importance ratios.
* **GSPO:** A wide peak near ~30 % clipped fraction; however, the *remaining* ratios cluster tightly around one.

The net effect is that GSPO discards noisy outliers early, preserving signal quality and allowing larger learning steps per iteration.

---

## 5. Putting It All Together: Why More Clipping Helps  

1. **Noise Reduction:** By clipping whole responses rather than individual tokens, GSPO eliminates many outlier gradients before they can corrupt the update.
2. **Consistent Scaling:** Length normalization keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) in a narrow numeric range regardless of response length, making a tiny clipping window sufficient.
3. **Better Sample Utilization:** Even though fewer tokens are used for backpropagation, each surviving token carries *equal* weight derived from a reliable sequence‑level estimate.
4. **Stability for MoE Models:** In mixture‑of‑experts architectures expert routing can change drastically across updates, breaking token‑level ratios in GRPO. Sequence likelihood changes far more smoothly, so GSPO remains stable without extra tricks like Routing Replay.

Consequently, **GSPO achieves higher sample efficiency despite clipping more tokens**—the algorithm trades quantity for quality in a way that pays off sharply when training massive LLMs with long generations.

---

## 6. Takeaways for Practitioners  

- **Don’t equate “more clipped data” with “wasted data.”** If your clipping unit matches the reward granularity (sequence vs token), aggressive clipping can actually protect you from high‑variance updates.
- When scaling RL to longer responses or MoE models, prefer sequence‑level importance sampling; it sidesteps expert activation volatility and reduces memory overhead.
- Smaller ε values are fine for sequence‐level ratios because those ratios are naturally bounded after length normalization; you can safely tighten clipping without sacrificing learning speed.
- If you still need per‑token advantage control (e.g., multi‐turn dialogue), use the GSPO‑token variant—it retains GSPO’s stable weighting while allowing finer advantage shaping.

---

## 7. Final Thoughts  

Clip more aggressively → discard more tokens → **faster**, more stable RL training—sounds paradoxical? Not when you recognize that *what you clip matters*. GSPO shows that aligning the clipping granularity with where rewards live (the whole response) yields cleaner gradients and lets us push reinforcement learning farther than ever before on today’s gigantic language models.

If you’re curious to try GSPO yourself or want deeper dive into its theoretical foundations, feel free to explore the original paper “Group Sequence Policy Optimization” and experiment with different group sizes \(G\), clipping windows, and advantage normalizations to fit your own workload.

Happy training!
