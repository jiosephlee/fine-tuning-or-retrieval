\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What exactly is meant by the “new scaling law” introduced for 1.58‑bit LLMs?
Question:
In Section 4 the paper mentions that BitNet b1.58 enables a new scaling law relating model performance and inference cost, and then lists heuristic equivalences (e.g., 13B BitNet ≈ 3B FP16). Is there a formal mathematical expression for this scaling law, or is it purely empirical? How should one extrapolate it to unseen model sizes or token counts?
Answer:
The “new scaling law” mentioned in the BitNet b1.58 paper is not a mathematically‑derived relation in the sense of the classic $N\times C\times T$ power‑law that appears in many LLM papers (where performance is expressed as a smooth function of model size $N$, compute $C$ and token count $T$).  
What the authors call a scaling law is simply an **empirical observation** that, when you replace 16‑bit floating‑point parameters by 1.58‑bit ternary parameters, the *inference cost* (latency, memory and energy) of a given model drops roughly in proportion to the reduction in storage‑bit‑width, while the *predictive quality* (perplexity, zero‑shot accuracy) stays essentially unchanged once the model reaches about 3 B parameters.

In practice they summarise this observation with a handful of “equivalences”, e.g.

* a 13 B BitNet b1.58 model uses less latency, memory and energy than a 3 B FP16 model,
* a 30 B BitNet b1.58 model is cheaper than a 7 B FP16 model,
* a 70 B BitNet b1.58 model is cheaper than a 13 B FP16 model.

These statements are derived directly from the measurements shown in Figures 2–4 of the paper (latency per token, GPU memory footprint and an energy‑per‑operation estimate). The authors do **not** provide an explicit formula such as  

$$
\text{Cost}_{\text{b1.58}}(N)=\alpha\, N^{\beta}\bigl(\tfrac{1.58}{16}\bigr)^{\gamma},
$$

nor do they fit a curve to their data. The “law” therefore consists of two ingredients:

1. **Cost reduction factor** – because each weight now occupies only 1.58 bits instead of 16, the memory required for the weight matrix shrinks by roughly $1.58/16 \approx 0.099$. Since most of the FLOPs come from matrix multiplications, the number of *addition* operations (which dominate the energy consumption) scales similarly, giving a proportional reduction in inference energy.
2. **Performance preservation** – empirical results show that for models ≥ 3 B parameters the loss in perplexity or downstream accuracy is negligible; consequently one can compare models of different bit‑widths on a *performance‑per‑cost* basis rather than on absolute size.

### How to extrapolate

Because there is no closed‑form expression, extrapolation must follow the same heuristic reasoning used for the three quoted pairs:

1. **Pick a target FP16 budget** (e.g., “I can afford the latency of a 7 B FP16 model”).  
2. **Divide the budget by the observed cost ratio** between b1.58 and FP16 at comparable scales. From the three data points you can infer an approximate linear relationship between *model size* and *cost advantage*: roughly each increase of ~10× in FP16 size corresponds to about a 2–3× increase in b1.58 size while keeping cost constant. In other words  

   $$
   N_{\text{b1.58}} \approx 2.5\, N_{\text{FP16}}
   $$

   gives roughly equal inference cost (latency, memory, energy) for the range examined (3–70 B).  

3. **Adjust for token count** – the paper keeps token count fixed when comparing sizes, so any change in training or inference tokens should be handled as in standard LLM scaling laws: multiply the compute cost by $T/T_{0}$ (where $T_{0}=100\,\text{B}$ tokens in their experiments). The bit‑width reduction still applies multiplicatively, so for longer sequences you would still use  

   $$
   \text{Cost}_{\text{b1.58}}(N,T)\;\approx\;0.10\,\frac{N}{N_{\text{FP16}}}\,\frac{T}{100\,\text{B}}
   $$

   as a rough rule‑of‑thumb.

4. **Validate with measurements** – since the “law’’ is empirical, it is advisable to run a small benchmark at your intended scale (e.g., measure latency on one or two layers) to confirm that the proportionality holds; hardware details (cache size, kernel implementation) can shift the factor.

### Summary

- The “new scaling law’’ is an empirical cost–performance trade‑off observed on three model families (13 B ↔ 3 B, 30 B ↔ 7 B, 70 B ↔ 13 B).  
- No formal analytic expression is given; it is essentially “$k\times$ more parameters at 1.58 bits give you roughly the same inference cost as a full‑precision model with $k/≈2.5$ parameters”.  
- To extrapolate, scale the FP16 size by about 2½ while keeping token count fixed; then apply the same factor to latency/memory/energy estimates as you would for an FP16 model at the original size.  
- Because this relationship rests on measured hardware behaviour rather than theory, users should verify it on their own platform before relying on it for large‑scale planning.

Thus, treating it as a *heuristic rule* rather than a strict mathematical law gives you a practical way to decide how large a BitNet b1.58 model you need to match the cost envelope of any given FP16 baseline.
