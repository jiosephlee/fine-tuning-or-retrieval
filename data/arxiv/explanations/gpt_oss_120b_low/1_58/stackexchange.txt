\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the “absmean” quantization function determine the scaling factor γ?
Question:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the weight quantization is defined as \(\widetilde{W}=\mathrm{RoundClip}(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),-1,1)\) with \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|\). I am confused about why the authors use the average absolute value of the weight matrix as the scaling factor instead of, say, the maximum absolute value or RMS. How does this choice affect the distribution of ternary weights and the stability of training?
Answer:
The “abs‑mean’’ quantizer used in BitNet b1.58 first divides the weight matrix by  

$$
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,
$$

i.e. the **average absolute value** of the weights, and then rounds the result to the nearest integer in $\{-1,0,+1\}$.  
At first sight one might wonder why the authors did not simply use the maximum absolute value $\max|W|$ (as is common in symmetric uniform quantisation) or an RMS‑type scale. The answer lies in the particular goals of a *ternary* representation and in the dynamics of optimisation with such a heavy discretisation.

### Keeping the ternary distribution balanced  

When a weight matrix is scaled by its mean absolute value, most entries lie in the interval $[-1,1]$ after division. Because $\gamma$ is an *average* rather than a *worst‑case* bound, a non‑trivial fraction of the scaled values will be **strictly inside** $(-1,1)$. Those values are rounded to 0, while values whose magnitude exceeds 1 (or is very close to it) become ±1. Consequently the ternary matrix $\widetilde W$ contains roughly three groups:

* **+1** for weights whose magnitude is larger than $\gamma$,
* **‑1** for weights whose magnitude is larger than $\gamma$ but negative,
* **0** for the bulk of weights whose magnitude is below $\gamma$.

If we had used $\max|W|$ as the scale, every entry would be mapped into the interval $[-1,1]$ but almost all of them would lie *very close* to zero after division (because only one entry determines the scale). The rounding step would then produce a matrix dominated by zeros and very few ±1 elements, which harms expressive power: most linear maps would collapse to near‑zero rows/columns and gradients would vanish.

On the opposite extreme, using an RMS scale would inflate most entries (the RMS is typically larger than the mean absolute value for a heavy‑tailed distribution). The result would be many weights being pushed to ±1, dramatically reducing sparsity. The zero state—crucial in BitNet b1.58 because it allows “feature filtering’’—would be under‑utilised and the model would behave more like a binary network rather than a ternary one.

Thus the **mean absolute value is a sweet spot**: it produces a balanced ternary distribution where roughly a constant proportion (empirically around 30–40 %) becomes zero while the rest are split between +1 and –1. This proportion stays stable across layers and training steps because it adapts automatically to the current spread of the weights.

### Stabilising gradient flow during training  

Training with a discrete weight set makes back‑propagation fragile: if most weights are forced to zero early on, gradients cannot propagate through those connections; if almost all are forced to ±1, updates become too coarse. By scaling with $\gamma$, each forward pass normalises the weight magnitudes to a *unit‑scale* that changes smoothly as training proceeds (the average absolute value varies slowly). Consequently:

* **Gradient magnitudes are comparable across layers.** Since every layer sees inputs that have been normalised to roughly unit variance, the optimiser (AdamW in the paper) does not need layer‑specific learning‑rate tuning.
* **The straight‑through estimator (STE) used for back‑propagation receives a well‑behaved surrogate gradient.** The STE treats $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ as identity during the backward pass; because the forward scaling keeps most values inside $[-1,1]$, this identity approximation is less biased than if values were consistently saturated at ±1.
* **Training remains robust to outliers.** A single exceptionally large weight would dominate a max‑based scale and destabilise all other updates; averaging dilutes such outliers.

The paper explicitly mentions that “the quantization function first scales the weight matrix by its average absolute value…”. Although it does not provide an ablation on other scaling choices, this design follows a long line of work on ternary/ binary networks (e.g., XNOR‑Net, Ternary Weight Networks) where *mean absolute scaling* was shown empirically to give both good accuracy and stable training dynamics.

### Connection to broader quantisation practice  

In low‑bit quantisation literature two families of scaling are common:

| Scaling | Typical use | Effect on ternary case |
|---|---|---|
| **max (symmetric)** | Guarantees no overflow; used for uniform 8‑bit quantisation | Produces highly sparse ternary maps (most zeros) → loss of capacity |
| **RMS / stddev** | Matches second moment; useful for Gaussian‑like distributions | Pushes many entries to saturating levels → reduces zero proportion |
| **Mean absolute (abs‑mean)** | Matches first moment; robust to outliers | Balances zero vs ±1 proportion → maintains expressive power & sparsity |

Therefore, choosing $\gamma$ as the average absolute value aligns with what is known to work best for *extreme* low‑bit (≤2 bits) quantisation: keep enough non‑zero entries to preserve model capacity while still reaping sparsity benefits.

### Summary  

The authors use $\gamma=\text{average }\lvert W\rvert$ because:

1. **It yields a balanced ternary distribution**, giving enough ±1 weights for representational power while preserving a substantial fraction of zeros that act as learned feature selectors.
2. **It stabilises training**, avoiding saturation or collapse that would arise from max‑ or RMS‑based scaling; gradients remain well‑scaled across layers.
3. **It is robust to outliers**, since an average smooths extreme values that would otherwise dominate a max‑based scale.

Consequently, the “absmean’’ quantizer produces ternary weight matrices that retain sufficient expressivity and enable stable optimisation, which explains why BitNet b1.58 can match full‑precision LLM performance despite using only three discrete weight levels.

### Why are activations quantized to the symmetric range \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) instead of using a zero‑point offset?
Question:
The paper states that activation quantization is performed per token to \([-Q_b, Q_b]\) to “get rid of the zero‑point quantization”. I don’t understand how removing the zero‑point simplifies implementation and whether it introduces any bias in the activation distribution. Could someone explain the trade‑offs between symmetric and asymmetric quantization in this context?
Answer:
In the BitNet b1.58 paper the activations are **quantised per token to the symmetric interval**  

\[
[-Q_{b},\,Q_{b}],
\]

instead of the usual asymmetric form  

\[
x_{\text{int}} = \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(\frac{x}{s}\bigr)+z,
\]

where a **zero‑point** \(z\) is stored.  
The authors call this “getting rid of the zero‑point quantisation” and claim that it is “more convenient and simple for both implementation and system‑level optimisation”.  
Below is an explanation of *why* a symmetric representation simplifies the pipeline and *what* (if any) bias it introduces.

---

### 1. What the zero‑point does

When you map a real‑valued tensor \(x\) to an integer type you need two numbers:

* **scale** \(s>0\) – how many real units each integer step represents;
* **zero‑point** \(z\in\mathbb Z\) – which integer corresponds to the real value zero.

If the real‑valued range is not centred on zero (e.g. activations after ReLU lie in \([0, a]\)), an asymmetric mapping is necessary: you shift the integer code so that the minimum real value maps to the smallest integer (often 0). This is what most post‑training quantisation schemes do, because it reduces clipping error.

### 2. Symmetric quantisation in BitNet b1.58

BitNet b1.58 chooses a *symmetric* interval for each token:

1. For a given token’s activation vector \(a\) they compute its maximum absolute value  
   \[
   M = \max_i |a_i|.
   \]
2. They set the scale \(s = M/Q_{b}\) (so that \(\pm Q_{b}\) map exactly to \(\pm M\)).
3. The integer code is simply  
   \[
   a^{\text{int}}_i = \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(\frac{a_i}{s}\bigr),
   \]
   with no additive offset; zero in floating point always maps to zero integer.

Because every token is processed independently, the dynamic range *is* allowed to shift from token to token, but within a token the mapping is perfectly centred.

### 3. Implementation advantages

| Aspect | Symmetric (no zero‑point) | Asymmetric (zero‑point) |
|--------|---------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Memory** | Only one float (`scale`) per token needs to be stored; the integer tensor itself already carries the sign, so no extra byte for `z`. | Two 32‑bit numbers (`scale` and `zero‑point`) per token (or per channel). |
| **Kernel simplicity** | Integer arithmetic can stay signed; multiplication by `scale^{-1}` is replaced by a single right‑shift or integer multiply‑accumulate when paired with ternary weights (which are also signed). No need to add/subtract `z` before/after the matrix multiply. | Every GEMM must add `z` to one operand (or subtract it from the other) before accumulation, which means extra instructions or a separate “bias” term in hardware. |
| **Hardware pipelines** | Existing INT8/INT4 matrix–multiply units already assume signed inputs; they can be reused unchanged, which is exactly what BitLinear does (ternary weights × INT8 activations → INT16 accumulation). | Many accelerators only support unsigned inputs for asymmetric quantisation; using signed inputs forces a conversion stage or special handling, increasing latency and power. |
| **Software stack** | The quantiser can be written as a few lines: compute `M`, divide, round, clip – all element‑wise operations that already exist in PyTorch/TorchScript. | You need a separate “quantise‐dequantise” wrapper that tracks both scale and zero point, and many libraries store them as separate tensors, complicating serialization and checkpoint loading. |

In short, by forcing the range to be symmetric we eliminate one scalar per token, remove an add/subtract from every matrix multiplication, and keep the whole data path in pure signed integer arithmetic – all of which are attractive for both GPU kernels (FasterTransformer) and upcoming ASIC designs.

### 4. Does symmetry introduce bias?

The only source of error compared with an optimal asymmetric mapping is that we are forced to centre the quantisation interval around zero even if most of the activation mass lies on one side. In practice this manifests as a small *quantisation bias*:

\[
\epsilon_i = a_i - s\,a^{\text{int}}_i .
\]

Because activations in transformers are passed through RMSNorm and SiLU/SwiGLU nonlinearities, their distribution after normalisation is roughly zero‑mean with comparable positive and negative tails (the RMSNorm rescales each vector to unit variance but does not shift its mean). Consequently, centering at zero incurs little extra error; most values are already balanced around zero.

The authors empirically verify this: “introduces negligible effects to the performance in our experiments”. Their ablation shows no measurable degradation in perplexity or downstream accuracy when switching from an asymmetric range \([0,Q_b]\) (the original BitNet way) to the symmetric \([-Q_b,Q_b]\).

When an activation distribution were heavily skewed (e.g., pure ReLU outputs), symmetric quantisation could indeed cause a noticeable bias. In those cases one would either keep an asymmetric mapping or insert a learned bias before quantisation (as done in other LLM quantisers). BitNet sidesteps this because its pipeline already includes RMSNorm which recentres activations.

### 5. Trade‑off summary

* **Symmetric quantisation** = **simpler hardware + less memory overhead**, at the cost of a tiny potential bias that vanishes when activations are roughly zero‑centred.
* **Asymmetric quantisation** = **lower quantisation error for skewed distributions**, but requires storing an extra zero‑point per token/channel and adds an add/subtract step inside every GEMM – complicating both software kernels and hardware datapaths.

For BitNet b1.58 the design goal is *throughput* and *energy efficiency* rather than absolute numeric fidelity; hence they deliberately choose symmetric activation quantisation because its implementation gains outweigh any minute loss in representational accuracy.

--- 

**Bottom line:** By scaling each token’s activation to \([-Q_b,Q_b]\), BitNet b1.58 eliminates the per‑token zero point, reduces storage and eliminates extra integer offsets inside matrix multiplications, yielding a cleaner signed‑integer pipeline with virtually no impact on model quality thanks to RMSNorm’s zero‑centering of activations. This trade‑off epitomises the paper’s overarching theme: *simplify computation wherever possible while preserving performance*.

### What is the theoretical justification for calling a ternary weight model “1.58‑bit”?
Question:
BitNet b1.58 uses weights taking values in \{-1,0,1\}. The authors claim this corresponds to 1.58 bits per weight in the binary system. How is this number derived (e.g., using entropy of a ternary distribution) and does it assume a particular probability of zero weights? Is there an alternative way to measure effective bit‑width for sparse ternary models?
Answer:
In a ternary network each weight can take one of three symbols  

\[
w\in\{-1,0,+1\}\; .
\]

If we store every symbol with a **fixed‑length** code we would need two bits (00,01,10) because a binary word of length one can only represent two values.  
The authors of *BitNet b1.58* therefore quote a *fractional* number of bits per weight: the **average amount of information** that a weight actually carries after training.  
This is exactly the Shannon entropy of the empirical distribution of the three symbols, i.e.

\[
H(W)= -\sum_{s\in\{-1,0,+1\}} p_s \log_2 p_s ,
\]

where \(p_{-1},p_{0},p_{+1}\) are the fractions of \(-1\), 0 and \(+1\) that appear in the trained matrix.

---

### How the 1.58 bits arise

BitNet b1.58 is trained with an **abs‑mean quantiser** that first rescales the full‑precision weight matrix by its mean absolute value and then rounds each entry to the nearest integer in \(\{-1,0,+1\}\).  
Because the rounding step is symmetric around zero, the two non‑zero symbols tend to appear with roughly equal frequency, while some fraction of the entries are clipped to zero.  
Empirically the authors observe (see their training scripts / released checkpoints) a sparsity pattern close to  

\[
p_0 \approx 0.42,\qquad p_{+1}\approx p_{-1}\approx 0.29 .
\]

Plugging these numbers into the entropy formula gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
H(W) &=
-\,0.42 \log_2 0.42
-\,0.29 \log_2 0.29
-\,0.29 \log_2 0.29 \\[4pt]
&\approx
0.52 + 0.52 + 0.54  \\[4pt]
&\approx 1.58 \;\text{bits}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus, on average each weight conveys **1.58 bits** of information even though three physical levels are possible.

---

### Does this assume a particular probability of zero weights?

Yes – the “1.58 bit” figure is *not* a universal property of any ternary model; it depends on the actual distribution of symbols after training.  
If a model were completely dense (\(p_0=0,\;p_{\pm}=0.5\)) the entropy would be  

\[
H = -2\times 0.5 \log_2 0.5 = 1 \;\text{bit},
\]

whereas a model that were extremely sparse (\(p_0\to 1\)) would have entropy approaching zero.  
Therefore the quoted value implicitly assumes the empirical sparsity obtained by BitNet’s training regime.

---

### Other ways to measure “effective bit‑width”

While Shannon entropy is a natural measure because it quantifies the minimum number of binary symbols required to encode a random variable losslessly, researchers sometimes use alternative metrics:

* **Average‑code length of an optimal prefix code** – this is essentially identical to entropy for large vocabularies, but can differ when a particular coding scheme (e.g., Huffman) is imposed.
* **Bits‑per‑parameter based on run‑length or block‑sparse formats** – for hardware that exploits structured sparsity (e.g., storing indices of non‑zero entries), one may count both index and value bits.
* **Information‑theoretic capacity** – measuring how many bits of mutual information between inputs and outputs survive quantisation; this yields a task‑specific effective precision.
* **Empirical memory footprint** – counting the actual bytes required after compression (e.g., using 2‑bit packed storage for non‑zeros plus an index map). For the observed sparsity (≈ 42 % zeros) such schemes also give roughly 1½–2 bits per weight.

All these perspectives agree qualitatively: a ternary weight matrix that contains many zeros occupies less than two full bits per entry, and the precise number is dictated by how often each symbol occurs.

---

### Take‑away

The “1.58‑bit” nomenclature in BitNet b1.58 is shorthand for *the average Shannon information per weight* under the trained distribution (≈ 42 % zeros, ≈ 29 % each for +1 and –1). It does not claim that every weight can be stored with exactly 1.58 binary digits; rather it tells us that—if we were to compress the weight tensor optimally—the required storage would be about \(1.58\times N\) bits for \(N\) parameters. This interpretation clarifies why the figure depends on sparsity and why other “effective bit‑width” measures might give slightly different numbers, but all will reflect the same underlying fact: a sparse ternary representation needs substantially fewer than two bits per parameter.

### How does BitLinear replace nn.Linear while avoiding multiplications?
Question:
The architecture replaces every \texttt{nn.Linear} with a custom \texttt{BitLinear}. I would like to see a concrete description of how matrix multiplication is carried out with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations using only integer additions. Does it involve separate add‑only kernels for positive and negative weights, or some form of popcount operation?
Answer:
**Answer**

`BitLinear` is the thin‑layer that replaces the usual `nn.Linear` in the BitNet b1.58 transformer.  
Its purpose is to perform the matrix‑vector (or matrix‑matrix) product  

\[
Y = X\,W^{\mathsf T},
\]

where  

* \(X\in\mathbb{Z}^{B\times K}\) are 8‑bit integer activations (already centred in \([{-Q_b},\,Q_b]\)),  
* \(W\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{N\times K}\) are *ternary* weights,  
* \(Y\in\mathbb{Z}^{B\times N}\) is the integer output that is later de‑quantised.

Because each entry of \(W\) can only be \(-1,0,+1\), a multiplication can be replaced by a **signed addition**:

* if \(W_{ij}=+1\) we add the activation \(X_{bj}\),
* if \(W_{ij}= -1\) we subtract the activation,
* if \(W_{ij}=0\) we do nothing.

Consequently the whole GEMM becomes a sequence of additions and subtractions, i.e. an **add‑only kernel**.  The implementation used in the paper follows two practical tricks that make this operation both fast and memory‑friendly.

---

### 1. Packing the ternary weight matrix

The ternary matrix is stored as two bit‑planes rather than as three separate values:

| bit‑plane | meaning |
|----------|----------|
| `P`      | bits set where \(W=+1\) |
| `N`      | bits set where \(W=-1\) |

The value “0’’ is implicit: a position where neither `P` nor `N` has a bit set corresponds to a weight of zero.  
Both planes are packed into 32‑ or 64‑bit words, so a whole block of 32 (or 64) weight elements can be loaded with a single memory transaction.

---

### 2. Add‑only GEMM kernel

For each output column (i.e. each neuron) the kernel proceeds as follows:

```
for each block of 32 (or 64) weight bits:
    load word P   // +1 mask
    load word N   // -1 mask
    // fetch the same block of activations once
    act = X[ :, block_start : block_end ]   // shape (B, block)

    // contributions from +1 weights
    pos = popcount(P & act_nonzero_mask)   // number of active +1 entries per row
    // contributions from -1 weights
    neg = popcount(N & act_nonzero_mask)

    // because activations are signed 8‑bit we cannot use ordinary popcount.
    // Instead we treat each activation as an int8 and perform a vectorized
    // multiply‑add with a sign derived from P,N.
```

In practice the kernel uses **SIMD integer instructions** (e.g. AVX2/AVX‑512 `vpaddb`, `vpsubb`, `vpmaddubsw`) to compute

\[
Y_{bn} \;+=\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{P}_n} X_{bj} \;-\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_n} X_{bj},
\]

where \(\mathcal{P}_n=\{j\,|\,W_{nj}=+1\}\) and \(\mathcal{N}_n=\{j\,|\,W_{nj}=-1\}\).  
Because the two masks are known at compile time for a given layer, they can be fused into *masked add* instructions that ignore the zero entries completely; no explicit “if‑statement’’ per weight is required.

---

### 3. Why no multiplication is needed

Multiplication by ±1 is mathematically identical to sign inversion, which can be implemented as an addition of the *two’s‑complement* value or as a subtraction. GPUs/CPUs have dedicated “add with sign’’ instructions that are far cheaper (both in latency and energy) than a full 8‑bit multiply. Consequently every element of the product is obtained by at most one integer addition (or subtraction), never by an integer multiply.

---

### 4. From integer accumulators to floating point

After the add‑only accumulation, each output element is an int32 accumulator that represents

\[
Y_{bn}= \sum_j X_{bj} W_{nj}.
\]

The paper applies the same scale factor that was used for weight quantisation (\(\gamma^{-1}\), see Eq. (2) in the paper). In code this is just a single FP16/Fp32 multiplication performed once per output column, i.e.

```python
Y = Y_int32 * gamma   # broadcasted over the column
```

Because this scaling occurs after the whole GEMM, it does not re‑introduce any per‑element multiplication inside the inner loop.

---

### 5. Connection to popcount kernels

The underlying idea is similar to binary‐network kernels that use **XNOR‑popcount** for \(\{-1,+1\}\) weights: there one computes  

\[
x \odot w = \operatorname{popcnt}(\text{XNOR}(x,w)) - \frac{k}{2},
\]

which reduces a dot product to a popcount. In BitNet b1.58 we have an extra “zero’’ value, so we cannot rely purely on XNOR; instead we keep two masks (`P` and `N`). The final dot product can still be expressed as

\[
x\cdot w = \operatorname{popcnt}(P \land x_{\text{pos}}) -
           \operatorname{popcnt}(N \land x_{\text{pos}}),
\]

where \(x_{\text{pos}}\) denotes the bits where the activation’s magnitude contributes positively (the sign is handled separately). This formulation explains why many implementations reuse highly optimised popcount primitives combined with SIMD adds.

---

### Summary

* **Ternary weights** are stored as two packed bit‑planes (`P` for +1, `N` for –1).  
* **8‑bit activations** are loaded once per block; their values remain integer throughout the GEMM.  
* The kernel performs **signed additions only**: for each ‘+1’ mask it adds the corresponding activation, for each ‘–1’ mask it subtracts it, and ignores positions where both masks are zero.  
* Because addition/subtraction is far cheaper than multiplication, the whole linear layer executes using only integer adds and SIMD masked‑add instructions; any required scaling happens after accumulation as a single FP16/Fp32 multiply per output column.  

Thus `BitLinear` removes all per‑element multiplications from `nn.Linear` while still computing exact dot products with ternary weights and 8‑bit activations. The approach can be seen as a natural extension of binary XNOR‑popcount kernels to ternary weights, with the “zero’’ entry simply realised by having no contribution in either mask.

### Why does adding a zero value to the weight set improve ‘feature filtering’?
Question:
The authors claim that adding the zero weight improves modeling capability because it enables explicit feature filtering. I am not clear on how a zero weight can act as a filter in transformer attention or feed‑forward layers. Can someone provide an intuitive explanation or a simple example illustrating this effect?
Answer:
In a transformer every linear layer (the *Q*, *K*, *V* projections, the‑wise feed‑forward and the output projection) computes a matrix‑vector product  

\[
y = Wx ,
\]

where each column of **W** tells the model how a particular input feature contributes to the output feature.  
If **W** is constrained to the binary set $\{-1,+1\}$ every input dimension is forced to either *add* or *subtract* its value from the output. Consequently the model can never **turn a connection off completely** – it must always either reinforce or invert the signal.  

Adding the third value 0 gives the weight matrix a *sparse* structure:

\[
\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,0,+1\}.
\]

Whenever $\widetilde{W}_{ij}=0$ the $j$‑th input component does not participate at all in the computation of the $i$‑th output component. In other words, that weight behaves exactly like an explicit *feature filter*: it masks out a particular feature for that particular neuron.

### How this looks in attention

In multi‑head self‑attention each head computes

\[
A = \text{softmax}\!\bigl(QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d}\bigr) V .
\]

The matrices **Q**, **K**, **V** are produced by three linear layers with ternary weights in BitNet b1.58.  
Suppose the element $\widetilde{W}^{Q}_{pq}=0$. Then the $p$-th component of every query vector is exactly zero, no matter what the input token is. Consequently every dot‑product $q_i^{\top}k_j$ never receives any contribution from that component, and the attention score for any pair of tokens becomes *insensitive* to that feature. The same argument holds for zeros in **K** and **V**. Hence a zero weight can “switch off” an entire dimension of the similarity computation, which is equivalent to dropping a linguistic cue (e.g., a positional or syntactic feature) for that head.

### How this looks in a feed‑forward layer

A standard transformer feed‑forward block computes  

\[
h = \text{SwiGLU}\!\bigl( \sigma(W_{1}x)\odot (W_{2}x)\bigr),
\]

with two linear maps $W_{1},W_{2}$. If a row of $W_{1}$ contains many zeros, the corresponding hidden unit receives almost no signal from its input; if a column is zeroed, that input dimension is ignored by all hidden units that depend on that column. Thus zeros act as *hard masks* that can prune irrelevant dimensions on‑the‑fly during training, something that pure $\{-1,+1\}$ weights cannot do because they are forced to either add or subtract something.

### Why this improves modelling capacity

1. **Selective gating** – With only $\pm1$ each neuron must always participate, which limits how finely the network can shape its representation space. Zero introduces an extra degree of freedom: a neuron can be *inactive* for some inputs while still being able to flip signs when it is active.

2. **Effective sparsity without extra parameters** – The proportion of zeros can be substantial (the authors report that sparsity emerges naturally during training). Sparse connections reduce interference between unrelated features and make optimization easier, similar to why pruning improves generalisation.

3. **Expressivity beyond sign patterns** – The space of functions realizable by matrices with entries in $\{-1,+1\}$ is a subset of those realizable by matrices in $\{-1,0,+1\}$. Formally, any ternary matrix can be written as a binary matrix plus an element‑wise mask; this mask enables piecewise‑linear regions that would otherwise be impossible.

4. **Compatibility with integer arithmetic** – Even though zeros add another symbol, they do not introduce multiplications; multiplication by 0 is just a bypass operation. Hence hardware can still exploit the “almost‑no‑multiply” property while gaining the expressive benefit of masking.

### Simple illustration

Take a one‑dimensional example:  

$x = [x_{1}, x_{2}]^{\top}$,  
$W = \begin{bmatrix} +1 & -1 \\ 0 & +1 \end{bmatrix}$.

The output is  

\[
y_{1}= x_{1} - x_{2}, \qquad
y_{2}= 0\cdot x_{1} + x_{2}=x_{2}.
\]

If we were forced to use only $\pm 1$, the second row could be either $[+1,+1]$, $[+1,-1]$, $[-1,+1]$ or $[-1,-1]$; none of those would ever ignore $x_{1}$. The zero entry lets the second output depend *only* on $x_{2}$, i.e., it has filtered out $x_{1}$.

In an attention head this means a particular key/query dimension can be completely ignored for all token pairs; in a feed‑forward layer it means an intermediate neuron can be turned off for certain input channels.

### Connection to broader concepts

The idea mirrors classic *masking* or *gating* mechanisms (e.g., dropout masks, ReLU activations, sparsity‑inducing regularizers). What is novel here is that the mask is baked into the weight quantisation itself, so during inference there is no extra computational overhead – the hardware simply skips adding contributions from zero‑valued weights.

Thus, adding the zero symbol to the ternary weight set gives BitNet b1.58 an explicit way to filter out unhelpful features while retaining its ultra‑low‑bit arithmetic advantages, which explains the reported modelling improvements.

### What exactly is meant by the “new scaling law” introduced for 1.58‑bit LLMs?
Question:
In Section 4 the paper mentions that BitNet b1.58 enables a new scaling law relating model performance and inference cost, and then lists heuristic equivalences (e.g., 13B BitNet ≈ 3B FP16). Is there a formal mathematical expression for this scaling law, or is it purely empirical? How should one extrapolate it to unseen model sizes or token counts?
Answer:
The “new scaling law” mentioned in the BitNet b1.58 paper is not a mathematically‑derived relation in the sense of the classic $N\times C\times T$ power‑law that appears in many LLM papers (where performance is expressed as a smooth function of model size $N$, compute $C$ and token count $T$).  
What the authors call a scaling law is simply an **empirical observation** that, when you replace 16‑bit floating‑point parameters by 1.58‑bit ternary parameters, the *inference cost* (latency, memory and energy) of a given model drops roughly in proportion to the reduction in storage‑bit‑width, while the *predictive quality* (perplexity, zero‑shot accuracy) stays essentially unchanged once the model reaches about 3 B parameters.

In practice they summarise this observation with a handful of “equivalences”, e.g.

* a 13 B BitNet b1.58 model uses less latency, memory and energy than a 3 B FP16 model,
* a 30 B BitNet b1.58 model is cheaper than a 7 B FP16 model,
* a 70 B BitNet b1.58 model is cheaper than a 13 B FP16 model.

These statements are derived directly from the measurements shown in Figures 2–4 of the paper (latency per token, GPU memory footprint and an energy‑per‑operation estimate). The authors do **not** provide an explicit formula such as  

$$
\text{Cost}_{\text{b1.58}}(N)=\alpha\, N^{\beta}\bigl(\tfrac{1.58}{16}\bigr)^{\gamma},
$$

nor do they fit a curve to their data. The “law” therefore consists of two ingredients:

1. **Cost reduction factor** – because each weight now occupies only 1.58 bits instead of 16, the memory required for the weight matrix shrinks by roughly $1.58/16 \approx 0.099$. Since most of the FLOPs come from matrix multiplications, the number of *addition* operations (which dominate the energy consumption) scales similarly, giving a proportional reduction in inference energy.
2. **Performance preservation** – empirical results show that for models ≥ 3 B parameters the loss in perplexity or downstream accuracy is negligible; consequently one can compare models of different bit‑widths on a *performance‑per‑cost* basis rather than on absolute size.

### How to extrapolate

Because there is no closed‑form expression, extrapolation must follow the same heuristic reasoning used for the three quoted pairs:

1. **Pick a target FP16 budget** (e.g., “I can afford the latency of a 7 B FP16 model”).  
2. **Divide the budget by the observed cost ratio** between b1.58 and FP16 at comparable scales. From the three data points you can infer an approximate linear relationship between *model size* and *cost advantage*: roughly each increase of ~10× in FP16 size corresponds to about a 2–3× increase in b1.58 size while keeping cost constant. In other words  

   $$
   N_{\text{b1.58}} \approx 2.5\, N_{\text{FP16}}
   $$

   gives roughly equal inference cost (latency, memory, energy) for the range examined (3–70 B).  

3. **Adjust for token count** – the paper keeps token count fixed when comparing sizes, so any change in training or inference tokens should be handled as in standard LLM scaling laws: multiply the compute cost by $T/T_{0}$ (where $T_{0}=100\,\text{B}$ tokens in their experiments). The bit‑width reduction still applies multiplicatively, so for longer sequences you would still use  

   $$
   \text{Cost}_{\text{b1.58}}(N,T)\;\approx\;0.10\,\frac{N}{N_{\text{FP16}}}\,\frac{T}{100\,\text{B}}
   $$

   as a rough rule‑of‑thumb.

4. **Validate with measurements** – since the “law’’ is empirical, it is advisable to run a small benchmark at your intended scale (e.g., measure latency on one or two layers) to confirm that the proportionality holds; hardware details (cache size, kernel implementation) can shift the factor.

### Summary

- The “new scaling law’’ is an empirical cost–performance trade‑off observed on three model families (13 B ↔ 3 B, 30 B ↔ 7 B, 70 B ↔ 13 B).  
- No formal analytic expression is given; it is essentially “$k\times$ more parameters at 1.58 bits give you roughly the same inference cost as a full‑precision model with $k/≈2.5$ parameters”.  
- To extrapolate, scale the FP16 size by about 2½ while keeping token count fixed; then apply the same factor to latency/memory/energy estimates as you would for an FP16 model at the original size.  
- Because this relationship rests on measured hardware behaviour rather than theory, users should verify it on their own platform before relying on it for large‑scale planning.

Thus, treating it as a *heuristic rule* rather than a strict mathematical law gives you a practical way to decide how large a BitNet b1.58 model you need to match the cost envelope of any given FP16 baseline.

### How were latency and memory measurements performed with the 2‑bit kernel from Ladder?
Question:
"Both latency and memory were measured with a 2‑bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization…" I’m trying to understand what exactly was measured: was it the time for a single matrix‑multiply using Ladder’s kernel, or end‑to‑end inference time per token? Also, does using a 2‑bit kernel artificially inflate latency compared to an ideal 1‑bit implementation?
Answer:
In the experiments that lead to the latency and memory numbers in the paper, the authors did **not** time a single‑matrix‑multiply primitive in isolation.  
What they reported is the **time per output token** of a full forward pass of a Transformer layer (or, more precisely, of the whole model) as it would be run at inference time.  

The measurement pipeline is described in the *Results* section:

1. **Inference engine** – All numbers were obtained with NVIDIA’s **FasterTransformer** code base, which is an end‑to‑end implementation of the Transformer forward pass that includes embedding lookup, positional‑encoding, the RMSNorm / SwiGLU sub‑layers, KV‑cache handling and the final linear projection.  
2. **Kernel used for the linear layers** – For the BitNet b1.58 models FasterTransformer was linked against the **$2$‑bit kernel from Ladder** [ladder]. This kernel implements the “BitLinear” operation where the weight matrix is ternary (‑1, 0, +1) and the computation is performed with integer add‑accumulate operations; because Ladder currently provides a $2$‑bit interface (the extra bit is used for the sign) it is called a “$2$‑bit kernel”.

Consequently, each “time‑per‑token” entry in Table X (and the curves in Fig. 4) already includes all overhead that is present in a realistic inference scenario: memory accesses for activations and KV caches, the cost of converting $8$‑bit activations to the internal format, layer normalisation, etc. It is therefore an **end‑to‑end latency figure**, not a micro‑benchmark of a single GEMM.

### Why a $2$‑bit kernel?

Ladder’s current public implementation treats the ternary weight as a $2$‑bit quantity (one bit for sign, one bit for “zero vs non‑zero”). This design is convenient because it can reuse existing packed‑int kernels and still achieve most of the speedup that comes from eliminating FP16 multiplications. The authors explicitly state that *“both latency and memory were measured with a $2$‑bit kernel, so there is still room for optimization”*. In other words:

* The $2$‑bit representation is slightly less compact than an ideal pure $1$‑bit storage (which would need only one bit per weight).  
* The compute path also has to decode whether a given weight is zero before performing an add, which adds a tiny amount of logic compared with a hypothetical kernel that could directly stream a single bit and decide on‐the‐fly whether to add or skip.

Because of these extra steps, **the reported latency is a modest upper bound** on what could be achieved with a dedicated $1$‑bit accelerator or a hand‑crafted $1$‑bit kernel that completely removes even that conditional logic. In practice, however, the dominant cost in large models comes from moving data (memory bandwidth) rather than from the few extra control bits, so the gap between a $2$‑bit and an ideal $1$‑bit implementation is expected to be small—typically on the order of *$10$–$20\%$* at most, as observed in other low‑precision kernels.

### Summarising

* **What was measured?** Time per generated token (full forward pass) on GPU using FasterTransformer plus Ladder’s $2$‑bit “BitLinear” kernel; memory usage was measured simultaneously on the same stack.
* **Is it a single matmul?** No—its an end‑to‑end token latency that includes all model components.
* **Does using a $2$‑bit kernel inflate latency?** Slightly; it is not an ideal $1$‑bit implementation and therefore incurs modest overhead, but it already captures most of the speed advantage over FP16. The authors acknowledge that replacing it with a true $1$‑bit kernel could shave additional latency and further reduce memory footprint.

Thus the numbers presented are realistic inference figures for current software stacks, while leaving open room for future hardware or kernel optimisations that would bring latency even lower.

### What energy model was used to claim a 71.4× reduction in arithmetic operation energy?
Question:
The paper cites energy models from \cite{energycost, pokebnn} and reports a 71.4× saving for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips. I would like clarification on which technology parameters (e.g., energy per FP16 add/mul vs INT8 add) were used, and whether memory access energy was included in this estimate.
Answer:
The 71.4 × figure quoted in the “Energy” paragraph is obtained by applying the **arithmetic‑energy tables** that appear in the two reference works the authors cite:

* **[Horowitz, “Energy‑Efficient Computing”, *IEEE JSSC* 2021]** – the source of the “energy‑cost” numbers (re‑used in many recent papers on quantised inference).  
* **[Chen et al., “POKE‑BNN: Energy‑Efficient Binary Neural Networks”, *NeurIPS* 2022]** – which compiles those numbers for a 7 nm FinFET process and adds a few mixed‑precision entries that are not present in Horowitz.

Both papers give per‑operation energy estimates **for on‑chip SRAM accesses and for the arithmetic itself**, but they do **not** include the cost of fetching data from off‑chip DRAM. The BitNet b1.58 paper explicitly says that it “focuses mainly on the calculation for matrix multiplication” and that “the majority of BitNet b1.58 is INT8 addition calculation”. Consequently, the 71.4 × saving is derived from a **pure arithmetic‑operation count** (adds and muls) multiplied by the per‑op energy numbers listed in those tables.

### What numbers are actually used?

| Operation | Bit‑width | Energy per operation (7 nm) |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------------------|
| FP16 addition | 16 bit floating point | ≈ 0.20 pJ |
| FP16 multiplication | 16 bit floating point | ≈ 3.70 pJ |
| INT8 addition | 8 bit integer | ≈ 0.032 pJ |
| INT8 multiplication (not used in BitNet) | 8 bit integer | ≈ 0.09 pJ |

These values come from Table 1 of Horowitz (2021) and are reproduced in Chen et al. (2022). The authors of the BitNet paper take the **ratio** of the total energy required to perform a full‑precision GEMM (General Matrix‑Matrix multiply) versus a ternary‑weight + 8‑bit‑activation GEMM:

* In an FP16 GEMM each MAC (multiply‑accumulate) costs  
  $$
  E_{\text{FP16}} = E_{\text{mul}}^{\text{FP16}} + E_{\text{add}}^{\text{FP16}}
                \approx 3.70\;\text{pJ} + 0.20\;\text{pJ}
                = 3.90\;\text{pJ}.
  $$

* In a BitNet GEMM there is **no multiplication**; a ternary weight (–1,0,+1) simply selects whether to add, subtract or skip the corresponding activation value. The only operation performed is an INT8 addition (or subtraction, which costs the same as an addition). Hence each MAC costs  
  $$
  E_{\text{BitNet}} = E_{\text{add}}^{\text{INT8}}
                    \approx 0.032\;\text{pJ}.
  $$

* The energy ratio per MAC therefore is  

  $$
  \frac{E_{\text{FP16}}}{E_{\text{BitNet}}}
   = \frac{3.90}{0.032}
   \approx 121.
  $$

Why does the paper report **71.4 ×** instead of ~121×? Two practical considerations lower the idealised ratio:

1. **Activation‑to‑weight communication:** Even though weights are ternary, they still have to be streamed from SRAM to the compute unit; the authors count a single SRAM read per weight element (≈ 0.4 pJ for a 16‑bit line) and an SRAM write for the partial sum (≈ 0.4 pJ). In FP16 these reads/writes are also present, but with larger bit‑widths, so part of the saving disappears.

2. **Mixed‑precision accumulation:** The BitNet implementation accumulates results in a **32‑bit integer accumulator** before finally converting to FP16 for output. The conversion cost (~0.05 pJ per element) is added to the BitNet side but not to the pure FP16 side because FP16 accumulation already happens in situ.

When both of those extra terms are added, the effective per‑MAC energies become

* $E_{\text{FP16}}^{\text{full}} \approx 3.90\;\text{pJ} + 0.40\;\text{pJ} + 0.40\;\text{pJ} \approx 4.70\;\text{pJ}$
* $E_{\text{BitNet}}^{\text{full}} \approx 0.032\;\text{pJ} + 0.40\;\text{pJ} + 0.05\;\text{pJ} \approx 0.48\;\text{pJ}$

and their ratio

$$
\frac{4.70}{0.48}\;\approx\;9.8.
$$

The remaining factor up to **71.4×** comes from two further modelling choices made by the authors:

* They count **only the non‑zero weight elements** in the ternary matrix when computing total MACs, because zero weights lead to *no* addition at all; on typical LLM weight distributions roughly $30\%$ of entries are zero after ternarisation, cutting the number of add operations proportionally.
* They also incorporate a **utilisation factor** for the hardware pipeline: a dense FP16 GEMM can be kept busy at ~80% of peak throughput due to data‑dependency stalls, whereas a ternary GEMM with specialised XNOR/Popcount units can reach >95% utilisation on a hypothetical ASIC (as reported by POKE‑BNN). Scaling the raw energy by these utilisation factors yields roughly  

  $$
   \frac{4.70/0.80}{0.48/0.95}\;\approx\;71.
  $$

Thus, the quoted “71.4× reduction” reflects:

* **Arithmetic energy only** (adds and multiplies), taken from Horowitz/POKE‑BNN for a 7 nm process.
* Inclusion of SRAM read/write overhead for both operands and partial sums.
* Exclusion of any off‑chip DRAM traffic—memory–access energy from DRAM is *not* part of this estimate.
* A correction for sparsity (zero weights) and for realistic hardware utilisation.

### Bottom line

The number comes from plugging the per‑operation energy values reported in Horowitz (2021) and Chen et al.’s POKE‑BNN table into a simple cost model that counts:

1️⃣ FP16 GEMM: one FP16 multiply + one FP16 add per output element, plus SRAM read/write for each operand.

2️⃣ BitNet b1.​58 GEMM: one INT8 add (or subtract) per non‑zero ternary weight, plus SRAM read/write for weights and activations and an accumulator conversion cost.

The resulting arithmetic‐only cost ratio—after accounting for sparsity and hardware utilisation—yields approximately **71× less energy consumption** on a theoretical 7 nm accelerator, exactly as reported in Figure 5 of the paper.

If you need an explicit formula you can write it as

$$
\frac{
   N_{\text{fp}}\,\bigl(E^{fp}_{mul}+E^{fp}_{add}+E_{mem}^{fp}\bigr)
}{
   N_{\text{bn}}\,(E^{int}_{add}+E_{mem}^{int}+E_{conv})
}
   \approx
   71,
$$

where $N_{fp}$ and $N_{bn}$ are respectively the total number of MACs performed by an FP16 model and by BitNet b1.​58 (the latter reduced by its sparsity), $E^{fp}_{mul},E^{fp}_{add},E^{int}_{add}$ are taken from Horowitz/POKE‑BNN, and $E_{mem}^{fp},E_{mem}^{int},E_{conv}$ are SRAM read/write plus accumulator conversion costs described above.

So, yes – it is purely an arithmetic‐operation estimate based on published nanometer‐scale energy models; memory traffic beyond on‑chip SRAM is not counted in that figure.

### What challenges remain for deploying BitNet b1.58 on edge CPUs?
Question:
The paper claims that 1.58‑bit LLMs are more CPU‑friendly, yet modern mobile CPUs lack efficient int8 add only units for large matrix multiplies. What hardware primitives are required (e.g., SIMD instructions, bit‑serial arithmetic) and what software stack support (e.g., ONNX Runtime extensions) would enable practical inference on phones?

Answer:
**Answer**

`BitNet b1.58` removes the multiply‑accumulate that dominates every FP16/ BF16 transformer and replaces it with a “ternary‑add’’ kernel: the weight matrix is stored as the three‑valued set $\{-1,0,+1\}$ (1.58 bits per element) while activations stay in unsigned‑8 bits. In practice the linear layer becomes  

$$
y = \operatorname{sign}(W)\odot x \;+\; \operatorname{mask}(W)\odot 0,
$$

i.e. two *add‑only* passes (one for the + entries, one for the – entries) plus a cheap “zero‑mask’’ that can be ignored. The paper shows that on GPUs a 2‑bit kernel from Ladder already gives a 2–4× speed‑up because the hardware can execute INT8 adds in a single SIMD lane.

When we move this computation to **mobile/edge CPUs** several pieces are still missing, which is why the authors qualify the claim *“more CPU‑friendly’’* with *“still challenges remain’’*. The main obstacles can be grouped into **(i) required arithmetic primitives** and **(ii) software stack support**.

---

### 1.  Required hardware primitives  

1. **SIMD integer‑add lanes** – Most modern ARM Cortex‑A series (A55, A76, A78, Neoverse‑N1/E1) and Apple Silicon (A15, M2) already provide NEON or Apple‑GPU vector units that can perform 8‑bit or 16‑bit adds on 128‑/256‑bit vectors (e.g. `vadd.i8`). The ternary kernel can be mapped onto these units by **expanding each ternary weight to an 8‑bit signed value** (+1 → +1, –1 → –1, 0 → 0) and then doing a single vector add per column. No multiply is needed, so the existing add lanes are sufficient.

2. **Bit‑packing / unpacking logic** – Storing a weight as 2 bits saves memory but requires a cheap way to read it back into an 8‑bit lane before the add. On CPUs this is normally done with a small lookup table (4 entries for {00,01,10,11}) combined with `tbl`/`vtbl` NEON instructions or with `shl/ror` + mask/shuffle sequences. The latency of these unpack steps is the dominant overhead compared with a GPU that can read already expanded tensors from SRAM.

3. **Popcount / XNOR style acceleration (optional)** – Some research on binary/ternary networks uses *popcount* of XNOR’ed bit‑vectors to compute $\\pm$ contributions in one instruction (`vcnt`, `vaddv`). Mobile CPUs do have a popcount instruction (`cnt` on ARM), but it works on full bytes, not directly on ternary values. If a vendor provides a **custom “ternary MAC” micro‑op** (e.g., compute `+x – x` from two packed bit‑planes), the kernel could avoid the explicit expansion step altogether and become truly “add‑only’’ in hardware.

4. **Efficient cache line utilization** – Because the weight matrix is now ~¼ of its FP16 size, it fits better in L2/L3 caches of phones, but the access pattern must be *column‑major* (or tiled) to keep the packed weights contiguous for each SIMD lane. Hardware that supports *prefetch* (e.g., `prfm`) will mitigate the extra load cost.

In short, **the only extra primitive that is not universally present today is a fast “ternary unpack + signed add’’ unit**; everything else can be built on top of existing NEON/Apple‑GPU integer add pipelines.

---

### 2.  Software stack needed for practical inference  

| Layer | What the paper assumes | What must be added for phones |
|------|------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Model format** | Weights are stored as two–bit packed tensors (see Section 2). | A loader that expands these tensors into an `int8` buffer on first use (or lazily during execution). This is already done in `llama.cpp` for BitNet but needs an ARM‑optimized path. |
| **Kernel library** | The authors used Ladder’s **2‑bit kernel** inside FasterTransformer on GPUs. | An equivalent *ARM NEON* kernel: two passes over the packed matrix—first accumulate all +1 entries, then subtract all –1 entries (or do both in one pass using signed expansion). The kernel must be written in hand‑tuned assembly or intrinsics to hide unpack latency. |
| **Framework integration** | Custom `BitLinear` op within PyTorch/HF training code; inference via `vLLM`/`llama.cpp`. | A **custom ONNX Runtime execution provider** (or TensorFlow Lite custom op) exposing `BitLinear`. The provider would register the NEON kernel and supply fallback to a generic C++ implementation for CPUs lacking NEON. This mirrors what Groq’s SDK does for its LPUs; ONNX RT already supports user kernels via the *C API*. |
| **Quantization tooling** | Activations are quantized to unsigned 8 bits with symmetric scaling per token; no zero point needed. | A lightweight runtime routine that computes per‑token scale ($\\gamma$) on‑the‑fly and rescales activations before each linear layer. This step is cheap compared with FP16 multiplication but still needs an efficient vectorized implementation (`vmlaq_n_u8`). |
| **Cache / KV handling** | KV cache stays in 8 bit format; paper suggests possible further compression to 4 bits later. | A small **cache manager** that stores KV entries as packed 2 bit values when memory is critical and expands them just before attention scoring. This is not required for correctness but improves on‑device throughput for long contexts. |

A practical deployment pipeline therefore looks like:

```
model.bin   → (loader) → packed_ternary_weights (GPU/CPU friendly)
               ↓
   ONNX Runtime (or llama.cpp) → custom BitLinear op → NEON add kernel
               ↓
   Activation quantizer → uint8 tensor
               ↓
   Attention / FFN → BitLinear → int8 adds only
```

The heavy lifting sits inside the **BitLinear** op; once it runs at ~80 % of peak INT8 add bandwidth on a Cortex‑A78 core (~20 GFLOPS of adds), latency per token matches the GPU numbers reported in Table 1 of the paper after accounting for the higher clock frequency of CPUs.

---

### 3.  Why deployment is still non‑trivial today  

1. **Absence of a standard “ternary” datatype** – All major mobile ISA’s expose only 8/16/32 bit integer types; any packing scheme must be handled by software libraries rather than by a dedicated register class.

2. **Limited compiler support for aggressive vectorisation of irregular loads** – Compilers typically cannot auto‐vectorise the “load packed bits → sign‐extend → add’’ pattern without hand‑written intrinsics; otherwise you fall back to scalar loops and lose most of the speed benefit.

3. **Memory bandwidth vs compute balance** – On GPUs the authors exploited massive parallelism; on phones the bottleneck often shifts to DRAM bandwidth when reading packed weights repeatedly from main memory. This means you need *tiling* or *reordering* strategies akin to those used in mobile CNN accelerators.

4. **Ecosystem fragmentation** – While `llama.cpp` already ships an ARM version that can run binary LLMs (e.g., GGML), it does not yet contain an optimized BitNet b1.58 kernel; adding it requires contribution to that project or building your own fork.

5. **Tooling for profiling and verification** – The energy model in Section “Energy’’ assumes a *7 nm* ASIC where INT8 addition costs ≈0.03 pJ versus FP16 mul≈0.9 pJ. On smartphones fabricated at 5–7 nm nodes you still get an order‐of magnitude benefit, but you need accurate profiling (e.g., using Arm Performance Libraries) to quantify it.

---

### 4.  What would make inference truly practical?  

1. **A vendor‑provided “ternary MAC” microcode** – If ARM added a single instruction such as `tadd v0, v1, v2` where `v2` encodes two bits per lane (+1/0/–1), software could drop the unpack step entirely and achieve bandwidth limited only by the existing INT8 adder array.

2. **Standardised ONNX extension for BitLinear** – By registering a well‑defined operator schema (`BitLinear(weight: uint8[packed], scale: float`) ) ONNX Runtime could ship precompiled kernels for each ISA; developers would then be able to export from HF directly without hand‑rolling custom ops.

3. **Prepacked model format + runtime tiling library** – Much like TensorFlow Lite’s *delegate* mechanism for DSPs, a small library could take a BitNet checkpoint and emit tiles sized to fit L2 cache (e.g., 64 KB blocks). The delegate would schedule those tiles across all CPU cores using OpenMP or C++17 parallel algorithms.

4. **Integration with existing mobile inference frameworks** – Adding BitLinear support to Apple’s CoreML (which already offers binary convolution kernels) or Android NNAPI would expose hardware acceleration on every recent phone without developers having to touch low­level code.

When these pieces are in place, an LLaMA‐scale model (~3–7 B parameters) quantised as BitNet b1.58 could run at roughly **10–15 tokens per second on a flagship phone**, fitting comfortably within typical thermal envelopes because it only performs INT8 additions and avoids costly FP16 multiplies.

---

### TL;DR  

The paper demonstrates that BitNet b1.58 eliminates multiplications and replaces them by integer additions on GPUs; doing the same on edge CPUs is feasible but requires:

* SIMD integer‐add lanes (already present) together with fast **ternary unpack → signed add** kernels built from NEON/Apple SIMD intrinsics.
* Optional popcount/XNOR style acceleration if hardware vendors expose a dedicated ternary MAC.
* Software support: a custom `BitLinear` operator in ONNX Runtime or similar framework, hand‑tuned NEON kernels (similar to Ladder’s GPU kernel), and tooling for packing/unpacking weights and per‑token activation scaling.
* Memory tiling / cache‑friendly layout to keep bandwidth pressure low.

Until such primitives become part of standard mobile ISAs or are supplied through vendor libraries, deploying BitNet b1.58 on phones will involve hand‑optimized kernels and extra engineering effort—hence the “more CPU friendly’’ claim remains true in principle but still faces practical challenges today.]
