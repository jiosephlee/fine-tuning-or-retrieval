\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

# Motivation and Challenges of Extending LLM Context Windows  

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide range of natural‑language tasks, from code generation to open‑ended reasoning.  A fundamental limitation that persists in most state‑of‑the‑art models, however, is the size of the **context window**—the maximum number of token positions that the model can attend to in a single forward pass.  The context window determines how much information a model can keep “in mind” when generating the next token.  In practice, many popular LLMs such as LLaMA‑2 and Mistral are constrained to a context length of only **4 k** tokens.  When an input exceeds this limit, the model must truncate the excess or otherwise discard it, causing a rapid degradation in performance.  

In this chapter we examine **why longer context windows are desirable**, **where current models fall short**, and **what obstacles have prevented extensions beyond roughly 128 k tokens**.  Understanding these motivations and challenges provides the necessary backdrop for the methodological contributions introduced later in the paper (LongRoPE).  

---

## Why Context Length Matters for LLMs  

### In‑Context Learning with Many Demonstrations  

One of the most celebrated emergent abilities of LLMs is *in‑context learning*: by presenting a few labeled examples together with a test query, the model can adapt its behavior without any gradient updates.  The effectiveness of this paradigm grows with the number of demonstrations that can be packed into the prompt.  When an application requires hundreds or thousands of examples (for instance, fine‑grained classification across many classes or program synthesis from many prior snippets), a short 4 k window forces the practitioner to either down‑sample examples or sacrifice detail, dramatically reducing performance.  

### Autonomous Agents and Long‑Running Dialogue  

LLM‑based agents—systems that plan, act, and reason over extended horizons—must retain a memory of past observations, actions, and internal thoughts.  Papers on generative agents and self‑refining systems emphasize that *the longer the accessible history*, the more coherent and goal‑directed the behavior becomes.  With only a few thousand tokens of memory, agents quickly lose track of earlier decisions, leading to inconsistent or contradictory actions.  

### Retrieval‑Augmented and Document‑Level Tasks  

Many downstream tasks involve processing documents that far exceed a few thousand words: legal contracts, scientific articles, books, or multi‑turn conversations.  Retrieval‑augmented generation approaches often retrieve several passages and concatenate them with the query; still, the combined text can easily surpass the native window size.  When truncation is performed naïvely, crucial evidence may be omitted, degrading factual accuracy and reasoning depth.  

In all these scenarios—few‑shot learning with many examples, long‑running agents, or document‑level reasoning—a larger context window directly translates into higher utility and more faithful behavior.

---

## Current Limits of Popular LLMs  

The architectural design of modern Transformers separates **positional information** from token embeddings using either learned position embeddings or functional schemes such as Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE).  For models released up to early 2024—including LLaMA‑2 (4 k tokens) and Mistral (also 4 k tokens)—the positional component is trained only within this modest range.  

When an input token appears at a position index **outside** the trained range, its positional encoding is effectively *unseen* during pre‑training.  Empirically this leads to a steep rise in **perplexity** and a collapse in downstream task performance once the sequence exceeds roughly four thousand tokens.  The degradation is not merely an artifact of insufficient training data; it is intrinsic to how attention scores are modulated by positional cosines/sines that have never been calibrated for those far‑away indices.

---

## Existing Extensions up to ≈128 k Tokens  

Researchers have proposed several strategies to push context windows beyond the native limit:

1. **Fine‑tuning on Longer Texts** – Methods such as LongLoRA, PI (Positional Interpolation), YaRN, and others train on sequences up to tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens.  
2. **Positional Interpolation** – By scaling down rotation frequencies in RoPE (linear interpolation) or applying more sophisticated non‑linear schemes (NTK‑based interpolation, YaRN grouping), new positions are mapped onto the “trained” interval.

These approaches have demonstrated extensions up to **≈128 k tokens**, which already represent a thirtyfold increase over the native window for many models.  Nonetheless they exhibit tangible shortcomings:

- **Performance Saturation** – Perplexity begins to climb sharply beyond roughly one hundred thousand tokens; on downstream tasks such as passkey retrieval accuracy collapses near zero.
- **Heavy Fine‑Tuning Costs** – Achieving even a 128 k window typically requires thousands of training steps on massive GPU clusters because long documents are scarce and expensive to process.
- **Architectural Modifications** – Some methods alter attention masks or introduce external memory modules, complicating deployment and breaking compatibility with existing optimizations (e.g., FlashAttention).

Thus while promising, these techniques fall short of providing truly *ultra‑long* context windows needed for million‑token applications.

---

## Three Core Obstacles to Ultra‑Long Context  

The paper identifies three intertwined challenges that together impede extensions beyond ~128 k tokens.

### 1. Catastrophic New Position Values  

When extending from a few thousand to millions of positions, over **90 %** of positional indices become *untrained*.  Naïvely assigning RoPE angles at those indices introduces “catastrophic values”: cosine/sine pairs that have never been observed during pre‑training produce attention scores far outside their learned distribution.  This out‑of‑distribution shift makes gradient descent unstable and prevents convergence even when fine‑tuning on longer texts.

### 2. Data Scarcity and Compute Explosion  

Training on sequences longer than a million tokens requires datasets containing documents of comparable length—a rarity in publicly available corpora.  Even when such data exist (e.g., long books), processing them entails enormous memory footprints and compute time: each training step must handle vastly more key/value pairs in attention layers, scaling quadratically with sequence length.  Consequently fine‑tuning for ultra‑long windows becomes prohibitively expensive for most research groups.

### 3. Attention Dispersion Across Vast Token Ranges  

Attention scores are distributed over all pairwise token positions.  As the window expands dramatically, each individual token’s contribution is diluted across millions of positions—*attention dispersion*.  This dilution harms performance on the original short window because high‐frequency (fine) positional information becomes compressed into an increasingly narrow region of embedding space.  Empirically this manifests as perplexity spikes even for inputs that remain within the original four‐kilotoken range after extending to millions of positions.

These three obstacles—catastrophic new positions, lack of sufficiently long training data coupled with massive compute requirements, and attention dispersion—constitute the primary reasons why prior work has plateaued at around one hundred thousand tokens.

---

## Setting the Stage for LongRoPE  

Recognizing these barriers motivates a new line of inquiry: can we devise *initialization* schemes for RoPE that preserve as much information as possible from the pretrained model while respecting both dimensionwise frequency differences and tokenwise importance?  If such an initialization reduces catastrophic values sufficiently, then only modest fine‑tuning (on readily available shorter long sequences) might suffice to bridge the gap to multi‑million token contexts.

Moreover, if we can exploit *non‑uniformities*—the fact that lower RoPE dimensions (high frequency) and early token positions carry disproportionate importance—we may avoid excessive interpolation where it matters most and apply stronger scaling where loss is tolerable.

The remainder of this textbook will detail how **LongRoPE** operationalizes these insights through an evolutionary search over per‑dimension scaling factors and an efficient progressive extension pipeline that ultimately yields LLMs capable of handling **2048 k** tokens while retaining performance on standard short‐context benchmarks.

# Foundations: Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) and Existing Interpolation Techniques  

Large language models (LLMs) rely on an explicit encoding of token order so that the self‑attention mechanism can distinguish “which token comes before which”.  In contemporary LLMs the dominant approach is **Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE)**, a sinusoidal scheme that integrates position information directly into the query‑key pairs of multi‑head attention.  This chapter derives the RoPE formulation, introduces the notation used when extending a model’s context window, and surveys the three main families of positional interpolation that have been proposed to reuse a pretrained model beyond its original length.  All equations are presented in LaTeX notation, and the exposition follows the definitions and empirical observations reported in the LongRoPE paper.

---  

## Derivation of RoPE Angles and Frequency Scaling  

Consider a Transformer model whose hidden dimension is \(d\).  For a token placed at absolute position \(n\) (counting from zero), RoPE augments each attention head with a pair of sinusoidal functions that rotate the query and key vectors in the complex plane.  The canonical RoPE vector for a single head can be written as  

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{r}(n) &=
\Bigl[\cos (n\theta_0), \,\sin (n\theta_0), \,
      \cos (n\theta_1), \,\sin (n\theta_1), \,
      \dots , \,
      \cos (n\theta_{d/2-1}), \,\sin (n\theta_{d/2-1}) \Bigr] ,
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

where the subscript \(i\) indexes the **RoPE dimension**.  Each dimension is associated with a **rotation frequency**  

\[
\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d},
\tag{2}
\]

and \(\theta\) is a base constant typically set to \(10\,000\).  The factor \(n\,\theta_i\) therefore grows linearly with the token index but decays exponentially across dimensions: low‑index dimensions correspond to high frequencies (rapid oscillation), while high‑index dimensions correspond to low frequencies (slow oscillation).  When RoPE is applied, the query vector \(\mathbf{q}_n\) at position \(n\) is multiplied element‑wise by \(\mathbf{r}(n)\), and similarly for keys, thereby inserting relative positional bias directly into dot‑product attention.

---  

## Extension Ratio \(s\) and Notation for Position Interpolation  

The **original context window** of a pretrained LLM contains at most \(L\) tokens.  To process longer inputs we desire an **extended length** \(L'\) where  

\[
s = \frac{L'}{L}
\tag{3}
\]

is called the **extension ratio**.  Directly feeding positions \(n > L-1\) into Eq. (1) would produce rotation angles that have never been seen during pretraining, which leads to catastrophic out‑of‑distribution behavior.  Consequently, existing works replace the raw angles by **scaled** versions so that every new position is effectively mapped back into the range covered during pretraining.

Let us write the scaling factor applied to dimension \(i\) as \(\lambda_i\).  It is convenient to introduce \(\beta = \theta^{2/d}\); then each original angle can be expressed as \(n/\beta^{i}\).  A generic **position interpolation** therefore produces

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{r}_{\,\text{interp}}(n)
&=
\Bigl[ 
   \cos \!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_0\, \beta^{0}}\Bigr), 
   \sin \!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_0\, \beta^{0}}\Bigr),
   \dots , 
   \cos \!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_{d/2-1}\, \beta^{d/2-1}}\Bigr),
   \sin \!\Bigl(\frac{n}{\lambda_{d/2-1}\, \beta^{d/2-1}}\Bigr)
   \Bigr] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

When all dimensions share a common factor (\(\lambda_i = s\) for every \(i\)), Eq. (4) reduces to **linear positional interpolation**, described next.

---  

## Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)  

The simplest strategy, introduced as **Positional Interpolation (PI)**, rescales *every* rotary angle by exactly the extension ratio:

\[
\lambda_i = s,\qquad
\forall i .
\tag{5}
\]

Intuitively, we compress the whole angular spectrum by a factor of \(s\) so that positions up to \(L' = sL\) map into the same interval originally spanned by positions up to \(L.\)  While PI guarantees that no new angles are introduced, it also makes adjacent tokens appear *closer* in angular space: tokens that were already distinguishable at short range become crowded when many more positions share a limited angular bandwidth.  Empirically this “crowding” harms performance once the extension exceeds roughly \(2\times – 4\times,\) because the model loses resolution for discriminating nearby tokens.

---  

## Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)‑Based Interpolation and Extrapolation  

A more nuanced view treats RoPE as an **approximation of a kernel function**.  By invoking Neural Tangent Kernel theory, one can reason about how different frequencies contribute to information capacity.  The NTK‑inspired approach redistributes interpolation pressure across dimensions: low‑frequency (high‑index) dimensions are *scaled more*, while high‑frequency (low‑index) dimensions are *scaled less*.  Formally,

| Dimension index | Scaling rule |
|----------------|---------------|
| low \(i\) (high frequency) | \( \lambda_i = s^{\,i}\) |
| high \(i$ (low frequency) | \( \lambda_i = s^{\,i}\) |

In other words, each dimension receives an exponentiated scaling factor that grows with its index.  This yields partial **extrapolation** for low‑frequency components (they receive a larger stretch) and partial **interpolation** for high‑frequency components (they receive a smaller stretch).   The result is a smoother mapping that mitigates crowding while still ensuring that all new positions lie within a learnable range.

Dynamic NTK variants further adapt the per‑position scaling based on the *current* sequence length, but they still respect the monotone relationship between dimension index and scaling factor.

---  

## YaRN: Frequency‑Based Grouping  

The **YaRN** method builds upon NTK intuition by explicitly partitioning RoPE dimensions into three groups:

1. **High‑frequency group** – dimensions with very fast oscillations; these receive *no* interpolation (\(\lambda=1,\) i.e., pure extrapolation).
2. **Medium‑frequency group** – dimensions receive an NTK‑style intermediate scaling.
3. **Low‑frequency group** – dimensions are handled by plain linear interpolation (\(\lambda=s.\))

Thus YaRN can be summarized as

```
if i ∈ high‑freq   → λ_i = 1
elif i ∈ medium    → λ_i = s^{α_i}    (α_i ∈ (0,1))
else               → λ_i = s
```

The grouping thresholds are chosen empirically from observed performance on a handful of models; they are not derived from a principled analytic criterion.  In practice YaRN yields sizable gains over pure PI or NTK when extending contexts up to roughly \(8\times – 16\times.\)

---  

## Limitations of Uniform or Manually Grouped Approaches  

Despite their success, both PI and NTK treat every position identically after scaling; they ignore two important sources of **non‑uniformity** identified in LongRoPE:

| Source of non‑uniformity | Observation |
|--------------------------|-------------|
| **RoPE dimension–wise variance** | Certain dimensions (especially those encoding lower frequencies) tolerate stronger scaling without losing discriminative power, while high‑frequency dimensions are more sensitive. |
| **Token‑position variance** | The *first* few tokens in any sequence dominate attention scores; preserving their original angles may be beneficial because they serve as anchors for downstream layers. |

Uniform schemes such as PI apply exactly one factor across all dimensions and positions, thereby discarding any opportunity to treat “important” parts of the embedding differently.  YaRN’s three‑group heuristic partially addresses dimension variance but still relies on hand‑crafted boundaries; it does not adapt to new architectures or target extension ratios.  Moreover, none of these methods consider adjusting the mapping for early tokens—a strategy later shown to improve performance when extending beyond several hundred thousand positions.

In summary, existing interpolation techniques provide useful baselines but suffer from two fundamental constraints:

1. **Uniformity across dimensions or coarse manual grouping** limits their ability to retain critical positional information when extending far beyond the pretrained window.
2. **Absence of token‐position awareness** leaves early‐token representations overly distorted under large extensions.

These shortcomings motivate the search for *non‑uniform* rescaling factors that are simultaneously dimension‑aware and position‑aware—a core idea that underlies the LongRoPE method described in subsequent chapters.

**Empirical Analysis of Non‑Uniformities in Positional Interpolation**  

# Experimental Setup: Perplexity Evaluation on PG19 and Proof‑Pile Without Fine‑Tuning  

To understand how positional interpolation affects the ability of a large language model (LLM) to handle sequences that exceed its native context length, the authors carried out a series of controlled language‑modeling experiments.  The core metric was **perplexity**, the exponentiated average negative log‑likelihood, which directly reflects how well a model predicts the next token given a context.  Lower perplexity indicates that the positional encoding supplies useful information for the attention layers, while higher perplexity signals a degradation caused by mismatched or “catastrophic” position values.

Two representative long‑text corpora were selected:

1. **PG19** – a collection of books from Project Gutenberg, commonly used to benchmark very long‑context language modeling.  Each document in the validation split contains tens of thousands of tokens, well beyond the default 4 k context limit of LLaMA2 and Mistral.  

2. **Proof‑Pile** – a subset of the Pile that emphasizes scientific and technical writing, which tends to contain dense information and long continuous passages.

Both datasets were evaluated **without any additional fine‑tuning**.  The models examined were the pre‑trained LLaMA2‑7B and Mistral‑7B families, each equipped with the standard rotary positional embedding (RoPE).  For each target context length \(L'\) (e.g., 8 k, 16 k, 32 k), the authors applied a particular interpolation scheme to rescale the RoPE angles, then fed sliding windows of size \(L'\) over the documents and recorded perplexity.  Because no fine‑tuning was performed, any change in perplexity could be attributed solely to how well the interpolation preserved useful positional information.

The evaluation protocol thus isolated **the pure effect of positional interpolation** and provided a clean laboratory for discovering systematic non‑uniformities that could be exploited later.  

# Finding 1: Dimension‑wise Non‑Uniformity – Optimal \(\lambda\) Varies Per RoPE Dimension  

RoPE encodes each token position \(n\) as a set of sinusoidal components with frequencies \(\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}\), where \(i\) indexes the dimension (half the embedding size) and \(d\) is the total hidden dimension.  In a naïve linear interpolation (PI), every dimension receives the same scaling factor \(\lambda = s\), where \(s = L'/L\) is the extension ratio.  However, this uniform treatment ignores that **different dimensions carry different amounts of information**: low‑frequency dimensions (large \(i\)) vary slowly with position, while high‑frequency dimensions (small \(i\)) vary rapidly and are crucial for distinguishing nearby tokens.

The authors used an **evolutionary search** to independently optimize a scaling factor \(\lambda_i\) for each RoPE dimension under a fixed extension ratio (e.g., extending to 8 k).  The search objective was minimizing perplexity on five randomly sampled PG19 validation documents.  The resulting best‐found \(\{\lambda_i\}\) displayed a clear monotonic pattern:  

\[
\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1},
\]

with **small values for low‑frequency dimensions** (often close to \(1.0\), i.e., almost no interpolation) and **larger values for high‑frequency dimensions** (approaching or slightly exceeding the naïve factor \(s\)).  

When these dimension‑wise rescaling factors were applied, perplexity dropped dramatically compared with PI, NTK‐based approaches, and even YaRN, which only uses three manually crafted groups.  For example, on PG19 at an 8 k context window, the searched non‑uniform configuration reduced perplexity by several points relative to PI and by an even larger margin relative to NTK.  Similar gains were observed on Proof‑Pile.

These results confirm that **the optimal interpolation is not uniform across dimensions**; higher‐frequency components must be interpolated more gently to avoid “crowding” nearby positions, while lower‑frequency components can tolerate stronger scaling without losing discriminative power.

# Finding 2: Token‑Position Non‑Uniformity – Early Tokens Benefit From Less Interpolation  

Beyond dimensional differences, the authors hypothesized that **the earliest tokens in a sequence deserve special treatment** because they typically receive larger attention scores throughout all later layers.  In many transformer variants (including streaming LLMs), early positions act as anchors for global context; if their positional encoding is overly distorted by interpolation, attention matrices may become unstable.

To test this hypothesis, they introduced a **position threshold \(\hat{n}\)**: for token indices \(n < \hat{n}\), no scaling is applied (\(\lambda_i = 1\) for all dimensions), whereas for \(n \ge \hat{n}\) the previously discovered dimension-wise factors are used.  They experimented with several values of \(\hat{n}\) ranging from \(0\) (no exemption) up to \(256\).

The empirical findings were striking:

* When \(\hat{n}=0\), performance coincided with standard PI/NTK baselines.
* As soon as a modest number of initial tokens (e.g., \(\hat{n}=16\) or \(32\)) were left untouched, perplexity improved consistently across both PG19 and Proof‑Pile.
* The *optimal* value of \(\hat{n}\) depended on the target extension length: longer extensions benefited from larger \(\hat{n}\).  For an 8 k window, \(\hat{n}=32\) yielded the best result; for a 16 k window, benefits continued up to \(\hat{n}=128\).

These observations validate that **early token positions should experience less interpolation**, preserving their original rotary angles and thereby retaining their strong influence in attention calculations.

# Finding 3: Non‑Uniform Interpolation Improves Both Fine‑Tuned and Non‑Fine‑Tuned Scenarios  

Having identified two complementary sources of non‑uniformity—per-dimension scaling and early-token exemption—the authors examined whether incorporating both would help not only in pure inference (no fine‑tuning) but also when limited fine‑tuning is available.

They first fine‑tuned LLaMA2‑7B on a modest dataset using the *searched* RoPE rescaling designed for a **64 k context window**.  After only a few hundred gradient steps (far fewer than typical long‑context fine‐tuning pipelines), they evaluated perplexity at several lengths without further training.  

The results demonstrated that:

* The model initialized with non‑uniform RoPE already outperformed PI and YaRN *before* any fine‐tuning.
* After fine‐tuning, the gap widened: at an evaluation length of 64 k, perplexity dropped by more than 10 points relative to PI-fine‐tuned models and remained lower than YaRN-fine‐tuned models across all tested lengths.
* Even when extending beyond the fine‐tuned length (e.g., evaluating at 128 k), the model with non‑uniform initialization degraded far less than its uniformly interpolated counterparts.

Thus, **non‑uniform positional interpolation serves as an excellent initialization**, reducing catastrophic drift introduced by many new positions and making subsequent fine­-tuning far more efficient.

# Summary of Insights That Motivate a Search‑Based Solution  

The systematic empirical study revealed three intertwined insights:

1. **RoPE dimensions are intrinsically non-uniform**: high‐frequency components require gentle scaling while low frequencies can be stretched more aggressively.  
2. **Token positions are also non-uniform**: early tokens should retain their original rotary angles because they dominate attention distributions; later tokens may be interpolated more heavily.  
3. **Exploiting both forms simultaneously yields tangible benefits** across both zero–fine‑tuning inference and limited fine‑tuning regimes, effectively mitigating catastrophic value introduction when extending context windows far beyond their original limits.

These findings jointly imply that any practical method for extending an LLM’s context window must treat *both* dimensions and token positions as separate axes in an expansive search space.  Because closed-form formulas (e.g., linear PI or static NTK exponents) cannot capture this nuanced landscape, the authors formulated an **evolutionary search algorithm** that directly optimizes per-dimension scaling factors together with an early-token threshold under a perplexity objective.  

The resulting algorithm—central to LongRoPE—systematically explores candidate configurations while enforcing monotonicity constraints inspired by NTK theory (ensuring \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_{i+1}\)) and leveraging known baselines as seeds in its initial population.  By doing so, it discovers high–quality non-uniform interpolation schemes that become reliable initializations for progressive fine-tuning strategies capable of reaching unprecedented context lengths such as two million tokens.

In sum, this chapter has demonstrated through rigorous perplexity experiments on long corpora that **positional interpolation is fundamentally non-uniform**, both across RoPE dimensions and across token positions, and that harnessing these non-uniformities is essential for scalable context window extension.

# Problem Formulation as a Search over Rescale Factors

In order to extend the context window of a pre‑trained large language model (LLM) far beyond its original limit, we must redesign the way the model’s rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) are applied to tokens that appear at positions never seen during pre‑training.  The central insight of LongRoPE is that this redesign can be expressed as a **search problem** over per‑dimension rescale factors \(\lambda_i\) together with a small prefix length \(\hat{n}\) that is left untouched by any interpolation.  In this chapter we formalize this problem, explain the notation that governs the piecewise scaling of RoPE, and argue why a direct gradient‑based solution is impractical, thereby motivating an evolution‑based search.

## 1. Formal Optimization Objective

Consider a Transformer‑based LLM that has been pre‑trained with a RoPE positional embedding of dimension \(d\).  For a token placed at absolute position \(n\) (counting from zero), the original RoPE encoding for dimension index \(i\in\{0,\dots ,\frac{d}{2}-1\}\) is

\[
\mathbf{r}_{i}(n) = 
\bigl[\cos(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}), \; \sin(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}})\bigr],
\qquad
\beta = \theta^{2/d},
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\theta\) is the base frequency (default \(10\,000\)).  When we wish to evaluate sequences whose length exceeds the original maximum \(L\), we must modify how the angles \(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}\) are computed.  LongRoPE introduces a **piecewise scaling function** \(\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\) that applies different factors to early token positions and to later ones:

\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n}) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } n < \hat{n},\\[4pt]
\displaystyle \frac{1}{\lambda_i} & \text{if } n \ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\tag{2}
\]

where:
- \(\lambda_i > 0\) is the **rescale factor** for RoPE dimension \(i\).  
- \(\hat{n}\in\mathbb{N}\) denotes the size of an initial prefix (e.g., the first few hundred tokens) for which we deliberately keep the original RoPE unchanged (the “no‑interpolation” region).  

Under this scheme, the modified RoPE for token position \(n\) becomes

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{i}(n)=
\Bigl[\cos\bigl( \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\, \tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}} \bigr),
      \sin\bigl( \mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})\, \tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}} \bigr)\Bigr].
\tag{3}
\]

The complete set of rescaling decisions for an LLM targeting an extended context size \(L'\) can be collected into a vector 
\[
\Lambda = (\lambda_0,\, \lambda_1,\, \dots ,\, \lambda_{\tfrac d2-1}), 
\qquad
\widehat{\Lambda}= (\Lambda,\; \hat n).
\]

Given a dataset of very long documents denoted by
\[
\mathbf X = \bigl( \mathbf{x}^{(1)},\, \mathbf{x}^{(2)},\, \dots\bigr), 
~~
|\mathbf{x}^{(j)}| \ge L',
\tag{4}
\]
our ultimate goal is to find the tuple \(\widehat{\Lambda}^{*}\) that **minimizes the language modelling loss** (equivalently perplexity) when the model processes these long inputs with the modified RoPE:

\[
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%
%\boxed{
%}
\\[4pt]
%
% 
%  
%  
%  
%    
%\underset{
%  {\mathbf{x}\in \mathbf X;\;|\mathbf{x}|\ge L'}
%}{
%   {\operatorname*{\arg\,min}}_{\;\widehat{\Lambda}}
% }
%   
%    ~
%    %
%    %
%    %
%    %
%    %
%   
%   {\cal L}\!\Bigl(
%      {\rm LLM}\bigl({\rm RoPE}_{\,\,\widehat{\Lambda}} ,\, 
%      \mathbf {X}\bigr)
%   \Bigr)
%\tag{5}
\\[6pt]
where:
- ${\cal L}$ denotes the standard next‑token cross‑entropy loss,
- ${\rm LLM}({{\rm RoPE}}_{\widehat{\Lambda}},\,\cdot)$ denotes running the frozen transformer weights with the *only* modification being the rescaled RoPE defined above.

Equation (5) encapsulates the full **search problem**: choose a per‑dimension scaling vector together with an early‑token prefix length so that, after re‑encoding all positions according to (3), the model’s predictive loss on extremely long texts is as low as possible.

---

## 2. Piecewise Scaling Function \(𝕀(·)\): Why It Matters

The definition of \(𝕀(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)\) embodies two distinct non‑uniformities discovered empirically in LongRoPE’s analysis:

1. **Dimension‑wise non‑uniformity** – Higher‑frequency RoPE dimensions (small \(i\)) encode fine‑grained relative positions and therefore tolerate little interpolation; consequently their optimal scaling factors tend toward values close to \(1.0.\) Conversely, low‑frequency dimensions (large \(i\)) can be stretched more aggressively without destroying useful information.

2. **Position‑wise non‑uniformity** – The first few hundred tokens in any long document typically receive disproportionately large attention scores across many layers (as shown in streaming LLM literature). Preserving their original rotational angles avoids degrading these high‑impact tokens. The scalar \(𝕀(\cdot)\) therefore leaves them untouched while applying interpolation only after token index \(n=\hat n.\)

By treating these two sources of heterogeneity jointly, LongRoPE can keep essential “high‑information” components of RoPE intact while still accommodating millions of new positions.

---

## 3. The Search Space and Its Exponential Growth

The set of admissible solutions is defined by all possible assignments of:
- a real-valued scaling factor for each RoPE dimension, i.e., a point in a continuous space of dimension \(\tfrac d2.\)
- an integer prefix length chosen from a modest discrete set (e.g., powers of two up to 256).

In practice each dimension’s factor is constrained to a bounded interval,
\(1.0 \leqslant   λ_i   \leqslant   s\times 1.25,\)
where \(s = L'/L>1.\)  
If we discretize this interval with step size \(Δ=0.01,\) each dimension offers roughly
\(K = ((s\times 1.25)-1)/Δ + 1 = O(s\times 100)\)
distinct values.  

Hence, for an embedding dimension typical of modern LLMs (\(d=4096,\) i.e., 2048 RoPE pairs), the total number of possible scaling configurations becomes

\[
|{\cal S}| = K^{d/2} = O((s\times100)^{2048}),
\tag{6}
\]

which grows astronomically even for modest extension ratios:  
- For \(s=4,\ |{\cal S}|≈10^{167};\\
- For \(s=128,\ |{\cal S}|≈10^{6000}.\)

Even before accounting for choices of $\hat n$, this combinatorial explosion makes exhaustive enumeration impossible.

---

## 4. Infeasibility of Gradient‑Based Optimization

One might wonder whether we could treat each $\lambda_i$ as a differentiable parameter and directly minimize Equation (5) using gradient descent, as is customary for fine‑tuning transformer weights. Several practical obstacles prevent this approach from succeeding:

| Issue | Explanation |
|-------|-------------|
| **Non‑smooth loss landscape** | The loss ${\\cal L}$ evaluated on very long sequences is highly stochastic; tiny changes in $\lambda_i$ can cause abrupt alterations in angle periodicity, leading to non‑continuous loss spikes. |
| **Discrete piecewise behaviour** | The function $𝕀(\hat λ_i,\hat n)$ introduces a hard switch at $n=\hat n.$ Gradient signals cannot flow across this discontinuity, making $\hat n$ itself non‑differentiable. |
| **Dimensional coupling** | Rotational angles appear inside trigonometric functions; gradients are periodic and vanish or explode depending on current phase, causing unstable updates especially when many dimensions are involved simultaneously. |
| **Computational cost** | Each evaluation of ${\\cal L}$ requires forward passes over sequences longer than $100$ k tokens, which already costs many seconds on modern GPUs. A gradient step would need dozens (or hundreds) of such forward–backward passes per minibatch, rendering it prohibitively expensive. |
| **Limited data** | Training corpora containing truly multi‑million token documents are scarce; without abundant data, stochastic gradient estimates become extremely noisy and fail to converge reliably. |

Because of these problems, it is far more efficient to treat Equation (5) as a *black‑box* optimization problem: given any candidate $\widehat{\Lambda}$ we can compute its objective value (perplexity on a small validation set). The task then reduces to searching for low‑loss points in an astronomically large discrete space—a setting where evolutionary algorithms excel.

---

## 5. Summary

We have precisely articulated LongRoPE’s core optimization challenge: identify per‑dimension rescaling factors $\lambda_i$ and a short prefix length $\hat n$ such that, after applying piecewise scaling $𝕀(\cdot)$ to RoPE’s rotational angles, the frozen transformer yields minimal next‑token loss on extremely long inputs.  
The formulation highlights two crucial sources of non‑uniformity—dimensional frequency differences and early token importance—and shows how they are incorporated into a compact mathematical representation (Eqns (2)-(5)).  
Finally, we explained why conventional gradient methods are unsuitable and justified casting the problem as an evolutionary search over an exponential space whose size grows rapidly with the desired extension ratio $s$.  

The next chapter details how LongRoPE implements this search efficiently using evolutionary strategies together with domain‐specific constraints that dramatically prune the space while preserving high‐quality solutions.

# Evolutionary Search Algorithm for LongRoPE  

Extending the context window of a large language model (LLM) by modifying its Rotary Positional Embedding (RoPE) entails choosing a set of rescaling factors $\{\lambda_i\}$—one for each RoPE dimension—and a token‑position threshold $\hat{n}$ that determines how many of the earliest positions retain their original embedding.  The space of admissible configurations grows combinatorially with the target extension ratio $s = L'/L$, quickly becoming astronomically large for the multi‑million‑token windows we aim to achieve.  To navigate this space efficiently, LongRoPE employs an **evolutionary search** that combines principled initialization, constrained variation operators, and a lightweight fitness function based on language‑model perplexity.  In this chapter we describe each component of the search in detail, explain why the constraints are theoretically justified by Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) considerations, and analyze the algorithmic complexity that makes the method practical even when $s$ exceeds $500$.

## 1. Designing a Search Space that Captures Non‑Uniformity  

The evolutionary population consists of candidate vectors  
\[
\mathbf{\Lambda} = (\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_{d/2-1}),\qquad \hat{n}\in\mathcal{N},
\]  
where $d$ is the hidden dimension of the Transformer and $\lambda_i$ rescales the rotary angle of the $i$‑th RoPE sub‑dimension according to Eq. (3) in the paper.  For a given target extension ratio $s$, each $\lambda_i$ is allowed to take any value in the interval  
\[
[1.0,\;1.25\,s],
\]  
with a granularity of $0.01$.  The lower bound $1.0$ corresponds to **direct extrapolation** (no interpolation) for that dimension, while the upper bound exceeds the naïve linear interpolation factor $s$ by $25\%$, thereby granting the search freedom to over‑interpolate when it may be beneficial for higher‑frequency dimensions.

The token‑position threshold $\hat{n}$ determines how many leading tokens are spared any rescaling; formally,
\[
\mathbb{I}(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat{n})=
\begin{cases}
1 & n<\hat{n},\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\lambda_i} & n\ge \hat{n},
\end{cases}
\]  
with $\hat{\lambda}_i=1/\lambda_i$.  Empirically we restrict $\hat{n}$ to a small discrete set that balances expressiveness and search tractability:  
\[
\mathcal{N}= \{0,1,2,4,8,12,16,20,24,28,32,64,128,256\}.
\]  
When $\hat{n}=0$ all positions undergo rescaling; larger values protect an increasingly long prefix of the sequence from any alteration.

Collectively these choices form a **multidimensional lattice** whose cardinality is on the order of $(1.25 s/0.01)^{d/2}\times|\mathcal{N}|$, which for typical values ($d=4096$, $s=512$) exceeds $10^{200}$.  Exhaustive enumeration is therefore impossible, motivating a guided stochastic exploration.

## 2. Seeding the Population with Knowledgeable Individuals  

Evolutionary algorithms typically begin with a randomly sampled population; however, such naïve initialization would waste vast computation on regions already known to be suboptimal.  LongRoPE augments random individuals with three **knowledge‑based seeds** derived from established positional interpolation schemes:

1. **Linear Positional Interpolation (PI)**: all dimensions share a uniform scaling $\lambda_i = s$.
2. **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) interpolation**: dimensions follow $\lambda_i = s^{i/(d/2-1)}$, implementing an exponential schedule that interpolates less in high‑frequency dimensions.
3. **YaRN**: a piecewise schedule in which low‑frequency groups use PI ($\lambda_i=s$), high‑frequency groups use pure extrapolation ($\lambda_i=1$), and intermediate groups follow NTK.

These three vectors are inserted unchanged into the initial population of size $P$.  The remaining $P-3$ individuals are generated by applying a small mutation probability $p$ (typically $0.3$) to each seed; mutation consists of shifting selected $\lambda_i$ values up or down by one step ($0.01$) while respecting the admissible interval.  This hybrid seeding ensures that early generations already explore promising baselines and their immediate neighborhoods.

## 3. Enforcing β–Monotonicity Across Dimensions  

A critical observation from NTK theory is that RoPE dimensions correspond to sinusoidal frequencies proportional to $\beta^{-i}$ with $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ and base $\theta=10\,000$.  Low indices ($i$ small) encode **high frequencies**, thus representing fine positional granularity; high indices encode **low frequencies**, capturing coarse positional structure.  Excessive interpolation in high‑frequency dimensions would collapse distinct token positions onto nearby angles, harming attention discrimination.

Consequently LongRoPE imposes a **monotonic non‑decreasing constraint** on the scaling factors:
\[
\lambda_0 \le \lambda_1 \le \dots \le \lambda_{d/2-1}.
\]  
Intuitively this forces low‑frequency dimensions (large $i$) to tolerate larger interpolation while protecting high‑frequency dimensions from being overly compressed.  From an NTK perspective this mirrors the kernel’s bandwidth adaptation: preserving high‑frequency components maintains expressivity for short‑range dependencies even after extreme lengthening.

During search, any candidate violating monotonicity is discarded before fitness evaluation; this culling reduces evaluation cost dramatically because only a tiny fraction of randomly generated vectors satisfy the ordering when $d/2=2048$.  Moreover, monotonic candidates align with prior successful heuristics (NTK and YaRN), lending additional empirical justification.

## 4. Fitness Evaluation via Perplexity on a Compact Validation Set  

Evaluating an individual requires measuring how well the modified RoPE enables the LLM to predict tokens at lengths up to $L'$.  Directly computing perplexity on a full corpus of multi‑million‑token documents would be prohibitive; instead LongRoPE adopts a **lightweight fitness proxy**:

*Select* five short excerpts from the PG19 validation split such that each excerpt’s length exceeds the target window $L'$ (the excerpts are padded or truncated as needed).  For each candidate $(\mathbf{\Lambda}, \hat{n})$, replace the model’s RoPE with the corresponding rescaled angles as prescribed by Eq. (3), then run forward passes over the five excerpts and compute average token-level negative log‑likelihood:
\[
\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{\Lambda}, \hat{n}) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{5}\sum_{t=1}^{|x_k|} -\log p_{\text{LLM}}(x_{k,t}\mid x_{k,<t}; \text{RoPE}_{(\mathbf{\Lambda}, \hat{n})}),
\]  
where $N$ is total token count across all excerpts.  The exponentiation of $\mathcal{F}$ yields perplexity; lower values indicate better preservation of positional information after scaling.

Because we evaluate only five samples per individual, each fitness call incurs merely seconds of GPU time even for large models such as LLaMA2‑7B or Mistral‑7B.  The stochastic nature of these samples does not harm selection pressure: over multiple generations different subsets are drawn, providing an implicit regularization that prevents overfitting to any single document.

## 5. Evolutionary Flow: Selection, Mutation, Crossover, and Termination  

The evolutionary loop proceeds for a fixed budget of iterations $\mathcal{T}$ (typically 40).  At iteration $t$, let $\mathcal{P}_{t-1}$ denote the current population:

1. **Fitness Computation** – Evaluate every individual in $\mathcal{P}_{t-1}$ using the perplexity metric described above.
2. **Selection** – Sort individuals by ascending fitness and retain the top $k$ (e.g., 32). These elite individuals constitute *parents* for subsequent variation.
3. **Mutation** – For each parent generate $N_1$ offspring by independently perturbing a random subset of its $\lambda_i$ entries: each selected entry is increased or decreased by exactly one discretization step ($±0.01$), provided it stays within $[1.0,\,1.25 s]$.  The threshold $\hat{n}$ may also be mutated by moving to an adjacent value in $\mathcal{N}$. After mutation we enforce monotonicity; any violating offspring are discarded.
4. **Crossover** – Pair parents randomly and produce $N_2$ children by exchanging contiguous blocks of their $\lambda_i$ vectors (single-point crossover).  The child inherits its $\hat{n}$ from one parent uniformly at random.  Again monotonicity is checked.
5. **Population Update** – Form $\mathcal{P}_t = \text{Elite}\cup \text{Mutants}\cup \text{Children}$; if this set exceeds size $P$, truncate by discarding worst–fitness individuals.
6. **Termination** – After completing $\mathcal{T}$ generations return the individual with minimal perplexity observed across all generations.

Algorithmically this process matches classic *$(μ+λ)$* evolutionary strategies but adapts two domain-specific tricks—knowledgeable seeding and monotonic pruning—to reduce wasteful evaluations dramatically.

### Pseudocode Summary  

```
Input: target model M, extension ratio s,
       validation set X_val,
       population size P,
       elite size k,
       mutation size N1,
       crossover size N2,
       max iterations T,
       mutation prob p

# Step 0: initialize
Init ← {PI_vec(s), NTK_vec(s), YaRN_vec(s)}   # three seeds
while |Init| < P:
    candidate ← Randomly perturb one seed with prob p
    if monotonic(candidate): Init ← Init ∪ {candidate}
Population ← Init

for t = 1 … T:
    # fitness
    for indiv ∈ Population:
        loss[indiv] ← Perplexity(M with RoPE(indiv), X_val)

    # select elite
    Elite ← top-k individuals by loss

    # mutation
    Mutants ← ∅
    for parent ∈ Elite:
        repeat N1 times:
            child ← Mutate(parent)
            if monotonic(child): Mutants ← Mutants ∪ {child}

    # crossover
    Children ← ∅
    while |Children| < N2:
        p1,p2 ← random pair from Elite
        child ← Crossover(p1,p2)
        if monotonic(child): Children ← Children ∪ {child}

    Population ← Elite ∪ Mutants ∪ Children
    truncate Population to size P if needed

return argmin_indiv loss[indiv]
```

The `Mutate` routine flips each selected λᵢ by ±0.01; `Crossover` swaps contiguous slices between two parents; `monotonic` implements λᵢ ≤ λᵢ₊₁ verification.

## 6. Complexity Considerations and Practical Guidance  

### Computational Complexity  

Each fitness evaluation entails a forward pass through the LLM on five validation excerpts; let $C_{\text{pass}}$ denote its wall‑clock cost (seconds).  With population size $P$, number of generations $T$, and assuming all individuals survive monotonic filtering (worst case), total runtime scales as
\[
O(T\,P\,C_{\text{pass}}).
\]
In practice—thanks to monotonic pruning—only about $10–20\,\%$ of generated offspring survive to evaluation; thus effective cost is roughly
\[
O(T\,(\text{k}+0.2(P-\text{k}))\,C_{\text{pass}}),
\]
where *k* is elite size (e.g., 32). For LLaMA2‑7B on four A100 GPUs we observe about 30 seconds per fitness call; with $(P,T,k)=(64,40,32)$ this yields roughly 20 minutes of GPU time per search run—trivial compared with days required for full fine‑tuning at million‑token lengths.

The memory overhead is negligible because only scalar vectors $(\lambda_i,\hat n)$ reside in CPU RAM; RoPE modification happens on‐the‐fly before inference.

### Scaling to Very Large Extension Ratios  

When $s$ grows large (e.g., achieving $2048\,k /4\,k =512\times$), two issues arise:

1️⃣ **Search Range Expansion** – The upper bound $1.25\,s$ becomes huge; however due to monotonicity most feasible solutions concentrate near lower values for early dimensions and near higher values only for later dimensions. Empirically we therefore cap exploration for low‐frequency indices ($i> d/4$) at $s$, reducing unnecessary sampling beyond what NTK predicts.

2️⃣ **Granularity vs Performance** – A step size of 0.01 remains sufficiently fine because even at high ratios small variations produce sizable angular changes only in low frequency dimensions where interpolation tolerance is greater.

If computational budget permits further refinement—e.g., after identifying a promising region—we can perform *local grid search* around the best individual: enumerate all combinations within ±0.02 around each λᵢ while preserving monotonicity; this adds negligible extra cost relative to full evolutionary runs.

### Practical Tips  

* **Validate early:** Run a short trial with $s=8\times$ to verify that your hardware can accommodate RoPE recomputation without overflow; adjust datatype precision if necessary.
* **Warm‐up learning rate:** Although not part of search proper, when finetuning after obtaining rescaling factors it helps to start with a modest learning rate (≈ 2e−5) and limit steps (< 1000) because most positional knowledge has already been encoded.
* **Checkpoint reuse:** Store RoPE parameters after every generation; if later you discover an even better configuration you can roll back without recomputing earlier forward passes.
* **Parallel fitness:** Since evaluating individuals is embarrassingly parallel, distribute them across multiple GPUs or processes; this linearly reduces wall time.
* **Reusing seeds across targets:** When extending sequentially (e.g., 4k→256k→2048k), keep PI/NTK/YaRN seeds calibrated for each intermediate ratio—they serve as useful anchors for subsequent searches.

---

Through these design choices—carefully bounded search space, knowledge-driven initialization, NTK‐motivated monotonic constraints, low‑cost perplexity fitness, and classic evolutionary operators—LongRoPE transforms an otherwise intractable combinatorial optimization into a tractable procedure that consistently discovers high‑quality non‑uniform rescaling schedules for RoPE embeddings across billions of tokens. This evolutionary search underpins all subsequent stages of LongRoPE’s progressive extension pipeline and ultimately enables LLMs with context windows exceeding two million tokens while retaining performance on conventional short contexts.
