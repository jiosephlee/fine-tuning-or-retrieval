\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: Motivation and Overview of Few‑Shot Tabular Learning  

In many real‑world applications, data naturally appear in tabular form: rows correspond to individual entities (patients, customers, experiments) and columns correspond to measured attributes (age, blood pressure, credit score).  While deep learning has dramatically reduced the need for hand‑crafted features on images or text, tabular datasets still pose a uniquely difficult problem when only a handful of labeled examples are available.  This chapter explains why few‑shot learning on tabular data is hard, surveys the traditional strategies that have been employed to mitigate the scarcity of labels, and then introduces the emerging role of large language models (LLMs) as external knowledge bases.  We conclude by outlining the key limitations of existing LLM‑centric methods and motivating the central idea behind **FeatLLM**, which treats an LLM as a feature engineer rather than as an end‑to‑end predictor.  

---  

## Why Tabular Data Remain Hard for Few‑Shot Learning  

Tabular data differ from images or natural language in several fundamental ways that make them resistant to the few‑shot techniques that have succeeded elsewhere.  

First, **heterogeneity of columns** is ubiquitous.  A single table may contain continuous measurements (e.g., serum glucose), ordinal scales (e.g., cancer stage), categorical identifiers (e.g., blood type), and even free‑text notes.  The statistical relationships among such diverse feature types are highly dataset‑specific, and there is no universal inductive bias—such as translation invariance for images—that a model can safely assume.  

Second, **lack of spatial or sequential structure** limits the effectiveness of convolutional or recurrent architectures that automatically share parameters across locations.  In a table, each column carries its own semantic meaning; swapping two columns changes the problem entirely.  Consequently, models must learn *which* columns matter from the training data itself.  When only a few labeled rows are present (the $k$‑shot regime), the risk of mistaking noise for signal grows dramatically.  

Third, **spurious correlations** become especially prevalent in low‑sample regimes.  With only $k$ labeled examples, any random coincidence between a column value and the target label can dominate the learning objective, leading to models that perform well on the training set but fail to generalize.  This phenomenon has been formalized as “overfitting to accidental patterns” in recent statistical learning theory work.  

Together, these properties mean that naïve application of standard supervised learners—logistic regression, gradient boosted trees, or neural networks—often yields unstable performance when $k$ is small.  Even sophisticated meta‑learning or self‑supervised pre‑training pipelines struggle because they cannot easily encode domain‐specific priors that would guide attention toward truly relevant columns.

## Traditional Approaches  

Researchers have explored three broad families of solutions to these challenges: manual feature engineering, transfer learning / self‑supervision, and semi‑supervised augmentation.  

### Feature Engineering and Domain Knowledge  

Historically, practitioners relied on domain experts to craft informative transformations (e.g., binning age into life‑stage categories or computing body‑mass index from height and weight).  These engineered features embed prior knowledge that can dramatically reduce sample complexity: a well‑designed rule may already separate classes with a handful of examples.  However, manual engineering is labor‑intensive, scales poorly across many tasks, and requires experts who may not be available for every domain.  

### Transfer Learning and Self‑Supervision  

More recent work has attempted to learn generic representations from large collections of unlabeled tables.  Methods such as **TabPFN**, **TransTab**, and various contrastive or masked reconstruction objectives train deep models on thousands of heterogeneous datasets, hoping that the resulting embeddings capture useful cross‑column semantics (e.g., “numeric columns tend to correlate linearly”).  After pre‑training, these models can be fine‑tuned on a new task with few labels.  While promising, the heterogeneous nature of tabular data hampers universal augmentations: unlike images where rotations or color jitter preserve semantics, there is no single transformation that reliably respects column semantics across datasets.  Consequently, gains in very low‑shot regimes have been modest.  

### Semi‑Supervised Augmentation  

Another line of work leverages abundant unlabeled rows from the same distribution using techniques such as pseudo‑labeling or consistency regularization (e.g., VIME).  These methods assume that many more unlabeled samples are accessible than labeled ones—a condition often violated in high‑cost domains like healthcare where privacy restrictions limit data sharing.  Moreover, they still depend on the underlying supervised learner to discover which features are truly predictive; if spurious correlations dominate early training iterations, semi‑supervised signals may reinforce them rather than correct them.

## The Rise of LLMs as Knowledge Bases  

Large language models—such as GPT‑3/3.5/4—are trained on massive text corpora spanning scientific literature, encyclopedic entries, news articles, and code repositories.  As a result, they internalize a broad spectrum of factual relationships (“older patients have higher risk of cardiovascular disease”) and procedural knowledge (“BMI = weight / height²”).  Two properties make LLMs attractive for few‑shot tabular learning:  

1. **Zero‑Shot Generalization** – By simply describing a task in natural language and providing a few exemplars (the essence of *in‑context learning*), an LLM can generate plausible predictions for inputs it has never seen during training.  
2. **Reasoning over Structured Descriptions** – When column names and brief textual definitions are supplied in a prompt, an LLM can use its world knowledge to infer causal links between attributes and targets (“smoking status is linked to lung disease”).  

Early attempts to exploit these abilities serialized tabular rows into natural‐language statements and fed them directly into an LLM at inference time (e.g., **TabLLM**, **TABLET**, **In‑Context Learning**).  Some approaches also fine‐tuned LLMs with parameter‐efficient adapters (LoRA, IA3) on a handful of labeled rows to specialize their internal representations for a specific table.

## Limitations of Existing LLM‑Based Tabular Methods  

Despite their ingenuity, current LLM–driven pipelines face practical obstacles that hinder deployment in real applications.

### Per‑Sample Inference Cost  

Methods that query the LLM for every test instance incur significant latency and monetary cost because each call must pass through a large transformer model (often hosted behind an API).  For tables containing thousands of rows this becomes prohibitive.

### Dependence on Fine‑Tuning APIs  

Parameter-efficient fine‑tuning requires direct access to model weights; many state‑of‑the‑art LLMs are only reachable via closed inference APIs (e.g., GPT‑4).  Without the ability to modify internal parameters, approaches such as IA3 cannot be applied.

### Prompt Length Constraints  

LLMs have finite context windows (typically a few thousand tokens).  As the number of columns grows—common in datasets like *Communities* with >100 attributes—the serialized prompt quickly exceeds this limit, forcing either truncation (loss of information) or degradation in performance.

### Susceptibility to Spurious Correlations  

When the prompt contains raw rows together with labels but no explicit guidance about causality, the LLM may latch onto coincidental patterns present in the few demonstrations (e.g., “all positive examples happen to have missing values in column X”).  This mirrors the classic overfitting problem seen in conventional learners.

Collectively, these issues motivate a redesign: rather than using an LLM as an end–to–end predictor for each row, we should harness its reasoning power once—to *extract* knowledge—and then apply lightweight downstream models.

---  

## High‑Level Idea: Using LLMs as Feature Engineers  

The central insight behind **FeatLLM** is to treat the LLM not as a black box that consumes every test sample but as an *expert analyst* that reads a handful of labeled examples, reasons about the underlying decision criteria, and outputs human–readable rules (e.g., “Age > 65 AND BloodPressure ≥ 140 ⇒ class = HighRisk”).  

These rules can be programmatically parsed into binary indicator features—each indicating whether a given row satisfies a specific condition.  Because rules are derived from both prior world knowledge (encoded in the LLM) and the few supplied demonstrations, they encode meaningful relationships while discarding irrelevant columns early on.  Once these binary features are constructed for all rows:

1. A simple linear model with non‑negative weights learns how much each rule contributes to each class (the training step requires only a few gradient updates).  
2. At inference time no further calls to the LLM are needed; predictions amount to evaluating logical conditions followed by dot products—a negligible computational burden.  
3. To mitigate prompt size limits and increase robustness, FeatLLM repeats this process under different random subsets of columns (“feature bagging”) and ensembles the resulting linear classifiers—a strategy reminiscent of classic random forests but applied at the level of rule generation.

By shifting the costly reasoning step onto a single batch query per ensemble member—and by limiting each query’s prompt via bagging—FeatLLM achieves high predictive accuracy comparable to full LLM inference while retaining low latency and modest API usage.  The remainder of this textbook will dive into each component of this pipeline: prompt engineering for rule extraction, parsing strategies for turning natural language conditions into executable code, training the downstream linear estimator, and ensemble techniques that endow robustness against noisy or spurious rules.

In summary, FeatLLM demonstrates that large language models can serve effectively as *feature engineers*: they translate rich textual priors into compact symbolic representations that enable efficient downstream learning even when only a few labeled instances are available. This paradigm opens new avenues for applying powerful generative models to practical tabular problems across finance, healthcare, engineering, and beyond.
