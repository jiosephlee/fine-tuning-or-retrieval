\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 7: Discussion, Limitations & Future Directions  

In this concluding chapter we reflect on the empirical findings presented earlier, situate **FeatLLM** within the broader landscape of few‑shot tabular learning, and critically examine the boundaries of the current design.  We then outline concrete avenues for extending the framework, emphasizing richer feature families, deeper interpretability, and scalability to larger data regimes.  Throughout we remain faithful to the concepts and observations introduced in the paper.

## Strengths of the FeatLLM Framework  

The experimental results across thirteen heterogeneous tabular datasets demonstrate that **FeatLLM** consistently attains top‑rank performance in low‑shot settings.  Several factors underpin this success:

1. **Effective exploitation of LLM prior knowledge.**  
   By prompting the language model to first reason about causal tendencies between features and the target (Step 1 of the reasoning instruction), FeatLLM elicits rules that are anchored in world knowledge (e.g., “older individuals have higher risk of certain diseases”).  This prevents the model from over‑fitting to spurious patterns that may appear in a handful of labeled examples.

2. **Separation of feature engineering from inference.**  
   Once the LLM has supplied a set of binary decision rules, downstream inference is performed by a lightweight linear model with non‑negative weights (Eq. \(\ref{eq:infer_prob}\)).  Consequently, at test time no further LLM calls are required, yielding inference latency comparable to traditional classifiers such as Logistic Regression or Random Forests.

3. **Robustness through bagging and ensembling.**  
   By repeatedly sampling subsets of features or instances (bagging) and generating rule sets under a relatively high temperature, FeatLLM produces diverse weak learners.  Averaging their predictions mitigates occasional rule generation errors and alleviates prompt‑size constraints on datasets with many columns (e.g., *Communities* and *Myocardial*).

4. **Minimal computational budget.**  
   The entire pipeline requires only a few dozen API queries to the LLM (30 queries in the reported Adult experiment).  Training the downstream linear model is inexpensive (Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.01 for 200 epochs).  Hence FeatLLM can be deployed even when only inference‑only LLM APIs are available.

5. **High-quality feature generation.**  
   The AUROC analysis shows that most generated binary features have predictive power exceeding 0.5 before any weight tuning, and irrelevant rules are automatically down‑weighted during training (“After tuning” column in Table \(\ref{tab:quality}\)).  This confirms that the rule extraction process reliably captures salient relationships.

Collectively, these strengths position FeatLLM as a practical solution for real‑world applications where labeling cost is prohibitive and access to fine‑tunable LLMs is restricted.

## Limitations  

Despite its advantages, FeatLLM exhibits several constraints that arise from design choices made to accommodate low‑shot scenarios and API‑only LLM access.

### 1. Dependence on Binary Rule Representation  

The current implementation translates every extracted rule into a single binary indicator (rule satisfied or not).  While this yields interpretable features and simplifies downstream modeling, it may discard richer information such as confidence scores, degree of violation, or continuous thresholds embedded in natural‑language expressions (“greater than 5”).  Consequently, subtle quantitative nuances can be lost.

### 2. Fixed Number of Rules per Class  

For each class we request exactly ten rules from the LLM.  This heuristic balances prompt length against expressive capacity, but it imposes an artificial ceiling on the number of potentially useful criteria, especially for complex tasks with many interacting variables.  Increasing the rule count inflates the dimensionality of \(\mathbf{z}_k^i\) and can lead to over‑fitting in extreme low‑shot regimes.

### 3. Low‑Shot Focus  

FeatLLM is explicitly evaluated with \(k \leq 8\) labeled examples per task.  The weight‑tuning stage relies on enough data points to estimate non‑negative importance weights; when \(k\) grows substantially (hundreds or thousands), a linear model may become insufficiently expressive compared to tree ensembles or deep networks that can exploit higher‑order interactions among raw features.

### 4. Reliance on Prompt Engineering  

The quality of extracted rules hinges on carefully crafted prompts: basic information description, reasoning instruction, and response instruction.  Slight variations in wording or order of demonstrations can affect output consistency—an effect noted by prior work on LLM self‑consistency \cite{wang2022self}.  Users must therefore devote effort to prompt design for each new domain.

### 5. Sensitivity to Noisy Feature Descriptions  

The parsing pipeline assumes that feature type annotations (numeric vs categorical) are accurate and that their textual definitions are trustworthy.  In domains where metadata are incomplete or erroneous, the LLM may produce malformed conditions (“less than ‘high’”) that hinder automatic code generation despite fallback parsing via the LLM itself.

### 6. Limited Interpretability Beyond Rules  

Although each binary feature corresponds directly to a human‑readable rule, the downstream linear weights obscure how multiple rules combine when predicting a class probability (the softmax aggregates logits across classes).  Moreover, ensemble averaging further dilutes traceability to any single rule set.

## Future Directions  

Addressing the above limitations opens numerous research pathways that retain FeatLLM’s core philosophy—leveraging LLMs as *feature engineers*—while broadening applicability and interpretability.

### A. Enriching Feature Families  

1. **Continuous Transformations:**  
   Instead of binarizing every condition, we could ask the LLM to output parametric functions (e.g., “\(x_3 / (x_5 + 1)\)” or “\(\log(x_2)\)”) that map raw columns onto real-valued engineered features.  Parsing such expressions would involve symbolic computation libraries (e.g., `sympy`) rather than solely code generation.

2. **Probabilistic Rules:**  
   The LLM could be prompted to provide confidence estimates (“with probability ≈ 0.8”) alongside each rule; these confidences could serve as soft weights before learning another layer of calibration.

3. **Higher‑Order Interactions:**  
   By allowing logical combinations beyond simple conjunctions (e.g., “(Age > 60) AND (Cholesterol > 200)”), we could generate composite binary features that capture interactions without requiring an explicit tree learner.

### B. Adaptive Rule Quantity  

A dynamic stopping criterion could be introduced where the LLM continues emitting rules until a marginal utility threshold is met (e.g., diminishing increase in validation AUROC).  This would tailor rule count to dataset complexity while preserving prompt length via incremental bagging.

### C. Scaling Beyond Low‑Shot Regimes  

When more labeled data become available:

- **Replace Linear Aggregation** with a shallow neural network or gradient boosted trees applied to the engineered feature matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\).  
- **Iterative Feature Refinement:** After an initial round of rule generation and weight training, feed back high‑weight features as additional context into a second LLM query to refine or specialize rules further.

### D. Automated Prompt Optimization  

Meta‑learning techniques could be employed to automatically discover effective prompt templates for a given domain; reinforcement learning over prompt space has shown promise in other LLM settings and could reduce manual engineering burden.

### E. Robust Parsing Pipelines  

While leveraging the LLM itself for parsing mitigates syntactic noise, incorporating a grammar‑aware parser (e.g., using abstract syntax trees) could provide stronger guarantees about correctness, especially when dealing with mathematically intensive conditions or nested logical expressions.

### F. Enhanced Interpretability Interfaces  

1. **Rule Attribution:** Extend Shapley value concepts \cite{lundberg2017unified} to attribute predictions back to individual rules across ensembles, yielding per‑sample explanations that highlight which criteria were decisive.

2. **Visualization Dashboards:** Present users with aggregated statistics such as rule frequency across ensembles, average learned weight per rule type, and confusion matrices conditioned on rule satisfaction.

### G: Mitigating Societal Biases  

Since FeatLLM imports world knowledge directly from pretrained LLMs, any embedded prejudices may manifest in generated rules (e.g., attributing health risks disproportionately to demographic groups). Systematic audits—testing generated rule sets on curated bias benchmarks—and incorporating fairness constraints during weight training constitute essential safeguards for deployment in sensitive sectors like finance or healthcare.

## Concluding Remarks  

FeatLLM illustrates that large language models need not be invoked at every prediction step; instead they can act as powerful *knowledge extractors* that synthesize domain‐relevant criteria from only a few labeled instances.  By converting these criteria into binary features amenable to simple linear models—and stabilizing them through bagged ensembling—the approach achieves state‑of‑the‑art performance while remaining computationally lightweight and compatible with inference‑only APIs.

Future work will focus on broadening the expressiveness of engineered features, automating prompt design, scaling to richer data regimes, and embedding rigorous interpretability and fairness mechanisms.  Pursuing these directions will further cement FeatLLM’s role as a practical bridge between foundation language models and real‑world tabular decision making.
