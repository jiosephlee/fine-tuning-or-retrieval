# Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion and the Need for Controlled Fine‑tuning  

## Overview of large‑scale text‑to‑image diffusion  

Text‑to‑image diffusion models have become the dominant paradigm for generating high‑fidelity images from natural‑language prompts.  Systems such as **Stable Diffusion**, **DALL‑E 2**, **Imagen**, and the earlier **GLIDE** model follow a common recipe: a large‑scale pretrained diffusion network learns to reverse a gradual noising process on either pixel space (as in GLIDE and Imagen) or a learned latent space (as in Stable Diffusion).  The diffusion process is conditioned on a text embedding produced by a language model (often frozen, as in Imagen) or by a jointly trained text encoder (as in GLIDE).  Training is performed on hundreds of millions of image–text pairs, resulting in models that capture a rich joint distribution over language and visual content.  

During inference, one samples a noise tensor, iteratively denoises it while feeding the same text embedding at each step, and finally obtains an image that matches the semantic description encoded in the prompt.  The learned denoising network contains many billions of parameters, most of which are stored in linear layers (e.g., the query–key–value projections of attention modules) and convolutional blocks that process the latent representation.  Because the model has seen such an enormous diversity of concepts during pretraining, it can render photorealistic scenes, artistic styles, and even novel objects that have never been explicitly annotated.

## Limitations of plain textual prompting  

Although the raw textual interface is remarkably expressive, it remains *coarse* for many practical applications.  A prompt such as “a portrait of a smiling person” specifies only high‑level semantics (person, smile, portrait) but does not control finer details like the exact identity of the person, clothing colors, or precise pose.  In practice, users encounter two major sources of ambiguity:

1. **Subject ambiguity** – The model may synthesize any person that satisfies “a smiling person”, ignoring a specific individual that the user wishes to portray.  
2. **Spatial or structural ambiguity** – Text does not convey explicit geometric constraints; for example, “a cat sitting on a chair” leaves the exact silhouette of the chair undefined.

These ambiguities become problematic when the downstream goal demands *precise* control over who or what appears in the generated scene, or when auxiliary signals (edges, segmentation masks, landmarks) must be respected.

## Two downstream tasks: subject‑driven generation and controllable generation  

To overcome textual ambiguity, recent research has formulated two complementary downstream tasks.

### Subject‑driven generation  

In this setting the user supplies **a few reference images** of a particular subject—often only three to five pictures of a person, pet, or object—along with a new textual description (“a portrait of *[subject]* wearing a red hat”).  The model must retain the visual identity captured by the reference images while adapting to novel contexts described by the prompt.  This problem is exemplified by DreamBooth \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, which fine‑tunes the entire diffusion network on the few images using a small learning rate so that the subject’s appearance becomes part of the model’s internal knowledge.

### Controllable generation  

Here an **additional modality** (edge map, segmentation map, landmark set, depth map …) is supplied together with the text prompt.  The model must generate an image that satisfies both modalities simultaneously.  Prior works such as ControlNet \cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter \cite{mou2023t2i} treat this as an image‑to‑image translation problem: they augment a pretrained diffusion backbone with extra conditioning pathways that ingest the control signal.

Both tasks can be interpreted as *fine‑tuning* problems: we start from a massive pretraining checkpoint and adapt it to a new objective while hoping to keep its original generative abilities.

## Desiderata for fine‑tuning: training efficiency and preservation of generative quality  

A viable fine‑tuning strategy must satisfy two orthogonal requirements.

### Training efficiency  

Large diffusion models contain millions to billions of parameters; updating all of them would be computationally prohibitive and would require massive amounts of data.  Efficient approaches therefore aim to **reduce either the number of trainable parameters or the number of training epochs** (or both).  Parameter‑efficient adapters such as LoRA \cite{hulora2022} achieve this by restricting weight updates to a low‑rank subspace; similar ideas appear in adapter modules for language models.

### Preservation of generative quality (generalizability preservation)  

The pretrained checkpoint encodes a delicate balance between diversity, fidelity, and semantic alignment learned from massive data.  Any fine‑tuning procedure should **maintain this balance**; otherwise the adapted model may collapse to mode‐seeking behavior, lose diversity, or forget how to render certain concepts that were never seen during fine‑tuning.  Existing methods implicitly assume that staying close to the pretrained weights in Euclidean norm (\(\| \mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^{0}\|_{2}\)) suffices for preservation, but such scalar distance measures ignore *structural* relationships among neurons that are critical for semantic information.

## Shortcomings of existing fine‑tuning approaches  

### Small learning‑rate updates  

The simplest adaptation technique updates every weight with a tiny step size (as done in DreamBooth).  Although this keeps \(\| \mathbf{M} - \mathbf{M}^{0}\|_{2}\) small, it provides **no guarantee** that higher‐order structures—such as pairwise angles between neurons—remain unchanged.  In practice this can lead to unstable training: after many iterations DreamBooth can produce corrupted images or lose subject identity.

### Low‑rank adapters (LoRA)  

LoRA adds an additive low‑rank matrix \(\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\) to each target weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\).  
Because \(\operatorname{rank}(\Delta\mathbf{W}) = r'\) is much smaller than \(\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\), only a limited number of directions in weight space are altered.  This reduces parameter count dramatically but **does not preserve pairwise neuron similarity** either; orthogonal components can still drift arbitrarily.  Consequently LoRA may struggle to retain subtle identity cues needed for subject‐driven generation.

### Adapter modules for controllable generation  

ControlNet and T2I‑Adapter prepend auxiliary conditioning branches that are themselves trained from scratch.  While they achieve strong control performance, they introduce **extra inference overhead** and require many additional parameters per task.  Moreover their training dynamics often exhibit sudden jumps in controllability—empirically observed as abrupt drops in landmark error after several epochs—indicating unstable convergence.

### Lack of principled structural metric  

All aforementioned approaches rely on either Euclidean distance or low‐rank constraints as proxies for “preserving knowledge”.  The paper introduces **hyperspherical energy (HE)** as a more faithful structural measure:

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \sum_{i\neq j} \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1},
\quad
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},
\]

where \(\mathbf{w}_{i}\) denotes the \(i\)-th column (neuron) of weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\).  
HE captures how uniformly neurons are distributed on the unit hypersphere; larger deviations indicate altered angular relationships among neurons, which empirical work links to semantic degradation.

Existing methods lack any mechanism to directly control HE during adaptation; consequently they cannot guarantee that fine­-tuned models retain the angular geometry that underlies semantic fidelity.

---

In summary, modern text‑to‑image diffusion models provide unprecedented generative power but remain difficult to steer precisely with plain text prompts alone.  Subject‑driven generation and controllable generation demand fine‑tuning strategies that are both *parameter efficient* and *structurally faithful* to the pretrained checkpoint.  Conventional small–learning‐rate updates, low–rank adapters, and auxiliary control branches fall short either in stability or in preserving hyperspherical structure.  These observations motivate an orthogonal approach—**Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT)**—that explicitly preserves hyperspherical energy while offering efficient adaptation for both downstream tasks.

# Geometric Perspective on Neural Representations: Hyperspherical Energy  

In modern deep‑learning systems, especially the large text‑to‑image diffusion models that power state‑of‑the‑art image synthesis, the hidden neurons of each layer form a high‑dimensional vector space.  Understanding how these neurons are arranged geometrically can give us a principled way to evaluate whether a finetuned model still retains the semantic knowledge that was learned during pretraining.  This chapter introduces **hyperspherical energy**, a quantitative measure of the pairwise angular relationships among neurons, and explains why preserving this quantity is crucial for controlling diffusion models without sacrificing their original generative abilities.

---  

## Definition of Hyperspherical Energy and Its Relation to Cosine Similarity  

Consider a fully‑connected layer (or, analogously, the row vectors of any linear weight matrix) with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}= \{\mathbf{w}_1,\dots,\mathbf{w}_n\}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]  

where each column \(\mathbf{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d\) denotes a **neuron** (i.e., the set of parameters that produce one output dimension).  To focus solely on direction, we first normalise each neuron onto the unit hypersphere:

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{w}_i}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|},\qquad i=1,\dots,n .
\]

The **pairwise angular similarity** between two neurons is captured by the cosine of the angle between them,

\[
\cos\theta_{ij}= \hat{\mathbf{w}}_i^{\!\top}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j .
\]

A natural geometric quantity that aggregates all these pairwise relationships is the **hyperspherical energy (HE)**, defined as

\[
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^{n} 
 \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\bigr\|^{-1}
   \;=\;
   \sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|}
   .
\tag{1}
\]

Because all vectors lie on the unit sphere, the Euclidean distance in the numerator can be expressed in terms of the cosine similarity:

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^2 
   = 2(1-\cos\theta_{ij}),
\]

so that

\[
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
   = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(1-\cos\theta_{ij})}} .
\]

Hence hyperspherical energy is a monotonic transformation of **pairwise cosine similarity**; minimizing (or preserving) HE is equivalent to encouraging (or maintaining) a particular distribution of angles among all neurons in a layer.  When two weight matrices \(\mathbf{W}\) and \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) have identical HE, every pairwise angle between their normalized neurons coincides.  This invariance property will be central to our later discussion on orthogonal finetuning.

---  

## Why Angles Matter: Insights from SphereNet and a Toy Auto‑Encoder Experiment  

### 1. Theoretical motivation from SphereNet  

SphereNet (Liu et al., 2017) demonstrated that training a network **with all neurons constrained to live on a unit hypersphere** does not impair expressive power.  In fact, such normalisation often improves generalisation because learning focuses on *directions* rather than magnitudes.  The key intuition is that for linear operations,

\[
z = \mathbf{w}^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}
     = \|\mathbf{w}\|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\cos(\theta),
\]

the output can be decomposed into an *amplitude* term \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|\) and an *angular* term \(\cos(\theta)\).  When data are roughly balanced in norm (as is the case after common normalisation layers), the angular term dominates semantic discrimination: two inputs that share similar directions produce similar activations regardless of their absolute magnitudes.

### 2. Toy auto‑encoder experiment  

The authors of the diffusion‑finetuning paper performed a concrete experiment to visualise this phenomenon.  They trained a standard convolutional auto‑encoder on a small set of flower images using the usual inner‑product forward pass:

```math
z = \mathbf{w}^{⊤}\mathbf{x}.
```

After training converged, three alternative ways to compute activations were evaluated on *test* images:

1. **Inner product** (the training rule): \(z_{\text{inner}} = \mathbf{w}^{⊤}\mathbf{x}\).  
2. **Magnitude only**: \(z_{\text{mag}} = \|\mathbf{w}\|\, \|\mathbf{x}\|\) (ignoring cosine).  
3. **Angular only**: \(z_{\text{ang}} = \cos(\theta)= \frac{\mathbf{w}^{⊤}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\, \|\mathbf{x}\|}\).

Reconstructing images from each type of activation revealed that *angular information alone* almost perfectly recovered the original flowers, while using only magnitudes produced unrecognisable outputs.  Crucially, this experiment was performed **without any cosine activation during training**—the network was never explicitly taught to rely on angles—yet the learned weights nevertheless stored most semantic content in their directions.

The takeaway is clear: **directions (angles) among neurons encode the bulk of semantic information**, whereas absolute scales are comparatively noisy or redundant.

---  

## Interpreting Hyperspherical Energy as a Measure of Semantic Structure  

Given that semantic content resides primarily in angular relationships, hyperspherical energy serves as an *aggregate statistic* of these relationships across an entire layer:

- **Low HE** means that many neurons are clustered together on the sphere, i.e., many pairwise angles are small.  This can indicate redundancy or over‑specialisation.
- **High HE** (larger sum of inverse distances) indicates that neurons are more uniformly spread, preserving diverse angular orientations.

In the context of large pretrained diffusion models, we do **not** wish to drive HE toward an extreme value; instead we want to keep it *unchanged* after finetuning.  If finetuning altered HE substantially, some pairwise angles would shift, potentially destroying relational structures that were crucial for representing concepts such as “cat”, “sunlight”, or “metallic texture”.  Preserving HE therefore acts as a proxy for preserving **semantic structure**.

Mathematically, consider a pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) and its finetuned counterpart \(\mathbf{W}\).  The difference in hyperspherical energy is

\[
\bigl|\, \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0})\,\bigr|
   = 
   \Bigl|
      \sum_{i\neq j}
      \bigl(
         \|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr)^{-1}
      -
      (\|\hat{\mathbf w}^{0}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}^{0}_j\bigr)^{-1}
      \Bigr|
   .
   \tag{2}
\]

When this quantity is zero, every pairwise distance—and thus every angle—is identical before and after finetuning.  The paper shows that this condition is met exactly when there exists an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf R\in O(d)\) such that

\[
\mathbf W = \mathbf R\, \mathbf W^{0}. 
\tag{3}
\]

Since orthogonal transformations preserve inner products (\(\langle \mathbf R u,\;\mathbf R v\rangle = \langle u,v\rangle\)), Eq. (3) guarantees invariance of all pairwise cosines and consequently of HE.  This observation underlies **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)**: rather than updating each weight arbitrarily, we learn a single orthogonal matrix per layer that rotates (or reflects) all neurons together, thus preserving the geometric fabric of the original model while still granting enough flexibility to adapt to downstream tasks.

---  

## Limitations of Euclidean Distance as a Finetuning Metric  

A naïve approach to measuring how much a model has drifted during finetuning is simply the Euclidean norm of weight differences:

\[
\bigl\|\, \mathbf W - \mathbf W^{0} \,\bigr\|_F .
\tag{4}
\]

Many existing methods implicitly minimise this quantity by using tiny learning rates or by adding low‑rank updates (e.g., LoRA).  However, Euclidean distance conflates two distinct geometric components:

1. **Scale changes** – multiplying all weights in a layer by \(c>0\) inflates (4) dramatically while leaving every angle untouched.
2. **Direction changes** – rotating even a single neuron slightly may cause only modest Euclidean change but can drastically alter its angular relationship with many other neurons.

Because semantic information resides chiefly in directions (as argued above), two models with identical Euclidean distance from the pretrained checkpoint may exhibit wildly different semantic preservation: one could have merely rescaled weights (harmless), while another could have distorted critical angles (harmful).  

Moreover, Euclidean distance treats every entry independently; it lacks any notion of *pairwise structure*.  In high dimensions, many weight configurations yield the same Euclidean norm yet encode completely different manifolds of representations.  

Consequently:

- **Euclidean proximity does not guarantee preservation of hyperspherical energy**, and therefore does not assure maintenance of learned semantics.
- **Relying solely on Euclidean regularisation forces practitioners to use overly small learning rates**, which slows convergence and makes hyperparameter tuning difficult.
- **It provides no clear stopping criterion**: one cannot know when sufficient semantic preservation has been achieved merely by monitoring norm differences.

Hyperspherical energy solves these issues by offering a *structural* metric that directly quantifies whether pairwise angular relationships—the true carriers of semantics—are intact after finetuning.  By enforcing equality of HE through orthogonal transformations (or by constraining deviations around identity as in COFT), we obtain a principled guarantee that finetuned models retain their pretrained generative competence while still being capable of learning new downstream behaviours such as subject‑driven generation or controllable image synthesis.

---  

### Summary  

In this chapter we have formalised hyperspherical energy as an aggregation of cosine similarities among normalized neurons, demonstrated empirically and theoretically why angular information dominates semantic encoding, and argued why preserving hyperspherical energy—rather than merely minimising Euclidean weight drift—is essential for stable and effective finetuning of large text‑to‑image diffusion models.  The subsequent chapters will build on this geometric foundation to describe Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT), showing how they leverage an orthogonal matrix per layer to keep hyperspherical energy unchanged while still granting enough expressive freedom to master downstream tasks.

**Orthogonal Transformations and Their Invariance Properties**

In the preceding sections we saw that naïve finetuning of large text‑to‑image diffusion models often harms the semantic capabilities that were learned during pre‑training.  A central insight of the paper *Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning* is that the pairwise angular relationships among neurons encode much of the semantic structure of a model.  If those relationships can be kept unchanged during adaptation, the model retains its original generative knowledge while still being able to incorporate new control signals.  This chapter develops the mathematical under‑pinning of this claim.  We first formalize orthogonal matrices, then prove that a common orthogonal transformation leaves the hyperspherical energy of a layer untouched, and finally provide intuition—drawn from the Fourier phase‑magnitude decomposition—about why preserving angles strikes a useful balance between flexibility and regularity in finetuning.

---

### Orthogonal Matrices: Definition, Rotation versus Reflection, and the Special Orthogonal Group

An **orthogonal matrix** $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is defined by the relation  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^{\top}= \mathbf{I},
\]

where $\mathbf{I}$ denotes the $d\times d$ identity matrix and $\mathbf{R}^{\top}$ is the transpose of $\mathbf{R}$.  This condition means that the columns (and rows) of $\mathbf{R}$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$; each column has unit Euclidean norm and is orthogonal to every other column.

A geometric way to view an orthogonal matrix is as a **rigid transformation** of Euclidean space that preserves distances and angles.  Every such transformation can be decomposed into two elementary components:

1. **Rotation** – an orientation‑preserving transformation with determinant $\det(\mathbf{R}) = +1$.  Rotations can be visualized as turning a vector around the origin without changing its length.

2. **Reflection** – an orientation‑reversing transformation with determinant $\det(\mathbf{R}) = -1$.  A reflection mirrors vectors across a hyperplane while also preserving lengths.

The set of all $d$‑dimensional orthogonal matrices forms a group under matrix multiplication, denoted $\mathrm{O}(d)$.  The subgroup consisting solely of rotations (determinant $+1$) is called the **special orthogonal group**, written $\mathrm{SO}(d)$.  In practice, the finetuning method described in this work uses any orthogonal matrix; when parameterized via the Cayley transform it automatically belongs to $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ because the transform can only generate matrices with determinant $+1$.  Empirically this restriction does not impair performance.

---

### Hyperspherical Energy and Its Invariance Under a Common Orthogonal Transformation

Consider a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W} = [\mathbf{w}_{1}, \dots , \mathbf{w}_{n}] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where each column $\mathbf{w}_{i}$ is a neuron (or filter) residing in a $d$‑dimensional space.  The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) of this layer quantifies how uniformly its neurons are spread on the unit hypersphere.  Formally, let  

\[
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}= \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},
\]

be the unit‑norm version of neuron $i$.  The HE is defined as  

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j} \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i} - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1}.
\tag{1}
\]

The term inside the sum is simply the Euclidean distance between two points on the unit sphere; its inverse emphasizes pairs that are close together, thereby penalizing clustering of neurons.

Suppose we apply *the same* orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm O(d)$ to all neurons in a layer:

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}.
\tag{2}
\]

Because orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean norms, each transformed neuron satisfies  

\[
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\| 
= \|\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{w}_{i}\|
= \|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|,
\]

and consequently their normalized versions obey  

\[
\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_{i}
=
\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf w}_{i}}{\|\widetilde{\mathbf w}_{i}\|}
=
\frac{\mathbf R\,\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}
=
\mathbf R\,\hat{\mathbf w}_i .
\tag{3}
\]

Now examine any pair $(i,j)$ after transformation:

\[
\bigl\|\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_{i} - 
      \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_{j}\bigr\|
=
 \bigl\|\mathbf R\,(\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j)\bigr\|
=
 \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|,
\tag{4}
\]

where we used the fact that an orthogonal matrix preserves Euclidean distances:
$\|\mathbf R\, \boldsymbol v\,\| = \|\boldsymbol v\|$ for any vector $\boldsymbol v$.
Thus each pairwise distance appearing in (1) remains exactly unchanged after applying $\mathbf R$.
Summing over all pairs gives

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\widetilde{\mathbf W})
=
   \sum_{i \neq j}
   \bigl\|\widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_{i}
           - 
         \widehat{\widetilde{\mathbf w}}_{j}
   \bigr\|^{-1}
=
   \sum_{i \neq j}
   \bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_{i}
           - 
         \hat{\mathbf w}_{j}
   \bigr\|^{-1}
=
   \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W).
\tag{5}
\]

Hence **hyperspherical energy is invariant under any common orthogonal transformation** applied to all neurons in a layer.  This invariance is precisely what Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) exploits: by learning an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$, we can modify how neurons interact with inputs while guaranteeing that their pairwise angular geometry—and therefore their hyperspherical energy—remains identical to that of the pretrained model.

---

### Intuition from Fourier Phase versus Magnitude Analogy

The invariance result resonates with an older observation from signal processing: when we compute a two‑dimensional Fourier transform of an image, it decomposes into a **magnitude spectrum** (absolute values of complex coefficients) and a **phase spectrum** (angles).  Experiments have shown that retaining only the phase spectrum—while replacing magnitudes with random values—still yields images that preserve most semantic content, whereas discarding phase destroys recognizability.  The phase encodes *where* structural elements lie relative to one another (angular relationships), while magnitude captures overall contrast or energy levels.

In a neural network layer, each neuron can be thought of as contributing a basis direction in feature space.  The **pairwise angles** among these bases play an analogous role to Fourier phase: they determine how different feature directions combine to represent semantic aspects of data.  By applying an orthogonal rotation (or reflection), we are essentially reshuffling these directions without altering their relative angular configuration—just as rotating phase does not change its intrinsic relationships.  Consequently, the network’s capacity to encode semantics remains intact even though individual weight vectors have changed.

This analogy clarifies why concentrating finetuning effort on *directions* rather than on arbitrary Euclidean adjustments yields superior control: changing angles directly manipulates semantics, whereas altering magnitudes alone contributes little to perceptual changes.

---

### Preserving Angles: A Sweet Spot Between Flexibility and Regularity

Finetuning demands two seemingly opposing qualities:

* **Flexibility** – enough degrees of freedom to adapt the model to a new downstream task (e.g., learning subject‑specific styles or obeying an extra control map).
* **Regularity** – constraints that prevent catastrophic forgetting of the pretrained knowledge and avoid instability such as model collapse.

Purely unconstrained gradient descent provides maximal flexibility but often leads to large deviations in weight space; this can drastically modify pairwise angles and consequently erase important semantic structure.  
On the opposite extreme, imposing a strict Euclidean proximity constraint (e.g., very small learning rates or low‑rank updates) limits flexibility so much that learning new behaviors becomes painfully slow or impossible.

Orthogonal transformations occupy an intermediate ground:

1. **Full Rank yet Structured** – An orthogonal matrix is full rank; it can rotate any vector into any other orientation while preserving norms.  Thus, despite being constrained, it still offers sufficient expressive power to shift semantics in desired ways.

2. **Angle Preservation** – As proved above, applying one common orthogonal matrix leaves every pairwise angle unchanged.  Hence hyperspherical energy—and more generally any angular similarity measure such as cosine similarity—remains exactly as in the pretrained network.  This guarantees that high‑level semantic relationships encoded by those angles are retained.

3. **Parameter Efficiency** – By parameterizing $\mathbf R$ through block‑diagonal skew‑symmetric matrices (or via low‑dimensional Cayley parameters), OFT drastically reduces trainable parameters compared with naïve full‑matrix updates while still maintaining full rotational capability within each block.

4. **Stability** – Because angles are invariant, optimization proceeds on a smooth manifold (the Stiefel manifold of orthogonal matrices).  Empirically this yields faster convergence and fewer training epochs needed for tasks such as DreamBooth subject adaptation or ControlNet‑style controllable generation.

Consequently, orthogonal finetuning provides just enough flexibility to encode new target behaviors while regularizing updates tightly enough to protect pretraining knowledge.  The subsequent sections of this text will build on these properties to describe concrete algorithmic designs—Cayley parameterization, block diagonal structures, and constrained variants—that make OFT practical for large diffusion models.

**Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT): Formulation and Algorithm**

In this chapter we develop a principled understanding of Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT), the core finetuning paradigm introduced for large text‑to‑image diffusion models.  We begin by stating the OFT optimization problem in a mathematically rigorous way, then explain how the method replaces the conventional gradient‑based weight update with a learned orthogonal matrix on a per‑layer basis.  Afterward we discuss the practical choices that make OFT work in practice—initialization, the way gradients are propagated to the orthogonal matrix, and how training proceeds.  Finally, we compare OFT with two widely used baselines—standard full‑parameter fine‑tuning and LoRA—highlighting the differences in their objective functions and regularization behavior.

---

### Formal Statement of the OFT Optimization Problem

Consider a pre‑trained text‑to‑image diffusion model whose parameters are collected in a set  
\[
\mathcal{M}^{0}=\{\,\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\mid \ell\in\mathcal{L}\,\},
\]  
where each \(\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\ell}\times n_{\ell}}\) denotes the weight matrix of layer \(\ell\).  For a downstream task (e.g., subject‑driven generation or controllable generation) we wish to adapt the model while preserving as much of its original generative knowledge as possible.

The central hypothesis of OFT is that *pairwise angular relationships* among the neurons of a layer encode the semantic structure learned during pretraining.  These relationships can be captured by **hyperspherical energy** (HE):
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) \;=\; \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\;\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
           -\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{j}\;\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad 
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
   = \frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|},
\]
where \(\mathbf{w}_i\) is the \(i^{\text{th}}\) column of \(\mathbf{W}\).  The HE measures how uniformly the normalized weight vectors are distributed on the unit hypersphere; it is invariant under any orthogonal transformation applied to all columns simultaneously.

Given a downstream loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\mathcal{M})\) (e.g., reconstruction loss on subject images, or an adversarial loss for controllable generation), OFT formulates the overall objective as
\[
\boxed{
   \min_{\{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\}}\;
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}\!\bigl(\,\mathcal{M}(\{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\})\,\bigr)
   \quad
   \text{s.t.}\;
   \operatorname{HE}\bigl(\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\bigr)
   = \operatorname{HE}\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\bigr),
   \;
   \forall \ell,
}
\tag{1}
\]
where each \(\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\in O(d_{\ell})\) is an orthogonal matrix (\(\mathbf{R}_{\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{\ell}=I_{d_{\ell}}\)).  The constraint enforces exact preservation of hyperspherical energy, which, as proved in the paper, is equivalent to requiring that all pairwise angles among neurons remain unchanged after finetuning.

Because the HE constraint is satisfied automatically by any orthogonal matrix (the inner product between any two columns is preserved), (1) reduces to an unconstrained minimization over orthogonal matrices:
\[
     \min_{\{\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\}} 
        \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}\!\bigl(
            \{\;\mathbf{R}_{\ell}\mathbf{W}^{0}_{\ell}\;\}
        \bigr).
        \tag{2}
\]
Equation (2) is the **OFT optimization problem**: instead of directly updating each weight element, we learn *one* orthogonal transformation per layer that re‑orients the entire set of neurons while keeping their mutual geometry intact.

---

### Layer‑wise Orthogonal Transformation: \( \mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0} \)

For an individual layer, let us denote its pretrained weight matrix by \( \mathbf{W}^{0} \).  The OFT update replaces this matrix with
\[
    \boxed{
        \mathbf{W}
        = 
        \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},
    }
    \tag{3}
\]
with \( \mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf R = I_{d} \).  Substituting (3) into the forward computation yields
\[
    \mathbf z 
      = (\mathbf W^{0})^{\top} (\mathbf R^{\top}  \mathbf x)
      = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}  \!  \mathbf x,
      \qquad 
      (\forall\,  \textbf{x} ).
      \tag{4}
\]
Equation (4) shows that applying an orthogonal matrix to the pretrained weights is equivalent to rotating (or reflecting) the input representation before it reaches the original linear map.  Because rotations preserve Euclidean norms and inner products, any downstream activation statistics that rely on cosine similarity among neurons remain unchanged.  

In practice OFT is applied only to selected layers (e.g., attention projection matrices in a transformer) so that computational overhead stays modest while still providing sufficient expressive power for adaptation.

---

### Initialization (\( \mathbf R = I \)) and Gradient‑Based Learning of \( \mathbf R \)

#### Identity Initialization

At training start we set every orthogonal matrix to the identity:
\[
    \mathbf R^{(0)} = I_{d}.
    \tag{5}
\]
With (5) we recover exactly the pretrained model: \( \mathbf W^{(0)} = I\,\mathbf W^{0} =  \mathbf W^{0}.\)  This mirrors conventional fine‑tuning where parameters are initialized from their pretrained values and then nudged by gradient descent.

#### Parameterizing Orthogonal Matrices

Directly optimizing over orthogonal matrices under an explicit constraint is expensive.  
The paper adopts **Cayley parameterization**, which maps any skew‑symmetric matrix \( \mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^{\top} \) to an orthogonal matrix:
```math
    \displaystyle 
    \mathbf R(\mathbf Q)
        = (\,I + \mathbf Q\,)\,
          (\,I -  {\bf Q}\,)^{-1}.
    ``tag(6)
```
Because \( (\!I-\!Q)^{-1} = I + Q + Q^{2}+ ...,\) for small \(Q\) this transform remains numerically stable and automatically yields determinant \(=1.\)  
The trainable parameters are thus all entries of \(Q_{ij}, i<j.\)

To reduce memory when \(d$ is large, a **block‑diagonal** structure is imposed:
```math
   {\bf R}= {\rm diag}( {\bf R}_1,\dots , {\bf R}_r ),
   {\qquad}
   {\bf R}_k\,{\in }\,O(d/r).
   ````tag(7)
Each block can be independently parametrized via its own skew‑symmetric \(Q_k.\)  
Optionally, blocks may be shared across positions, further shrinking parameter count from
\( O(d^2)\) to \(O(d^2/r)\) or even \(O(d^2/r^2)\).

#### Gradient Flow

During back‑propagation we compute gradients of the downstream loss w.r.t. \( {\bf R}.\)  
Because \( {\bf R}=f({Q}) ,\: f^{-1}(·)\) involves matrix inversion, automatic differentiation libraries handle it efficiently.  
The resulting gradient w.r.t. \(Q_k,\;k=1\dots r,\) obeys:
```math
   {\partial L}/{\partial Q_k}
      = 
      J_f(Q_k)^{T}
      ({partial L}/{partial R_k}),
```
where \(J_f(Q_k)\) denotes Jacobian of Cayley transform at block \(k.\)  
After a gradient step we obtain an updated skew‑symmetric matrix; applying (6) again guarantees that each updated block remains in the orthogonal group.

#### Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (COFT)

A further refinement adds an explicit deviation bound:
```math
    ||\,{\bf R}-I||_F  ≤ ε .
```
Using first–order approximation of (6),
```math
    {\bf R} ≈ I + 2Q ,
```
the bound translates into a simple Frobenius norm constraint on each skew‑symmetric block,
```math
    || Q ||_F ≤ ε' .
```
During training one projects each updated \(Q_k←Q_k/\max(1,\;||Q_k||_F/ε') ,\
ensuring that (8) holds while still preserving exact orthogonality via Cayley reconstruction.

---

### Comparison with Standard Fine‑tuning and LoRA

| Aspect | Standard Full‑parameter Fine‑tuning | LoRA | Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT / COFT) |
|-------|-----------------------------------|------|--------------------------------------|
| **Objective** | Minimize downstream loss; implicit Euclidean regularization: \\( ||{\bf M}-{\bf M}^{0}||_{F}^{2}.\\) | Same loss + explicit low‑rank additive update: \\({{\bf M}}={\\bf M}^{0}+\\Delta\\), \\(\\textup{rank}(\\Delta)=r'.\\) | Same loss + *exact* hyperspherical energy preservation: \\({\\bf M}= {\\bf R}{\\bf M}^{0}\\), \\({\\bf R}^{\\top}{\\bf R}=I\\). |
| **Parameterization** | All entries of each layer are trainable. | Two low‑rank matrices \\(A,B\\); total trainable params ≈ \\(r'(d+n).\\) | One orthogonal matrix per layer; with block diagonal → trainable params ≈ \\(d(d/r-1)/2\\). |
| **Geometric effect** | Arbitrary movement in Euclidean space; pairwise angles among neurons may change arbitrarily → risk of destroying semantic structure. | Additive low‑rank shift; angles can still change widely depending on rank and magnitude of \\(A B^{\\top}.\\ ) | Pure rotation/reflection of *all* neuron vectors in a layer → pairwise cosine similarities stay exactly unchanged. |
| **Stability** | Sensitive to learning rate and number of epochs; may cause collapse or overfitting. | More stable than vanilla fine‑tuning due to low rank but still can drift if rank too high or learning rate large. | Provably stable w.r.t. semantic structure because hyperspherical energy is invariant; COFT adds explicit radius bound for extra safety. |
| **Inference cost** | No extra cost beyond original model. | No extra cost after merging low-rank adapters into weight matrix. | Identical inference cost: after training we substitute \\({\\bf W}= {\\bf R}{\\bf W}^{0}\\); no additional modules required. |

Thus OFT differs fundamentally from LoRA: LoRA limits *how much* new information can be injected via a low–rank additive term, whereas OFT limits *how* new information can be injected by constraining it to an orthogonal rotation of existing weights. Both approaches reduce parameter count relative to full fine‑tuning, but OFT does so while guaranteeing preservation of pairwise neuron angles—a property directly linked in the paper to retention of pretraining generative ability.

---

### Summary

We have derived the OFT formulation from first principles: starting with hyperspherical energy as a faithful descriptor of semantic geometry, we turned its invariance under orthogonal transformations into a concrete finetuning constraint.  The resulting algorithm replaces conventional weight updates by learning per‑layer orthogonal matrices via efficient Cayley parameterization and optional block diagonal sparsification.  Initialization at identity guarantees zero deviation at epoch zero, while gradient descent on skew‑symmetric parameters respects strict orthogonality throughout training.  

Compared with vanilla fine‑tuning and LoRA, OFT offers a *structural* regularizer—preservation of pairwise neuron angles—rather than merely penalizing Euclidean distance or limiting rank. This leads to more stable optimization, faster convergence, and better preservation of pretrained generative quality, as demonstrated empirically throughout the paper.

# Efficient Parameterization of Orthogonal Matrices

In many modern deep‑learning pipelines, especially those that fine‑tune large pre‑trained models, it becomes desirable to restrict a set of learnable parameters to the orthogonal group.  Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles, which in turn guarantees that the *hyperspherical energy* of a weight matrix – a measure of the pairwise angular relationships among its neurons – remains unchanged.  This property is at the core of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) and its constrained variant (COFT).  However, naïvely storing a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix requires \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) parameters and entails expensive operations such as matrix inversion.  This chapter explains two practical strategies that make orthogonal parameterization tractable for high‑dimensional layers:

1. **The Cayley transform**, which builds an orthogonal matrix from a skew‑symmetric generator.
2. **Block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices**, which dramatically cut the parameter budget while retaining the essential geometric invariance.

Both techniques are rooted in well‑established linear‑algebraic facts, yet their careful application is crucial for scaling OFT to modern diffusion models whose hidden dimensions \(d\) can easily reach several hundreds or even thousands.

---

## The Cayley Transform: From Skew‑Symmetry to Orthogonality

An orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}\).  Directly enforcing this constraint during gradient‑based optimisation is cumbersome because it couples all entries of \(\mathbf{R}\).  A classic workaround is to *parameterize* \(\mathbf{R}\) through an auxiliary matrix that automatically satisfies the orthogonality condition.  The **Cayley transform** provides exactly such a mapping.

Let \(\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) be a *skew‑symmetric* matrix, meaning \(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\).  The Cayley transform defines

\[
\boxed{\;\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})\,(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\;}
\tag{1}
\]

where \(\mathbf{I}\) denotes the identity matrix.  Because \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric, it holds that \((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top} = \mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\).  Multiplying the transpose of \(\mathbf{R}\) by \(\mathbf{R}\) yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{R}^{\top}\!\mathbf{R}
&= \big[(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\big]^{\!\top} (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})^{\top} (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\\
&= (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1} (\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\\
&= \mathbf{I},
\end{aligned}
\]

proving that any \(\mathbf{R}\) generated by (1) is orthogonal.  Consequently, optimisation can be performed on the *unconstrained* entries of \(\mathbf{Q}\); after each gradient step we rebuild \(\mathbf{R}\) via (1), automatically satisfying the orthogonality requirement.

### Parameter Count and Skew‑Symmetry

A generic \(d\times d\) matrix contains \(d^{2}\) real numbers.  Skew‑symmetry removes half of these degrees of freedom because the diagonal must be zero and each off‑diagonal entry determines its counterpart: for every pair \((i,j)\) with \(i<j\), we store only one value \(q_{ij}\), and set \(q_{ji}=-q_{ij}\).  Thus the number of independent parameters in \(\mathbf{Q}\) – and consequently in the whole transform – is

\[
N_{\text{sparse}} = \frac{d(d-1)}2 .
\tag{2}
\]

Although this reduces the raw count relative to a dense orthogonal matrix (\(d^{2}\) entries), the cost remains quadratic in \(d\).  For layers where \(d=1024\), (2) still yields more than half a million trainable scalars, which can be prohibitive when many layers are fine‑tuned simultaneously.

### Expressivity: Only Special Orthogonal Transformations

The Cayley transform cannot produce every orthogonal matrix; it only covers those with determinant \(+1\), i.e., matrices belonging to the **special orthogonal group** \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\).  Matrices with determinant \(-1\) represent reflections that are not reachable via (1).  In practice this limitation rarely harms performance:

- Most useful rotations for fine‑tuning are continuous deformations of identity, which naturally have determinant \(+1\).
- Empirically, experiments reported in the OFT literature show no degradation when restricting to \(\mathrm{SO}(d)\); learning a pure reflection rarely offers an advantage for preserving hyperspherical energy.

If a task truly requires a reflection, one can prepend a fixed sign‑flip (a diagonal matrix with entries \(\pm1\)) before applying the Cayley transform, thereby recovering full orthogonal expressivity without altering the learning pipeline.

### Approximate Implementation via Neumann Series

Computing \((\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}\) exactly incurs an explicit matrix inversion, which can be expensive for large \(d\).  When training steps keep \(\|\mathbf Q\|\) small (a typical situation because we start from \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf 0\)), we may use a **Neumann series** expansion:

\[
(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}
= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \!\! \mathbf Q^{k}
\simeq \mathbf I + \mathbf Q + \mathcal O(\|\mathbf Q\|^{2}),
\tag {3}
\]

which leads to an inexpensive first‑order approximation:

\[
\boxed{\;\displaystyle 
   \mathbf R \approx (\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\,\mathbf I+\! \mathbf Q\,)
   = \,\mathbf I + 2\,\!\mathbf Q + O(\|\!\bf Q|^{2})
   .}
   }
   \tag {4}
   }

This linearisation is sufficient for the **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning** (COFT), where an additional radius constraint forces $\|\!\bf R-\bf I||$ to stay small; thus higher‑order terms become negligible.  The approximation also enables a straightforward projection step onto the feasible set $\|\!\bf Q||_F^2\,{\leq}{\,{\epsilon'}^2}$ during gradient updates.

---

## Block‑Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices: Reducing Quadratic Complexity

Even with the Cayley parametrization, storing all $\frac12 d(d-1)$ skew components can overwhelm memory when many layers participate in OFT.  A pragmatic compromise is to **restrict** each orthogonal matrix to be block diagonal:

\[
\boxed{\;
   \mathbf R = 
      {\rm diag}\bigl(
        \underbrace{\!\!\;\!{\bf R}_{1}}_{\frac d r\times\nolimits\tfrac d r},
        {\bf R}_{2},
        {\dots},
        {\bf R}_{r}
      )
    ,\
    {\bf R}_{i}^\top {\bf R}_{i}= {\bf I}_{\,d/r}.
    }
    \tag {5}
    }
   ```

Here we split the original dimension $d$ into $r$ equal blocks; each block $\bf R_i$ acts independently on a subset of neurons.  The number of learnable parameters now scales with $r$:

- Each block $\bf R_i$ is itself an orthogonal matrix of size $\tfrac d r$, thus needs $\frac12 (\tfrac d r)(\,{\tfrac d r}-1)$ independent skew entries.
- Summing over $r$ blocks yields

\[
N_{\text{block}}  
= r\,\,\,   \frac12 
          \Bigl(
               {\frac d r}
               ({\,{\frac d r}-1})
           \Bigr)
 =   \frac {d(d/r-1)} {2}.
       \tag {6}
   }
   }

When $r=1$ we recover the full‐matrix case; as $r$ grows,
the parameter count drops roughly by a factor $r$.

### Optional Block Sharing

Further reduction is possible if **all blocks share the same parameters**, i.e., ${\bf R}_i={\bf R}_j$ for any $i,j$.  In this scenario only one unique block needs to be stored, giving

\[
N_{\text{shared}} 
=    \frac12 
     {\bigl({d}/{r}\bigr)}
     ({\,{\! d}/{r}-1})
       .
    =    {\cal O}(d^{2}/r^{2}) .
    \tag {7}
   }
   }

Intuitively, shared blocks amount to applying an identical rotation within each channel group—a form reminiscent of depthwise convolutions common in modern vision architectures.

### Handling Convolutional Layers

When OFT is applied to convolutional kernels, each input channel can be mapped to one block.  
If we set $r$ equal to the number of input channels,
each ${\bf R}_i$ transforms precisely one channel’s weight vector.
Thus block‑diagonal OFT in this setting behaves like learning *orthogonal depthwise filters*, preserving hyperspherical energy channel by channel while keeping memory overhead minimal.

---

## Parameter Efficiency Compared with LoRA

Low‑Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has become a popular baseline for parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning.  
LoRA introduces two low‑rank matrices $\bf A$ and $\bf B$ (with rank $r'$), updating a weight $\bf W^0$ as

\[
{\bf W}= {\bf W}^{0}+{\bf A}{\,}{\bf B}.
    % note: shapes: A∈ℝ^{d×r'}, B∈ℝ^{r'×n}
     .
     }
   }

The trainable parameter count equals  

\(N_{\text {LoRA}} = r'(d+n)\).

Below we illustrate concrete numbers for a typical fully connected layer in diffusion models:

| Dimension                | Full weight size                               | LoRA ($r'=8$) params | OFT ($r=8$, full blocks) params |
|--------------------------|------------------------------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------------|
| $d=n=128$                | $128\times128 =16,\!384$                       | $8(128+128)=2,\!048$ | $\displaystyle
      N_{\text {block}}=
      \frac {128(128/8-1)}2
      =960$
|
| $d=n=512$                | $262,\!144$                                    | $8(512+512)=8,\!192$ | $\displaystyle
      N_{\text {block}}=
      {\frac {512(512/8-1)}2}=15,\!360$
|

Even though OFT’s raw count can exceed LoRA’s when $d$ grows very large (because each block still contains quadratic terms inside its subspace), note two important points:

1. **Effective degrees of freedom** – OFT manipulates *directions* of neurons rather than adding arbitrary low‑rank perturbations; this often translates into better preservation of pretrained semantics.
2. **Scalable sharing** – By sharing blocks across channels ($N_{\text {shared}}=\mathcal O(d^{2}/r^{2})$), OFT can easily drop below LoRA’s budget while retaining the key geometric invariance.

Thus OFT provides a continuum between full orthogonal fine‑tuning and extreme sparsity, allowing practitioners to trade off memory against flexibility according to their hardware constraints.

## Practical Recommendations for Large Diffusion Models

When integrating these parameterizations into real diffusion pipelines (e.g., Stable Diffusion’s UNet backbone), consider the following workflow:

1. **Select Layer Granularity**  
   OFT works best when applied uniformly across all linear / attention projection matrices in a given stage.  
   For attention heads whose projection dimension is relatively small (e.g., $64$), use a full Cayley parameterization; for larger feed‑forward dimensions ($>256$), switch to block diagonal with $r=4$ or $8$, optionally sharing blocks across heads.

2. **Initialize at Identity**  
   Initialize all skew blocks $\bf Q_i=0$, guaranteeing $\bf R_i=\bf I$.  
   This aligns perfectly with DreamBooth/ControlNet setups where finetuning should start from the pre‑trained weights.

3. **Enforce Orthogonality Efficiently**  
   - Use automatic differentiation through PyTorch’s `torch.linalg.solve` or `torch.inverse` for $(\!\bf I-\!\bf Q)$ when dimensions are modest (< 256).  
   - For larger blocks employ iterative solvers or first-order approximations from (4); combine them with a simple Frobenius norm projection onto $\|\!\bf Q||_F^2{\le}{\,{\epsilon'}^2}$ if COFT constraints are needed.

4. **Monitor Hyperspherical Energy**  
   Although OFT theoretically preserves hyperspherical energy exactly when $\bf R=\exp({\,^\wedge})$, numerical errors may accumulate.  
   Periodically compute 
   \(HE({\bf W}) = 
        {\sum_{i<j}}\bigl
           /
           {\|\hat{{w}}_i - 
            \hat{{w}}_j\bigr|}
          ^{-1}
       ,    
     }\)
   where $\hat{{w}}_i={w}_i/\|w_i\\|$, ensuring it stays within machine tolerance of its pretrained value.

5. **Combine with Scaling (Optional)**  
   If additional flexibility is required—e.g., when only angular changes are insufficient—augment each neuron with an independent scalar factor as described in “Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning”. This incurs only $n$ extra parameters and does not affect hyperspherical energy because scaling occurs after normalisation.

By adhering to these guidelines, researchers can harness orthogonal transformations at scale without incurring prohibitive computational overhead, thereby preserving semantic fidelity while steering large text‑to‑image diffusion models toward new downstream tasks.

**Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (COFT)**  

---

In this chapter we develop the constrained variant of Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT), namely **Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (COFT)**.  COFT augments the basic orthogonal transformation with an explicit *radius* constraint that forces the learned orthogonal matrix to stay within an ε‑ball around the identity.  The addition of this constraint improves the stability of the fine‑tuning process while preserving the desirable property that the hyperspherical energy of each layer remains unchanged.  We begin by motivating why an ε‑ball is useful, then formalize the “R – I” norm constraint, describe how it can be enforced efficiently using a projected gradient step on the underlying skew‑symmetric matrix Q after a Cayley transform, and finally discuss how the size of ε governs a trade‑off between flexibility and preservation of the pre‑trained model.  Throughout we compare COFT to its predecessor OFT and to conventional fine‑tuning with a small learning rate.

---

### 1. Why Impose an ε‑Ball Around the Identity?

Fine‑tuning a massive diffusion model inevitably risks drifting away from the well‑calibrated representation discovered during pre‑training.  Classical fine‑tuning mitigates this drift by employing a *tiny* learning rate; mathematically one hopes that the Euclidean distance  

\[
\| \mathbf{W} - \mathbf{W}^{0} \|_{F}
\]

between the adapted weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) and its pre‑trained counterpart \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) stays small.  However, a small Euclidean distance alone does not guarantee that the *geometric* relationships among neurons—captured by pairwise angles on the unit hypersphere—remain intact.  OFT solves this issue by restricting updates to an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R}\), i.e.

\[
\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0},\qquad \mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}.
\]

Because any orthogonal transformation preserves all inner products (and thus all cosine similarities) among columns of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\), OFT exactly preserves hyperspherical energy:

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}^{0}).
\]

Nevertheless, orthogonal matrices form a large, non‑compact manifold (the orthogonal group \(O(d)\)).  Even though all members preserve pairwise angles, some may rotate neurons drastically, leading to *semantic* changes that are undesirable when only a modest adaptation is required (e.g., learning a few subject images or a weak control signal).  Empirically this manifests as occasional instability or slower convergence: gradients may push \(\mathbf{R}\) toward rotations that are far from the identity, producing weight matrices that, while still orthogonal, differ substantially from their pre‑trained initialization.

To curtail such excessive rotations we deliberately restrict \(\mathbf{R}\) to lie inside an ε‑ball centred at the identity:

\[
\| \mathbf{R} - \mathbf{I} \|_{F} \leq \varepsilon.
\]

Geometrically this enforces that each learned transformation be a *small* rotation (or reflection).  The bound directly mirrors what a tiny learning rate achieves in standard gradient descent: it limits how far each parameter can move from its start point.  Crucially, because we apply the bound **after** insisting on orthogonality, COFT never sacrifices hyperspherical energy preservation—we merely narrow the admissible sub‑manifold to those orthogonal matrices close to \(\mathbf{I}\).

---

### 2. Formalizing the “\( \mathbf R - \mathbf I\)” Norm Constraint

Let a particular layer of the diffusion model have pre‑trained weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W^{0} \in \mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]

with columns \(\{\mathbf w^{0}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\).  In COFT we parametrize the adapted weight matrix as  

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
    \mathbf W = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\top}, 
}
\qquad
\text{s.t. }\;
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf R^{\top}\!\!\,\mathbf R = \mathbf I ,\\[4pt]
        \bigl\|\mathbf R - \mathbf I\bigr\|_{F} \leq \varepsilon .
    \end{cases}
\tag{1}
\]

The first line enforces orthogonality; the second line is an explicit radius (ε) constraint.  
The Frobenius norm is used because it aggregates rotation magnitude across all dimensions and is easy to compute and differentiate.

A useful perspective is to rewrite (1) in terms of a *skew‑symmetric* generator matrix \(\mathbf Q = -\mathbf Q^{\top}\).  The Cayley transform provides an analytically tractable map from any such generator to an orthogonal matrix with determinant + 1:

\[
\boxed{\displaystyle
    \mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)\bigl( \mathbf I - \mathbf Q\bigr)^{-1}.
}
\tag{2}
\]

When \(\|\mathbf Q\|\) is small, expanding Eq.(2) into its Neumann series yields  

\[
\mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)\!\Bigl[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (\mathbf Q)^{k}\Bigr]
          = \mathbf I + 2\,\mathbf Q + O(\|\!\mathbf Q\!\|^{2}),
\tag{3}
\]

demonstrating that for modest values of ε, constraining $\|\!\,\mathbf R-\!\,\mathbf I\,\|_{F}$ is essentially equivalent to limiting $\|\!\,\mathbf Q\,\|_{F}$.

Thus we replace the original ball constraint on $\mathbf R$ by an analogous ball on $\mathbf Q$:

\[
    \bigl\|\!\,\bigl( (\!-\!\,\! )? )\,\,?\\

Actually let's correct: constrain Q.

We impose  

\[
    \|\,\mathbf Q\,\|_{F} \leqslant {\varepsilon}',
    \qquad {\varepsilon}' = f(\varepsilon)
    .
    % omitted precise mapping
    .
    
This reformulation permits a simple **projected gradient descent** step: after each gradient update on $\mathbf Q$, we project back onto the Frobenius‑norm ball of radius ${\varepsilon}'$:

```python
Q ← Q - η ∇_Q L
if ||Q||_F > ε′:
    Q ← ε′ * Q / ||Q||_F
```

Because $Q$ remains skew‑symmetric throughout training (we enforce $Q←(Q−Q^\top)/2$ after each projection), Eq.(2) always yields a valid orthogonal matrix satisfying both constraints.

---

### 3. Implementing the Constraint Efficiently

Directly forming $(\!\!-\!\!	)$ leads us through these steps for each layer:

1. **Parameterisation of $Q$**  
   Store only independent entries of $Q$ (i.e., upper‑triangular part).  
   After each optimizer step, enforce skew symmetry:  
   \(Q ← (Q - Q^{⊤})/2.\)

2. **Projection onto an ε′‑ball**  
   Compute \(s = ||Q||_F.\)  
   If \(s > ε′,\) rescale: \(Q ← ε′\,Q/s.\)

3. **Construction of $R$ via Cayley**  
   Compute \(A = I+Q,\; B = I-Q.\)  
   Solve \(B^{-1}A.\) Since $B$ is close to identity for small $ε′$, solving by a few iterations of conjugate gradients or using a truncated Neumann series  
   \(B^{-1} ≈ I + Q + Q^{2}+⋯,\)  
   provides an accurate and cheap approximation.

4. **Forward pass**  
   Replace every weight matrix $\mathbf W^{0}$ with $\widetilde{\!{\bf W}} = (\!R\,W^{0})^{⊤}$ in the diffusion network; no extra runtime overhead appears at inference because we can pre‑multiply $R$ into $W^{0}$ once training finishes.

If memory is at premium one may employ block–diagonal parametrisation for $R$, as described for OFT: each block undergoes its own Cayley transform but shares a common ε′ value, further reducing parameter count while keeping all constraints valid.

---

### 4. The Role of ε: Balancing Flexibility and Preservation

The hyperparameter ε controls how far $\mathbf R$ may deviate from identity:

* **Very small ε** (e.g., \(10^{-3}\)):  
  - $\|R-I‖_F$ stays tiny; updates resemble classic fine‑tuning with a small learning rate.  
  - The model changes hardly at all; hyperspherical energy stays identical and semantic knowledge is maximally preserved, but only modest adaptation can be achieved—sometimes insufficient for tasks like subject insertion or edge‐to‐image translation.

* **Moderate ε** (e.g., \(10^{-1}\)):  
  - Allows appreciable rotations while still limiting them; fine‐tunes enough to embed new semantics yet retains most pre‑trained structure.  
  - Empirically this regime yields rapid convergence: training curves show smooth monotonic improvement without sudden jumps.

* **Large ε** (approaching values that make $R$ approach any element of $O(d)$):  
  - The radius constraint becomes ineffective; COFT collapses to ordinary OFT, regaining full rotational freedom but losing much of its stabilising advantage.

A convenient way to visualise this trade‑off is through **illustrative plots**:

1. **Training loss vs epoch** for three values of ε on a subject‐driven task shows early rapid decline for moderate ε, slower but steady descent for very small ε, and erratic spikes when ε is large (mirroring OFT’s occasional instability).

2. **Hyperspherical energy difference** remains zero across all ε because orthogonality guarantees invariance; however, when measuring **semantic similarity** between generated images and reference subjects (via CLIP or DINO), moderate ε attains higher scores than both extremes—underscoring that some angular movement is necessary for meaningful adaptation.

These plots confirm that COFT offers a *continuum*: ranging from strict preservation (tiny ε) to full flexibility (large ε), with an optimal middle ground delivering both stability and strong downstream performance.

---

### 5. Relationship Between COFT, OFT, and Conventional Small‐Learning‐Rate Fine‑tuning

| Aspect | Conventional Small LR Fine‑tuning | OFT | COFT |
|--------|----------------------------------|-----|------|
| **Update Form** | Additive: \(W = W^{0}+ΔW,\; ΔW≈0.\) | Multiplicative orthogonal: \(W = R\,W^{0}, R∈O(d).\) | Same as OFT **plus** radius bound on \(R.\) |
| **Preservation Metric** | Implicit Euclidean distance ‖ΔW‖ small → low distortion but no guarantee on pairwise angles. | Exact preservation of hyperspherical energy via orthogonality. | Same energy preservation **and** explicit control over ‖R−I‖. |
| **Stability** | Relies heavily on LR schedule; can still diverge if loss landscape sharp. | Generally stable because all updates stay on manifold O(d); however large rotations may still cause semantic drift. | Most stable: manifold + ball constraint eliminates large rotations while retaining O(d) structure. |
| **Expressivity** | Limited by linear additive changes; often needs many epochs or higher LR → risk of collapse. | High expressivity because any rotation/reflection is allowed; may overshoot for subtle tasks. | Controlled expressivity: enough freedom to encode new semantics yet bounded to avoid destroying pretraining knowledge. |
| **Parameter Efficiency** | Updates entire weight tensor (full rank). | Only need to store orthogonal matrix per layer (often reduced via block diagonal). | Same as OFT; extra scalar ε does not affect parameter count. |

In practice COFT inherits OFT’s crucial property—preserving pairwise neuron similarity—while reintroducing an explicit *proximity* term reminiscent of conventional fine ­tuning’s small step size. This synergy explains why COFT often converges faster than either baseline: gradients immediately project onto a compact region where curvature is smoother, leading to more predictable optimisation dynamics without sacrificing expressive capacity.

--- 

### 6. Summary

Constrained Orthogonal Fine‑tuning augments Orthogonal Fine‑tuning with a simple yet powerful norm bound around the identity matrix:

* By enforcing both orthogonality and an ε‑ball constraint we guarantee exact hyperspherical energy preservation while simultaneously preventing excessive rotations that could destabilise training.
* The constraint is efficiently realised through projected gradient descent on the skew‑symmetric generator \(Q\), thanks to the differentiable Cayley transform.
* The hyperparameter ε provides an intuitive dial between pure preservation (tiny ε), flexible adaptation (moderate ε), and unrestricted rotation (large ε → OFT).
* Empirically COFT delivers smoother loss curves, faster convergence, and higher downstream quality than both vanilla OFT and classical fine‑tuning with tiny learning rates.

Consequently, COFT constitutes a principled, parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning strategy for large text‑to‑image diffusion models, especially when preserving semantic fidelity while incorporating new controllability signals is essential.
