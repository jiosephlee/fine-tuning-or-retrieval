# Introduction to Efficient Finetuning of Large Language Models

Finetuning large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a central technique for adapting a pretrained foundation model to new tasks, improving performance on specialized domains, and altering undesirable behaviors. The promise of finetuning is exemplified in a wide range of recent works that demonstrate gains on instruction following, few‑shot learning, and domain‑specific adaptation. Yet, the practical deployment of these gains is often thwarted by the extreme memory demands of standard finetuning pipelines. In this chapter we explore the motivations behind LLM finetuning, the memory bottlenecks that arise when using conventional 16‑bit training, why earlier quantization approaches have failed to support training, and finally we introduce the high‑level design goals of **QLoRA**, an approach that makes it possible to finetune a 65 B‑parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU without sacrificing task performance.

---

## Motivation for Finetuning LLMs

Large pretrained language models acquire a broad base of linguistic knowledge through massive unsupervised pretraining on internet‑scale text. Although this pretraining yields impressive zero‑shot abilities, downstream applications typically require more precise behavior:

* **Task‑specific performance** – Benchmarks such as GLUE, MMLU, or Vicuna show that modest finetuning can lift accuracy or conversational quality far beyond what the raw pretrained model achieves.
* **Instruction following** – By exposing the model to pairs of instructions and appropriate responses, the model learns to obey natural‑language prompts, a capability essential for chatbots and virtual assistants.
* **Safety and alignment** – Finetuning enables the removal or reduction of harmful outputs (e.g., toxic language), as demonstrated in works that alter undesirable behaviors through supervised or reinforcement learning.
* **Domain adaptation** – Specialized corpora (medical notes, legal documents, code) can be injected via finetuning so that a general LLM becomes useful in niche settings.

Because these benefits arise from updating only a relatively small subset of parameters while keeping the bulk of the pretrained weights fixed, parameter‑efficient methods such as Low‑rank Adapters (LoRA) have become popular. Nonetheless, even with LoRA the dominant memory consumption stems from *activations* and *optimizer states*, not from the adapter weights themselves. Consequently, scaling finetuning to the largest publicly released models remains prohibitive without new efficiency tricks.

---

## Memory Bottlenecks of Standard 16‑bit Finetuning

When training in full 16‑bit precision (typically BF16 or FP16), each parameter occupies two bytes. For a transformer with \(N\) parameters, the storage cost is simply \(2N\) bytes. However, during training additional tensors are needed:

| Tensor type | Typical size (per parameter) | Example for LLaMA‑65B |
|-------------|------------------------------|-----------------------|
| Model weights (FP16) | 2 bytes | ~130 GB |
| Activation gradients (during backprop) | 2 bytes | > 600 GB |
| Optimizer states (e.g., Adam moments) | 4 bytes (momentum & variance) | > 1 TB |
| LoRA adapters | negligible (≈ 0.2 % of total) | ~25–30 MB |

Even after applying gradient checkpointing—where intermediate activations are recomputed rather than stored—the activation footprint remains on the order of hundreds of gigabytes for long sequence lengths. The result is that finetuning a 65 B‑parameter LLaMA model in naïve 16‑bit mode requires **more than 780 GB of GPU memory**, far beyond any single modern accelerator.

These memory constraints manifest in three practical problems:

1. **Hardware accessibility** – Only large research labs with multi‑GPU clusters can attempt full‑model finetuning at this scale.
2. **Experimentation cost** – The need for tens or hundreds of GPU hours escalates financial barriers.
3. **Environmental impact** – Excessive hardware usage translates into higher energy consumption.

Thus, any method that can dramatically shrink the memory footprint while preserving predictive quality directly addresses a critical bottleneck in LLM research and deployment.

---

## Limitations of Existing Quantization Methods for Training

Quantization reduces the number of bits required to store each weight by mapping high‑precision floating‑point values to low‑precision representations (e.g., 8‑bit integers). Recent advances have shown that **inference** with 4‑bit or even 3‑bit representations can be performed with modest accuracy loss using techniques such as GPTQ, SmoothQuant, or rounding optimizations. However, when one attempts to **train** through quantized weights several fundamental obstacles appear:

1. **Static quantization constants** – Traditional blockwise k‑bit quantization stores a per‑block scaling factor \(c\). During backpropagation gradients must be computed with respect to these constants; if they are kept frozen, gradient flow stalls.
2. **Outlier sensitivity** – Quantizing an entire tensor based on its global maximum (“absmax”) causes most bins to be underutilized when outliers are present. Blockwise quantization mitigates this but introduces many per‑block constants that inflate memory usage.
3. **Quantization error accumulation** – During training each weight update is applied to a dequantized version of the parameter; repeated rounding errors quickly degrade model quality unless the quantizer is near lossless.
4. **Lack of differentiable kernels** – Efficient GPU kernels that support backpropagation through low‑bit arithmetic are historically missing; most libraries only provide inference‐only paths.

Because of these issues earlier works reported severe performance degradation when attempting to train directly on quantized models: backpropagation either diverged or yielded final accuracies far below their full‑precision counterparts. Consequently, while quantization turns inference into a cheap operation, it did not unlock affordable training for giant LLMs—until the innovations introduced by QLoRA.

---

## Overview of QLoRA and Its Goals

**QLoRA** (Quantized LoRA) unifies three complementary ideas to enable high‑fidelity fine‑tuning under strict memory budgets:

1. **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization** – A data type specifically crafted for normally distributed weight tensors. By aligning the distribution of pretrained weights with an information‑theoretically optimal quantile scheme, NF4 assigns an equal expected number of values to each bin and preserves zero exactly. This yields lower quantization error than generic 4‑bit floats (FP4) or ints (Int4).
2. **Double Quantization** – The per‑block scaling factors themselves are quantized a second time using an 8‑bit float format (FP8). This reduces the overhead contributed by scaling constants from about \(0.5\) bits per parameter to roughly \(0.13\) bits per parameter—a savings of nearly \(3\) GB for a 65 B model.
3. **Paged Optimizers** – By storing optimizer states in NVIDIA’s unified memory (a CPU–GPU paging system), QLoRA avoids out‑of‑memory spikes that arise when processing long sequences with gradient checkpointing. When GPU memory saturates, pages are automatically evicted to host RAM and fetched back as needed, preserving training speed while keeping peak GPU usage low.

Combining these techniques yields an overall workflow:

* The pretrained base model is frozen and stored on disk in NF4 format together with its double‑quantized scaling constants.
* During each forward pass a block is dequantized on-the-fly to BF16 (the computation datatype), multiplied with the input activation matrix, and immediately re‑quantized after use—so only transient BF16 buffers occupy GPU RAM.
* LoRA adapters—parameter‐efficient low‐rank matrices—are inserted at *every* linear layer; they remain in full BF16 precision throughout training and receive gradients via standard backpropagation.
* Optimizer states for the adapters are placed in paged unified memory; only when an update step runs are they migrated back onto the GPU.

The design goals embodied by QLoRA are therefore:

* **Memory reduction:** Bring average GPU memory consumption for finetuning from > 780 GB down to < 48 GB for a 65 B model—a reduction by more than an order of magnitude.
* **No performance loss:** Empirically match—or even slightly exceed—the task accuracy achieved by conventional 16‑bit full finetuning across GLUE, Super-NaturalInstructions, MMLU, and Vicuna benchmarks.
* **Scalability:** Enable researchers with a single consumer or professional GPU (24–48 GB) to train models ranging from 7 B up to 65 B parameters.
* **Ease of integration:** Provide CUDA kernels and Hugging Face Transformers wrappers so that QLoRA fits naturally into existing training pipelines.

By achieving these objectives, QLoRA shifts LLM finetuning from an exclusive operation requiring large clusters to an accessible technique available to most academic labs and even individual developers. The remainder of this textbook will detail each component—NF4 quantization, double quantization, paged optimizers—and expose how they interact mathematically with LoRA adapters during forward and backward passes. Understanding these mechanisms equips students not only to reproduce QLoRA results but also to critically evaluate future innovations in efficient large‐scale model adaptation.

# Background Concepts

Understanding the methodology introduced in **QLoRA** requires a solid grasp of several fundamental techniques that underlie modern large‑language‑model (LLM) finetuning. This chapter develops the intuition and formalism for (i) quantization of neural‑network weights, (ii) block‑wise *k*-bit quantization and its handling of outliers, (iii) the Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) formulation, and (iv) the memory profile of parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods. We also discuss why applying LoRA alone does not suffice to overcome the memory constraints that arise when finetuning very large models.

---

## Quantization: Definitions, Scaling, and Dequantization  

Quantization is the process of mapping a high‑precision tensor—typically stored in 32‑bit floating point (FP32) or 16‑bit Brain Float (BF16)—to a representation that uses fewer bits per element. The primary motivation is to reduce the memory footprint and bandwidth requirements of a model without drastically sacrificing its predictive ability.

Consider a real‑valued weight tensor \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}} \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times h}\). To convert this tensor to an 8‑bit integer format we first compute a **quantization constant** (or scale) \(c^{\text{FP32}}\) that rescales the tensor so that its maximal absolute value fits the target integer range \([-\!127, 127]\). Formally,

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{Int8}} = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl( c^{\text{FP32}} \cdot \mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}} \Bigr),
\qquad
c^{\text{FP32}} = \frac{127}{\operatorname{absmax}\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}}\bigr)}.
\]

The operation “round” maps each rescaled floating‑point entry to the nearest integer representable in Int8. The inverse operation—**dequantization**—reconstructs an approximation of the original tensor by dividing by the same constant:

\[
\operatorname{dequant}\!\bigl(c^{\text{FP32}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{Int8}}\bigr)
   = \frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{Int8}}}{c^{\text{FP32}}}
   \approx \mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}} .
\]

In this simple scheme all elements share a single scale factor. While easy to implement, it suffers from a well‑known **outlier problem**: if one or a few entries have unusually large magnitude, the shared scale forces most entries into a narrow band of integer values, dramatically reducing effective precision.

---

## Block‑wise *k*-bit Quantization and Outlier Handling  

To mitigate the outlier issue, modern LLM quantization pipelines adopt **block‑wise quantization**. The idea is to partition the flattened weight tensor into contiguous blocks, each quantized independently with its own scale factor. Let the flattened weight vector be \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{b h}\) and let each block contain \(B\) elements. The number of blocks is then

\[
n = \frac{b h}{B}.
\]

For each block \(i \in \{1,\dots,n\}\), we compute an individual scale \(c_i^{\text{FP32}}\) using the same absolute‑maximum rule restricted to that block:

\[
c_i^{\text{FP32}} = \frac{s_{\max}}{\operatorname{absmax}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{i}\bigr)},
\]

where \(s_{\max}\) denotes the maximal representable integer for the chosen *k*-bit data type (e.g., \(s_{\max}=7\) for signed 3‑bit integers). The block is then quantized:

\[
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\,k{\rm -bit}} = 
    \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(c_i^{\text{FP32}}\cdot \mathbf{x}_i\bigr).
\]

During inference we dequantize each block separately:

\[
\operatorname{dequant}\bigl(c_i^{\text{FP32}},\,\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\,k{\rm -bit}}\bigr)
    = \frac{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\,k{\rm -bit}}}{c_i^{\text{FP32}}}.
\]

Because each block uses its own scale, even a single outlier influences only its own block’s precision; all other blocks retain fine granularity. Consequently, block‑wise quantization preserves more information per parameter than a global scale while keeping memory overhead modest: we must store an extra scale per block, which typically costs only a few bits per parameter.

In practice QLoRA adopts a block size of 64 for 4‑bit weights; this strikes a balance between quantization fidelity (smaller blocks → finer scales) and auxiliary memory consumption (more scales). The authors demonstrate that such fine granularity enables faithful backpropagation through quantized weights—a prerequisite for training adapters on top of frozen low‑precision models.

---

## Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) Formulation  

Parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) seeks to adapt a large pretrained model while updating only a tiny fraction of its parameters. **LoRA** achieves this by augmenting each linear transformation with an additional low‑rank term.

Assume a standard linear layer computes

\[
\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W},
\qquad
\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{b\times h},\
\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times o},
\]

where \(b,h,o\) denote batch size, input dimension, and output dimension respectively. LoRA introduces two trainable matrices,
\(\mathbf{L}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) and 
\(\mathbf{L}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\), where \(r \ll h,o)\ is a small rank hyperparameter (often called *r*). The forward pass becomes

\[
\boxed{
   \mathbf{Y}
   = 
   \underbrace{\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}}_{\substack{\text{frozen}\\
   \text{{pretrained weights}}}}
   + 
   s\,\,\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{L}_1\bigr)\,\mathbf{L}_2}_{%
   \substack{\text{{adapter output}}}}
}
,
\tag{1}
\]

with scalar scaling factor \(s\). Crucially, during training gradients flow **through** the frozen weight matrix \(\mathbf W\) to update only the adapter parameters \(\mathbf L_1,\mathbf L_2.\) This yields two major practical benefits:

1. **Memory savings:** The adapter matrices contain far fewer parameters than full model weights—typically on the order of 0.1–0.5 % of total parameters.
2. **Computational efficiency:** Since forward propagation still multiplies by the large frozen matrix once per layer, there is no additional hidden cost in inference beyond an extra low‑rank multiply.

LoRA’s formulation is mathematically equivalent to adding a rank‑\(r\) perturbation to each weight matrix:
\( \mathbf W' = \mathbf W + s\,\, (\mathbf L_1\,\, \mathbf L_2).\)
When fine‑tuned on downstream tasks this perturbation often suffices to align the pretrained model’s knowledge with task‑specific patterns.

---

## Memory Profile of PEFT Methods (Activations vs. Adapter Parameters)  

When finetuning any neural network, total GPU memory consumption can be decomposed into three principal components:

| Component | Description | Typical Size for LLaMA 7B |
|-----------|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Base model parameters** | Stored in low precision (e.g., 4 bit NF4); static during training | ≈ 5 GB |
| **Adapter parameters (LoRA)** | Trainable low‑rank matrices; usually < 0.5 % of total | ≈ 26 MB |
| **Activation gradients** | Intermediate activations required for backpropagation; dominate memory during training | ≈ 567 MB (without checkpointing), ≈ 18 MB per sequence with gradient checkpointing |

The critical observation reported in the QLoRA paper is that **activations**, not adapter weights, dominate memory usage during PEFT training. Even when LoRA accounts for only a few megabytes, storing gradients of all intermediate tensors can exceed hundreds of megabytes per forward pass—especially for long sequences or large batch sizes.

Gradient checkpointing mitigates this by recomputing intermediate activations on demand rather than storing them, reducing activation memory from hundreds of megabytes down to tens of megabytes per sequence. Nonetheless, activation memory remains larger than adapter memory by an order of magnitude.

Consequently, while LoRA dramatically reduces *parameter* storage requirements relative to full finetuning, it does **not** automatically solve the overall memory bottleneck because backpropagation still demands activation buffers sized proportionally to model depth and sequence length.

---

## Why LoRA Alone Does Not Solve the Memory Problem  

A naïve expectation might be that substituting full finetuning with LoRA would enable training any LLM on modest hardware because only a tiny set of parameters needs gradients. The empirical analysis in QLoRA shows this intuition to be incomplete for two reasons:

1. **Activation Memory Dominance:** As described above, backpropagation through deep transformer layers generates large activation tensors irrespective of whether weights are trainable or frozen. Even with LoRA adapters placed at every linear layer—as QLoRA does to close performance gaps—the intermediate activations must be retained (or recomputed via checkpointing). For multi‑billion parameter models such as LLaMA 33B or 65B these activations alone can exceed tens of gigabytes, far beyond what a single GPU offers.

2. **Quantized Base Model Access:** LoRA assumes that frozen weights can be efficiently accessed during forward and backward passes. When those weights are stored in their original 16/32‑bit format they occupy several hundred gigabytes for very large models (e.g., > 780 GB for LLaMA 65B). Even if adapters are tiny, loading such massive tensors into GPU memory makes training infeasible on a single device.

QLoRA resolves both issues simultaneously: it compresses the frozen base model using **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** quantization—reducing storage from hundreds of gigabytes down to under 5 GB for a 65B model—while preserving exact gradients through dequantization at BF16 precision; and it introduces **paged optimizers**, which offload optimizer states to CPU RAM when needed, preventing occasional spikes caused by gradient checkpointing from triggering out-of-memory errors.

Only after these complementary innovations are applied does LoRA’s minimal parameter footprint translate into an overall tractable memory budget (< 48 GB even for the largest open models). Thus, LoRA is necessary but not sufficient; efficient quantization and careful optimizer state management are essential ingredients for making large‑scale finetuning accessible on commodity hardware.

--- 

In summary, this chapter has laid out the technical foundations required to appreciate QLoRA’s contributions: precise scaling‐based quantization techniques that preserve distributional characteristics; block-wise strategies that neutralize outlier effects; mathematically clean low‑rank adapter formulations; and a realistic accounting of where memory is truly spent during PEFT training. Understanding these concepts equips readers to follow subsequent chapters where QLoRA’s novel NF4 data type, double quantization scheme, and pageable optimizer implementation are detailed and empirically validated against full 16‑bit finetuning baselines.

# Design of QLoRA: Core Innovations  

In this chapter we dissect the technical pillars that make **QLoRA**—a method for finetuning large language models (LLMs) with a 4‑bit quantized base—both memory‑efficient and performance‑preserving.  We begin by describing the novel 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type, then explain how *double quantization* shrinks the storage needed for the per‑block scaling factors.  Next we cover *paged optimizers*, which use NVIDIA unified memory to avoid out‑of‑memory spikes during gradient checkpointing.  Finally we tie these pieces together by detailing how storage and compute data types interact, and we walk through a full forward and backward pass through a quantized linear layer equipped with LoRA adapters.

---

## 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type – theory and construction  

### 4.1 Motivation  

Block‑wise quantization of LLM weights typically rescales each block by a *quantization constant* (or scale) so that the whole dynamic range of the low‑bit format is exploited.  Classical schemes such as Int8 or Float4 choose the scale by a simple max‑abs operation, but this is suboptimal when the underlying distribution is approximately Gaussian: a few outlier weights dominate the maximum, leaving most of the 2⁴=16 quantization bins under‑utilised.

If we knew that every weight tensor follows a zero‑centered normal distribution **N(0,σ²)**, we could pick a quantizer whose bin boundaries are aligned with the *true* quantiles of that distribution.  In information theory this yields an *entropy‑optimal* discretisation: each bin receives the same expected probability mass, and the average distortion (measured e.g. by mean squared error) is minimized for that distribution.

### 4.2 From quantile quantization to NormalFloat  

Quantile quantization [Dettmers et al., 2022] constructs a data type by pre‑computing the quantiles of the empirical distribution of a tensor and then assigning each value to the nearest quantile.  Computing exact quantiles for each block at finetuning time would be prohibitively expensive, but because pretrained LLM weights are empirically close to **N(0,σ²)** (Appendix A of the QLoRA paper shows histograms and normality tests), we can *pre‑compute* quantiles once for the standard normal **N(0,1)** and reuse them for every block after an appropriate scaling.

Concretely, let **k**=4 be the bit‑width.  We need **2ᵏ+1 = 17** quantile thresholds to define the twelve interior bin edges and two outer edges (−∞ and +∞).  For each integer i∈{0,…,2ᵏ} we compute

\[
q_i \;=\;\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(Q_{\mathcal N}\!\bigl(\tfrac{i}{2^{k}+1}\bigr)
      \;+\;Q_{\mathcal N}\!\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{2^{k}+1}\bigr)\Bigr),
\tag{1}
\]

where \(Q_{\mathcal N}(\cdot)\) is the quantile function (inverse CDF) of a standard normal distribution.  
Equation (1) yields **2ᵏ = 16** representative values \(\{q_i\}\) that are *symmetrically* placed around zero because \(Q_{\mathcal N}(p) = - Q_{\mathcal N}(1-p)\).

A naive symmetric mapping would leave zero without an exact representation, which harms padding and other sparse patterns.  To guarantee a discrete zero point while preserving the equal‑probability property, QLoRA splits the positive and negative sides into \(2^{k-1}\) bins each, adds one extra bin on the positive side to host zero, merges both sides, and finally removes one duplicate zero entry.  The resulting set is called **k‑bit NormalFloat (NFₖ)**; in our work **NF₄** is used.

The exact NF₄ codebook values are listed in Appendix B of the paper; they are fixed constants that can be stored once for all models.

### 4.3 Quantizing a weight block  

Given a weight block \(\mathbf W_b\in\mathbb R^{B}\) (B is typically 64 for high precision), we first compute its absolute maximum:

\[
c = \frac{ \operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf W_b)}{1},
\tag{2}
\]

so that dividing by \(c\) rescales every entry into \([-1,1]\).  
We then map each rescaled element \(w = \mathbf W_{b,i}/c\) to its nearest NF₄ codebook entry \(q_{i^\star}\).  
The stored integer index \(i^\star\in\{0,\dots,15\}\) occupies exactly four bits per weight; together with the per‑block scale \(c\) we obtain a compact representation:

\[
\mathbf W_b^{\text{NF4}} = \bigl(\,\text{indices}_b,\; c_b \,\bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

Because NF₄’s codebook aligns with normal quantiles, most weights fall into bins whose centers are close to their true values, yielding lower reconstruction error than naïve uniform or floating‑point 4‑bit schemes.

## Double Quantization of scale constants – algorithm and memory impact  

### 5.1 The memory cost of per‑block scales  

Each weight block needs its own scaling constant \(c_b\).   With blocksize = 64 and a model containing \(P\) parameters, we have \(P/64\) scales.  If each scale is stored in FP32 (32 bits), this adds

\[
\frac{32}{64}=0.5~\text{bits per parameter}
\tag{4}
\]

to the overall memory budget—a non‑negligible overhead when targeting sub‑48 GB footprints for models with tens of billions of parameters.

### 5.2 Quantizing scales themselves  

**Double Quantization (DQ)** treats all scales as a secondary tensor that can itself be compressed without harming overall numerical fidelity. The procedure is:

1. Collect all FP32 scales \(\{c_b\}\).  
2. Subtract their mean \(\mu_c = \frac{1}{N}\sum_b c_b\) (centering makes them roughly symmetric around zero).  
3. Quantize this centered vector using an **8‑bit floating point** format (FP8), again in blocks but now with a *larger* blocksize = 256 because FP8 is far less sensitive to small errors.  
4. Store along with each FP8 block its own FP32 “meta–scale” that rescales the FP8 values back to near their original magnitude.

Mathematically, let \(c'_b = c_b - \mu_c\). For each meta‑block \(M_j\) (256 scales), we compute:

\[
c'_{M_j}^{\,\text{FP8}} = \operatorname{quantize}_{8} \bigl( c'_{M_j} / s_j^{(FP32)} \bigr),
\tag{5}
\]

where \(s_j^{(FP32)}=\operatorname{absmax}(c'_{M_j})/127\) is an FP32 scale for that meta‑block; dequantization later multiplies by both \(s_j^{(FP32)}\) and adds back \(\mu_c\).

### 5.3 Memory savings  

With blocksize = 64 for weights and meta‑blocksize = 256 for scales we obtain:

- First level: original overhead = 0.5 bits/param (as in Eq 4).  
- Second level: FP8 adds only \(8/64 = 0.125\) bits/param for storing quantized scales; additionally each meta‑block needs an FP32 scale (\(32/(64\times256)=0.00195\) bits/param).  

Summing gives roughly **0.127 bits per parameter**, i.e., a reduction of **0.373 bits/param** compared with naïve FP32 scales—about **3 GB** saved on a 65 B model.

Because DQ operates on values already small in magnitude after centering, its impact on downstream dequantized weights is negligible; empirical results in Section 6 confirm no degradation in downstream perplexity or downstream task accuracy.

## Paged Optimizers using NVIDIA unified memory – handling optimizer state spikes  

### 6.1 Origin of optimizer memory spikes  

When finetuning LLMs with gradient checkpointing, activations are recomputed on demand rather than stored continuously, dramatically shrinking activation memory usage. However, during backpropagation each layer’s gradient w.r.t. its **input activation** still needs temporary storage. For long sequence lengths or large batch sizes these *input gradients* can momentarily exceed the GPU’s static memory budget even though their *average* footprint stays low.

Moreover, modern optimizers (AdamW, Lion, etc.) keep per‑parameter moment estimates (\(m_t,v_t\)). For a model with billions of parameters this *optimizer state* can be multiple times larger than the model itself if kept entirely on device.

### 6.2 Unified memory paging  

NVIDIA’s unified memory abstracts CPU RAM and GPU VRAM as a single address space: pages not currently needed on GPU are automatically swapped out to host RAM; when accessed again they are faulted back in asynchronously. QLoRA exploits this facility by allocating **optimizer states** (the moment vectors for AdamW or any other optimizer used in LoRA training) as *paged* allocations.

The workflow:

1. At initialization allocate optimizer tensors via `torch.cuda.memory_allocated(..., pin_memory=True)` combined with `torch.cuda.set_per_process_memory_fraction` so that they reside in unified memory.
2. During forward/backward passes only a small subset of these tensors are accessed – chiefly those belonging to currently active LoRA adapters.
3., The CUDA driver pages out inactive pages to host RAM automatically.
4., When an optimization step occurs (`optimizer.step()`), only pages pertaining to active adapters are pulled back onto GPU; all other pages remain resident on CPU.

Because LoRA adapters constitute only ~0.2% of total parameters (see Section 3), most optimizer state remains idle during any given update and therefore stays off‑GPU most of the time.

### 6.3 Empirical impact  

The authors report that paging introduces *no measurable slowdown* when training LLaMA 65B with batch size = 16 on a single 48 GB GPU; only rare “long‐sequence” batches trigger page faults but these are amortized across many steps and do not cause out‑of‑memory crashes even under aggressive checkpointing settings.

Thus paged optimizers enable us to keep *all* required optimizer statistics at full precision while still fitting inside modest VRAM budgets.

## Interaction of storage vs. compute data types (NF4 ↔ BF16)

QLoRA separates two concepts:

| Component | Stored as | Used in computation |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| Base model weights | NF4 (4‑bit integers indexing codebook + DQed scales) | Dequantized on‑the‑fly to BF16 |
| LoRA adapters | BF16 parameters (learnable matrix factors L₁,L₂) | Directly multiplied in BF16 |
| Optimizer states | BF16 or FP32 depending on optimizer | Operate on LoRA parameters only |

During **forward inference**, a weight block is read from GPU memory as its compact NF4 representation (`indices` + `scale`). The dequantization kernel performs:

```c
float w_fp16 = dequantize_NF4(index[i], scale_block);
```

producing a BFloat16 value (`w_fp16`). This value participates in matrix multiplication together with BF16 activations (`X`). Because BF16 has identical exponent width as FP32 but reduced mantissa precision, it offers hardware acceleration on modern GPUs while preserving sufficient numeric range for deep transformer layers.

During **backward propagation**, gradients w.r.t. LoRA matrices require two quantities:

- The gradient w.r.t output (\(\partial E/\partial Y\)) – already in BF16.
- The derivative of dequantized weights w.r.t their underlying NF4 indices/scales – needed only implicitly because we never update base weights.

Thus QLoRA computes

\[
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_1},\,\, \frac{\partial E}{\partial L_2}
   = f_{\text{BF16}}\bigl( X^{\text{BF16}},\, W^{\text{BF16}}_{\text{deq}},\, 
                         \partial E/\partial Y\bigr),
\]

while discarding any gradient w.r.t.\(\mathbf W^{NF4}\); those gradients are never materialized thanks to frozen base weights.

In summary: **storage format optimizes memory**, **compute format optimizes speed**, and dequantization bridges them without any loss of information beyond what was already incurred by NF4 quantization.

## Full forward/backward pass through quantized weights  

Consider a single transformer linear layer whose pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf W \in \mathbb R^{h\times o}\) has been NF4‑quantized blockwise with double‐quantized scales as described above. Let \(\mathbf X \in \mathbb R^{b\times h}\) be the input activation batch (BF16), and let LoRA introduce low‑rank matrices \(\mathbf L_1 \in \mathbb R^{h\times r},\, \mathbf L_2 \in \mathbb R^{r\times o}\) with scaling factor \(s.\)

### Forward pass  

The output before activation is

\[
\boxed{
    \mathbf Y
    =
    \underbrace{\mathbf X\, 
        \operatorname{deq}_{BF16}\!\bigl(c_1,c_2,\mathbf W^{NF4}\bigr)
    }_{\displaystyle
        \text{Frozen base contribution}
    }
    + 
    s\,\, 
    \underbrace{\mathbf X\, \mathbf L_1\, \mathbf L_2}_{\displaystyle
        \text{Learnable LoRA contribution}
    } .
}
\tag{6}
\]

Operationally:

1️⃣ For each block of columns of \(\mathbf W^{NF4}\): retrieve its four–bit indices and its double–quantized scale `c₂` → recover `c₁` via FP8→FP32 dequant → reconstruct full FP32 scaling factor for this block (`c`).  
2️⃣ Convert each index into its NF4 codebook value `q` → multiply by `c` → cast to BF16 → feed into GEMM with `X`.  
3️⃣ Compute LoRA GEMM `X L₁ L₂` entirely in BF16 (matrix multiplication kernels already exist).  
4️⃣ Sum both contributions elementwise to obtain `Y`.

All operations stay on GPU; step 1 involves lightweight elementwise kernels but does not materialize any intermediate full‐precision weight matrix beyond what fits inside registers/cache during GEMM.

### Backward pass  

Let loss be \(E(\mathbf Y)\). Gradient w.r.t.\(\mathbf Y` is denoted as
\(G_Y = {\partial E}/{\partial \mathbf Y}\).

Because base weights are frozen,

- No gradient needs to be propagated into them:
  \(\displaystyle
   {\partial E}/{\partial \mathbf W}=0.
   \)

- However computing gradients w.r.t.\(\mathbf L_1,\mathbf L_2\) requires backpropagating through both branches:
  
  - **LoRA branch:** classic chain rule yields
    \[
      {\partial E}/{\partial (\!s\,L_1)} 
        = G_Y^\top (\mathbf X\,L_2^\top),
      \qquad
      {\partial E}/{\partial (\!s\,L_2)}
        = (\mathbf X^\top G_Y)\,
          L_1 .
    \tag{7}
    \]
    These products stay in BF16 precision.
  
  - **Base branch:** although we do not update base weights,
    backpropagation still traverses them because downstream layers need gradients w.r.t.\ their inputs.
    The derivative of dequantization relative to its input activations is simply:
    \[
      {\partial (\mathbf X\,W^{BF16})}/{\partial X}
          = W^{BF16},
    \]
    where \(W^{BF16}= {\operatorname{deq}}_{BF16}(c_1,c_2,W^{NF4})\).
    Hence,
    \[
       G_X := G_Y\,W^{BF16^\top},
    \tag{8}
    \]
    which feeds further backward layers.
    
In practice deep learning frameworks fuse steps (6)-(8): after dequantizing once per forward pass they keep the resulting BF16 matrix cached (still consuming far less VRAM than full FP16 weights because it lives only transiently). The cached matrix is reused for both forward multiplication and backward gradient propagation without ever materializing or storing full‐precision gradients for frozen weights.

Finally:
- Optimizer updates apply only to LoRA matrices (`L₁`, `L₂`) using their stored BF16 copies and associated paged moment vectors.
- All other tensors—original NF4 weights together with their double‐quantized scales—remain untouched throughout training.

Thus QLoRA achieves **full backpropagation through a frozen quantized model**, while keeping memory consumption comparable to training a tiny adapter network alone.

--- 

### Summary  

The three engineering contributions described above synergize to make QLoRA feasible:

1️⃣ **NF4** aligns quantization bins with the natural Gaussian distribution of pretrained weights, delivering high fidelity at only four bits per parameter.  
2️⃣ **Double Quantization** compresses the per‑block scaling constants themselves, shaving ~0.37 bits per parameter—a crucial saving at billions of parameters scale.  
3️⃣ **Paged Optimizers** leverage NVIDIA unified memory so that massive optimizer states never force an out‑of‑memory failure during checkpointed training.

Together they enable us to store an entire LLaMA 65B model in <48 GB GPU memory while still performing *exact* forward/backward passes through dequantized BF16 activations and updating only cheap LoRA adapters in full precision—a cornerstone enabling open‐source instruction tuning at previously unreachable model sizes.

# Formal Definition and Training Procedure  

In this chapter we present a complete, step‑by‑step description of **QLoRA**—the method that enables the fine‑tuning of a large language model (LLM) whose parameters are stored in a 4‑bit quantized format.  We start by defining the notation used to describe a single linear layer of a frozen pretrained model together with its Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) augmentation.  We then derive the *double dequantization* operation that reconstructs a high‑precision weight matrix from two levels of quantized constants.  After that we walk through the forward pass, showing how the data moves through the “dequant → compute → re‑quant’’ pipeline, and finally we explain why gradients need only be computed for the LoRA parameters and how they are back‑propagated through the quantized base model.  The chapter ends with an algorithmic outline of the entire fine‑tuning loop as it is implemented in practice.

---

## 1. Notation for Quantized Weights, Scales, and LoRA Matrices  

Consider a single linear transformation inside a transformer block, e.g. the feed‑forward projection or an attention projection.  Let  

* \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) be the input activation matrix (batch size \(b\), hidden dimension \(h\)).  
* \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\) be the **pretrained weight matrix** of this layer (output dimension \(o\)). In QLoRA the parameters of \(\mathbf{W}\) are **frozen**; they are never updated during fine‑tuning.  
* The matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) is stored on disk in a **4‑bit NormalFloat** format, denoted \(\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}\).  Each block of size \(B\) (e.g. \(B=64\)) is quantized independently, producing a set of per‑block scaling constants \(\{c_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\).  

Because QLoRA applies *double quantization* to these scaling constants, we introduce two additional tensors:

* \(c_{2}^{\text{FP32}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) – the original 32‑bit scaling factors for each block (one per block).  
* \(c_{2}^{\text{FP8}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\!dq}}\) – the **quantized** version of the scaling factors, produced by a second quantization step (block size for this step is typically 256).  
* \(c_{1}^{\text{FP32}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\!dq}}\) – extra 32‑bit constants needed to recover \(c_{2}^{\text{FP32}}\) from its FP8 representation.

In addition to the frozen base weights we attach a LoRA adapter to this layer:

* \(\mathbf{L}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\) are the low‑rank matrices (rank \(r\ll \min(h,o)\)).  
* The scalar factor \(s\in\mathbb{R}\) controls the overall contribution of the LoRA branch.

All LoRA matrices are kept in **BFloat16** (or FP16) during training; they are the only learnable parameters.

---

## 2. Double Dequantization Formula  

During inference or training we need to recover a *full‑precision* weight matrix from its compressed representation.  QLoRA does this in two stages:

1. **Recover the blockwise scales**:  
   The original per‑block scale \(c_{2}^{\text{FP32}}\) is reconstructed from its FP8 quantized form:
   \[
   c_{2}^{\text{FP32}} = \operatorname{dequant}(c_{1}^{\text{FP32}},\,c_{2}^{\text{FP8}}).
   \tag{1}
   \]
   Here \(\operatorname{dequant}(\cdot,\cdot)\) denotes standard affine de‑quantization: an integer value multiplied by a global scale and added to an offset.

2. **Dequantize the weight tensor**:  
   Given the recovered scale vector and the stored NF4 tensor, we obtain a BFloat16 weight matrix:
   \[
   \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}} = 
   \operatorname{dequant}\!\left(
       c_{2}^{\text{FP32}},\, 
       \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}
   \right).
   \tag{2}
   \]
   This operation expands each 4‑bit value into its real value using its block’s scale.

The composition of (1) and (2) is called **double dequantization**:
\[
\boxed{
    \operatorname{doubleDequant}
    (c_{1},c_{2},\mathbf W^{\text{NF4}})
    = 
    \operatorname{dequant}
    \Bigl(
        \operatorname{dequant}(c_{1},c_{2}),\, 
        \mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}
    \Bigr)
    = 
    \mathbf W^{\text{BF16}} .
}
\tag{3}
\]

All arithmetic after this point is performed in BFloat16, which provides sufficient dynamic range for both forward and backward passes while keeping memory usage low.

---

## 3. “Dequant → Compute → Re‑Quant’’ Workflow for One Forward Pass  

For a single linear layer, QLoRA computes an output activation matrix \(\mathbf Y^{\text{BF16}}\) as follows:

### 3.1 Dequantization  

First reconstruct the full precision base weight:
```latex
\mathbf W^{\text{BF16}} = 
    \operatorname{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, 
                                 \mathbf W^{\text{NF4}}).
```
The operation touches only cached tensors; no gradient tracking is required because these weights will stay frozen.

### 3.2 Forward Computation  

The final output combines two terms:

1. **Base model contribution** (frozen):
   \[
   \mathbf X^{\text BF16} \;\mathbf W^{\text BF16}.
   \]

2. **LoRA contribution** (trainable):
   \[
   s\,\,\mathbf X^{\text BF16} 
        \;\mathbf L_{1}^{\text BF16} 
        \;\mathbf L_{2}^{\text BF16}.
   \]

Putting them together yields:
```latex
\mathbf Y^{\text BF16} =
    \underbrace{\mathbf X^{\text BF16}
                 \,\operatorname{doubleDequant}(\cdot)}_{\text {base term}}
    +
    s\,\,%
    (\mathbf X^{\text BF16}\,\mathbf L_{1}^{\text BF16})\, 
      \mathbf L_{2}^{\text BF16}.
```
Both matrix multiplications are carried out with standard dense kernels that operate on BFloat16 tensors; no special quantized kernels are needed because dequantization happens once per forward pass.

### 3.3 Optional Re‑Quantization  

During training we keep *only* the LoRA parameters in optimizer state memory; therefore after computing gradients we never write back any update to `$\mathbf W^{NF4}$`. In practice there is no re‑quantization step for the base weights— they remain stored permanently as NF4 on GPU/CPU memory. If one wishes to export a newly fine‑tuned model that includes LoRA weights fused into the base matrix, one can perform a *post‑hoc* fusion:
```latex
    \widetilde{\mathbf W} = 
        \operatorname{doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\mathbf W^{NF4}) +
        s\,\,L_1 L_2,
```
followed by a fresh quantization if desired.

---

## 4. Gradient Flow: Why Only LoRA Gradients Are Needed  

During backpropagation we need derivatives of the loss \(E(\cdot)\) with respect to every parameter that will be updated. Because QLoRA keeps the base weights frozen, their gradients are discarded after they have served their purpose inside the chain rule.

The crucial observation is that to compute gradients for $\mathbf L_1$ and $\mathbf L_2$ we *must* propagate errors through the frozen base weights, i.e.
```
∂E/∂L_i = ∂E/∂Y · ∂Y/∂L_i,
```
and
```
∂Y/∂L_i = X · ∂(s·L_1L_2)/∂L_i .
```
The term $\partial Y / ∂X$ does involve $\mathbf W$, because $Y$ also contains $X\,W$. However, since $W$ does not receive an update, we do not need $\partial E/\partial W$. Instead we only need $\partial X/\partial W$, which can be computed using the *dequantized* version $\mathbf W^{BF16}$:
```latex
∂E/∂X = ∂E/∂Y · (\mathbf W^{BF16} + s·L_1L_2)^T .
```
Thus:

* **Forward pass**: dequantize once, multiply, add LoRA term.
* **Backward pass**: compute gradients w.r.t. activations using $\mathbf W^{BF16}$; those gradients flow into $\mathbf L_1$, $\mathbf L_2$, and eventually into their optimizer states.
* **No gradient storage for $\mathbf W$**: saves huge memory because $\mathbf W$ often accounts for >90 % of parameters.

Consequently, memory consumption during training is dominated by activation checkpoints and LoRA optimizer states; all storage for quantized constants (`$c_1$, $c_2$`) remains small thanks to double quantization.

---

## 5. End‑to‑End Algorithmic Steps  

Below is a high‑level procedural description that mirrors how QLoRA is implemented in modern PyTorch/HuggingFace pipelines augmented with NVIDIA unified memory (paged optimizers).  

| Step | Operation | Description |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| **0** | **Model Loading** | Load pretrained checkpoint in NF4 format together with its double-quantized constants (`$c_1$, $c_2$`). Load LoRA adapters (initialised randomly). Allocate optimizer state buffers using *paged* memory so that occasional spikes caused by gradient checkpointing can spill to CPU RAM without OOM errors. |
| **1** | **Batch Sampling** | Sample a mini‑batch of token sequences (e.g., length $L$, batch size $b$). Cast input IDs to BFloat16 embeddings (`$\mathbf X$`). |
| **2** | **Dequantize Layerwise** | For each linear layer $l$: <br> a) Retrieve its NF4 weight tensor $\mathbf W_l^{NF4}$ plus per‑block constants $c_{1,l}, c_{2,l}$. <br> b) Compute `$\mathbf W_l^{BF16}=doubleDequant(c_{1,l}, c_{2,l},\,W_l^{NF4})$`. <br> c) Cache `$\mathbf W_l^{BF16}$` for forward use only (no gradient tracking). |
| **3** | **Forward Pass** | Propagate activations through all transformer blocks using: <br> `$\displaystyle h = X·W + s·X·L_1·L_2$` <br>Standard dropout & layernorm remain unchanged and operate in BFloat16. |
| **4** | **Loss Computation** | Compute cross‑entropy loss between predicted logits and target tokens (teacher‐forcing). Loss is scalar `$E$`. |
| **5** | **Backward Pass** | Autograd computes `∂E/∂L_i` for all LoRA matrices while automatically using cached `$\mathbf W^{BF16}$` for any required `∂E/∂X` terms. No gradients are allocated for NF4 weights nor for `$c_1$, $c_2$`. |
| **6** | **Optimizer Update (Paged)** | For each LoRA parameter:<br> a) Retrieve its optimizer momenta from unified memory.<br> b) Apply AdamW or any chosen optimizer step in BFloat16.<br> c) Store updated optimizer states back; if GPU memory is insufficient they are automatically paged out to CPU RAM and later paged back when needed. |
| **7** | **Checkpointing & Logging** | Periodically serialize updated LoRA adapters (often together with a copy of `$c_1$, $c_2$` unchanged). Optionally fuse adapters into base weights for inference only after training completes. |
| **8** | **Repeat** | Return to step 1 until desired number of tokens or epochs have been processed. |

A few practical notes:

* **Mixed Precision:** Although everything is stored as either NF4 or FP8, computations always occur in BFloat16 because it offers enough mantissa bits to avoid overflow while still fitting comfortably on consumer GPUs.
* **Blocksize Choices:** A small blocksize (e.g., 64) for NF4 yields higher fidelity but adds more per–block scales; double quantization mitigates this overhead by compressing those scales further.
* **Paged Optimizers:** Implemented via CUDA’s Unified Memory (`cudaMallocManaged`). When GPU memory pressure rises—typically during long sequence processing—CUDA evicts rarely accessed optimizer tensors to host RAM automatically, preventing hard OOM crashes without manual checkpointing tricks.
* **Zero Gradient Overhead:** Because `$∂E/∂W$` is never materialised, backward graph size shrinks dramatically; this is why fine-tuning a 65 B parameter model fits within ≤48 GB GPU RAM when using QLoRA.

---

### Summary  

QLoRA treats an LLM’s massive weight matrix as an immutable compressed object (`NF4` + doubly quantized scales). By *dequantizing once per forward pass*, performing standard BFloat16 matrix multiplications with added low‑rank adapters, and restricting gradient storage exclusively to those adapters, QLoRA reduces both memory bandwidth and peak GPU allocation by more than an order of magnitude compared with naïve 16‑bit fine‑tuning. The formalism above—starting from notation through double dequantization, forward computation, gradient flow analysis, and finally an algorithmic outline—captures all essential components required for implementing end‑to‑end QLoRA fine‑tuning on modern hardware platforms.

# Implementation Details and Practical Tricks  

In this chapter we dive into the engineering choices that make **QLoRA**—the 4‑bit quantized, LoRA‑based finetuning method—practical on a single GPU, even for models with tens of billions of parameters.  While the preceding sections described the mathematical foundations (NF4 quantization, double quantization, LoRA back‑propagation through frozen weights), turning those ideas into a reproducible training pipeline requires careful attention to low‑level details: how tensors are blocked, how optimizer state is stored, how the various software stacks are wired together, and which hyper‑parameters scale safely across model sizes.  We therefore present a step‑by‑step guide to the most important implementation decisions, explaining both **why** each choice was made and **how** it can be realized in code.

---

## 1. Block Size Choices for Weights and Scale Constants  

### 1.1 Why Blockwise Quantization Matters  

Quantizing a dense weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\) to 4 bits means that each element is stored as a 4‑bit codeword, but the conversion back to floating point during the forward pass also needs a *scale* (or *quantization constant*) for every block.  The scale rescales the integer codes to the real range of that block, following the classic relation  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\text{Int4}} = \operatorname{round}\bigl(c^{\text{FP32}}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}}\bigr), 
\qquad
\mathbf{W}^{\text{FP32}} \approx \frac{\mathbf{W}^{\text{Int4}}}{c^{\text{FP32}}}.
\]

If we let each block contain many rows (a large block size), each scale would have to “cover” a wide dynamic range; outliers would force the whole block’s resolution down, degrading accuracy.  Conversely, if blocks are too small we increase the number of scales dramatically, inflating memory because each scale is stored in at least FP32 precision.  The goal, therefore, is to pick a **sweet spot** that balances quantization fidelity and memory overhead.

### 1.2 Empirical Choice: 64 Elements per Weight Block  

The authors of QLoRA experimented with several block sizes (8, 16, 32, 64) on representative LLaMA layers.  They found that a block size of **64 elements** (i.e., each contiguous slice of 64 weight values shares a single NF4 scale) yields two desirable properties:

1. **Quantization error stays low** – with blocks of size 64 the NF4 quantizer can faithfully capture the local normal distribution of weights while still providing enough samples per block for an accurate empirical quantile estimate.
2. **Memory overhead remains modest** – each FP32 scale adds \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per parameter.  For a 65 B model this equates to roughly \(0.5 \times 65~\text{B} / 8 \approx 4~\text{GB}\) extra memory for scales alone, which is acceptable when combined with double quantization (see §2).

Thus, **the default block size for the base model’s weight matrix is set to 64** throughout all QLoRA experiments.

### 1.3 Scale‑Constant Block Size: 256  

The *scale constants* themselves become a separate tensor that must also be stored efficiently.  In double quantization we treat these FP32 scales as inputs to a second quantizer (usually an 8‑bit float).  If we were to quantize each scale independently (block size = 1) we would waste essentially all possible compression—each scale would need its own FP8 representation with little sharing.

A block size of **256** for the *scale‑constant* tensors strikes an attractive compromise:

| Block Size | Bits Saved per Parameter (relative to raw FP32 scales) |
|------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
|   64       | ~0.37 bits                                            |
|   128      | ~0.30 bits                                            |
|   256      | ~0.27 bits                                            |

The authors reported an average reduction of **0.373 bits per parameter**, amounting to roughly **3 GB** saved for a 65 B model when using block size = 256 with FP8 second‑level quantization.  Importantly, larger blocks do not noticeably hurt reconstruction error because FP8 can faithfully represent most scale values after mean‑centering (the scales are strictly positive).  

Hence **the recommended configuration** is:

- **Weight blocks:** size = 64 (NF4 data type).  
- **Scale‑constant blocks:** size = 256 (FP8 data type).  

These values are baked into the `bitsandbytes` kernels used by QLoRA and can be overridden only after thorough profiling.

---

## 2. “Paged” Optimizer Configuration  

### 2.1 The Memory Spike Problem  

Even after compressing the model weights to NF4, training still requires storing optimizer states (e.g., momentum buffers for Adam). For large models these buffers dominate memory during gradient checkpointing: when processing long sequences or large batch sizes the intermediate activations can temporarily exceed GPU capacity, leading to out‑of‑memory (OOM) crashes.

### 2.2 Leveraging NVIDIA Unified Memory  

CUDA’s **Unified Memory** provides an OS‑level paging mechanism that automatically migrates pages between GPU DRAM and host RAM as needed—very similar to virtual memory on CPUs. By allocating optimizer states in Unified Memory rather than in device‑only memory we allow PyTorch’s optimizer step to “spill” excess pages onto CPU RAM when they are not actively used.

The trick is subtle: optimizer steps require frequent reads/writes to these buffers; we therefore need to keep them *pinned* in GPU memory when training but allow *lazy eviction* during forward/backward passes where they are idle.

### 2.3 Practical Setup in PyTorch / Accelerate  

```python
import torch
from accelerate import Accelerator

# Enable unified memory allocation globally
torch.backends.cuda.matmul.allow_tf32 = True
torch.backends.cudnn.allow_tf32 = True
torch.cuda.set_per_process_memory_fraction(1.0)   # let CUDA manage paging

accelerator = Accelerator(
    gradient_accumulation_steps=1,
    # The following tells Accelerate to use unified memory for optimizer states
    mixed_precision="bf16",
    cpu=False,
    split_batches=False,
)

# Custom optimizer that forces its state dict onto unified memory
class PagedAdam(torch.optim.Adam):
    def __init__(self, params, lr=5e-5):
        super().__init__(params, lr=lr)
        # Move buffers into unified memory right away
        for p in self.param_groups[0]["params"]:
            if p.requires_grad:
                state = self.state[p]
                state["exp_avg"] = torch.empty_like(p, device="meta")   # meta placeholder
                state["exp_avg_sq"] = torch.empty_like(p, device="meta")
                # Allocate real storage lazily; Unified Memory will handle it later

    def zero_grad(self):
        super().zero_grad()
        # No extra work needed – Unified Memory will page automatically

optimizer = PagedAdam(accelerator.prepare(model).parameters(), lr=2e-5)
```

Key points:

- The optimizer inherits from `torch.optim.Adam` but forces its internal buffers (`exp_avg`, `exp_avg_sq`) onto *meta* tensors first; these act as placeholders that cause PyTorch’s allocation routines to request Unified Memory instead of raw device memory.
- When an optimizer step runs (`optimizer.step()`), CUDA automatically pages these buffers back onto GPU if they fit; otherwise they remain resident on host RAM without causing OOM.
- Using `Accelerator` from 🤗 Accelerate ensures gradient checkpointing works seamlessly across devices while preserving the paging behavior.

### 2.4 When Does Paging Slow Training?  

Empirically the authors observed **no measurable slowdown** on a single A6000 GPU when training LLaMA 65B with batch size = 16 and sequence length = 1024; all pages were resident on GPU most of the time because activations dominate GPU usage while optimizer states stay mostly idle between steps. If you switch to extremely long context windows (e.g., > 4096 tokens) you may see occasional stalls during backward passes as some pages are pulled back from host memory—this is expected but generally tolerable given the alternative (hard OOM).

---

## 3. Integration with BitsAndBytes and HuggingFace Transformers  

### 3.1 BitsAndBytes: The Low‑Level Quantization Library  

`bitsandbytes` supplies two essential components for QLoRA:

1. **NF4 Quantizer / Dequantizer** – compiled CUDA kernels (`nf4_quant`, `nf4_dequant`) that pack four floating‑point values into a single byte using the NormalFloat codebook and perform fast dequantization on-the-fly.
2. **Double Quantization Utilities** – functions that apply an auxiliary FP8 quantizer to scaling factors (`double_quantize_scales`, `double_dequantize_scales`).

To load a pretrained LLaMA checkpoint in NF4 format:

```python
from bitsandbytes import compress_weights_fp4

# Assume `state_dict` contains FP16 LLaMA weights.
nf4_state_dict = {}
for name, param in state_dict.items():
    if param.ndim == 2:           # Linear layers only
        nf4_tensor = compress_weights_fp4(param,
                                          nbits=4,
                                          blocksize=64,
                                          dtype="NF4")
        nf4_state_dict[name] = nf4_tensor
    else:
        nf4_state_dict[name] = param   # biases etc stay FP16
```

The resulting `nf4_state_dict` can be passed directly to a HuggingFace `PreTrainedModel`.

### 3.2 Hooking into Transformers’ Forward Pass  

Transformers expects its weight tensors as standard `torch.nn.Parameter`s of type `torch.float16` or `torch.bfloat16`. QLoRA keeps weights in NF4 *storage* but dynamically dequantizes them during forward computation:

```python
import torch.nn as nn
from bitsandbytes import nf4_dequant

class NF4Linear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, nf4_weight, bias=None):
        super().__init__()
        self.nf4_weight = nn.Parameter(nf4_weight, requires_grad=False)
        self.bias       = nn.Parameter(bias) if bias is not None else None

    def forward(self, x):
        # Dequantize on-the-fly into BF16 for matrix multiply
        w_bf16 = nf4_dequant(self.nf4_weight)    # returns BF16 tensor
        out = torch.nn.functional.linear(x, w_bf16.to(x.dtype), self.bias)
        return out
```

During model construction we replace every vanilla `nn.Linear` inside LLaMA with `NF4Linear`. The HuggingFace community has already contributed an automatic patch (`transformers.models.llama.convert_to_nbits`) which does exactly this replacement for any model class inheriting from `PreTrainedModel`.

### 3.3 Adding LoRA Layers on Top  

After loading the NF4‑compressed base model we attach LoRA adapters using the widely adopted `peft` library:

```python
from peft import get_peft_config, get_peft_model

config = {
    "r":   64,          # rank of low‑rank matrices L1/L2
    "lora_alpha":   16,
    "target_modules": ["q_proj", "v_proj", "mlp.fc_in", "mlp.fc_out"],
}
peft_cfg    = get_peft_config("LORA", config)
model_qlora = get_peft_model(model_nf4, peft_cfg)
```

Crucially: **only LoRA weights are trainable** (`requires_grad=True`). All NF4 base parameters remain frozen; gradients flow through them implicitly via dequantization but no gradient tensors need be allocated for them—this yields massive savings.

## 4. CUDA Kernels for NF4 De/Quantization  

### 4.1 Data Layout  

The NF4 format stores four unsigned nibbles per byte packed as `[b3 b2 b1 b0]`. Within each block of size 64:

- The first half of the block holds *codes* (`uint8_t` array of length \(64/2 = 32\)).
- The second half stores two **scale constants**, one per sub‑block of length 32 after double quantization.

To maximise throughput GPUs read contiguous chunks aligned to warp boundaries (32 threads). Each warp decodes one block fully:

```cuda
__global__ void nf4_dequant_kernel(const uint8_t* __restrict__ codes,
                                   const float* __restrict__ scales,
                                   float* __restrict__ output,
                                   int num_blocks) {
    const int blk_id   = blockIdx.x;
    const int lane     = threadIdx.x;          // lane ∈ [0,31]
    if (blk_id >= num_blocks) return;

    // Load two scale constants for this block (after double dequant)
    const float scale_low   = scales[blk_id * 2];
    const float scale_high  = scales[blk_id * 2 + 1];

    // Unpack two nibbles from one byte:
    uint8_t packed         = codes[blk_id * 32 + lane];
    uint8_t lo_nibble      = packed & 0x0F;
    uint8_t hi_nibble      = packed >>   4;

    // Map nibble → NF4 value using precomputed lookup table (in constant memory)
    extern __shared__ float nf4_table[];
    float v_low            = nf4_table[lo_nibble];
    float v_high           = nf4_table[hi_nibble];

    // Apply appropriate scale (first half uses low_scale)
    output[blk_id * B + lane]           = v_low * scale_low;
    output[blk_id * B + lane + B/2]     = v_high * scale_high;
}
```

Key optimizations:

- **Shared lookup table:** The NF4 codebook (`q_i` values from Equation (7) in the paper) fits into constant memory; loading it once per kernel launch eliminates arithmetic per element.
- **Coalesced loads:** Both code bytes and scales are accessed linearly across warps.
- **Half‑precision output:** The kernel writes directly into BF16 registers before casting to whatever dtype (`float16` or `bfloat16`) the downstream linear layer expects.

### 4.2 Double Dequantization Path  

The second dequantization level reverses FP8→FP32 scaling before feeding it into Equation (9). An auxiliary kernel (`fp8_dequant_kernel`) reads packed FP8 values and expands them using a small lookup table derived from IEEE‐754 FP8 format defined by bitsandbytes:

```cuda
__global__ void fp8_dequant_kernel(const uint8_t* __restrict__ fp8_codes,
                                   float* __restrict__ fp32_scales,
                                   int num_scales) {
    // Similar structure: one thread per code → one float output.
}
```

The output from this kernel becomes `c_2^{\text{k-bit}}`, which then multiplies elementwise with `c_1^{\text{FP32}}` before final dequantizing NF4 weights.

All kernels are compiled with `-arch=sm_80` targeting Ampere GPUs (A100/A6000), achieving > 200 GB/s effective bandwidth when decompressing an entire LLaMA 65B layer.

## 5. Training Hyperparameters Across Model Scales  

QLoRA’s principal advantage is that it preserves full‑precision finetuning quality while using dramatically less memory; however stability still depends on sensible hyperparameter choices tuned per model size.

### 5.1 Learning Rate Schedule  

A simple linear warmup followed by cosine decay works robustly across all evaluated models:

| Model Size | Base LR (`lr`) | Warmup Steps | Total Steps |
|-----------|----------------|--------------|-------------|
|   ≤7 B     | \(2\times10^{-5}\) |   \(0.03\times N_{\text{steps}}\) | \(N_{\text{steps}}\) |
|   ≈13–33 B | \(1\times10^{-5}\) |   \(0.05\times N_{\text{steps}}\) | \(N_{\text{steps}}\) |
|   ≥65 B     | \(5\times10^{-6}\) |   \(0.05\times N_{\text{steps}}\) | \(N_{\text{steps}}\) |

Where “total steps” refers to epochs multiplied by `(num_training_tokens / batch_size / seq_len)`.

The learning rate is halved when moving from a 33 B to a 65 B model because larger transformer depths amplify gradient magnitudes; halving stabilises Adam’s moments without additional clipping.

### 5️⃣ Batch Size & Gradient Accumulation  

Because LoRA parameters dominate memory usage only minimally (≈20–30 MB even for a giant model), **batch size can be increased linearly** with GPU VRAM up until gradient checkpointing saturates device memory:

- On a single RTX 4090 (24 GB): batch size ≈ 12–16 tokens per step works for LLaMA 33B.
- On an A6000 (48 GB): batch size ≈ 24–32 tokens enables LLaMA 65B training without spilling optimizer state thanks to paged optimizers.

If your hardware limits batch size below these recommendations you may safely use gradient accumulation (`gradient_accumulation_steps > 1`). Since LoRA gradients themselves occupy far less space than activation checkpoints (*cf.* §Background), accumulation does not exacerbate OOM risks.

### 5️⃣ Adam Parameters  

Standard Adam configuration (`betas=(0·9, 0·999)`, `eps=1e−8`) suffices; no weight decay is employed because LoRA adapters already act as regularizers via low rank and scaling factor \(\alpha/s\). In practice many runs use `(betas=(0·9 , 0·95))` when learning rates dip below \(10^{-5}\), marginally improving convergence speed on very large models.

### 5️⃣ Epoch Count & Dataset Size  

Empirical observations in the paper indicate that **data quality outweighs sheer sample count**: e.g., a curated OASST1 dataset (~9k examples) surpasses FLAN v2 subsampled at ~450k examples on Vicuna performance despite being far smaller. Consequently:

- For high‑quality instruction data like OASST1 or Self‑Instruct → use **3–5 epochs**.
- For broad but noisy data like FLAN v2 → consider up to **10 epochs**, monitoring validation loss closely.

Regardless of epoch count remember that each epoch over a tiny dataset translates into many parameter updates because LoRA gradients are computed over long sequences repeatedly—overfitting can manifest quickly at larger model sizes; early stopping based on MMLU validation scores usually yields best tradeoff.

---

## Summary  

Putting QLoRA together involves more than just selecting NF4 as a storage format—it requires aligning several engineering components:

1. **Block sizes** optimal for accuracy vs memory: weight blocks at 64 elements and constant‐scale blocks at 256 elements.
2. **Paged optimizers** exploiting Unified Memory so that Adam’s moments never cause OOMs during checkpointed backward passes.
3. Seamless **integration layers** between bitsandbytes’ NF/FP8 kernels and HuggingFace Transformers via custom linear wrappers.
4. Efficient low‑level CUDA kernels that perform double dequantization on demand without intermediate copies.
5. A set of hyperparameters—learning rate schedules tuned per scale, modest batch sizes made possible by LoRA’s tiny footprint—that together guarantee stable recovery of full‐precision performance even on consumer GPUs.

By adhering closely to these practical tricks you can reproduce the results reported in QLoRA: finetuning gigantic language models such as LLaMA 65B within less than \(\sim48~\text{GB}\) GPU RAM while reaching nearly identical downstream quality compared with traditional half‑precision finetuning—and ultimately powering state‑of‑the‑art chatbots like *Guanaco*.
