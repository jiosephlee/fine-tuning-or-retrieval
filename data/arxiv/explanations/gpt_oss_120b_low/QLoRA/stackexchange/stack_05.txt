\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### Why do default LoRA hyperparameters (e.g., adapters only on Q and V projections) fail to match full finetuning performance for large models?
Question:
Figure 1 in the paper shows a large gap when only Q and V matrices receive LoRA adapters, but performance recovers when adapters are added to every linear layer. I'm trying to understand:
- What role do adapters in earlier feed‑forward layers play that cannot be compensated by larger rank \(r\) in Q/V only settings?
- Is there any theoretical justification for needing "full‑layer" LoRA at 7B+ scales, or is it purely empirical?
- How does this observation affect scaling laws for PEFT methods?
Answer:
In the QLoRA paper the authors discovered that the “standard’’ LoRA recipe – inserting low‑rank adapters only into the query (**Q**) and value (**V**) projection matrices of the self‑attention sub‑layer – is enough for small‑to‑mid‑size models but quickly falls short when the backbone reaches 7 B + parameters. Figure 1 (the hyper‑parameter study) shows a pronounced gap between full‑model fine‑tuning and a Q‑V‑only LoRA, and the gap disappears only after adapters are added to **every** linear transformation in the transformer block (i.e. also to the key, output, and the two feed‑forward (FFN) layers).

### Why adapters in the earlier feed‑forward layers matter

A transformer block consists of two main parts:

1. **Self‑attention:** three projection matrices (Q, K, V) that mix information across tokens, followed by an output projection.  
2. **Feed‑forward network (FFN):** two dense layers (often called “intermediate’’ and “output’’) that act independently on each token and have a much larger hidden dimension (typically 4× the model dimension).

When we place LoRA only on Q and V we are allowing the model to learn a low‑rank correction to *how* token representations attend to each other, but we leave the rest of the computation unchanged. For a 7 B‑scale model the FFN contains **the majority of parameters** ($≈70\%$ of all weights) and is responsible for most of the non‑linear transformation capacity. If we keep those layers frozen, any task‑specific knowledge that would normally be expressed by adjusting the FFN’s weight matrix must be approximated by moving only in the attention subspace.  

Increasing the rank $r$ of the Q/V adapters can enlarge the space of possible attention corrections, but it does not give access to the directions that live inside the FFN weight space. Empirically the authors observed that even very large $r$ (e.g., $r=256$) does not close the gap: the model simply cannot represent certain task‑specific functions without changing at least one FFN layer. In other words, there are **functions that are easy to encode by shifting an FFN bias or weight** but are *hard* or impossible to mimic through an altered attention pattern alone.

### Is there a theoretical justification?

The observation aligns with a simple dimensionality argument that appears in Section 3 (“Memory Requirement of Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning”). The total number of trainable LoRA parameters grows linearly with both $r$ and the number of targeted linear layers:

\[
\text{#LoRA params} \;=\; \sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}} 2\, r \, d_{\ell}^{\text{in}} d_{\ell}^{\text{out}} .
\]

If we restrict $\mathcal{L}$ to just Q and V we obtain only a few percent of the total parameter budget ($≈0.2\%$ for LLaMA‑7B). Adding LoRA to **every** linear layer multiplies this budget by roughly a factor of 10–12, giving us $≈2\%$ of the original parameters. That extra capacity is exactly what is needed to span a richer subspace that includes directions from both attention and FFN layers.

From a more formal standpoint, recent work on *intrinsic dimension* of neural networks shows that for very large models many task‑specific directions lie deep inside the parameter manifold; a low‑rank perturbation confined to a small subspace can miss them entirely (Li et al., 2020). By placing adapters everywhere we increase the *intrinsic rank* of the trainable subspace, making it more likely to intersect with the optimal fine‑tuning direction even when each individual adapter remains low rank.

Thus, while no theorem in QLoRA proves “full‑layer LoRA is necessary for >7 B models”, both empirical evidence (the gap disappears when all layers receive adapters) and these capacity arguments provide a clear justification.

### Consequences for scaling laws of PEFT

The key takeaway for scaling PEFT methods is that **the ratio of trainable parameters to total model parameters is not constant across scales**. For small models you can get away with < 0.5 % trainable parameters; as you move past ≈ 6–8 B you need an order of magnitude more—still well below 5 %—to retain full fine‑tuning performance. This modifies earlier scaling intuitions that suggested a fixed low‑rank budget would be sufficient regardless of size.

In practice this means:

* When designing LoRA‑style adapters for > 7 B models you should enable them on **all linear modules**, not just Q/V.
* The rank $r$ can stay modest (the paper uses $r=64$ or $r=128$) because the sheer number of targeted layers supplies most of the extra capacity.
* Memory consumption does not increase dramatically: as noted in §3, gradient memory dominates training cost, so adding adapters to every layer adds only a few megabytes while still fitting comfortably within a single GPU thanks to QLoRA’s 4‑bit quantisation and paged optimisers.

Overall, Figure 1 teaches us that “default” LoRA hyperparameters are **scale‑dependent**; what works for 1–3 B models no longer suffices at 7 B and above. The remedy is simple – inject adapters everywhere – and it is supported both by an intuitive view of where most model capacity resides (the feed‑forward network) and by a rough parameter‑count argument showing why only expanding Q/V cannot cover enough directions in weight space.
