\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### How does the Elo rating aggregation work for GPT‑4 vs. human pairwise comparisons, and why might their rankings disagree?
Question:
The paper aggregates pairwise judgments into Elo scores using 10 000 random seeds. I would like clarification on:
1. How are ties handled in Elo updates?
2. What does a K‑factor of 32 imply for convergence speed given many matches per model?
3. Why could GPT‑4 assign higher scores to its own outputs compared to human annotators—does this stem from systematic bias in prompt phrasing or from different evaluation criteria?
Answer:
The Elo scores in the paper are obtained from the *tournament‑style* pair‑wise comparisons that the authors run for every model on each benchmark prompt.  
In each “match’’ two model outputs (or a model output vs. a human answer) are shown to an annotator – either a human rater or GPT‑4 – and the annotator decides which response is better, or whether they are tied.  
All of the matches are then fed into the standard Elo rating system (the same algorithm that is used in chess).  
The authors repeat the whole sampling‑and‑rating process 10 000 times with different random seeds in order to average out any ordering effects (e.g., which pair of models is presented first).

Below is a concise answer to each of your three points, staying faithful to the details that appear in the QLoRA paper.

---

### 1.  How are ties handled in the Elo updates?

When an annotator declares a **tie**, the outcome for that match is treated as a draw: both players receive an actual score of ½.  
In the Elo update formula  

\[
R_{\text{new}} = R_{\text{old}} + K\bigl(S - E\bigr),
\]

\(S\) is set to 0.5 for each side, while \(E\) (the expected win probability computed from the current ratings) remains unchanged.  
Consequently both models receive exactly the same rating increment (or decrement) for that match.  
This treatment is explicitly mentioned in the “Elo rating” paragraph of §4.2, where the authors say that they “simplify the rating scheme to a three‑class labeling problem that accounts for ties”.

---

### 2.  What does a K‑factor of 32 imply for convergence speed given many matches per model?

The **K‑factor** determines how much a single result can move a player’s rating.  
With \(K=32\) a completely unexpected upset (e.g., a low‑rated model beating a high‑rated one) changes each model’s rating by up to \(32 \times (1 - E)\), which at most equals 32 points because \(E\le 1\).  

In practice each model participates in **thousands of matches** because the tournament is sampled repeatedly (10 000 seeds).  
When many independent outcomes are aggregated, individual match effects cancel out and the overall rating stabilises quickly; the variance of the rating after \(n\) matches scales roughly as \(\sqrt{K^{2}/n}\).  
Hence with \(K=32\) and several thousand games per model the Elo estimates converge after only a few hundred matches, which is why the authors can report stable rankings even though they use random seeding.  A larger K would make the ratings more volatile (slower convergence), while a much smaller K would need many more matches to reach the same precision.

---

### 3.  Why can GPT‑4 give higher scores to its own outputs than human annotators do?

Two closely related factors explain why GPT‑4 sometimes favours its own responses:

1. **Prompt‑induced systematic bias** – The evaluation prompt asks GPT‑4 to compare *two* answers and assign a score “out of ten” to each, with an explicit instruction to “pick the best response or declare a tie”. Because GPT‑4 sees its own answer in the prompt, it tends to treat it as more credible and often assigns it a slightly higher relative score (the paper notes an order effect: “GPT‑4 increasing the score of the response occurring earlier in its prompt”). This bias stems from how the evaluation prompt frames the task rather than from any intrinsic superiority of GPT‑4.

2. **Different evaluation criteria** – Human raters usually judge based on *overall usefulness, factual correctness and style*, whereas GPT‑4’s scoring is driven by its internal language‑model probabilities plus whatever heuristics it has learned from its pre‑training data about what makes an answer “good”. Those heuristics can weight fluency or alignment with its own generation style more heavily than humans do, leading to systematic over‑rating of GPT‑4 outputs relative to human judgments.

The paper’s Section 4.2 explicitly reports that “GPT‑4 assigns significantly higher scores to its own outputs compared to human ratings, Elo of 1348 vs 1176”, and attributes part of this discrepancy to the ordering effect mentioned above. The authors therefore caution that while GPT‑4 provides a cheap proxy for human evaluation, its scores must be interpreted with these biases in mind.

---

**In short:** ties are treated as draws (½–½) in the Elo update; a K = 32 gives enough flexibility for individual upsets but still lets ratings stabilise after only a few hundred matches because each model plays thousands of games; and GPT‑4’s higher self‑scores arise from prompt‑order bias and from its own internal criteria for what constitutes a “good” answer, not from any fundamental superiority over humans.
