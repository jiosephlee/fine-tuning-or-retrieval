\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How is numerical error measured for FP8 FlashAttention‑3 and why is RMSE used?
Question:
Section 6.3 compares RMSE against an FP64 reference on synthetic data with outliers. I'm unclear whether RMSE is computed on the final output O or also on intermediate matrices like S or P. Also, does keeping softmax accumulators in FP32 affect this metric? Would another metric such as max absolute error give a different picture of where errors are introduced?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the numerical‑error experiments (Section 6.3) the authors evaluate **only the final attention output $\mathbf O$**.  
For each synthetic test case they first run a *reference* implementation of the full attention formula in **FP64** (the ground‑truth). Then they run the three kernels that they want to compare – FlashAttention‑2 (FP16), FlashAttention‑3 (FP16) and the FP8‑FlashAttention‑3 variant – and compute the **root‑mean‑squared error (RMSE)** between the two $\mathbf O$ tensors:

\[
\text{RMSE}= \sqrt{\frac{1}{N d}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{d}
\bigl(O_{ij}^{\text{kernel}}-O_{ij}^{\text{FP64}}\bigr)^{2}} .
\]

No intermediate quantities such as the score matrix $\mathbf S=\alpha\mathbf Q\mathbf K^{\!\top}$ or the soft‑max matrix $\mathbf P=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf S)$ are involved in the reported numbers; those matrices are never materialised in global memory and their per‑block values are overwritten as the algorithm proceeds. Consequently, the RMSE in Table 5 reflects *the cumulative effect* of every rounding, quantisation, and accumulation step that ultimately contributes to $\mathbf O$.

---

### Why keeping softmax accumulators in FP32 matters

The soft‑max computation requires two numerically sensitive operations:

1. **Row‑wise subtraction of the maximum** (to avoid overflow in the exponentials);
2. **Row‑wise sum of the exponentials** (used to normalise).

Both steps involve reductions over many elements. If these reductions are performed in low precision (e.g., FP16), rounding errors accumulate quickly, especially when the synthetic data contain outliers (the heavy‑tailed term with variance 100).  

FlashAttention‑2 and FlashAttention‑3 deliberately perform these reductions **in FP32**, even though the surrounding GEMMs run in FP16 or FP8. The FP32 accumulator provides a *much larger dynamic range* and a *smaller unit round‑off*, so the row maxima and row sums are far more accurate. As a result, both FP16 kernels achieve roughly **1.7 × lower RMSE** than a “standard” (non‑fused) attention implementation that does all intermediate work in FP16. The improvement comes entirely from this higher‑precision softmax path; it does not depend on any other algorithmic change between FlashAttention‑2 and FlashAttention‑3.

---

### Would another metric give a different picture?

RMSE is an *average* error measure: it squares every entry’s deviation before averaging, so many small errors are down‑weighted while large outliers dominate because of squaring. In practice this is useful for quantifying how much overall fidelity is lost, which is what matters for downstream training where loss functions are themselves averages.

If one were to report **maximum absolute error** ($\max_{i,j}|O_{ij}^{\text{kernel}}-O_{ij}^{\text{FP64}}|$) you would see a different distribution of error sources:

* The max error would be heavily influenced by any single extreme outlier that survives quantisation or suffers from catastrophic cancellation during the softmax normalisation.
* Because FlashAttention’s softmax reduction is done in FP32, the worst‑case error is still largely controlled by the quantisation of $\mathbf Q,\mathbf K,\mathbf V$ (for FP8) rather than by softmax itself. In other words, you would likely observe that the *relative ranking* of kernels (standard → FP8 baseline → FP8‑FlashAttention‑3) remains similar, but the absolute numbers could be an order of magnitude larger for those rare outliers.

Therefore, max‑abs error could highlight *where* an individual entry goes wrong (e.g., a particular row containing an outlier), whereas RMSE gives a single scalar that summarises overall degradation. The authors chose RMSE because it aligns with prior work on attention numerical stability (e.g., Golden et al., 2024) and because it smooths out stochastic variation caused by the random orthogonal matrix used in incoherent processing.

---

### Summary

* The reported RMSE values are computed **only on the final output $\mathbf O$** against an FP64 reference.
* Intermediate matrices ($\mathbf S,\mathbf P$) are not measured; their errors are implicitly folded into the final RMSE.
* Performing softmax reductions in **FP32** dramatically reduces rounding error, which explains why both FlashAttention‑2 and FlashAttention‑3 attain ~1.7× lower RMSE than a naïve FP16 implementation.
* Using a different metric such as maximum absolute error would show larger absolute numbers and would be more sensitive to isolated outliers, but it would not change the qualitative conclusion that FP32 softmax accumulation and block quantisation/incoherent processing improve numerical fidelity.

Thus, RMSE is an appropriate metric for the paper’s goal—demonstrating that their low‑precision kernels maintain comparable overall accuracy to high‑precision baselines while gaining substantial speedups.
