Chapter 1: Direct Preference Optimization: Motivation and Background
# Why Steer Large Language Models?

Large language models (LLMs) are trained on massive corpora that contain the full breadth of human writing: news articles, code repositories, social media posts, and fiction.  As a result, an LLM learns a rich collection of factual knowledge, linguistic patterns, and reasoning skills.  However, the data also reflects the full diversity of human goals, beliefs, and errors.  When such a model is deployed “as‑is”, its generations may echo undesirable or unsafe behaviors that were present in the training text.  For example, a coding assistant that has seen both correct and incorrect snippets might reproduce common bugs; a conversational agent that has read many opinionated forum posts might repeat misinformation or hateful tropes.

The core need for **steering**—or controllable generation—is to extract the *desired* portion of the model’s capabilities while suppressing the rest.  Desired behavior can be defined in many ways: adhering to a user’s instruction, maintaining a particular tone (e.g., politeness or positivity), avoiding disallowed content, or faithfully following a code style guide.  In practice, steering is essential for safety, reliability, and usefulness: a user expects the system to answer a question truthfully, not to hallucinate, and to do so in a manner that matches the user’s intent.  Without explicit steering, the model’s output is essentially a random draw from a distribution that was never optimized for any particular downstream objective.

# Limitations of Unsupervised Pre‑training

Unsupervised pre‑training treats every token in the training set as an equally valid target.  The objective is to maximize the likelihood

\[
\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{unsup}}}\bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where \(x\) denotes a context and \(y\) the next token (or sequence) to predict.  This objective yields a **generic** language model that captures statistical regularities but has **no notion of desirability**.  Two fundamental shortcomings arise:

1. **No Preference Signal.**  The model never sees a signal that says “this answer is better than that one”.  Consequently, it cannot learn to prefer one continuation over another when both are plausible.
2. **Distribution Drift.**  The training distribution includes many low‑quality or harmful texts.  Since the objective rewards reproducing the data distribution, the model will allocate probability mass to such undesirable generations.

Because pre‑training is purely unsupervised, any attempt to make the model obey a downstream specification must happen *after* pre‑training, via an additional fine‑tuning stage that introduces an external signal of what constitutes a good output.

# Overview of RLHF and Its Components

**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)** is the de‑facto pipeline for aligning LLMs with human preferences.  It consists of three sequential phases:

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT).**  
   Starting from the pre‑trained LM \(\pi^{\text{pre}}\), a developer collects a dataset of high‑quality prompt–response pairs \(\{(x_i, y_i^{\text{high}})\}\).  The model is then fine‑tuned by maximum likelihood to obtain a policy \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) that already exhibits basic instruction following.

2. **Preference Collection and Reward Modeling.**  
   The SFT policy is used to generate *pairs* of completions \((y_{1}, y_{2})\) for each prompt \(x\).  Human annotators label which completion they prefer, denoted \(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x\).  Assuming a latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\) governs these preferences, a parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) is trained by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences under the **Bradley‑Terry** model:

   \[
   p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
   \]

   The corresponding loss is a binary cross‑entropy:

   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi})=
   -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[
   \log\sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)
   \bigr],
   \]
   where \(\sigma\) is the logistic function.

3. **Reinforcement Learning Fine‑Tuning.**  
   The learned reward is used as a scalar feedback signal for a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  The optimization problem balances reward maximization with **KL‑regularization** to keep the policy close to the reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}\):

   \[
   \max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[
   r_{\phi}(x,y)
   \bigr]
   \;-\;
   \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
   \]

   In practice this objective is optimized with a policy‑gradient method such as **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**.  The KL term acts as a “conservatism” constraint, preventing the policy from straying into regions where the reward model is unreliable.

# Challenges of Reinforcement Learning for LMs

Although RLHF has produced impressive chat‑bots and code assistants, its reliance on reinforcement learning introduces several practical difficulties.

1. **Algorithmic Complexity.**  
   RLHF requires training *two* neural networks (the reward model and the policy) and coordinating their updates.  PPO itself involves multiple loss terms (policy loss, value‑function loss, entropy bonus) and a clipping mechanism that must be tuned carefully.  The overall pipeline is considerably more involved than a single supervised fine‑tuning run.

2. **Training Instability.**  
   Policy‑gradient methods suffer from high variance gradient estimates, especially when the action space is the set of all possible token sequences.  The KL constraint mitigates divergence but can itself cause oscillations if \(\beta\) is mis‑specified.  Reward over‑optimization is another known pathology: the policy learns to exploit quirks of the learned reward model, producing degenerate or unsafe outputs.

3. **Computational Cost.**  
   PPO requires *on‑policy* sampling: at each update step the current policy must generate many completions to estimate the expected reward and the KL term.  This sampling loop is expensive because generating even a single token sequence from a large transformer costs non‑trivial GPU time.  Moreover, the value network that approximates the KL‑regularized return must be trained concurrently, adding further overhead.

4. **Hyper‑parameter Sensitivity.**  
   The KL weight \(\beta\), learning rates for the policy and value networks, clipping thresholds, and batch sizes all affect convergence.  Finding a stable configuration often demands extensive trial‑and‑error, especially when scaling to billions of parameters.

Collectively, these challenges raise the barrier for researchers and practitioners who wish to align LLMs with human preferences, limiting the rapid proliferation of safe, controllable models.

# Goal of a Simpler, More Stable Alignment Method

Given the obstacles outlined above, an ideal alignment technique would satisfy the following desiderata:

* **Single‑Stage Training.**  The method should operate directly on a policy network without requiring a separate reward model or a value network.
* **Closed‑Form Objective.**  Rather than estimating expectations through sampling, the loss should be computable analytically from the available preference pairs.
* **KL‑Awareness.**  The algorithm must still respect a constraint that keeps the fine‑tuned policy close to the original SFT model, preserving the knowledge learned during unsupervised pre‑training.
* **Computational Efficiency.**  Training should avoid the costly on‑policy roll‑outs characteristic of PPO, allowing the use of standard supervised‑learning infrastructure (e.g., mini‑batch stochastic gradient descent).
* **Stability Across Scales.**  The approach should remain robust when applied to models ranging from a few hundred million to many billions of parameters, with minimal hyper‑parameter tuning.

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is introduced exactly to meet these goals.  By re‑parameterizing the reward function in terms of the optimal policy under the KL‑constrained objective, DPO transforms the traditional RLHF objective into a binary cross‑entropy loss that can be optimized directly on the policy parameters.  The resulting algorithm eliminates the need for explicit reward modeling, policy sampling during training, and auxiliary value networks, while retaining the theoretical guarantees of the original RLHF formulation.  In the subsequent chapters we will derive the DPO objective, discuss its theoretical foundations, and present empirical evidence that it matches or exceeds the performance of PPO‑based RLHF with far lower engineering overhead.
