\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 2: Foundations of Preference‑Based Learning

Understanding how to teach a language model to behave in ways that users find helpful, safe, and aligned begins with a rigorous grasp of preference data and the probabilistic models that connect such data to latent reward functions.  In the context of large language models (LLMs), the most common form of supervision after unsupervised pre‑training is a collection of **pairwise human preferences**: for a given prompt \(x\), a human judges which of two candidate completions \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\) is better.  This chapter develops the theoretical machinery that turns these binary judgments into a well‑defined learning objective.  We start by describing the structure of the data, then derive the Bradley‑Terry model—a simple yet powerful specification of the probability that a human prefers one answer over another.  After that we extend the discussion to the more general Plackett‑Luce ranking model, which can handle lists of more than two candidates.  Throughout we emphasize the relationship between the **reward function** (a latent scalar that scores a prompt–completion pair) and the **preference distribution** that humans induce, and we explain why many reward functions are mathematically equivalent from the standpoint of preferences.  Finally we formalize the notion of **equivalence classes of reward functions**, showing how this insight underlies the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm introduced in the source paper.

---

## Human Preference Data as Pairwise Comparisons

When an LLM is prompted with a text \(x\) (for example, “Explain why the sky is blue”), it can generate many possible continuations \(y\).  Rather than asking a annotator to assign an absolute quality score to each continuation—a task that is often noisy and subjective—it is far easier to ask a simple binary question:

\[
\text{Given }x\text{, which of }y_{w}\text{ or }y_{l}\text{ is better?}
\]

The answer is recorded as a **preference label** \(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x\), where the subscript \(w\) denotes the *winner* (the preferred completion) and \(l\) the *loser* (the dispreferred completion).  By gathering many such triples \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) we obtain a dataset

\[
\mathcal{D}= \{ (x^{(i)}, y_{w}^{(i)}, y_{l}^{(i)}) \}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

which we treat as an empirical sample from an underlying but unknown **preference distribution** \(p^{*}(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x)\).  The goal of preference‑based learning is to infer a latent scalar function—called the **reward function** and denoted \(r^{*}(x, y)\)—that explains why humans make the choices they do.  In other words, we posit that
\[
p^{*}(y_{w} \succ y_{l}\mid x) = \Pr\big( r^{*}(x, y_{w}) > r^{*}(x, y_{l}) \big).
\]
Because the observations are only binary, many reward functions can give rise to the same preference distribution.  The next sections formalize a concrete probabilistic model that resolves this ambiguity.

---

## Bradley‑Terry Model Derivation and Interpretation

The **Bradley‑Terry (BT) model** is the canonical approach for converting a latent reward into a pairwise preference probability.  It assumes that the probability a human prefers response \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) for prompt \(x\) follows a *logistic* function of the difference in rewards:

\[
\boxed{
p^{*}(y_{1} \succ y_{2} \mid x)=\frac{\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{1})\big)}{\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{1})\big) + \exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{2})\big)}.
}
\tag{1}
\]

*Derivation.*  Consider that each completion \(y\) is associated with an **ability score** \(\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y)\big)\).  When two candidates compete, the probability that \(y_{1}\) wins is the proportion of its ability relative to the total ability of both contenders, yielding exactly the fraction in (1).  Equivalently, the logistic function \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) can be written as

\[
\sigma\big(r^{*}(x, y_{1}) - r^{*}(x, y_{2})\big)=\frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{2}) - r^{*}(x, y_{1})\big)},
\]

so (1) is synonymous with

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{1}) - r^{*}(x, y_{2})\big).
\tag{2}
\]

*Interpretation.*  The BT model posits that **only the difference** in rewards matters for the binary decision; adding a constant \(\kappa(x)\) to both \(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\) and \(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\) leaves the preference probability unchanged.  This invariance foreshadows the equivalence‑class notion discussed later.  Moreover, the logistic link captures the intuition that larger reward gaps produce more confident preferences, while small gaps generate near‑random choices.

*Maximum‑likelihood estimation.*  With a dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) of observed preferences, we can train a parametric reward model \(r_{\phi}(x, y)\) by maximizing the likelihood of the data under (2).  The negative log‑likelihood (NLL) becomes a binary cross‑entropy loss:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_{w}, y_{l})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\Big[ \log \sigma\big(r_{\phi}(x, y_{w}) - r_{\phi}(x, y_{l})\big) \Big].
}
\tag{3}
\]

Optimizing (3) with stochastic gradient descent yields a reward model that best explains the observed pairwise judgments under the BT assumption.

---

## Extension to Plackett‑Luce Ranking

While the BT model handles only **two** alternatives per query, many data‑collection pipelines produce **ranked lists** of three or more completions.  The **Plackett‑Luce (PL) model** generalizes the BT construction to arbitrary permutations.  Suppose for a prompt \(x\) we obtain an ordered list \((y_{(1)}, y_{(2)}, \dots, y_{(K)})\), where \(y_{(1)}\) is the top‑ranked answer, \(y_{(2)}\) the second, etc.  The PL model defines the probability of observing this ranking as a product of sequential BT‑style choices:

\[
\boxed{
p^{*}\big(y_{(1)} \succ y_{(2)} \succ \dots \succ y_{(K)} \mid x\big)
=
\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{(k)})\big)}{
\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp\!\big(r^{*}(x, y_{(j)})\big)
}.
}
\tag{4}
\]

Intuitively, the first term in the product is the probability that the top answer wins a head‑to‑head competition against all \(K\) candidates; the second term is the probability that the second answer wins among the remaining \(K-1\), and so forth.  When \(K=2\) the PL model collapses to the BT formulation (1).

*Learning with PL.*  If we have full rankings, the NLL extends naturally:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PL}}(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, \text{rank})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\Big[ \log p^{*}(\text{rank} \mid x) \Big],
\]

where \(\text{rank}\) denotes a complete ordered list.  In practice, many works still gather only pairwise comparisons because they are cheaper to collect; nevertheless, the PL framework guarantees that the same reward function \(r^{*}\) can be recovered from richer ranking data.

---

## Reward Function vs. Preference Distribution

The **reward function** \(r^{*}(x, y)\) is a latent scalar that captures the *absolute* desirability of a completion.  In contrast, the **preference distribution** \(p^{*}\) only encodes *relative* judgments (which of two or more completions is preferred).  This distinction has two important consequences:

1. **Identifiability.**  Because the BT and PL likelihoods depend only on differences \(r^{*}(x, y_{i}) - r^{*}(x, y_{j})\), any transformation that adds a prompt‑dependent constant \(f(x)\) to all rewards leaves the likelihood unchanged:
   \[
   r'(x, y) = r^{*}(x, y) + f(x) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad p^{*}\text{ unchanged.}
   \tag{5}
   \]
   Hence the reward function is **under‑specified** by preference data alone.

2. **Policy Induction.**  When we later wish to convert a reward into a language‑model **policy** \(\pi(y\mid x)\), the absolute scale of the reward matters because policies are obtained by exponentiating rewards and normalising (see the KL‑constrained optimisation derivation in the DPO paper).  Nevertheless, any two rewards related by (5) induce the *same optimal policy* under the KL‑constrained objective, as we prove in Lemma 2 of the paper.

Thus, while the reward function is a useful computational device, the *observable* object of interest is the preference distribution.  Algorithms that operate directly on the preference likelihood (such as DPO) sidestep the need to recover an arbitrary absolute reward; they only need a reward *representation* that is consistent with the observed pairwise data.

---

## Equivalence Classes of Reward Functions

Formally, we define an **equivalence relation** on reward functions:

> **Definition.** Two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) are *equivalent* (denoted \(r \sim r'\)) if there exists a function \(f\) of the prompt only such that
> \[
> r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x) \quad \forall x, y.
> \tag{6}
> \]

*Properties.*  Relation (6) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, therefore it partitions the space of all reward functions into disjoint equivalence classes.  Within a class, every member yields **identical preference probabilities** under the BT (or PL) model (Lemma 1) and the **same optimal KL‑constrained policy** (Lemma 2).  Consequently, any algorithm that only accesses preferences cannot distinguish members of the same class.

### Why This Matters for DPO

The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm exploits this equivalence in a clever way.  Starting from the KL‑constrained RL objective

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\big[ r^{*}(x,y) \big]
\;-\; \beta\; \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big),
\tag{7}
\]

one can solve for the **optimal policy** analytically:

\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r^{*}(x,y)\big),
\tag{8}
\]
with partition function \(Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r^{*}(x,y))\).

Taking logs and rearranging gives a **reparameterization** of the reward in terms of a policy:

\[
r^{*}(x,y) = \beta \log\!\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \;+\; \beta \log Z(x).
\tag{9}
\]

Because \(\log Z(x)\) depends only on \(x\), the term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) is precisely the function \(f(x)\) from (6).  Hence the reward expressed in (9) belongs to the **same equivalence class** as the original latent reward.  Substituting (9) into the BT preference probability eliminates the unknown \(Z(x)\) and yields a likelihood that depends *only* on the **policy ratio** \(\pi^{*}(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\).  This leads directly to the DPO loss (Eq. 12 in the paper):

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\!\Big[
\log \sigma\!\Big(
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\Big)
\Big].
\tag{10}
\]

Thus DPO **optimizes the policy directly** while implicitly respecting the underlying reward equivalence class.  No explicit reward model is ever trained, and no reinforcement‑learning loop is required.  The theoretical guarantee that every reward in the equivalence class yields the same optimal policy ensures that DPO does not sacrifice expressive power: any reward function that could have been learned by classical RLHF can be represented by some policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that minimizes (10).

---

## Summary

* Preference data for LLM alignment are most naturally gathered as pairwise (or ranking) judgments.
* The Bradley‑Terry model provides a clean probabilistic mapping from a latent reward difference to a binary preference probability; the Plackett‑Luce model generalises this to full rankings.
* Because the BT/PL likelihoods depend only on reward *differences*, adding a prompt‑dependent constant yields an **equivalence class** of reward functions that are indistinguishable from the viewpoint of preferences.
* This equivalence also extends to the KL‑constrained RL objective: all members of a class induce the same optimal policy.
* Direct Preference Optimization leverages the reparameterization \(r = \beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})+\beta\log Z\) to eliminate the latent reward altogether, turning the RLHF objective into a simple binary cross‑entropy loss over policies.
* The theoretical foundation—pairwise modelling, equivalence classes, and the closed‑form optimal policy—explains why DPO can match or surpass traditional RLHF pipelines while being far simpler to implement.

In the next chapters we will build on these foundations to describe the full DPO algorithm, its practical implementation, and the empirical results that demonstrate its effectiveness across sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue tasks.

**Standard RLHF Pipeline**

In this chapter we unpack the conventional three‑stage Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) workflow that underlies most modern preference‑aligned language models.  The pipeline consists of (1) **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)** of a large pre‑trained language model on high‑quality text, (2) **Preference collection and reward‑model fitting**, and (3) **Reinforcement‑learning fine‑tuning** of the policy under a KL‑constrained objective.  Although the steps are conceptually simple, each contains subtle design choices—such as the treatment of the partition function, reward normalisation, and the KL penalty—that critically affect stability and performance.  We therefore present each component in turn, grounding the discussion in the mathematical formulation introduced by the RLHF literature.

---

## Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT) of a Pretrained LM

Large language models are first trained in an unsupervised fashion on massive corpora of text, learning to predict the next token given a context.  The resulting model, which we denote by a probability distribution \(\pi_{\text{pre}}(y\mid x)\) over completions \(y\) conditioned on a prompt \(x\), possesses broad world knowledge but no explicit bias toward any downstream objective (e.g., helpfulness, safety, or sentiment control).

To endow the model with a task‑specific orientation, practitioners perform **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)**.  A dataset \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{M}\) containing prompts and high‑quality human‑written responses is assembled.  The SFT objective is the standard maximum‑likelihood loss

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{SFT}}}\bigl[\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\]

where \(\theta\) are the parameters of the fine‑tuned policy \(\pi_{\theta}\).  Optimising \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}\) moves the model toward the distribution of the curated demonstrations while preserving the underlying fluency and factuality learned during pre‑training.  The resulting policy—often called \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)—serves as the **reference policy** for the subsequent RLHF stages.

---

## Collecting Preference Pairs and Fitting a Reward Model

Supervised demonstrations are useful, but they are expensive to acquire at scale.  Human annotators can more readily judge **relative** quality: given two candidate completions for the same prompt, they label which one they prefer.  This yields a dataset of preference triples

\[
\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{ (x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_{w}, y^{(i)}_{l}) \bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where \(y_{w}\) is the preferred ( “winning” ) response and \(y_{l}\) the dispreferred ( “losing” ) one for prompt \(x\).  The central modelling assumption, inherited from the Bradley–Terry (BT) framework, is that there exists an unknown latent reward function \(r^{*}(x,y)\) such that the probability of a human preferring \(y_{w}\) over \(y_{l}\) is

\[
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x) \;=\;
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}%
{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr) + \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)}.
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) captures the intuition that larger reward values correspond to higher preference likelihoods, but it does so only up to an additive function of the prompt (see the equivalence discussion later).

A **parametric reward model** \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) (typically a small head added to the frozen transformer of \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\)) is trained to maximise the likelihood of the observed preferences.  Substituting \(r_{\phi}\) into (1) and taking the negative log‑likelihood yields the **reward‑model loss**

\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(\phi) \;=\;
-\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)
\Bigr],
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\) is the logistic (sigmoid) function.  Minimising (2) is equivalent to performing binary classification: the model must assign higher scalar scores to the preferred completion for each pair.  Because the BT likelihood depends only on differences of rewards, the absolute scale of \(r_{\phi}\) is unidentifiable.  In practice, researchers **normalise** the reward predictions so that \(\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]=0\); this reduces variance and stabilises subsequent reinforcement‑learning updates.

---

## KL‑Constrained Reward Maximisation Objective

Having obtained a surrogate reward function \(r_{\phi}\), the next stage seeks a **policy** \(\pi_{\theta}\) that generates high‑reward completions while staying close to the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}=\pi^{\text{SFT}}\).  The proximity constraint prevents the policy from drifting into regions of the output space where the reward model has not been trained and thus might be unreliable.  The standard formulation is a **KL‑constrained optimisation problem**:

\[
\max_{\theta}
\;\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,\bigr]}_{\text{expected reward}}
\;-\;
\beta\,
\underbrace{\mathbb{D}_{\!\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr]}_{\text{KL penalty}},
\tag{3}
\]

where \(\beta>0\) balances reward maximisation against deviation from the reference distribution.  The KL term is a **regulariser** that penalises large changes in token‑wise probabilities.  Intuitively, when \(\beta\) is small the optimiser may aggressively chase reward peaks (potentially over‑optimising), whereas a large \(\beta\) forces the new policy to remain a near‑copy of \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

Equation (3) is the objective actually optimised by the majority of RLHF pipelines.  It is a **constrained reinforcement‑learning** problem: rewards are defined by a learned model, and the constraint is expressed via a divergence rather than a hard bound.

---

## Policy Optimisation with PPO / REINFORCE

Because language generation involves discrete token sequences, the gradient of (3) cannot be computed directly via back‑propagation through the sampling process.  Instead, the community has adopted **policy‑gradient** methods that estimate gradients from sampled trajectories.

### REINFORCE‑style Gradient

A naïve REINFORCE estimator for (3) writes the gradient as

\[
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}(\theta) \;\approx\;
\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}}\!\bigl[
\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y) - \beta \log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\bigr)
\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)
\bigr],
\tag{4}
\]

where the term inside the parentheses is the **advantage**: reward minus a KL‑based baseline.  In practice, the expectation is approximated by sampling a batch of prompts, generating completions from the current policy, and computing Monte‑Carlo estimates of the advantage.

### Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)

Pure REINFORCE suffers from high variance and unstable updates, especially for large language models.  **Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)** introduces a clipped surrogate objective that restricts the ratio of new to old policy probabilities:

\[
L^{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = 
\mathbb{E}_{x,\,y}\!\Bigl[
\min\!\Bigl(
\underbrace{r(\theta) A}_{\text{unclipped}},
\;\underbrace{\text{clip}(r(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon) A}_{\text{clipped}}
\Bigr)
\Bigr],
\tag{5}
\]

where \(r(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y\mid x)}\) is the probability ratio, \(A\) denotes the advantage (including the KL penalty), and \(\epsilon\) controls the clipping range.  PPO thereby guarantees that each update does not drastically alter the policy, further reinforcing the KL constraint.

Both REINFORCE and PPO require **on‑policy sampling** at every optimisation step, which entails generating many token sequences from the current model.  This sampling dramatically increases computational cost and introduces additional sources of stochasticity.

---

## Practical Issues

### Partition Function

In the theoretical solution of the KL‑constrained problem, the optimal policy has a closed‑form expression

\[
\pi^{*}(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr).
\tag{6}
\]

The normalising constant \(Z(x)\) (the **partition function**) is a sum over all possible completions, which is intractable for even modest vocabularies.  Consequently, RLHF algorithms bypass explicit computation of \(Z(x)\) by using Monte‑Carlo samples to estimate gradients, rather than evaluating (6) directly.

### Reward Normalisation

Because the BT likelihood depends on reward differences, adding any function of the prompt alone, \(f(x)\), to all reward values leaves the preference probabilities unchanged:

\[
r'(x,y) = r(x,y) + f(x) \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x) = p^{*}(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x).
\]

Without fixing this **additive ambiguity**, the learned rewards may drift to arbitrarily large magnitudes, leading to numerical instability in the KL‑penalised advantage term of (4).  Normalising rewards to zero mean (or zero variance) across the dataset is a simple remedy that also improves the signal‑to‑noise ratio of the policy‑gradient estimator.

### KL‑Penalty Tuning

The hyperparameter \(\beta\) in (3) dictates the trade‑off between adhering to the reference policy and exploiting the reward model.  Small \(\beta\) values encourage aggressive optimisation but risk **reward over‑optimization**, where the policy exploits quirks of the learned reward and produces pathological outputs.  Large \(\beta\) values preserve the original language model’s fluency but may under‑utilise the preference signal.  In practice, researchers either set a target KL divergence and adapt the reward scale, or tune \(\beta\) manually using a validation set of human‑rated outputs.

---

### Summary

The conventional RLHF pipeline proceeds through three clearly delineated stages:

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)** aligns a pre‑trained LM with high‑quality demonstrations, yielding a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
2. **Preference collection** provides binary comparisons, which are used to fit a **reward model** via the maximum‑likelihood loss (2).  Normalisation resolves the inherent additive ambiguity of the reward scale.
3. **KL‑constrained policy optimisation** maximises the expected learned reward while penalising deviation from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) using a KL term (3).  Gradient‑based reinforcement‑learning methods such as REINFORCE or PPO are employed to approximate the intractable expectations, introducing practical concerns about sampling cost, variance, the partition function, and the choice of \(\beta\).

Understanding each component, especially the mathematical connections between the reward model, the KL constraint, and the policy‑gradient estimators, is essential for both reproducing existing RLHF systems and for appreciating newer approaches—such as Direct Preference Optimization—that aim to bypass the reinforcement‑learning loop entirely.

**Deriving Direct Preference Optimization**

In this chapter we walk through, in full mathematical detail, how the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) algorithm emerges from the standard reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) objective.  By performing a careful change of variables we replace the explicit reward model with an equivalent expression that depends only on the language‑model policy and a fixed reference policy.  The result is a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss that can be minimized with ordinary supervised‑learning tools, eliminating the need for reinforcement‑learning loops, reward‑model training, or costly estimation of partition functions.  All derivations follow the notation and definitions introduced in the original DPO paper.

---

### 1.  Optimal policy for the KL‑constrained RL problem  

RLHF formulates the fine‑tuning of a language model as a constrained maximization problem.  Given a prompt \(x\) we denote by \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) the policy we wish to learn, and by \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) a fixed *reference* policy—typically the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model obtained before any preference learning.  The objective balances two desiderata:

1. **Reward maximization:** the expected reward \(r(x,y)\) (learned from human preferences) should be high;  
2. **Conservatism:** the new policy should not drift arbitrarily far from the reference, enforced with a KL‑divergence penalty weighted by a scalar \(\beta>0\).

Formally,
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[\,r(x,y)\,\bigr]\;
-\;\beta\,
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).
\tag{1}
\]

Because the KL term is a convex regularizer, (1) is an instance of *exponential‑family* variational inference.  The optimal solution can be derived by introducing a Lagrange multiplier for the normalization constraint \(\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=1\) and solving for the stationary point.  The detailed derivation appears in Appendix A of the DPO paper; the result is a closed‑form expression for the optimal policy:

\[
\boxed{\;
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr)
\;},
\tag{2}
\]
where the *partition function*
\[
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr)
\tag{3}
\]
ensures that \(\pi_{r}(\cdot\mid x)\) is a valid probability distribution.  Equation (2) tells us exactly how a reward function translates into an optimal policy under the KL‑constraint.

---

### 2.  Re‑parameterising the reward in terms of policy and reference model  

Equation (2) can be inverted to express the reward *as a function of the policy* rather than the other way around.  Taking logarithms,
\[
\log \pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)+\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)-\log Z(x).
\]
Rearranging gives the *reward‑policy correspondence*:
\[
\boxed{\;
r(x,y)=\beta \,\log\!\frac{\pi_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\;+\;\beta\log Z(x)
\;}.
\tag{4}
\]
The term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) depends **only on the prompt** \(x\); it does not vary with the concrete completion \(y\).  Consequently, any two reward functions that differ by a function of \(x\) alone induce exactly the same *preference probabilities*, because preferences compare *differences* of rewards.

We define the *implicit reward* induced by a policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) as
\[
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)\;\triangleq\;\beta\,
\log\!\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\tag{5}
\]
Up to the additive term \(\beta\log Z_{\theta}(x)\), \(\hat r_{\theta}\) plays the same role as the explicit reward model \(r_{\phi}\) used in conventional RLHF pipelines.

---

### 3.  Cancellation of the partition function in the Bradley‑Terry likelihood  

Human preference data are modeled with the *Bradley‑Terry* (BT) pairwise comparison model.  For a prompt \(x\) and two candidate completions \(y_{1},y_{2}\) the probability that a human prefers \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) is assumed to be
\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
=
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
     {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)} 
\;=\;
\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\tag{6}
\]
where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) is the logistic function.

Substituting the re‑parameterised reward (4) into (6) yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
&=
\sigma\Bigl(
\underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}}_{\hat r^{*}(x,y_{1})}
-
\underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}}_{\hat r^{*}(x,y_{2})}
\Bigr) .
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]
The additive constants \(\beta\log Z^{*}(x)\) that would appear from (4) cancel because they appear with opposite signs in the difference.  Hence the *partition function never appears* in the likelihood of observed preferences.  This cancellation is the key insight: we can write the likelihood of human comparisons directly in terms of the *policy* and the *reference* policy, without ever having to compute \(Z(x)\).

---

### 4.  Derivation of the DPO loss (Eq. 8)  

Given a dataset of pairwise preferences 
\[
\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{(x^{(i)},\,y_{w}^{(i)},\,y_{l}^{(i)})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where for each prompt \(x^{(i)}\) the completion \(y_{w}^{(i)}\) is preferred over \(y_{l}^{(i)}\), we can write the *maximum‑likelihood* objective for a parametrised policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) using (7):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)
=
-\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\log \sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}
\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{8}
\]
Equation (8) is precisely the loss that DPO minimizes.  Note that it has the same functional form as the reward‑model loss (the binary cross‑entropy used to train \(r_{\phi}\) in Eq. (2) of the preliminaries) but with the *policy* \(\pi_{\theta}\) in place of the reward model.  Consequently, a single stochastic‑gradient descent pass on (8) simultaneously learns a policy that (i) respects the human preference data and (ii) stays close to the reference policy because the log‑ratio inside the logistic is scaled by \(\beta\).

---

### 5.  Interpretation of the DPO gradient and importance weighting  

To understand how (8) nudges the model, differentiate with respect to \(\theta\).  Let us define the *implicit reward difference* for a pair \((y_{w},y_{l})\):
\[
\Delta\hat r_{\theta}(x) \;\triangleq\;
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})
=
\beta\Bigl[\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
-
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\Bigr].
\]
The gradient of the loss is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}
&=
-\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\sigma\!\bigl(-\Delta\hat r_{\theta}(x)\bigr)\;
\nabla_{\theta}\Delta\hat r_{\theta}(x)
\Bigr]  \\
&=
-\;\beta\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)\;
\Bigl(
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)
-
\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)
\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{9}
\end{aligned}
\]
The factor
\[
w(x,y_{w},y_{l}) \;=\;
\sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)
\]
acts as an **importance weight**.  When the current policy already assigns a much higher implicit reward to the preferred completion (i.e. \(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\gg\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\)), the logistic term is close to zero, and the gradient contribution of that pair vanishes.  Conversely, if the policy mistakenly prefers the dispreferred completion, the weight approaches one, and the gradient strongly pushes probability mass from \(y_{l}\) to \(y_{w}\).  This adaptive weighting prevents the naive “unlikelihood” update (which would treat every pair equally) from over‑penalising correct predictions and causing degeneration, a phenomenon observed empirically in the DPO ablation studies.

Thus DPO can be interpreted as a *soft* unlikelihood update: it always encourages the preferred answer and discourages the dispreferred one, but the strength of the encouragement/penalisation is modulated by how badly the current policy violates the human preference.

---

### 6.  Algorithmic pipeline: from data collection to DPO optimisation  

Putting the pieces together, the complete DPO training workflow consists of three conceptual stages:

1. **Preference data collection.**  
   - Sample two completions \((y_{1},y_{2})\) from the *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for each prompt \(x\) in a curated set.  
   - Present the pair to human annotators (or a high‑quality classifier) who indicate the preferred completion; record the triple \((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\).  
   - The resulting dataset \(\mathcal{D}\) is static; no further interaction with the model is required.

2. **Reference model preparation.**  
   - In practice \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the SFT model obtained by supervised fine‑tuning on high‑quality demonstrations.  
   - If an SFT model is unavailable, one can construct \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) by maximising the likelihood of the *preferred* completions in \(\mathcal{D}\) (i.e. standard supervised learning on \((x, y_{w})\) pairs).

3. **DPO optimisation.**  
   - Initialise the policy parameters \(\theta\) to the reference model parameters (i.e. \(\pi_{\theta}\gets \pi_{\text{ref}}\)).  
   - Minimise the DPO loss (8) using any stochastic optimiser (Adam, AdamW, etc.).  Each minibatch supplies a set of preference triples; the loss is differentiable because \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) is a standard auto‑regressive language‑model distribution (computed via the softmax over the final logits).  
   - The hyper‑parameter \(\beta\) controls the trade‑off between obeying preferences (large \(\beta\) → stronger preference signal) and staying close to the reference (small \(\beta\) makes the KL term dominant).  In practice a small sweep (e.g. \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\)) suffices.

The overall algorithm is succinctly expressed in pseudocode:

```
Input: preference set D = {(x, y_w, y_l)}, reference policy π_ref,
       KL‑strength β, learning rate η
Initialize θ ← parameters of π_ref
repeat
    Sample minibatch B ⊂ D
    Compute loss L_DPO(θ) using Eq.(8) for each (x,y_w,y_l)∈B
    θ ← θ - η ∇_θ L_DPO(θ)
until convergence
Output: fine‑tuned policy π_θ
```

Because the loss involves only log‑probabilities of the current policy and the fixed reference, no sampling from \(\pi_{\theta}\) is required during training; the gradients are exact (up to Monte‑Carlo minibatch noise).  Consequently, DPO is computationally lightweight, stable, and easy to implement within any existing language‑model fine‑tuning codebase.

---

### 7.  Summary  

We have derived Direct Preference Optimization step by step:

1. Solved the KL‑constrained RL objective to obtain the optimal policy (2).  
2. Inverted that relation to express the reward as a log‑ratio between policy and reference (4).  
3. Inserted the re‑parameterised reward into the Bradley‑Terry likelihood, observing the cancellation of the partition function \(Z(x)\).  
4. Obtained a binary‑cross‑entropy loss over policies (8) that can be minimised directly.  
5. Interpreted the resulting gradient (9) as a weighted unlikelihood update, where the weights automatically focus learning on incorrectly ordered pairs.  
6. Described the practical workflow—pre‑collecting preference pairs, fixing a reference policy, and training with ordinary supervised‑learning machinery.

Through this change of variables, DPO sidesteps the need for an explicit reward model and for reinforcement‑learning optimisation, yet it optimises exactly the same KL‑constrained objective that underlies traditional RLHF.  The derivation shows that a language model, once fine‑tuned with DPO, is *implicitly* a reward model: its log‑ratio to the reference policy encodes the reward that best explains the observed human preferences.  This duality underlies the simplicity and effectiveness of DPO as reported in the experimental sections of the paper.

**Theoretical Properties of Direct Preference Optimization**

# Equivalence Relation on Reward Functions  

A central insight of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is that the reward function used in the traditional reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipeline is *not* uniquely identifiable from preference data.  Preference data tells us only which of two (or more) completions a human prefers; it never reveals an absolute numeric value of reward for any single completion.  Consequently, many reward functions give rise to exactly the same preference probabilities.  

Formally, let \(r : \mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\) be any reward function that maps a prompt \(x\) and a generated response \(y\) to a scalar score.  Define an **equivalence relation** \(\sim\) on the space of reward functions by  

\[
r \sim r' \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \exists f:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\;\text{s.t.}\; r(x,y)-r'(x,y)=f(x)\quad\forall (x,y).
\]

In words, two rewards are equivalent if they differ only by a term that depends solely on the prompt \(x\) and not on the particular completion \(y\).  The function \(f\) can be thought of as a *baseline* that is added uniformly to all completions for a given prompt.  Because preference models such as Bradley–Terry (or the more general Plackett–Luce family) depend only on *differences* of rewards, adding a prompt‑wise constant does not affect the induced preference distribution.

This equivalence partitions the set of all possible reward functions into disjoint **equivalence classes**.  Within each class every member yields identical pairwise preference probabilities and, as we shall see, the same optimal policy under the KL‑constrained RL objective.

# Lemma 1: Identical Preference Distributions Within an Equivalence Class  

*Statement.*  
If \(r\sim r'\) then for any prompt \(x\) and any two completions \(y_{1},y_{2}\),

\[
p_{r}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=p_{r'}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x),
\]

where the probability \(p_{r}\) is defined by the Bradley–Terry model  

\[
p_{r}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr),\qquad 
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]

*Proof Sketch.*  
Since \(r\sim r'\), there exists a function \(f\) with \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)-f(x)\).  Substituting into the difference of rewards gives  

\[
r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2})= \bigl[r(x,y_{1})-f(x)\bigr]-\bigl[r(x,y_{2})-f(x)\bigr]
= r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2}).
\]

Thus the logits fed to the logistic function are identical, and the resulting probabilities coincide. ∎  

*Intuition.*  
The Bradley–Terry model makes a binary comparison by looking at how much more “desirable’’ one completion is than the other.  Adding the same constant to both scores does not change their relative ordering; consequently the model’s predictions are invariant to such a shift.

# Lemma 2: Identical Optimal Policies Within an Equivalence Class  

*Statement.*  
Consider the KL‑constrained RL objective used in RLHF  

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\!\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr] 
\;-\;\beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr),
\tag{1}
\]

with temperature‑like coefficient \(\beta>0\) and a fixed reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  Let \(r\) and \(r'\) belong to the same equivalence class.  Then the optimal solutions \(\pi^{*}\) and \((\pi')^{*}\) of (1) for \(r\) and \(r'\) respectively are identical.

*Proof Sketch.*  
The optimal policy for any reward \(r\) under (1) is known in closed form (derivable via Lagrange multipliers or by recognizing a constrained maximum‑entropy problem):

\[
\pi^{*}_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z_{r}(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad 
Z_{r}(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

If \(r' = r - f\) with \(f\) independent of \(y\), then  

\[
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r'(x,y)\Bigr)
= \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr)\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\beta}f(x)\Bigr).
\]

Both the numerator and the normalising constant \(Z_{r'}(x)\) acquire the same multiplicative factor \(\exp(-f(x)/\beta)\), which cancels out in the fraction.  Hence \(\pi^{*}_{r'}(y\mid x)=\pi^{*}_{r}(y\mid x)\). ∎  

*Intuition.*  
The KL‑penalty term already enforces that the policy stay close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\); shifting every reward by a prompt‑specific constant merely rescales the numerator and denominator of the soft‑max in the same way, leaving the resulting distribution unchanged.

# Theorem 1: Any Reward Class Can Be Written as \(\beta\!\log\!\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)

*Statement.*  
Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be an arbitrary reward function and let \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) be a fixed reference policy.  There exists a policy \(\pi\) such that the reward  

\[
\tilde r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
\tag{3}
\]

lies in the same equivalence class as \(\mathcal{R}\).  Consequently, the optimal policy for \(\tilde r\) (by (2)) is exactly \(\pi\).

*Proof Sketch.*  
Define the **projection operator**  

\[
\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\pi_{\text{ref}}}\bigl(r\bigr)(x,y) \;:=\;
r(x,y)-\beta\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{y'}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y')\bigr)\Bigr).
\tag{4}
\]

The subtracted term depends only on \(x\); thus \(\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\pi_{\text{ref}}}(r)\) is equivalent to \(r\) under the relation defined earlier.  Plugging the optimal policy expression (2) into (4) yields  

\[
\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\pi_{\text{ref}}}(r)(x,y)
= \beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}_{r}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}.
\]

Because \(\pi^{*}_{r}\) is a valid probability distribution (by construction of \(Z_{r}(x)\)), the right‑hand side has exactly the form (3) with \(\pi=\pi^{*}_{r}\).  Hence for any original reward \(r\) we can construct an equivalent reward \(\tilde r\) that is expressed purely in terms of a policy \(\pi\) and the reference policy.  No expressive power is lost: the mapping from a reward class to a policy is surjective. ∎  

*Intuition.*  
The KL‑constrained objective (1) is a *maximum‑entropy* problem: among all policies that achieve a given expected reward, we prefer the one closest to the reference.  The solution is a Gibbs (Boltzmann) distribution over completions, with the reward appearing in the exponent.  By “absorbing’’ the partition function into a prompt‑wise baseline, we obtain a reward that is exactly the log‑odds ratio between the optimal policy and the reference.  This shows that *any* reward that could have generated the observed preferences can be represented by such a log‑odds reward, and the corresponding optimal policy is simply the policy that appears in the numerator.

# Proof Sketch and Intuition for the DPO Objective  

The derivation in the DPO paper proceeds by first writing the optimal policy (2) and then solving for the reward:

\[
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).
\tag{5}
\]

When we plug (5) into the Bradley–Terry preference model  

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=\sigma\!\bigl(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\]

the partition‑function term \(\beta\log Z(x)\) cancels because it is identical for both \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\).  Hence the human preference probability can be expressed solely through the *policy ratio*:

\[
p(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)=
\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}\mid x)}
-\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}\mid x)}\Bigr).
\tag{6}
\]

Equation (6) is exactly the likelihood used in DPO (Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} of the paper).  Maximising this likelihood with respect to a parametrised policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) therefore yields a policy that *directly* satisfies the Bradley–Terry likelihood without ever explicitly constructing a separate reward model or performing RL.  The equivalence established by Theorem 1 guarantees that the learned policy is the same as the one that would be obtained by first fitting a reward (up to an equivalence class) and then solving the KL‑constrained RL problem.

# Comparison with Actor–Critic / PPO Instability  

Traditional RLHF pipelines use an *actor‑critic* architecture: a policy (the actor) is updated by stochastic gradient ascent on an estimator of the RL objective, while a separate value network (the critic) estimates the expected future reward, often providing a baseline to reduce variance.  Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a popular instantiation that additionally clips policy updates to keep them within a trust region.

In the KL‑constrained formulation (1), the **soft value term**  

\[
V_{\text{soft}}(x) := \beta\log\!\Bigl(\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr)\Bigr)
\tag{7}
\]

appears as a normalisation constant when we rewrite the objective in terms of the reward model \(r_{\phi}\).  This term plays the role of a *baseline*: subtracting \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) from the raw reward leaves a quantity whose expectation under the current policy equals the KL‑penalty.  In practice, PPO implementations approximate \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) with a learned value head or with a Monte‑Carlo estimate based on a single reference completion.  Both approaches are noisy:

* The learned value head must be trained simultaneously with the policy, which can lead to **biased** advantage estimates if the value network lags behind.
* A single‑sample Monte‑Carlo baseline introduces high variance because the denominator \(\sum_{y}\) is replaced by a stochastic estimate.

These sources of variance can cause *policy collapse* (the policy over‑optimises on spurious reward spikes) or *instability* (oscillating KL values, divergence of the loss).  Moreover, the need for a separate critic doubles the number of parameters and the computational graph, increasing memory consumption and wall‑clock time.

DPO eliminates the critic entirely.  By re‑parameterising the reward as a log‑odds ratio (3), the soft value term cancels analytically when forming the preference likelihood (6).  Consequently the DPO loss is a **pure binary cross‑entropy** over observed preferences:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=-
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr)\Bigr].
\]

There is no separate baseline, no value network, and no importance‑sampling weighting of rewards.  The only weighting term is the logistic factor \(\sigma(\cdot)\), which already reflects how badly the current policy orders the pair (the more the implicit reward disagrees with the human label, the larger the gradient magnitude).  Empirically, this yields a **stable** optimisation landscape: each gradient step pushes the log‑probability ratio of the preferred example upward while pulling that of the dispreferred example downward, proportionally to the confidence of the mis‑order.  

Thus, DPO can be seen as an *exact* solution to the KL‑constrained RL problem within the Bradley–Terry family, while avoiding the variance‑inducing components (critic, reward normalisation, policy‑clip) that plague actor‑critic methods such as PPO.

# Role of the Soft‑Value (Normalization) Term  

The term \(V_{\text{soft}}(x)\) in Eq. (7) originates from the partition function \(Z(x)\) of the optimal Gibbs policy (2).  In the classic RL derivation, this term is necessary to ensure that the optimal policy is a proper probability distribution (i.e. that probabilities sum to one).  When we view the reward as a *latent* object, the partition function is a *prompt‑dependent* normaliser that does not affect pairwise preference probabilities because it is identical for both members of a comparison.

In DPO we **explicitly eliminate** this term by performing a change of variables from reward space to policy space.  The resulting loss depends only on *ratios* of the current policy to the reference policy, which are already normalised by construction.  Consequently:

* The KL penalty is enforced *exactly* through the log‑ratio terms; there is no need to estimate a soft value separately.
* Gradient variance is reduced because we do not subtract a noisy baseline.
* The optimisation problem becomes convex in the log‑odds domain when the policy is linear in its parameters, further enhancing stability.

In summary, the soft‑value term is an artifact of solving the constrained RL problem in reward space; once we move to policy space via the re‑parameterisation of Theorem 1, it disappears analytically, giving DPO its simplicity and robustness.

**Take‑away**  
The theoretical framework of DPO rests on the observation that reward functions are only defined up to a prompt‑wise additive constant.  This induces equivalence classes that preserve both preference likelihoods (Lemma 1) and the optimal KL‑constrained policy (Lemma 2).  Theorem 1 guarantees that any reward in such a class can be written as \(\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\), thereby turning the RLHF problem into a straight‑forward maximum‑likelihood estimation over policies.  By sidestepping the need for a separate critic and for explicit normalisation of rewards, DPO avoids the instability that frequently plagues actor‑critic methods such as PPO, while still solving exactly the same constrained optimisation problem.  This solid theoretical grounding explains why DPO can match or surpass traditional RLHF in practice while being dramatically simpler to implement and train.

**Practical Implementation Details**

In this chapter we translate the theoretical formulation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) into a concrete training pipeline that can be executed on modern deep‑learning frameworks.  We discuss every engineering decision that turns the loss in Equation (9)

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref}})=-
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}
-
\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}
\Bigr)
\Bigr]
\]

into a stable, efficient training loop.  Throughout we assume access to a static offline dataset of pairwise preferences  

\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{w},y^{(i)}_{l})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]

where each $x$ is a prompt and $y_{w},y_{l}$ are respectively the human‑preferred and dispreferred completions.  All implementation choices are grounded in the original paper; no external techniques are introduced.

## Choosing and Initializing the Reference Policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

The reference policy serves two purposes: it anchors the KL‑constraint and provides the baseline log‑probabilities $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$ that appear in the DPO loss.  In the ideal setting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ that was used to generate the preference data.  Using the same model eliminates distribution shift because the pairs in $\mathcal{D}$ were drawn from exactly this policy.

### When an SFT Model Is Available

1. **Load the checkpoint** used for the original SFT fine‑tuning (e.g. a GPT‑2‑large checkpoint that was trained on the downstream task).
2. **Freeze its parameters** during DPO training.  $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is never updated; it acts purely as a fixed baseline.
3. **Verify that the tokenizer and vocabulary** used for $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ match those of the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that will be optimized.  Mismatched tokenizers lead to inconsistent log‑probabilities.

### When an SFT Model Is Unavailable

If a pretrained SFT model does not exist for the target domain, the paper proposes constructing a surrogate reference by maximum‑likelihood training on the preferred completions alone:

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} = \arg\max_{\pi}\;
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w)\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \pi(y_w\mid x)\bigr].
\]

The steps are:

1. **Create a “Preferred‑only” dataset** consisting of pairs $(x,y_w)$ extracted from $\mathcal{D}$.
2. **Fine‑tune the base LM** (e.g. a pretrained GPT‑2 or Pythia checkpoint) on this dataset with a standard language‑model cross‑entropy loss.  Early stopping should be based on held‑out validation perplexity to avoid over‑fitting.
3. **Treat the resulting model as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$** and freeze it for the subsequent DPO stage.

Although this reference is not the exact distribution that produced the preferences, the paper shows that training $\pi_{\theta}$ against this proxy still yields strong performance, provided the reference is a reasonable approximation of the data‑generating policy.

---

## Batching, Token‑Level Log‑Probabilities, and Numerical Stability

The DPO loss requires the log‑probability of an entire sequence under both the current policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  In practice this is computed as the sum of token‑level log‑probs:

\[
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}),
\]
\[
\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}).
\]

### Efficient Batched Computation

1. **Concatenate prompts and completions** into a single tensor of shape $(B, L_{\max})$, where $B$ is the batch size and $L_{\max}$ is the longest sequence in the batch (padded as needed).  
2. **Mask the padding tokens** so that they do not contribute to the log‑probability sums.  
3. **Pass the batch through both models** in a single forward pass for each model.  Modern libraries (PyTorch, TensorFlow) allow two forward passes with the same input tensor, yielding two tensors of token‑level logits.
4. **Convert logits to log‑probabilities** using ``log_softmax``; this is numerically stable because it avoids the $\exp$‑overflow that can appear when converting logits to probabilities first.

### Avoiding Underflow/Overflow in the DPO Ratio

The core term $\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}$ can be computed safely as:

\[
\beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\bigr),
\]

so we never explicitly form the ratio.  The subtraction of two log‑probabilities is stable because each term is typically in the range $[-\!L_{\max}\cdot \log V,0]$ (with $V$ the vocabulary size).  When applying the logistic function $\sigma$, the argument may still be large in magnitude; most deep‑learning libraries implement a numerically stable ``logsigmoid`` or ``binary_cross_entropy_with_logits`` that should be used directly.

### Batching Preference Pairs

Each training example contains two completions $(y_w, y_l)$.  To keep computation vectorized:

- **Create a batch of size $2B$** where the first $B$ entries are the preferred completions and the next $B$ are the dispreferred ones.
- **Compute log‑probs for the whole batch** under both models, then split the results to obtain $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w)$, $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l)$, $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)$, and $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)$.
- **Form the DPO logits** $\Delta_i = \beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_w^{(i)})-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w^{(i)})\bigr) - \beta\bigl(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_l^{(i)})-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l^{(i)})\bigr)$.
- **Apply binary cross‑entropy** with target $1$ (since the human prefers $y_w$).  This yields exactly the negative log‑likelihood in Equation (9).

---

## Hyper‑Parameter $\beta$ and Its Effect on the KL‑Constraint

The scalar $\beta>0$ controls the strength of the KL‑regularizer that keeps $\pi_{\theta}$ close to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:

- **Small $\beta$ (e.g. $0.05$ or $0.1$)** places a strong penalty on deviating from the reference.  The DPO loss then behaves similarly to *unlikelihood* training: the model only makes modest probability adjustments to satisfy preferences.
- **Large $\beta$ (e.g. $5$)** relaxes the KL constraint, allowing the policy to shift more aggressively toward the preferred completions.  In the limit $\beta\!\to\!\infty$, the loss reduces to a pure log‑ratio maximization, which can cause *reward over‑optimization* and degrade the diversity of generations.

The paper empirically sweeps $\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and finds that moderate values ($\beta\approx0.1$–$1$) give the best trade‑off between reward improvement and KL increase.  A practical workflow is:

1. **Pick a target KL budget** (e.g. average sequence‑level KL $\le 6$).
2. **Run short validation experiments** with a few $\beta$ values.
3. **Select the $\beta$ that attains the target KL** while maximizing the validation reward (often a sentiment classifier or a human‑derived proxy).

Because the DPO loss directly contains the KL term, monitoring the empirical KL during training provides immediate feedback on whether $\beta$ is appropriately set.

---

## Training Schedule, Optimizer Choices, and Early Stopping

### Optimizer

The original experiments employ AdamW with weight decay, a de‑facto standard for transformer fine‑tuning.  Recommended settings are:

- Learning rate: $5\times10^{-5}$ to $1\times10^{-4}$ (warm‑up for the first $1\%$–$2\%$ of steps).
- $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, epsilon $=10^{-8}$.
- Weight decay: $0.01$.

These hyper‑parameters require little tuning; the paper reports stable convergence across models up to 6 B parameters.

### Training Schedule

1. **Shuffle $\mathcal{D}$** each epoch to avoid any ordering bias.
2. **Iterate over the dataset** for a fixed number of epochs (e.g. 3–5) or until the validation KL plateaus.
3. **Every $k$ steps** (commonly $k=100$) evaluate on a held‑out validation set of preference pairs:
   - Compute the average DPO loss.
   - Compute the empirical KL $\frac{1}{|x|}\sum_t \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x_t)\,\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x_t)\bigr)$ using the token‑wise distributions.
   - Optionally compute an external proxy reward (e.g. a sentiment classifier) to gauge progress.

### Early Stopping

Terminate training when **either**:

- The validation KL exceeds a pre‑specified budget (indicating the model is drifting too far).
- The validation DPO loss does not improve for a patience of $p$ evaluation checkpoints (typically $p=5$).

Early stopping prevents over‑fitting to the finite preference set and preserves the KL‑regularization effect.

---

## Implementation Tricks: Caching $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ Log‑Probs, Mixed‑Precision, and Distributed Training

### Caching Reference Log‑Probabilities

The log‑probabilities of the reference model $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)$ are **static** throughout DPO training.  To avoid recomputing them every step:

1. **Pre‑compute and store** $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)$ and $\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)$ for every pair in the training set.  This requires a single forward pass over the entire dataset.
2. **Save the results** in an efficient on‑disk format (e.g. NumPy ``.npy`` or HDF5).  Each entry is a single floating‑point number representing the sequence log‑probability.
3. **During training**, load the cached values as part of the dataset object.  Only the mutable $\log\pi_{\theta}(\cdot)$ needs to be recomputed.

The paper reports a **2–3× speedup** when caching is used, especially for large batch sizes.

### Mixed‑Precision Training

Because the DPO loss involves only log‑probabilities and a binary cross‑entropy, it is well‑suited to FP16 (or bfloat16) training:

- Use NVIDIA’s Apex or native PyTorch autocast to run the model forward pass in FP16.
- Keep the loss accumulator and optimizer states in FP32 to avoid gradient underflow.
- This halves memory consumption, enabling larger batch sizes and reducing wall‑clock time.

### Distributed Data Parallel (DDP)

When fine‑tuning models with $>1$ B parameters, DDP across multiple GPUs is essential:

1. **Wrap the policy model** $\pi_{\theta}$ with ``torch.nn.parallel.DistributedDataParallel``.
2. **Synchronize the random seed** across workers to ensure each GPU sees a distinct subset of $\mathcal{D}$.
3. **Cache the reference log‑probs locally** on each node (they are read‑only), avoiding communication overhead.

The paper’s compute comparison (Section 6) shows DPO requires **one order of magnitude fewer GPU‑hours** than PPO because it eliminates on‑policy sampling and the actor‑critic value head.

---

## Comparison of Compute Cost vs. PPO

| Aspect | PPO‑based RLHF | Direct Preference Optimization |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| **Policy‑gradient sampling** | Requires generating (often dozens of) rollouts per update; each rollout involves a full forward pass through the policy for every token. | No on‑policy sampling; only a single forward pass of the *current* policy on the two completions per preference pair. |
| **Reward model training** | Separate phase: fit $r_{\phi}$, then freeze; additional forward passes for reward evaluation during RL. | Implicit reward is never materialized; the loss directly uses $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. |
| **Value function** | Must train a critic (or use Monte‑Carlo baselines) to reduce gradient variance. | No critic needed; the KL‑term provides an analytic baseline, eliminating variance‑reduction code. |
| **Memory footprint** | Policy + reward model + value head (+ often a separate reference copy for KL). | Policy + frozen reference model (weights can be placed on CPU or lower‑precision GPU memory). |
| **Wall‑clock time per epoch** | 3–5× slower due to rollout generation and advantage estimation. | Approximately 1× the cost of a standard supervised fine‑tuning epoch (the same as training with cross‑entropy). |
| **Hyper‑parameter tuning** | PPO clip‑epsilon, value loss coefficient, KL‑target schedule, reward normalization. | Only $\beta$ (KL trade‑off) and standard LM fine‑tuning hyper‑parameters. |

Empirically (see Figure 2 of the paper), DPO reaches a higher reward‑KL frontier using **≈ 10 % of the compute** required by PPO, while also being far easier to implement—no custom RL loop, no advantage estimation, and no separate reward‑model checkpointing.

---

### Summary

Turning the DPO equations into a production‑ready training loop involves:

1. Selecting an appropriate $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (preferably the SFT model, otherwise a preferred‑only fine‑tune).
2. Computing token‑level log‑probabilities for both policy and reference, with careful batching and numerical safeguards.
3. Setting $\beta$ to balance reward improvement against the KL constraint, guided by validation KL measurements.
4. Using a standard AdamW optimizer, a modest learning‑rate schedule, and early stopping based on validation loss/KL.
5. Applying practical engineering tricks—caching reference log‑probs, mixed‑precision, and DDP—to achieve maximal throughput.

Following these steps faithfully reproduces the DPO algorithm described in the paper, delivering a stable, lightweight alternative to PPO‑based RLHF that scales to multi‑billion‑parameter language models with minimal computational overhead.

**Experimental Evaluation – Controlled Sentiment Generation**

---

In this chapter we examine how Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) performs on a tightly controlled sentiment‑control benchmark.  The experiment is deliberately synthetic: we can compute an exact “ground‑truth’’ reward for every generated continuation by means of a pre‑trained sentiment classifier, and we can also evaluate the Kullback‑Leibler (KL) divergence between any fine‑tuned model and its reference policy.  Because both quantities are available, we are able to plot the full **reward–KL frontier** for a variety of learning algorithms and thereby isolate the efficiency with which each method balances the twin desiderata of (i) maximizing the desired sentiment and (ii) staying close to the original supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) language model.

The discussion proceeds through five sections:

1. **Task definition and data generation using a sentiment classifier** – we describe the prompt–completion format, the construction of a preference dataset from a classifier, and the formal definition of the reward signal.  
2. **Baselines** – we enumerate the six competing training procedures (SFT, PPO, PPO‑GT, Unlikelihood, Preferred‑FT, Best‑of‑N) and explain how each implements the KL‑constrained reward‑maximisation objective.  
3. **Measuring reward (sentiment score) and sequence‑level KL** – we detail the exact equations used to compute the sentiment reward and the per‑sequence KL term, and we justify why these measurements faithfully reflect the quantities appearing in the RLHF objective.  
4. **Results: frontier plots and interpretation** – we present the empirical reward–KL curves, discuss the relative positions of the methods, and highlight the statistical robustness of the observations.  
5. **Why DPO dominates PPO and even PPO‑GT** – we provide a theoretical and intuitive account of the observed superiority, linking it to the closed‑form optimal‑policy reparameterisation that DPO employs.

All mathematical expressions are written in LaTeX notation.

---

## Task Definition and Data Generation Using a Sentiment Classifier

The controlled sentiment‑generation task is formulated as a **conditional language‑modeling** problem.  Each training example consists of a prefix \(x\) drawn from the IMDB movie‑review corpus and a continuation \(y\) to be generated.  The goal of the fine‑tuned model is to produce continuations that convey **positive sentiment** irrespective of the sentiment expressed in the prefix.

### Prompt construction

For each review we truncate after the first sentence, thereby obtaining a prompt
\[
x \;=\; \text{``The movie …''},
\]
which may be neutral, positive, or negative in tone.  The model must then generate a full review continuation \(y\) that is judged as positive.

### Synthetic preference acquisition

Rather than collecting human judgments, we employ an off‑the‑shelf sentiment classifier \(C(\cdot)\) that outputs a probability \(C_{\text{pos}}(y|x)\) of the continuation being positive.  For any prompt \(x\) we sample two candidate completions
\[
y_1, y_2 \;\sim\; \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x),
\]
where \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the SFT policy trained on the raw IMDB data.  The classifier determines the preferred pair:
\[
\bigl(y_w, y_l\bigr) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\bigl(y_1, y_2\bigr) & \text{if } C_{\text{pos}}(y_1|x) > C_{\text{pos}}(y_2|x),\\[4pt]
\bigl(y_2, y_1\bigr) & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus we obtain a **preference dataset**
\[
\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{\, (x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}) \,\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
with \(N\) large enough (typically a few hundred thousand) to approximate the distribution of human preferences for the purpose of method comparison.

### Ground‑truth reward

Because the classifier yields a real‑valued sentiment score, we can define the **true reward** for any generated continuation as
\[
r_{\text{gt}}(x, y) \;=\; \log \frac{C_{\text{pos}}(y|x)}{1 - C_{\text{pos}}(y|x)}.
\]
The log‑odds transform makes the reward additive under the Bradley‑Terry model and aligns it with the logistic link used in the preference likelihood.

---

## Baselines

All baselines share the same underlying language‑model architecture (GPT‑2‑large, 774 M parameters) and the same SFT reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The differences lie in how they incorporate the preference information \(\mathcal{D}\) and how they enforce the KL‑regularisation term.

1. **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT).**  
   Trains the model on the raw IMDB reviews using maximum likelihood.  No preference data are used; the policy simply reproduces the distribution of the pre‑training corpus.

2. **Preferred‑FT.**  
   Performs standard supervised learning **only on the preferred completions** \((x, y_w)\) extracted from \(\mathcal{D}\).  This is equivalent to a naïve “positive‑only’’ fine‑tuning and does **not** penalise the dispreferred completions.

3. **Unlikelihood.**  
   Optimises a **mixed objective**,
   \[
   \mathcal{L}_{\text{UL}} \;=\;
   -\!\!\sum_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\mathcal{D}}\!\!\!
   \bigl[\,\log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) \;-\;
   \alpha\,\log\bigl(1-\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)\bigr)\bigr],
   \]
   where \(\alpha\in[0,1]\) controls the strength of the anti‑likelihood term.  The KL‑constraint is implicit: by reducing the probability of dispreferred tokens the model is forced to move away from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) only where necessary.

4. **PPO (Proximal Policy Optimisation).**  
   Implements the standard RLHF pipeline: a reward model \(r_{\phi}\) is first trained on \(\mathcal{D}\) by maximising the Bradley‑Terry likelihood (Eq. (2) in the paper).  Then PPO updates the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) to maximise
   \[
   \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}, y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\!\!
   \bigl[\, r_{\phi}(x,y) \bigr] \;-\;
   \beta\,
   \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr),
   \]
   where \(\beta\) is a hyper‑parameter that sets the desired KL budget.  PPO requires on‑policy sampling, advantage estimation, and a separate value‑function baseline.

5. **PPO‑GT (oracle).**  
   Identical to PPO except that the **ground‑truth reward** \(r_{\text{gt}}\) (the classifier log‑odds) is used directly instead of a learned reward model.  This provides an upper bound on what PPO can achieve when supplied with perfectly accurate feedback.

6. **Best‑of‑\(N\).**  
   Samples \(N\) continuations from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) for each prompt, scores them with the learned reward model \(r_{\phi}\), and returns the highest‑scoring sample.  This method does not change the policy; it simply exploits the sampling budget at inference time.  It is computationally expensive (sampling \(N\) times per query) but serves as a strong baseline for reward maximisation.

All methods are trained until convergence; for those that require a KL‑budget (PPO, PPO‑GT) we sweep several target KL values \(\{3,6,9,12\}\) to obtain a set of points on the reward–KL curve.

---

## Measuring Reward (Sentiment Score) and Sequence‑Level KL

Because we have an explicit classifier, we can evaluate the **expected reward** of a policy \(\pi\) on a held‑out test set \(\mathcal{T}\) by Monte‑Carlo averaging:

\[
\hat{R}(\pi) \;=\;
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\!\bigl[\,r_{\text{gt}}(x,y)\bigr]
\;\approx\;
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} r_{\text{gt}}(x, y^{(m)}),
\]
where we draw \(M\) Monte‑Carlo samples per prompt (typically \(M=8\)).

The **sequence‑level KL divergence** between a candidate policy \(\pi\) and the reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is defined as the sum of per‑token KLs across the generated sequence:

\[
\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}};x) \;=\;
\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\!
\log\frac{\pi\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}
          {\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigl(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}\bigr)}.
\]

Averaging over the test prompts yields the **average KL budget**:
\[
\hat{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi) \;=\;
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}}
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}\!\bigl[\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}};x)\bigr].
\]

Both \(\hat{R}\) and \(\hat{D}_{\text{KL}}\) are directly comparable to the terms appearing in the original RLHF objective (Eq. (3) of the paper).  Consequently, plotting \(\bigl(\hat{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi), \hat{R}(\pi)\bigr)\) for each trained model yields the **reward–KL frontier**, i.e. the set of achievable trade‑offs.

---

## Results: Frontier Plots and Interpretation

Figure 1 (reproduced conceptually below) displays the reward–KL points obtained by each method after sweeping over their respective hyper‑parameters.  The horizontal axis denotes the **average KL** (lower is more conservative), and the vertical axis denotes the **average sentiment reward** (higher is more positive).

```
Reward
 ↑
 │                     ● DPO (β=0.1)
 │                 ● DPO (β=0.05)
 │            ● PPO‑GT (target KL=6)
 │          ● PPO (target KL=6)

 │   ● Preferred‑FT
 │● SFT
 └─────────────────────────────→ KL
```

### Observations

1. **DPO achieves the Pareto‑optimal frontier.**  
   For every KL budget examined, the DPO point lies **above** the corresponding PPO point.  In other words, DPO extracts **more sentiment reward** while deviating **no more** from the reference distribution.

2. **Dominance over PPO‑GT.**  
   Even when PPO is supplied with the *exact* sentiment log‑odds (PPO‑GT), DPO with a modest \(\beta\) (e.g., \(\beta=0.05\)) still yields a higher reward at comparable KL.  This indicates that the **closed‑form optimal‑policy reparameterisation** used by DPO can exploit the preference data more efficiently than the stochastic policy‑gradient estimator of PPO.

3. **Unlikelihood and Preferred‑FT are sub‑optimal.**  
   Both methods improve over raw SFT, but their points lie well below the DPO curve.  Unlikelihood suffers from a static weighting of the anti‑likelihood term; Preferred‑FT does not penalise dispreferred completions, so it cannot control the KL budget.

4. **Best‑of‑\(N\) provides an upper bound for PPO but not for DPO.**  
   Sampling many continuations from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and selecting the highest‑scoring one yields a higher reward than any single‑policy method at low KL; however, the **computational cost at inference time** is orders of magnitude larger.  DPO reaches a comparable reward with a single forward pass, illustrating its practical advantage.

5. **Stability across seeds and hyper‑parameters.**  
   Repeating each training run with five random seeds yields a tight clustering of points, confirming that the observed advantage is not a stochastic artefact.

Overall, the frontier analysis demonstrates that DPO is **more sample‑efficient** (no on‑policy rollouts), **more stable** (no variance due to advantage estimation), and **more effective** (higher reward for a given KL) than all of the baselines.

---

## Why DPO Dominates PPO and Even PPO‑GT

The empirical superiority of DPO can be traced to the **theoretical insight** that the KL‑constrained RLHF objective admits a *closed‑form* optimal policy (Eq. (4) in the paper):
\[
\pi^{*}(y|x) \;=\;
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr),
\]
where \(Z(x)\) is a normalising partition function.  By solving this expression for the reward (Eq. (5)),
\[
r(x,y) \;=\;
\beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z(x),
\]
the authors observe that the **difference of rewards** between two completions cancels the unknown \(Z(x)\).  Substituting this identity into the Bradley‑Terry likelihood yields a **policy‑only likelihood** (Eq. (7)):
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
\;=\;
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(
\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Bigr).
\]

### Consequences

1. **Exact optimisation of the RLHF objective.**  
   Because the loss is precisely the negative log‑likelihood of the human preference model *with the reward eliminated*, minimising \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}\) is mathematically equivalent to solving the original KL‑constrained RL problem.  No approximation (e.g., Monte‑Carlo advantage estimates) is introduced.

2. **No need for a separate reward‑model training phase.**  
   PPO must first fit \(r_{\phi}\) (Eq. (2)) and then suffers from any bias or variance in that estimate.  DPO sidesteps this step entirely; the reward is implicitly represented by the log‑ratio \(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\).

3. **Dynamic per‑example weighting.**  
   The gradient of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}\) (Eq. (8)) includes the factor
   \[
   \sigma\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_l)-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_w)\bigr),
   \]
   which up‑weights examples where the current policy incorrectly favours the dispreferred completion.  PPO’s surrogate loss, by contrast, uses a fixed clipping coefficient that does not adapt to the magnitude of the mismatch, leading to slower correction of systematic errors.

4. **Elimination of variance‑inducing baselines.**  
   In PPO the KL term is treated as a penalty, but the reward term still requires a baseline (e.g., Monte‑Carlo estimate of the value function) to reduce variance.  DPO’s loss is a pure binary cross‑entropy; the logistic term already normalises the contribution of each pair, so no additional baseline is needed.

5. **Exact handling of the KL constraint.**  
   The factor \(\beta\) appears explicitly inside the log‑ratio, making the KL‑budget a *scale* on the reward differences rather than an external penalty that must be tuned indirectly.  When \(\beta\) is set small, the policy is forced to stay very close to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\); when \(\beta\) is large, the policy can deviate more aggressively.  This direct control simplifies hyper‑parameter selection and explains why even the *oracle* PPO‑GT, which still relies on stochastic gradient estimation, cannot reach the same points on the frontier.

In sum, DPO’s **reparameterisation** turns the RLHF problem into a **pure supervised learning** problem with a binary‑cross‑entropy loss that captures the entire preference structure.  Consequently, the optimisation landscape is smoother, the gradient signal is better aligned with the true objective, and the KL‑budget is honoured by construction.  These factors jointly account for the empirical dominance of DPO over both standard PPO and the oracle PPO‑GT on the controlled sentiment‑generation benchmark.

**Experimental Evaluation – Summarization and Dialogue**

# Datasets and preprocessing  

The empirical part of the study relies on two publicly‑available collections of human‑annotated preferences that were originally assembled for reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback experiments.  The first is the **Reddit TL;DR** dataset, which consists of short forum posts harvested from Reddit together with human‑written summaries.  For each post a pair of candidate summaries is generated by a pretrained language model and a human annotator indicates which of the two is preferred.  Formally the data can be written as  
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{TL;DR}}=\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]  
where \(x^{(i)}\) is the Reddit post, \(y_{w}^{(i)}\) the preferred summary and \(y_{l}^{(i)}\) the dispreferred one.  The raw posts are tokenized with the same byte‑pair‑encoding (BPE) vocabulary used by the underlying language model; padding or truncation is applied only to enforce a maximum context length that comfortably fits the longest post plus two candidate completions.  No additional filtering beyond the original collection is performed, guaranteeing that the preference distribution reflects the original annotation protocol.

The second collection is the **Anthropic Helpful‑Harmless (HH)** dataset, designed for single‑turn dialogue.  Each entry contains a user query \(x\) and two assistant responses \(y_{1},y_{2}\) generated by a large language model.  Human raters label the pair with a binary preference indicating which response is more helpful and less likely to produce harmful content.  The processed dataset has the same structure as above:
\[
\mathcal{D}_{\text{HH}}=\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{M}.
\]  
Again, tokenization follows the BPE scheme of the base model.  Because dialogue turns are typically short, the maximum sequence length is set primarily by the length of the responses rather than the prompt.

Both datasets are **offline**: the preference pairs are fixed before training begins.  This matches the setting assumed in the Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) derivation, where a static collection \(\mathcal{D}\) is used to estimate the policy parameters \(\theta\) by minimizing the DPO loss (Eq. (7) of the paper).  No further human interaction is required during training, which dramatically reduces the operational cost compared with online preference‑query loops.

# Reference model creation for each task  

DPO requires a *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) that plays the role of the KL‑regularizer in the original RLHF objective.  In practice the reference model is taken to be the **supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT)** model that was obtained by maximum‑likelihood training on high‑quality examples of the target task.

* **Summarization (TL;DR).**  An SFT model is first trained on the Reddit TL;DR training split, where each example consists of a post \(x\) and its human‑written summary \(y\).  The loss is the standard cross‑entropy over the token sequence:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)}\bigl[\log \pi_{\text{SFT}}(y\mid x)\bigr].
\]  
The resulting policy, denoted \(\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{TL;DR}}\), captures the distribution of acceptable summaries while remaining faithful to the original unsupervised language model.  Because the preference data were generated by a model that is *similar* to this SFT policy, using it as the reference minimizes the distribution shift term that appears in Eq. (5).

* **Dialogue (HH).**  No dedicated SFT checkpoint exists for the Helpful‑Harmless task in the original releases.  To construct a reference, the authors fine‑tune an off‑the‑shelf causal language model (Pythia‑2.8B) on the *preferred* completions alone.  Concretely, they maximize
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{ref}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{HH}}}\bigl[\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)\bigr],
\]  
which yields a policy that places most probability mass on responses that humans have already judged as helpful.  This “preferred‑FT” reference is then used in the DPO objective for the HH experiment.

Using the SFT or preferred‑FT model as \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) ensures that the KL term penalizes deviations from a distribution that is already reasonably aligned with human expectations, thereby respecting the theoretical constraint that the KL‑divergence be kept small.

# Evaluation metrics: GPT‑4 win‑rate, temperature sweeps, human study  

Because the true reward function \(r^{*}\) is unavailable for the real‑world datasets, performance is measured indirectly.

## GPT‑4 win‑rate  

The principal automated metric is a **pairwise win‑rate** obtained from GPT‑4.  For each test prompt the system generates a response from the model under evaluation and a baseline response (either the original SFT output or the human‑preferred answer).  A GPT‑4 prompt asks the model to decide which of the two completions better satisfies the task (summarization quality or helpfulness).  The win‑rate is then
\[
\text{WinRate} = \frac{1}{| \mathcal{T} |}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{T}} \mathbf{1}\bigl[\text{GPT‑4 prefers model output}\bigr],
\]  
where \(\mathcal{T}\) is the test set.  Two variants of the GPT‑4 prompt are explored: a **simple** version that only asks for the better summary, and a **concise** version that additionally rewards brevity.  The concise prompt aligns more closely with human judgments, as shown in the human study (Section 6.4 of the paper).

## Temperature sweeps  

Generation temperature \(\tau\) controls the stochasticity of the softmax over logits:
\[
p_{\tau}(y\mid x)=\frac{\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\tau}\, \text{logits}(y\mid x)\bigr)}{\sum_{y'}\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{\tau}\,\text{logits}(y'\mid x)\bigr)}.
\]  
For each method (DPO, PPO, Preferred‑FT, Best‑of‑\(N\), zero‑shot prompts) the authors evaluate a grid of temperatures \(\tau\in\{0.0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}\).  The win‑rate is reported as a function of \(\tau\), revealing how robust a method is to the choice of sampling stochasticity.  In the TL;DR experiments DPO achieves higher win‑rates across the entire sweep and degrades gracefully at higher temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance collapses rapidly when \(\tau\) exceeds 0.0.

## Human study  

To validate the reliability of GPT‑4 as a proxy for human assessment, a small human evaluation is performed on a subset of TL;DR outputs.  Three systems are compared: the best‑performing DPO configuration (temperature 0.25), the lowest‑performing PPO configuration (temperature 1.0), and a middle‑performing SFT model (temperature 0.25).  Each pairwise comparison is judged by multiple human annotators, and inter‑annotator agreement is measured.  The results show that GPT‑4’s decisions correlate with human choices at a level comparable to human‑human agreement, especially when using the concise prompt.  Consequently, the paper adopts the concise GPT‑4 win‑rate as the primary automated evaluation for the remaining experiments.

# Comparison of DPO with PPO, Preferred‑FT, Best‑of‑N, zero‑shot prompts  

The experiments juxtapose DPO against several baselines that represent distinct families of preference‑learning algorithms.

* **PPO (proximal policy optimization).**  A reward model is first fitted to the preference data using the Bradley‑Terry loss (Eq. (2)).  The resulting reward is then used in a KL‑constrained PPO update, exactly matching the objective in Eq. (4).  Two PPO variants are reported: one that learns from the *learned* reward model (standard PPO) and one that has access to the *ground‑truth* sentiment classifier (PPO‑GT) in the controlled sentiment experiment.  In the TL;DR and HH tasks PPO serves as the canonical RLHF baseline.

* **Preferred‑FT (preferred fine‑tuning).**  This method treats the preferred completion \(y_{w}\) as a supervised target and discards the dispreferred example.  Training proceeds with standard maximum‑likelihood on \((x,y_{w})\) pairs only, without any KL regularization.

* **Best‑of‑\(N\).**  The reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is sampled \(N\) times per prompt; the learned reward model scores each candidate and the highest‑scoring one is returned.  This approach isolates the quality of the reward model from the policy‑optimization step but is computationally infeasible for large \(N\) at inference time.  The paper reports results for \(N=128\), where performance saturates.

* **Zero‑shot prompts.**  For TL;DR a large pretrained GPT‑J model is queried with a handcrafted prompt (no fine‑tuning).  For HH a 2‑shot prompt is used with Pythia‑2.8B.  These baselines illustrate the capability of the raw pretrained language model without any preference‑based adaptation.

Across both tasks, the **win‑rate curves** demonstrate that DPO consistently matches or exceeds PPO.  In summarization DPO attains ≈ 61 % win‑rate at temperature 0.0, surpassing PPO’s ≈ 57 % peak.  In dialogue, DPO’s best temperature (≈ 0.25) yields a win‑rate comparable to the Best‑of‑128 baseline, while PPO fails to improve over the raw Pythia model.  Preferred‑FT offers modest gains over the untreated SFT but falls short of DPO, confirming that the binary cross‑entropy loss that directly incorporates the dispreferred example is essential.

# Robustness to sampling temperature and hyper‑parameter choices  

A notable strength of DPO is its **insensitivity to temperature**.  Because the DPO loss (Eq. (7)) explicitly weights each pair by the sigmoid of the implicit reward difference, the policy learns to increase the log‑probability ratio \(\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w})}{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l})}\) while preserving the overall KL distance to the reference.  Consequently, when the model is later sampled with higher temperatures, the relative ordering of preferred versus dispreferred completions remains stable, leading to a gentle decline in win‑rate.  PPO, in contrast, optimizes a stochastic policy under a sampled‑reward signal; when temperature is increased the policy’s logits are scaled down, eroding the learned advantage and causing performance to revert to the reference baseline.

Hyper‑parameter robustness is also evident.  The sole DPO hyper‑parameter is the KL‑strength \(\beta\).  Experiments sweep \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\) and observe that the **frontier** of reward versus KL (Fig. 3 of the paper) is dominated by DPO for all \(\beta\) values, whereas PPO’s frontier is strictly inferior even when its KL target is tuned aggressively.  No additional tricks such as reward normalization, baseline subtraction, or value‑function learning are required for DPO, simplifying the training pipeline considerably.

# Analysis of convergence speed and stability  

The training dynamics are monitored by measuring the DPO loss and the corresponding win‑rate at regular intervals (every 100 gradient steps).  In the dialogue experiment (Fig. 4 of the paper) the win‑rate plateaus after roughly **10 k** optimization steps, far earlier than the PPO runs which require several times more updates to reach comparable performance.  This rapid convergence stems from the **closed‑form** relationship between reward and policy (Eq. (5)).  Since DPO directly optimizes the policy that would be optimal for the underlying reward, there is no need for a separate critic or for Monte‑Carlo estimation of the advantage, both of which introduce variance and slow learning in actor‑critic methods.

Stability is further illustrated by the **absence of mode collapse**.  In naive probability‑ratio training (unweighted unlikelihood) the model can assign excessive probability to preferred responses while driving the dispreferred likelihood to zero, which distorts the overall distribution.  The DPO gradient (Eq. (8)) includes the factor \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\), which down‑weights examples that are already correctly ordered, preventing runaway updates.  Empirically, the loss curve declines smoothly without spikes, and the KL divergence to the reference remains bounded throughout training, confirming that the theoretical KL constraint is respected in practice.

---  

In summary, the experimental section of the paper demonstrates that **Direct Preference Optimization** provides a **simple, stable, and computationally light** alternative to conventional RLHF pipelines.  By re‑parameterizing the reward‑maximization problem, DPO eliminates the need for a separate reward model, policy‑gradient variance reduction, and extensive hyper‑parameter tuning, while achieving equal or superior performance on real‑world summarization and dialogue benchmarks.  The results suggest that DPO may become the default method for aligning large language models with human preferences.

**Generalisation and Out‑of‑Distribution Behaviour**

# OOD test on CNN/DailyMail after TL;DR training  

When a language model is fine‑tuned on a preference dataset that originates from one domain, it is natural to ask whether the resulting policy continues to behave sensibly on a different, previously unseen distribution.  The paper investigates this question by taking the model that was trained on the Reddit TL;DR summarisation benchmark and evaluating it on the CNN/DailyMail news‑article summarisation corpus.  The TL;DR data consist of short forum posts together with human‑generated summaries, whereas CNN/DailyMail contains longer, well‑structured news articles and reference abstracts.  Crucially, the **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) used during Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model that was itself trained on the TL;DR data.  After DPO updates, the policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) has never seen any news‑article inputs during training, so any performance gain on CNN/DailyMail must stem from the way DPO modifies the underlying probability distribution rather than from exposure to the new domain.

The evaluation protocol mirrors the in‑domain sentiment experiment: the authors generate a summary for each test article, compute the **sequence‑level KL‑divergence** \(\operatorname{KL}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr)\) to verify that the policy has not drifted excessively from its reference, and then ask a large language model (GPT‑4) to judge which of two summaries – the one produced by the DPO‑trained policy and the one produced by a PPO‑trained baseline – better captures the salient information of the article.  Because the true reward function for news summarisation is unavailable, the win‑rate against a human‑written reference abstract serves as a proxy for alignment quality.

# Quantitative win‑rate results  

The experiments report win‑rates for three temperature settings (the sampling temperature controls randomness of the decoder).  Table 1 (reproduced here in narrative form) summarises the outcomes:

| Sampling temperature | PPO win‑rate vs. reference | DPO win‑rate vs. reference |
|----------------------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| 0.0 (greedy)         | 48 %                      | **55 %**                   |
| 0.25                 | 46 %                      | **53 %**                   |
| 0.5                  | 44 %                      | **51 %**                   |

A win‑rate above 50 % indicates that the model’s output is preferred over the baseline reference summary more often than not.  Across all temperatures, the DPO‑trained policy consistently outperforms the PPO‑trained policy by 6–7 percentage points, despite never having been exposed to news articles during training.  Moreover, the gap does not shrink as the temperature increases, suggesting that the advantage is not confined to the deterministic regime but persists when stochastic sampling introduces more diversity.

In addition to win‑rate, the authors verify that the KL‑divergence of the DPO policy to its reference remains comparable to that of PPO.  This observation rules out the trivial explanation that DPO simply “over‑fits” to the TL;DR dataset and then behaves arbitrarily on new inputs; instead, the policy preserves a controlled distance from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) while shifting probability mass in a direction that aligns better with the implicit reward defined by the preference data.

# Why DPO may retain or improve generalisation  

The empirical advantage of DPO in the out‑of‑distribution (OOD) setting can be interpreted through the theoretical lens provided in the paper.  DPO reframes the RLHF objective by **parameterising the reward implicitly as a log‑ratio of the learned policy and the reference policy**:

\[
r_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
\]

Because the reward is defined *relative* to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), learning merely rescales the logits of the underlying language model rather than constructing a completely new scoring function.  Consequently, the parameters that encode linguistic competence—syntax, world knowledge, and factual grounding—remain largely untouched; only the **direction** in which probability mass is transferred between competing completions is altered.  This limited perturbation helps preserve the model’s ability to generate fluent, coherent text on inputs that differ from the fine‑tuning distribution.

Moreover, the DPO loss introduces a **dynamic importance weight** \(\sigma\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l}) - \hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\bigr)\) (see Eq. 9 of the paper).  When the current policy already ranks a preferred completion higher than a dispreferred one, the weight becomes small, preventing unnecessary updates that could lead to over‑specialisation.  When the ranking is wrong, the weight is large, concentrating learning on the most harmful errors.  This adaptive scheme is reminiscent of curriculum learning and can be viewed as a regulariser that discourages catastrophic changes on examples that the model already handles well, thereby improving OOD robustness.

Finally, the **KL‑constraint** in the original RLHF objective is retained implicitly in DPO because the log‑ratio formulation directly penalises large deviations from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).  The parameter \(\beta\) controls the strength of this penalty; a modest \(\beta\) (e.g. 0.1) allows the policy to shift enough to satisfy the preferences while still respecting the prior distribution learned from massive unsupervised data.  This balance mirrors the intuition behind PPO’s clipping or KL‑penalty terms, but DPO enforces it analytically rather than through approximate policy‑gradient updates, which can be noisy and prone to instability.

# Limitations of current OOD experiments and future probes  

While the CNN/DailyMail evaluation provides a compelling first demonstration of DPO’s OOD behaviour, several limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn:

1. **Single target domain** – The study examines only one out‑of‑distribution corpus (news articles).  Different shifts—style, length, domain‑specific terminology—might interact differently with the DPO update.  Future work should probe a broader suite of benchmarks (e.g., scientific abstracts, dialogue, code generation).

2. **Reliance on GPT‑4 as a proxy evaluator** – Win‑rate is measured by an LLM rather than direct human annotation.  Although the authors report high correlation between GPT‑4 judgments and human preferences, systematic biases (e.g., a preference for longer or more fluent text) could mask subtle degradations in factual accuracy or relevance that only humans would notice.

3. **Absence of ablation on \(\beta\) and reference choice** – The experiments fix \(\beta\) and use the TL;DR‑SFT model as the reference.  It remains unclear how sensitive OOD performance is to the magnitude of the KL‑penalty or to the quality of the reference policy.  A systematic sweep over \(\beta\) values and alternative references (e.g., a base pre‑trained LM without SFT) would clarify whether the observed robustness is intrinsic to the DPO formulation or contingent on a well‑matched reference.

4. **No explicit measurement of catastrophic forgetting** – While KL statistics suggest limited drift, the study does not directly test whether the model’s ability to summarise forum posts deteriorates after OOD evaluation.  A bidirectional test—measuring in‑domain performance before and after exposure to OOD data—could reveal hidden trade‑offs.

5. **Statistical significance and variance** – The reported win‑rates are point estimates; confidence intervals or bootstrap analysis are not provided.  Because the differences are on the order of a few percentage points, establishing statistical significance is essential, especially when the evaluation relies on a single LLM evaluator.

Future probing strategies could include: (i) **counterfactual sampling**, where one forces the model to generate low‑probability completions under \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and measures whether DPO still respects preferences; (ii) **latent‑space analysis**, examining how DPO reshapes token‑level logits across different contexts; (iii) **adversarial OOD tests**, where inputs are deliberately constructed to trigger failure modes (e.g., prompting the model with ambiguous queries).  Together, these investigations would deepen our understanding of why DPO maintains—or even improves—generalisation beyond the training distribution.

---  

In summary, the paper’s OOD experiment demonstrates that Direct Preference Optimization can yield a policy that not only respects human preferences within its fine‑tuning domain but also transfers those improvements to a markedly different corpus.  Theoretical insights about the log‑ratio reward parametrisation, the adaptive weighting in the loss, and the built‑in KL regularisation collectively explain this empirical robustness.  Nevertheless, a richer set of OOD benchmarks, more rigorous evaluation, and targeted ablations are needed to confirm that DPO’s advantage generalises across the full spectrum of tasks and domains encountered in real‑world language‑model deployment.
