\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the DPO loss weight examples by \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) and how does this prevent degeneration?
Question:
Equation (7) shows the gradient of the DPO loss, where the term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x, y_w))\) acts as a weight. The authors state that without this weighting the model collapses (see Appendix Table X). I would like to understand intuitively why weighting by the sigmoid of the reward difference is necessary and how it discourages the model from assigning high probability to dispreferred completions. How does this compare to the unlikelihood loss term used in the "Unlikelihood" baseline?
Answer:
The DPO gradient (Eq. 7 in the paper) can be written as  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
 = -\beta\;\mathbb E_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal D}
   \underbrace{\sigma\!\Bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\Bigr)}_{\text{importance weight}}
   \bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr],
$$

where  

$$
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
$$

The factor $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ is the *sigmoid* of the **reward‑difference** estimated by the current policy.  
Understanding why this weighting is crucial amounts to looking at two extremes.

---

### 1. What would happen without the weight?

If we simply dropped the sigmoid and used  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L_{\text{naïve}} = -\beta\;
   \bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr],
$$

every training pair would receive the same magnitude of update, regardless of how the model currently scores the two completions.  
When the model already assigns a **much higher probability** to the dispreferred completion ($y_{l}$) than to the preferred one ($y_{w}$), the naïve update still tries to push probability mass from $y_{w}$ to $y_{l}$ with exactly the same strength as it does for a well‑behaved pair where the model already prefers $y_{w}$.  

Because the loss is symmetric in the two logits, the optimiser can satisfy it by **collapsing both logits toward the reference distribution** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In the extreme, the model learns to output the reference policy for every input, which is precisely the degeneration reported in Appendix Table X: the policy stops improving the preferred completions and reverts to the SFT (or reference) model.

---

### 2. What does the sigmoid weight do?

The sigmoid is a smooth, bounded function that maps a reward difference to a value in $[0,1]$.  
* If the current implicit reward already ranks the preferred completion higher ($\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})>\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})$), the difference is negative and the sigmoid is **close to 0**. The corresponding gradient contribution is tiny: the model does not waste effort pushing an already‑correct ranking farther apart.  
* If the model **gets the ranking wrong** (the dispreferred answer receives a larger reward), the difference is positive and the sigmoid approaches **1**. In that case the full gradient magnitude is applied, strongly encouraging the model to increase $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)$ and decrease $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)$.

Thus the weighting implements an *importance sampling* that focuses learning on the **mistakes** of the current policy. By scaling the update proportionally to the probability that the model would choose the wrong answer, the algorithm automatically respects the KL‑constraint encoded in $\beta$; when the KL term already forces the model close to the reference, the reward differences become small and the weight shrinks, preventing the policy from drifting too far.

---

### 3. Connection to the “unlikelihood” baseline

The unlikelihood loss (Welleck et al., 2019) also contains a term that penalises the probability of a dispreferred token or sequence:  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{unlik}} = -\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w,t}\mid x,\!y_{w,<t}) 
      + \alpha \sum_{t}\log\bigl(1-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l,t}\mid x,\!y_{l,<t})\bigr),
$$

where $\alpha$ is a hand‑tuned scalar. The key difference is that the coefficient $\alpha$ is *global*: every dispreferred example receives the same push‑down strength, regardless of how badly the model currently scores it. Consequently, as observed in the paper’s ablation, unlikelihood can still drive the model toward a degenerate solution where it merely learns to “anti‑copy” the dispreferred outputs without respecting the KL‑regularisation; the loss lacks a mechanism that recognises when the model already assigns low probability to a bad answer.

In contrast, DPO’s weight **adapts per example** and **per training step** based on the model’s own reward estimate. When the model already suppresses a bad completion, the weight becomes negligible, so the KL‑penalty dominates and the policy stays near the reference. When the model is over‑confident in a bad completion, the weight becomes near‑one and the update directly counteracts that over‑confidence. This dynamic weighting is what prevents the collapse observed for naïve ratio or unlikelihood objectives.

---

### 4. Intuitive picture

Imagine a teacher correcting a student’s answers. If the student already gets a question right, the teacher says nothing (weight ≈ 0). If the student gets it wrong, the teacher gives a strong correction (weight ≈ 1). A static penalty that “always scolds” the student, even for correct answers, would waste effort and could even encourage the student to give the same answer every time to minimise the teacher’s attention—a behaviour analogous to the policy collapsing to the reference model. The sigmoid weight implements exactly this “only correct the mistakes” principle, and because the mistake magnitude is measured by the model’s own reward estimate, the correction is automatically calibrated to respect the KL budget.

---

**In summary**, the sigmoid weighting term in the DPO gradient is essential because it turns the loss into an *error‑driven* objective: it amplifies updates where the implicit reward mis‑orders the pair and attenuates them where the ordering is already correct. This prevents the model from uniformly pushing probability mass toward the reference distribution (the collapse observed without the weight) and distinguishes DPO from the static unlikelihood loss, which cannot adapt its strength per example and therefore lacks this stabilising effect.
