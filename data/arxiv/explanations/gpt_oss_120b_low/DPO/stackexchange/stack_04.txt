\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How is the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) chosen when an SFT model is unavailable, and what impact does this have on DPO performance?
Question:
Section 4 mentions that if a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model is not present, \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is obtained by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions. Does this choice guarantee that \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is close enough to the true data distribution for the KL‑penalty to be meaningful? Have the authors evaluated the sensitivity of DPO to a mismatched reference policy, and what practical guidelines should be followed when constructing \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DPO pipeline the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ plays the same role that the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model plays in classic RLHF: it is the distribution from which the KL‑penalty is measured and against which the new policy is discouraged from drifting too far.  
When an SFT model is not available the authors propose to *learn* a reference policy by maximum‑likelihood on the **preferred** completions $\left(y_{w}\right)$ of the preference dataset:

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} \;=\;\arg\max_{\pi}\;
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w})\sim\mathcal D}\left[\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\right].
\]

This is essentially a supervised‑fine‑tuning step using only the “winner’’ side of each pair, i.e. the same data that would be used for a Preferred‑FT baseline.  

---

### Why this is sufficient for the KL‑penalty

The KL‑term in the original RLHF objective

\[
\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)\bigr]
\]

does not require that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ be the *true* data distribution; it only needs a tractable distribution that is **reasonably close** to the distribution from which the preference pairs were drawn. The reason is two‑fold:

1. **Preference data are already biased toward the preferred completions.**  
   Preference pairs are generated by sampling two candidates from some policy (often an SFT model) and then labeling which one a human prefers. By construction, the *preferred* side of each pair lies in a region of the output space that the original policy already assigns non‑negligible probability. Fitting a maximum‑likelihood model on these preferred completions therefore recovers a policy that lives in roughly the same sub‑space as the original (unknown) reference distribution.

2. **The KL‑penalty is a *relative* regulariser.**  
   In the DPO derivation the KL term appears inside the log‑ratio that defines the implicit reward  

   \[
   \hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
   \]

   Any constant shift of the reward (i.e. adding a function of $x$ only) cancels out when we form the preference probability
   \[
   \sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
   \]
   so the exact normalisation of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ does not affect the objective. As long as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ puts mass on the same set of completions that appear in the training pairs, the KL‑penalty will correctly penalise large departures from that region.

Consequently, the “MLE on preferred completions’’ strategy yields a reference that is *good enough* for the KL‑penalty to be meaningful, even though it is not the true pre‑training distribution.

---

### Sensitivity to a mismatched $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

The paper does not present an exhaustive study of how DPO behaves when $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is far from the true data distribution, but a few observations can be inferred from the experiments and the theoretical analysis:

* **Empirical robustness.**  
  In the dialogue experiment (“single‑turn dialogue”) the authors had *no* SFT model at all. They constructed $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by fine‑tuning a raw language model on the *preferred* completions (the “Preferred‑FT” step). DPO still achieved a win‑rate that exceeded PPO‑style baselines and the “Best‑of‑N” oracle, indicating that a reference built this way does not cripple performance.

* **Partition‑function cancellation.**  
  Because the Bradley‑Terry preference model depends only on *differences* of rewards, the unknown normalising constant (the partition function) disappears when we rewrite the likelihood in terms of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$. This mathematical property makes DPO intrinsically less sensitive to the absolute scale of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

* **Theoretical guarantee.**  
  Theorem 1 in the paper shows that *any* reward function that is compatible with the Bradley‑Terry model can be expressed as  

  \[
  r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
  \]

  for some policy $\pi$. In other words, even if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is an imperfect proxy, there always exists a policy $\pi$ that reproduces the same preference distribution. Hence DPO can, in principle, recover the optimal policy regardless of the exact shape of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, provided the preference data are sufficient.

Overall, while an extremely poor $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (e.g. a uniformly random language model) would give the KL term little regularising effect and could lead to instability, the *preferred‑completion MLE* construction is usually close enough to guarantee stable training.

---

### Practical guidelines for building $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

1. **Use the same data source that generated the preference pairs.**  
   If the pairs were sampled from a publicly released SFT checkpoint, set $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ = that checkpoint. This is what the authors do in the sentiment and summarisation experiments and yields the best KL‑anchor.

2. **When no SFT checkpoint exists, fine‑tune on the *winners* only.**  
   – Take every $(x,\,y_{w})$ from the preference dataset.  
   – Train a language model with a standard cross‑entropy loss (no weighting, no KL).  
   – Stop once the training loss plateaus; the resulting model is $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
   This simple “Preferred‑FT’’ step is cheap (a few hundred gradient steps) and already aligns the reference with the region of interest.

3. **Check the KL magnitude before full DPO training.**  
   Compute the average sequence‑level KL between the freshly trained $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and the *original* pre‑trained LM (or a known good checkpoint). If the KL is *very* high (e.g. > 10 nats per token), consider training the reference longer or adding a small amount of data from the original model to prevent it from collapsing to a narrow mode.

4. **Avoid mixing *dispreferred* completions into $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.**  
   Including the low‑quality side would pull the reference distribution toward undesirable regions and weaken the regulariser. The theory relies on the reference representing the *baseline* behaviour, not the *negative* behaviour.

5. **Choose $\beta$ consistently with the KL scale you desire.**  
   $\beta$ controls how aggressively DPO pushes probability mass from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to the preferred side. A common practical choice is $\beta\in[0.05,\,0.5]$ for a 2‑to‑6 B model; larger $\beta$ makes the KL term weaker and can tolerate a looser reference.

---

### Take‑away

*If an SFT model is unavailable, training a reference policy by maximum‑likelihood on the preferred completions is a theoretically sound and empirically robust solution.* The KL‑penalty does not demand an exact replica of the true data distribution; it only needs a reference that has support on the same answer space, which the “preferred‑only’’ MLE model provides. Experiments in the paper (dialogue and summarisation) show that DPO’s performance is not dramatically harmed when $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is constructed this way, and the authors’ theoretical analysis guarantees that any reward compatible with the Bradley‑Terry model can be expressed through such a reference. In practice, one should build $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from the winners of the preference dataset, verify that its KL to the original model is reasonable, and then run DPO with a $\beta$ that reflects the desired trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to the baseline.
