\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Can the DPO framework be extended to multi‑turn dialogues or ranking more than two candidates?
Question:
The current derivation relies on the binary Bradley‑Terry model. For tasks like multi‑turn conversation or when we have a ranked list of >2 completions (e.g., Plackett‑Luce), how would the DPO objective change? Is there a straightforward generalization, or does the closed‑form policy reparameterization break down when moving beyond pairwise comparisons?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective was derived by exploiting the fact that, under the Bradley‑Terry (BT) model, a human preference depends only on the *difference* of the (latent) rewards of two candidates.  Because the optimal KL‑constrained policy can be written in closed form as  

$$
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr) ,
$$

the reward can be eliminated in favour of the policy itself:

$$
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).
$$

When this expression is substituted into the BT likelihood, the normalising term $ \log Z(x) $ cancels, yielding the binary‑cross‑entropy loss used by DPO (Eq. 9 in the paper).  

The same algebra works for *any* Plackett‑Luce (PL) family of ranking models, including the special case of BT, because a PL model also depends only on the *relative* values of the underlying scores.  The paper’s Appendix D shows the derivation for a general PL model; the key steps are reproduced here.

---

### From BT to general Plackett‑Luce

For a set of $K$ candidates $\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots ,y_{K}\}$ the PL model assigns the probability of a particular ranking $\sigma$ (a permutation of the candidates) as  

$$
p^{*}(\sigma\mid x)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{\sigma(k)})\bigr)}
{\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{\sigma(j)})\bigr)} .
\tag{1}
$$

If we replace each reward by the re‑parameterised expression  

$$
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x) ,
\tag{2}
$$

the factor $\beta\log Z(x)$ appears in **every** term of the numerator and denominator of (1) and therefore cancels out.  After cancelling we obtain exactly the PL likelihood expressed **only** through the policies:

$$
p^{*}(\sigma\mid x)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\displaystyle\frac{\pi(y_{\sigma(k)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{\sigma(k)}\mid x)}}
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{K}\frac{\pi(y_{\sigma(j)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{\sigma(j)}\mid x)}} .
\tag{3}
$$

Thus the PL likelihood is a function of the *importance weights*

$$
w_\theta(y\mid x)\;:=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
$$

---

### A DPO‑style loss for rankings

Given a data point consisting of a *partial* ranking (most common in practice is a top‑1 or top‑2 label) we can write the negative log‑likelihood as  

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO-PL}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,\sigma)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!
\Bigl[ \log p^{*}(\sigma\mid x) \Bigr] ,
\tag{4}
$$

where $p^{*}$ is the expression in (3).  When the observed ranking contains only two items,
$\sigma=(y_{w}\succ y_{l})$, the product in (3) collapses to a single term and (4) reduces exactly to the binary DPO loss (Eq. 9 of the paper).  For a *full* ranking of three or more candidates the loss expands to a sum of log‑softmax terms, e.g. for a three‑item ranking $y_{1}\succ y_{2}\succ y_{3}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO-3}}(\theta) = &
-\log\frac{w_\theta(y_{1}\mid x)}
{w_\theta(y_{1}\mid x)+w_\theta(y_{2}\mid x)+w_\theta(y_{3}\mid x)}  \\
& -\log\frac{w_\theta(y_{2}\mid x)}
{w_\theta(y_{2}\mid x)+w_\theta(y_{3}\mid x)} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

Every term is a standard multinomial‑logit (softmax) loss on the *importance‑weighted* probabilities.  Consequently the gradient has exactly the same form as (10) in the paper: the update pushes up the log‑probability of a higher‑ranked item and pushes down that of a lower‑ranked item, but now the *weight* of each pairwise comparison is modulated by the current importance ratios of all items that remain in the slate.  No new hyper‑parameters are required; the temperature $\beta$ that appears in the definition of $w_\theta$ plays the same role as in the binary case.

---

### Multi‑turn dialogue

A multi‑turn conversation can be treated as a *single* decision problem whose “action’’ is the *entire* dialogue suffix generated by the model.  If human annotators provide a ranking of several complete dialogues (or simply a binary win/loss for a pair of dialogues) the same PL‑based loss can be applied directly: each dialogue is a candidate $y$ and the policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ is the probability of generating that complete suffix given the conversation history $x$.

In practice we rarely enumerate every possible continuation because the space is astronomically large.  The usual pipeline is therefore:

1. **Sample a modest set of completions** $\{y^{(1)},\dots ,y^{(K)}\}$ from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (or from a current $\pi_\theta$ in an on‑policy variant).  
2. **Obtain a human ranking** of the sampled completions (full ranking, top‑1, or pairwise judgments).  
3. **Apply the PL‑derived loss** (4) using the sampled set as the candidate set $\mathcal{Y}$.

Because the loss only depends on the *relative* weights $w_\theta$ of the sampled completions, we never need to compute the intractable partition function $Z(x)$ for the full language‑model space.  This is the same trick that makes DPO tractable in the binary case, and it carries over unchanged.

---

### Does the closed‑form policy re‑parameterisation break down?

No.  The re‑parameterisation

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)
$$

is an identity that holds for **any** reward function $r$ and **any** policy that solves the KL‑constrained optimisation (Eq. 1).  The only step where a restriction could appear is when we *eliminate* the unknown term $\log Z(x)$.  This term cancels **whenever** the preference model depends only on *differences* of rewards—exactly the case for the whole Plackett‑Luce family.  Therefore the derivation stays valid for:

* binary BT comparisons (the original DPO),
* top‑k or full rankings under PL,
* any other PL‑type model that can be written as a product of “choice‑probabilities’’ of the form $\sigma(r_i-r_j)$.

If one were to use a preference model that depends on *absolute* scores (e.g. a Gaussian‑noise model where $p(y\_w\succ y\_l)=\Phi(r(y_w)-r(y_l))$ with a fixed variance), the cancelling trick still works because the variance term is constant across candidates.  Only models that introduce *context‑dependent* normalisers (e.g. a softmax over a *different* set of items for each comparison) would break the clean cancellation and would require an explicit estimate of a partition function.
