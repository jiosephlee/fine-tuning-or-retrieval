\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the "Best of N" baseline sometimes outperform PPO and DPO, and is this a fair comparison?
Question:
The authors report that sampling many candidates from the SFT model and picking the highest‑scoring one (according to a learned reward) can beat PPO and DPO, yet it is computationally impractical. Does this indicate that the reward model is still useful for ranking, and could DPO be combined with a sampling‑and‑ranking step to further improve performance? How should we interpret the "Best of N" results when evaluating alignment methods?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “Best‑of‑$N$” curve in the DPO paper is not meant to claim that plain sampling and ranking is a viable alternative to reinforcement‑learning‑based alignment; it is a *diagnostic* tool that isolates the quality of the learned reward (or preference) model from the optimisation algorithm that produces the final policy.  

---

### Why can “Best‑of‑$N$” sometimes beat PPO or DPO?

Both PPO and DPO train a *single* policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that must satisfy two competing pressures:

1. ** maximise the reward** (or, equivalently, the human‑preference probability), and  
2. ** stay close to the reference SFT model** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (the KL‑constraint).

The KL term is essential: the reward model is only trustworthy in the neighbourhood of the data on which it was trained (the SFT‑generated samples). If the policy drifts too far, the reward estimates become unreliable and the model can degenerate (e.g. “reward hacking”). Consequently, PPO and DPO deliberately limit how much probability mass they can move from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

The “Best‑of‑$N$” baseline sidesteps this trade‑off. It keeps the reference policy unchanged, draws $N$ independent completions $\{y_{1},\dots ,y_{N}\}\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$, evaluates each with the *same* reward model that PPO/DPO use, and returns the highest‑scoring sample. Because the reference distribution is never altered, the reward model is always evaluated on data that lies exactly where it was trained, so its predictions are as accurate as possible. When $N$ is large enough, the probability of sampling a near‑optimal answer becomes high, and the resulting “chosen” answer can have a higher expected reward than the answer produced by a constrained policy that is forced to be a *single* point in the distribution.

In short, the advantage comes from **more exhaustive search** rather than a better optimisation algorithm.

---

### Does this mean the reward model is still useful for ranking?

Absolutely. The fact that “Best‑of‑$N$” can beat the learned policies demonstrates that the reward (or preference) model captures a meaningful ordering over the candidate completions. If the reward model were random, picking the top‑scoring sample among many would not systematically improve over a single policy sample. The authors therefore include the curve to show an *upper bound*: given the current reward model, how far could we go if we were willing to enumerate many candidates at inference time?

---

### Can we combine DPO with a sampling‑and‑ranking step?

Yes, and the paper hints at this possibility. A straightforward hybrid would be:

1. **Train with DPO** (or PPO) to obtain a policy that already respects the KL constraint and therefore stays in a region where the reward model is reliable.  
2. **At inference time**, draw a modest number of samples (e.g. $N=8$ or $N=16$) from the DPO‑trained policy instead of from the original SFT policy, and rank them with the same reward model.

Because the DPO policy is already biased toward higher‑reward outputs, the *distribution* of its samples is shifted toward the top of the reward landscape. Consequently, even a small $N$ can give a noticeable boost over a single sample, while keeping the computational cost far lower than the “Best‑of‑$N$” experiments that use $N$ in the hundreds or thousands. This idea has been explored in earlier RLHF work under the name “sampling‑and‑ranking” or “top‑$k$ re‑ranking”, and the DPO paper’s discussion of the “Best‑of‑$N$” baseline validates that the reward model can be safely reused for that purpose.

---

### How should we interpret the “Best‑of‑$N$” results when evaluating alignment methods?

1. **Upper‑bound reference, not a competitor** – The curve tells us the *potential* performance of the current reward model if we were willing to expend large amounts of inference compute. It is not a realistic baseline for most deployments because the cost grows linearly with $N$.  

2. **Indicator of reward‑model quality** – If “Best‑of‑$N$” only marginally outperforms the learned policy, the reward model’s ranking power is limited. Conversely, a large gap indicates that the reward model is strong and that the policy optimisation (PPO/DPO) is the bottleneck.  

3. **Guide for practical trade‑offs** – Practitioners can choose a modest $N$ (e.g. 4–16) that gives a measurable quality boost while keeping latency acceptable. The paper’s experiments show that even with $N=8$ the “Best‑of‑$N$” score is often close to the DPO curve, suggesting that modest re‑ranking is a practical way to squeeze extra performance out of a DPO‑trained model.

4. **Not a fairness issue** – Comparing a single‑policy method to an exhaustive search is analogous to comparing a greedy algorithm to a beam search: the latter is inherently more powerful because it explores a larger set of hypotheses. The authors are transparent about this discrepancy and use the baseline purely to illustrate the *room for improvement* that a better optimisation algorithm could capture.

---

### Bottom line

* The “Best‑of‑$N$” baseline outperforms PPO/DPO because it leverages the reward model’s ranking ability on many independent draws, avoiding the KL‑constraint that limits a single policy.  
* This result confirms that the reward model is useful for ranking; it is a useful component even after the policy has been trained.  
* A practical system can combine DPO (or PPO) with a modest‑size sampling‑and‑ranking step to obtain a better trade‑off between quality and inference cost.  
* When reading the paper, treat the “Best‑of‑$N$” curve as an *oracle‑style* ceiling rather than a competitor—its purpose is to reveal how much of the remaining gap could be closed by simply improving the policy optimisation or by allowing a limited amount of additional inference.
