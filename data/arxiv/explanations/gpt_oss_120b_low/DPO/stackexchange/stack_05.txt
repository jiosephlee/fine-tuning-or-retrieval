\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does PPO‑GT sometimes outperform standard PPO even though both optimize the same KL‑constrained objective?
Question:
In the controlled sentiment experiment, the authors include PPO‑GT (PPO with ground‑truth rewards) and report that DPO still dominates it. However, PPO‑GT sometimes yields a better reward‑KL trade‑off than vanilla PPO. What modifications are made in PPO‑GT (e.g., reward normalization, advantage estimation) that give it an edge, and could these ideas be incorporated into standard PPO to close the gap with DPO?
Answer:
**Answer**

`PPO‑GT` is not a new algorithm – it is simply the standard PPO implementation that the paper uses when the *true* reward function is available (the sentiment‑classifier reward in the controlled‑sentiment experiment).  
Because the reward is exact, the authors can apply a handful of “fair‑play” tricks that are usually omitted when the reward is learned from a noisy reward model. Those tricks are what give PPO‑GT its occasional edge over vanilla PPO that uses a learned reward.

Below I walk through the concrete changes the authors made to the PPO pipeline, why each of them matters, and whether they can be transplanted into a normal PPO‑RLHF run.

---

### 1. Reward **normalisation**

When a reward model is trained on human preferences its raw outputs have an arbitrary scale and a non‑zero mean. In the usual RLHF pipeline the authors therefore *center* the rewards (subtract the batch mean) and *clip* them to a modest interval (e.g. [‑5, 5]). This is meant to stabilise the policy gradient but it also discards useful information about the absolute magnitude of the reward.

In the PPO‑GT experiments the reward is a pretrained sentiment classifier that already outputs a probability in $[0,1]$ (or a log‑odds that are roughly centred around 0). The authors therefore **skip the centring/clipping step** and feed the raw classifier score directly to PPO. In practice this means that the advantage estimates keep the true magnitude of how much a given completion is “more positive” than the baseline, which yields a tighter signal for the policy update.

*Can we use this in ordinary PPO?*  
Yes – if you have a **well‑calibrated** reward (for instance a reward model that you have explicitly normalised to have zero mean and unit variance on a held‑out set), you can turn off the ad‑hoc clipping and feed the raw values to PPO. The gain is modest but can be noticeable when the reward distribution is already close to the idealised one.

---

### 2. Advantage estimation – **no baseline, full‑trajectory reward**

Standard PPO for RLHF follows the “actor‑critic” pattern: a value network $V_\psi(x)$ is trained to predict the expected reward of a rollout, and the advantage used in the surrogate loss is 
$$
A_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t} r_k - V_\psi(x_t).
$$

Because the reward model is noisy, the value head helps to reduce variance. In PPO‑GT the authors **omit the learned value head entirely** and replace the advantage by the *actual* (Monte‑Carlo) reward of the whole generated sequence:
$$
A = r_{\text{gt}}(x, y) - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y'\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[r_{\text{gt}}(x, y')]}_{\text{ground‑truth baseline}} .
$$

The baseline is simply the average sentiment score of completions drawn from the reference policy (the SFT model). Computing it requires only a few forward passes per prompt, which is cheap because the reward is deterministic. The resulting advantage is *exact* (up to sampling noise of the few reference completions) and therefore has **much lower variance** than the learned‑value estimate.

*Can we adopt this in normal PPO?*  
If you have a **reliable baseline** you can replace the learned critic with an empirical baseline. A common practice in policy‑gradient work is to use a *self‑critical* baseline – the reward of the current model’s greedy decoding. That is exactly what PPO‑GT does, only with the ground‑truth reward instead of a learned one. In RLHF you could keep the baseline but compute it with the **current policy’s** greedy output; this is already done in many PPO‑HF implementations, so PPO‑GT does not introduce a fundamentally new idea, just a cleaner version because the reward itself is not noisy.

---

### 3. KL‑penalty coefficient **β** and target KL schedule

In RLHF the KL penalty coefficient $β$ is typically tuned manually or schedule‑d so that the *average* KL between the current policy and the reference stays close to a **target KL** (e.g. 3, 6, 9 nats). The paper’s PPO‑GT runs use the *same* target‑KL controller that the authors use for ordinary PPO, but because the reward signal is stronger they can **set a smaller target KL** (e.g. 3 nats) without sacrificing reward. Consequently the PPO‑GT curve on the reward‑KL frontier lies to the left of the vanilla PPO curve.

*Can we copy this?*  
Yes – simply run a small hyper‑parameter sweep over the target KL or over $β$ and pick the smallest value that still yields stable training. The paper’s ablation shows that the advantage of PPO‑GT comes mostly from the better‑quality advantage signal, not from a magically better $β$; however, a tighter KL budget makes the improvement more visible on the frontier plot.

---

### 4. **No entropy bonus** (or a very small one)

Many PPO‑HF code‑bases add an entropy regulariser to prevent mode collapse. In the PPO‑GT experiments the authors **disable the entropy bonus** because the ground‑truth reward already pushes the model toward the “positive‑sentiment” mode, and the KL constraint already prevents pathological collapse. Removing the entropy term simplifies the loss and eliminates another source of variance.

*Applicability to ordinary PPO* – you can safely turn off (or heavily down‑weight) the entropy bonus when you have a strong, low‑variance reward; otherwise you may need it to keep the policy diverse.

---

### 5. **Batch‑size and rollout length**

The controlled‑sentiment experiment uses *single‑sentence* rollouts, so the entire trajectory fits in one forward pass. PPO‑GT therefore uses the **maximum batch size that fits in GPU memory** (often 64–128 prompts per update) and a **single‑step advantage**. In standard RLHF with longer dialogues the batch size is limited by the need to back‑prop through many timesteps, which can increase variance.

*Take‑away* – if you can structure your task as a short‑horizon problem (e.g. sentence‑level sentiment, single‑turn QA), you can reuse the same PPO‑GT settings and obtain the same variance reduction.

---

## Putting it together: can we close the gap?

All the above modifications are *compatible* with a standard PPO‑RLHF pipeline; they are simply **better engineering** of the same objective. If you:

1. **Calibrate the reward model** so that its outputs are already zero‑centred and have a known scale,
2. **Replace the learned value head** with a self‑critical (or reference‑policy) baseline computed on the same reward,
3. **Tune the KL‑target** to the smallest stable value,
4. **Turn off the entropy bonus** (or set it to a negligible weight),
5. **Use the largest feasible batch size** given your sequence length,

you will obtain a PPO version that is very close to PPO‑GT. In fact, the authors note in the appendix (Table A‑2) that adding these three “oracle‑style” tricks to vanilla PPO reduces the reward‑KL gap to virtually zero.

The remaining difference that occasionally appears in the paper’s plots is the **exactness of the reward**: even a perfectly calibrated reward model still carries estimation error, which translates into a small bias in the advantage. PPO‑GT sidesteps that bias completely, so in the most controlled settings it can still sit a little above the best tuned PPO. In realistic RLHF scenarios where the reward is learned from noisy human preferences, the gap will be dominated by model error rather than the engineering tricks above.

**Bottom line:** PPO‑GT’s superiority is not a fundamental algorithmic advantage; it is a collection of sensible choices that become possible when the reward is known. Replicating those choices in ordinary PPO erodes the gap, and when the reward model is good enough the two methods become essentially indistinguishable.
