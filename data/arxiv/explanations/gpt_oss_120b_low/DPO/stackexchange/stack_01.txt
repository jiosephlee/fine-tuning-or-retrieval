\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the DPO objective recover the optimal KL‑constrained policy without estimating the partition function?
Question:
In Section 4 the paper derives Eq. (4) from the optimal policy form in Eq. (3) and then substitutes this into the Bradley‑Terry model to obtain Eq. (5). The authors claim that the partition function \(Z(x)\) cancels out, yielding a preference probability that depends only on the ratios \(\pi^*(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\). I am unsure how this cancellation works algebraically, especially since \(Z(x)\) appears inside a logarithm in Eq. (4). Could someone walk through the step‑by‑step algebra showing the cancellation, and explain why no approximation of \(Z(x)\) is needed in practice?
Answer:
**Answer**

The key to the “partition‑function cancellation’’ is that the Bradley–Terry (BT) model never uses the *absolute* value of a reward, but only *differences* of rewards for two candidates.  
Because of this, any term that is the same for both candidates – in particular a term that depends only on the prompt \(x\) – disappears when we write the BT probability in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\).

Below I walk through the algebra that the paper hides in the short sentence *“the partition function cancels’’* and explain why, in practice, we never have to estimate \(Z(x)\).

---

### 1. From the KL‑constrained RL problem to the optimal policy

The RL‑HF objective (Eq. (2) in the paper)

\[
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]
\;-\;
\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr]
\]

is a standard constrained‑maximisation problem.  
Using the method of Lagrange multipliers (or equivalently the “control‑as‑inference’’ trick) one obtains the closed‑form optimal policy

\[
\boxed{\;
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr)
\;},
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

\(Z(x)\) is the normalising constant that makes \(\pi_{r}(\cdot|x)\) a proper distribution.

---

### 2. Solving for the reward in terms of the policy

Take the logarithm of (3) and rearrange:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log \pi_{r}(y|x) 
&= -\log Z(x) + \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y) 
&= \log \pi_{r}(y|x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) + \log Z(x) .
\end{aligned}
\]

Multiplying by \(\beta\) gives the **re‑parameterisation** used in the paper

\[
\boxed{ \;
r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
      +\beta\log Z(x)
\;}. \tag{4}
\]

Notice that the only place where \(Z(x)\) appears is the additive term \(\beta\log Z(x)\), which is *independent of the particular completion* \(y\).

---

### 3. Plugging the re‑parameterisation into the Bradley–Terry model

The BT model for a human preference between two completions \(y_{1},y_{2}\) given the same prompt \(x\) is

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
   =\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
          {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}
   =\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})\) is the logistic function.

Now replace the (unknown) ground‑truth reward \(r^{*}\) by the expression (4) that holds for the *optimal* policy \(\pi^{*}\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{i})
&= \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i}|x)}
   + \beta\log Z(x) ,\qquad i\in\{1,2\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

When we form the difference that appears in the logistic, the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel:

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})
&= \beta \Bigl[ \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}
                -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]

Substituting this back into the BT probability yields exactly Eq. (6) of the paper:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
 = \frac{1}
        {1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}
                       -\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}\Bigr)} .
\tag{6}
\]

All dependence on the partition function \(Z(x)\) has vanished because it was **identical for the two alternatives**; the BT likelihood only cares about the *ratio* of the two exponentiated rewards.

---

### 4. Why we never need to approximate \(Z(x)\)

The cancellation shows that, for the purpose of matching human preferences, the *only* quantities that matter are the **policy‑to‑reference odds** \(\pi^{*}(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\).  
Consequently the loss we actually optimise (Eq. (7) in the paper)

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
  = - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
        \bigl[\,\log\sigma\bigl(
               \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
              -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}
               \bigr)\bigr]
\]

contains **no** \(Z(x)\). During training we only need the *log‑probabilities* of the two candidates under the current model \(\pi_{\theta}\) and under the fixed reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Both are directly available from the language‑model logits; no extra normalisation over the whole vocabulary or over all possible completions is required.

In other words, the partition function is *implicitly* handled by the re‑parameterisation: we have chosen a specific representative of each reward‑equivalence class (Lemma 1 of the paper) that is already normalised, i.e. it satisfies \(\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=1\). Because the BT likelihood is invariant to adding an *x‑only* constant to the reward, we are free to drop that constant (\(\beta\log Z(x)\)) without changing the optimisation target.

---

### 5. Intuition

Think of the BT model as a **pairwise comparison**: a human judges which of two utterances is better. If we imagine each utterance receiving a score, the human only cares about the *relative* height of the two scores, not about where the whole set of scores sits on the absolute axis. Adding the same constant to every score (which is exactly what \(\beta\log Z(x)\) does) slides the whole landscape up or down but leaves every pairwise ordering unchanged. Hence the constant can be ignored.

This is the same phenomenon that appears in *log‑linear* models, conditional random fields, and any maximum‑entropy model: the normalising constant (partition function) is required to define a bona‑fide probability distribution, but it disappears when we form *conditional* odds or *likelihood ratios* of two configurations that share the same context.

---

### 6. Summary

1. The optimal KL‑constrained policy has the form \(\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r/\beta)\).  
2. Solving for \(r\) gives \(r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\).  
3. In the BT likelihood the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel because they are identical for the two candidates.  
4. Consequently the DPO loss depends only on the ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\) and never requires estimating \(Z(x)\).
