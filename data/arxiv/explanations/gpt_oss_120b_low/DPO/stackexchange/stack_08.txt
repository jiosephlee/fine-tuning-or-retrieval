\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### What is the role of the temperature‑like hyperparameter \(\beta\) in DPO, and how should it be tuned?
Question:
The paper treats \(\beta\) as a KL‑constraint strength, analogous to the PPO KL‑penalty coefficient. In experiments they sweep over \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\). How does changing \(\beta\) affect the implicit reward (Eq. 7) and the resulting policy’s conservativeness? Is there a principled way (e.g., target KL) to select \(\beta\) instead of brute‑force grid search?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Direct Preference Optimization the scalar $\beta$ appears in exactly the same place as the KL‑penalty coefficient in the standard RLHF objective  

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_\theta}\!\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

After the change‑of‑variables that turns the reward \(r\) into a *policy‑based* reward  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\tag{2}
\]

$\beta$ also multiplies the log‑probability ratio inside the DPO loss (Eq. 7 of the paper)

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-
\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{3}
\]

Thus **$\beta$ controls the strength of the KL‑constraint**: a larger $\beta$ makes the term
\(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) grow more slowly with a change in the policy
probabilities, which in turn reduces the magnitude of the per‑example weight
\(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) that appears in the gradient (Eq. 8).
Consequently:

* **Small $\beta$ (e.g. 0.05)**  
  – The log‑ratio is amplified, so the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta\) changes a lot for a modest shift in probabilities.  
  – The weighting factor \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) becomes close to 1 for almost every pair, so the update behaves like an un‑weighted “prefer‑the‑winner” loss.  
  – The resulting policy moves aggressively toward the preferred completions, often at the cost of a large KL divergence from the reference model (i.e. a **less conservative** policy).

* **Large $\beta$ (e.g. 5)**  
  – The log‑ratio is damped; a given change in the policy’s logits yields only a small change in \(\hat r_\theta\).  
  – The weight \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) now distinguishes well‑aligned from badly‑aligned examples: if the current policy already ranks the preferred response higher, the weight is close to 0, and the update is almost ignored.  
  – The optimisation therefore only nudges the policy when it makes a clear mistake, keeping the KL to the reference model low (a **more conservative** policy).

Because the DPO objective is mathematically equivalent to the KL‑constrained RL objective, **$\beta$ can be interpreted as the inverse temperature of a Boltzmann‑type policy**: $\beta = 1$ corresponds to the “standard” trade‑off used in most RLHF work, while $\beta < 1$ tightens the reward term relative to the KL term and $\beta > 1$ does the opposite.

---

### Choosing $\beta$ without a brute‑force sweep

The paper only reports a small grid sweep (0.05, 0.1, 1, 5), but the same theory that underlies KL‑penalised PPO gives a principled way to set $\beta$:

1. **Target KL (or KL budget).**  
   In PPO the practitioner often specifies a desired average KL per update, e.g. 3–6 nats. Since the optimal policy under (1) satisfies  

   \[
   \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
   \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\Bigr),
   \tag{4}
   \]

   the expected KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a monotonic function of $\beta$. In practice one can:
   * Run a few *short* DPO updates with a tentative $\beta$, compute the empirical KL on a validation set, and adjust $\beta$ (e.g. halve it if KL > target, double it if KL < target).  
   * Because the KL is computed analytically from the two policies (no sampling needed), this “$\beta$‑tuning loop” is cheap.

2. **Reward‑scale normalisation.**  
   The Bradley‑Terry model cares only about *differences* of rewards. If the learned reward values have a known variance $\sigma^2$ (e.g. after normalising the reward model to zero mean and unit variance), setting  

   \[
   \beta = \sigma
   \]

   aligns the magnitude of the log‑ratio term with the spread of the reward, yielding a balanced trade‑off. The authors normalise rewards to zero mean in the reward‑model stage; the same normalisation can be applied to the implicit reward (2) after a few DPO steps.

3. **Adaptive $\beta$.**  
   One may treat $\beta$ as a hyper‑parameter that is updated during training to keep a running KL estimate close to a target. This mirrors the KL‑penalty schedule used in KL‑penalised PPO, where the coefficient is increased if the KL exceeds the budget and decreased otherwise. Because the KL can be evaluated exactly, an update such as  

   \[
   \beta_{t+1} \gets \beta_t \times
   \exp\!\bigl(\eta\,(\text{KL}_t - \text{KL}_{\text{target}})\bigr)
   \]

   (with a small learning rate $\eta$) gives a stable, automatic schedule.

These three ideas let you move from a coarse grid search to a **target‑KL driven** selection of $\beta$, which is what many RLHF pipelines already do for PPO. The only extra cost is the evaluation of the KL, which is trivial compared with sampling‑based RL.

---

### Summary

* $\beta$ scales the KL‑penalty: **small $\beta$ → aggressive reward maximisation, high KL; large $\beta$ → conservative updates, low KL**.  
* The implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ grows linearly with $\beta$, so changing $\beta$ directly rescales the weight given to each preference pair in the DPO gradient.  
* Instead of a blind grid search, you can choose $\beta$ by **matching a desired KL budget**, by **normalising the reward scale**, or by **adapting $\beta$ online** to keep the KL close to a target—exactly the same philosophy used for the PPO KL‑penalty.  

Using any of these strategies will give you a principled, data‑driven way to set $\beta$, preserving the simplicity of DPO while avoiding expensive hyper‑parameter sweeps.
