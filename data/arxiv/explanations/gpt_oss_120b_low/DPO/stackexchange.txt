\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### How does the DPO objective recover the optimal KL‑constrained policy without estimating the partition function?
Question:
In Section 4 the paper derives Eq. (4) from the optimal policy form in Eq. (3) and then substitutes this into the Bradley‑Terry model to obtain Eq. (5). The authors claim that the partition function \(Z(x)\) cancels out, yielding a preference probability that depends only on the ratios \(\pi^*(y\mid x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\). I am unsure how this cancellation works algebraically, especially since \(Z(x)\) appears inside a logarithm in Eq. (4). Could someone walk through the step‑by‑step algebra showing the cancellation, and explain why no approximation of \(Z(x)\) is needed in practice?
Answer:
**Answer**

The key to the “partition‑function cancellation’’ is that the Bradley–Terry (BT) model never uses the *absolute* value of a reward, but only *differences* of rewards for two candidates.  
Because of this, any term that is the same for both candidates – in particular a term that depends only on the prompt \(x\) – disappears when we write the BT probability in terms of the optimal policy \(\pi^{*}\).

Below I walk through the algebra that the paper hides in the short sentence *“the partition function cancels’’* and explain why, in practice, we never have to estimate \(Z(x)\).

---

### 1. From the KL‑constrained RL problem to the optimal policy

The RL‑HF objective (Eq. (2) in the paper)

\[
\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x,y)]
\;-\;
\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr]
\]

is a standard constrained‑maximisation problem.  
Using the method of Lagrange multipliers (or equivalently the “control‑as‑inference’’ trick) one obtains the closed‑form optimal policy

\[
\boxed{\;
\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\Bigr)
\;},
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr).
\tag{3}
\]

\(Z(x)\) is the normalising constant that makes \(\pi_{r}(\cdot|x)\) a proper distribution.

---

### 2. Solving for the reward in terms of the policy

Take the logarithm of (3) and rearrange:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log \pi_{r}(y|x) 
&= -\log Z(x) + \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) + \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\\[4pt]
\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y) 
&= \log \pi_{r}(y|x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) + \log Z(x) .
\end{aligned}
\]

Multiplying by \(\beta\) gives the **re‑parameterisation** used in the paper

\[
\boxed{ \;
r(x,y)=\beta \log\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
      +\beta\log Z(x)
\;}. \tag{4}
\]

Notice that the only place where \(Z(x)\) appears is the additive term \(\beta\log Z(x)\), which is *independent of the particular completion* \(y\).

---

### 3. Plugging the re‑parameterisation into the Bradley–Terry model

The BT model for a human preference between two completions \(y_{1},y_{2}\) given the same prompt \(x\) is

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
   =\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
          {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}
   =\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr),
\tag{5}
\]

where \(\sigma(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})\) is the logistic function.

Now replace the (unknown) ground‑truth reward \(r^{*}\) by the expression (4) that holds for the *optimal* policy \(\pi^{*}\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{i})
&= \beta \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{i}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{i}|x)}
   + \beta\log Z(x) ,\qquad i\in\{1,2\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

When we form the difference that appears in the logistic, the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel:

\[
\begin{aligned}
r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})
&= \beta \Bigl[ \log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}
                -\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}\Bigr].
\end{aligned}
\]

Substituting this back into the BT probability yields exactly Eq. (6) of the paper:

\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
 = \frac{1}
        {1+\exp\!\Bigl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}
                       -\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}\Bigr)} .
\tag{6}
\]

All dependence on the partition function \(Z(x)\) has vanished because it was **identical for the two alternatives**; the BT likelihood only cares about the *ratio* of the two exponentiated rewards.

---

### 4. Why we never need to approximate \(Z(x)\)

The cancellation shows that, for the purpose of matching human preferences, the *only* quantities that matter are the **policy‑to‑reference odds** \(\pi^{*}(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\).  
Consequently the loss we actually optimise (Eq. (7) in the paper)

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})
  = - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}
        \bigl[\,\log\sigma\bigl(
               \beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)}
              -\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}
               \bigr)\bigr]
\]

contains **no** \(Z(x)\). During training we only need the *log‑probabilities* of the two candidates under the current model \(\pi_{\theta}\) and under the fixed reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Both are directly available from the language‑model logits; no extra normalisation over the whole vocabulary or over all possible completions is required.

In other words, the partition function is *implicitly* handled by the re‑parameterisation: we have chosen a specific representative of each reward‑equivalence class (Lemma 1 of the paper) that is already normalised, i.e. it satisfies \(\sum_{y}\pi_{\theta}(y|x)=1\). Because the BT likelihood is invariant to adding an *x‑only* constant to the reward, we are free to drop that constant (\(\beta\log Z(x)\)) without changing the optimisation target.

---

### 5. Intuition

Think of the BT model as a **pairwise comparison**: a human judges which of two utterances is better. If we imagine each utterance receiving a score, the human only cares about the *relative* height of the two scores, not about where the whole set of scores sits on the absolute axis. Adding the same constant to every score (which is exactly what \(\beta\log Z(x)\) does) slides the whole landscape up or down but leaves every pairwise ordering unchanged. Hence the constant can be ignored.

This is the same phenomenon that appears in *log‑linear* models, conditional random fields, and any maximum‑entropy model: the normalising constant (partition function) is required to define a bona‑fide probability distribution, but it disappears when we form *conditional* odds or *likelihood ratios* of two configurations that share the same context.

---

### 6. Summary

1. The optimal KL‑constrained policy has the form \(\pi_{r}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r/\beta)\).  
2. Solving for \(r\) gives \(r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log Z(x)\).  
3. In the BT likelihood the \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms cancel because they are identical for the two candidates.  
4. Consequently the DPO loss depends only on the ratio \(\pi_{\theta}(y)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\) and never requires estimating \(Z(x)\).

### Why does the DPO loss weight examples by \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))\) and how does this prevent degeneration?
Question:
Equation (7) shows the gradient of the DPO loss, where the term \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x, y_w))\) acts as a weight. The authors state that without this weighting the model collapses (see Appendix Table X). I would like to understand intuitively why weighting by the sigmoid of the reward difference is necessary and how it discourages the model from assigning high probability to dispreferred completions. How does this compare to the unlikelihood loss term used in the "Unlikelihood" baseline?
Answer:
The DPO gradient (Eq. 7 in the paper) can be written as  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
 = -\beta\;\mathbb E_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal D}
   \underbrace{\sigma\!\Bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})\Bigr)}_{\text{importance weight}}
   \bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr],
$$

where  

$$
\hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
$$

The factor $\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))$ is the *sigmoid* of the **reward‑difference** estimated by the current policy.  
Understanding why this weighting is crucial amounts to looking at two extremes.

---

### 1. What would happen without the weight?

If we simply dropped the sigmoid and used  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal L_{\text{naïve}} = -\beta\;
   \bigl[\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)-\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)\bigr],
$$

every training pair would receive the same magnitude of update, regardless of how the model currently scores the two completions.  
When the model already assigns a **much higher probability** to the dispreferred completion ($y_{l}$) than to the preferred one ($y_{w}$), the naïve update still tries to push probability mass from $y_{w}$ to $y_{l}$ with exactly the same strength as it does for a well‑behaved pair where the model already prefers $y_{w}$.  

Because the loss is symmetric in the two logits, the optimiser can satisfy it by **collapsing both logits toward the reference distribution** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In the extreme, the model learns to output the reference policy for every input, which is precisely the degeneration reported in Appendix Table X: the policy stops improving the preferred completions and reverts to the SFT (or reference) model.

---

### 2. What does the sigmoid weight do?

The sigmoid is a smooth, bounded function that maps a reward difference to a value in $[0,1]$.  
* If the current implicit reward already ranks the preferred completion higher ($\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})>\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})$), the difference is negative and the sigmoid is **close to 0**. The corresponding gradient contribution is tiny: the model does not waste effort pushing an already‑correct ranking farther apart.  
* If the model **gets the ranking wrong** (the dispreferred answer receives a larger reward), the difference is positive and the sigmoid approaches **1**. In that case the full gradient magnitude is applied, strongly encouraging the model to increase $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)$ and decrease $\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)$.

Thus the weighting implements an *importance sampling* that focuses learning on the **mistakes** of the current policy. By scaling the update proportionally to the probability that the model would choose the wrong answer, the algorithm automatically respects the KL‑constraint encoded in $\beta$; when the KL term already forces the model close to the reference, the reward differences become small and the weight shrinks, preventing the policy from drifting too far.

---

### 3. Connection to the “unlikelihood” baseline

The unlikelihood loss (Welleck et al., 2019) also contains a term that penalises the probability of a dispreferred token or sequence:  

$$
\mathcal L_{\text{unlik}} = -\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{w,t}\mid x,\!y_{w,<t}) 
      + \alpha \sum_{t}\log\bigl(1-\pi_{\theta}(y_{l,t}\mid x,\!y_{l,<t})\bigr),
$$

where $\alpha$ is a hand‑tuned scalar. The key difference is that the coefficient $\alpha$ is *global*: every dispreferred example receives the same push‑down strength, regardless of how badly the model currently scores it. Consequently, as observed in the paper’s ablation, unlikelihood can still drive the model toward a degenerate solution where it merely learns to “anti‑copy” the dispreferred outputs without respecting the KL‑regularisation; the loss lacks a mechanism that recognises when the model already assigns low probability to a bad answer.

In contrast, DPO’s weight **adapts per example** and **per training step** based on the model’s own reward estimate. When the model already suppresses a bad completion, the weight becomes negligible, so the KL‑penalty dominates and the policy stays near the reference. When the model is over‑confident in a bad completion, the weight becomes near‑one and the update directly counteracts that over‑confidence. This dynamic weighting is what prevents the collapse observed for naïve ratio or unlikelihood objectives.

---

### 4. Intuitive picture

Imagine a teacher correcting a student’s answers. If the student already gets a question right, the teacher says nothing (weight ≈ 0). If the student gets it wrong, the teacher gives a strong correction (weight ≈ 1). A static penalty that “always scolds” the student, even for correct answers, would waste effort and could even encourage the student to give the same answer every time to minimise the teacher’s attention—a behaviour analogous to the policy collapsing to the reference model. The sigmoid weight implements exactly this “only correct the mistakes” principle, and because the mistake magnitude is measured by the model’s own reward estimate, the correction is automatically calibrated to respect the KL budget.

---

**In summary**, the sigmoid weighting term in the DPO gradient is essential because it turns the loss into an *error‑driven* objective: it amplifies updates where the implicit reward mis‑orders the pair and attenuates them where the ordering is already correct. This prevents the model from uniformly pushing probability mass toward the reference distribution (the collapse observed without the weight) and distinguishes DPO from the static unlikelihood loss, which cannot adapt its strength per example and therefore lacks this stabilising effect.

### What is the equivalence class of reward functions and why does it not affect the optimal policy?
Question:
The paper defines two reward functions as equivalent if they differ by a function \(f(x)\) that depends only on the prompt. Lemma 2 claims that reward functions in the same class induce the same optimal policy under the KL‑constrained objective. I am confused because adding a constant (or prompt‑dependent) offset changes the absolute values of rewards. How does the KL constraint make these offsets irrelevant, and could anyone provide a short proof or intuition for Lemma 2?
Answer:
Below is a concise, self‑contained explanation of why the **reward‑function equivalence class** introduced in the DPO paper does not affect the optimal policy of the KL‑constrained RLHF objective, together with an intuitive sketch of the proof of Lemma 2.

---

### 1. What does “equivalent’’ mean for reward functions?

The paper defines two reward functions \(r\) and \(r'\) to be *equivalent* when they differ only by a term that depends on the prompt (the context) \(x\) but **not** on the continuation \(y\):

$$
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x),\qquad\text{for some function }f.
$$

In other words, the two rewards assign the **same relative advantage** to any pair of completions for a fixed prompt, because the additive term \(f(x)\) cancels out when we take a difference:

$$
r'(x,y_{1})-r'(x,y_{2})=\bigl[r(x,y_{1})+f(x)\bigr]-\bigl[r(x,y_{2})+f(x)\bigr]
 =r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2}).
$$

Hence the *preference model* (Bradley–Terry or any Plackett‑Luce variant) that depends only on reward differences assigns exactly the same probability to a human preferring \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\).

---

### 2. Why does the KL‑constraint make the additive term irrelevant?

The RLHF objective (Eq. (2) of the paper) is

$$
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\;y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\bigl[\,r(x,y)\bigr]\;
-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\tag{1}
$$

Suppose we replace \(r\) by an equivalent reward \(r' = r+f\). The first term becomes

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\,r'(x,y)\,]=
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\,r(x,y)\,] + \mathbb{E}_{x}[\,f(x)\,],
$$

because the expectation over \(y\) does **not** affect \(f(x)\). The second term, the KL divergence, does not involve the reward at all. Since \(\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(x)]\) does not depend on the policy parameters \(\theta\), it is a *constant offset* for the optimisation problem. Adding a constant to an objective does not change the set of maximisers, i.e. the optimal policy stays the same.

Thus, any two rewards that differ only by a prompt‑only function produce identical optimisation problems, and consequently the same optimal policy.

---

### 3. Formal statement (Lemma 2) and short proof

**Lemma 2 (restated).**  
Let \(r\) and \(r'\) be two reward functions such that \(r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)\) for some \(f\). Then the solutions of (1) with reward \(r\) and with reward \(r'\) are identical.

**Proof (sketch).**  
Define the objective with reward \(r'\) as

$$
J_{r'}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\,r(x,y)\,] + \mathbb{E}_{x}[f(x)] -\beta\,\mathrm{KL}[\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}].
$$

The term \(\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(x)]\) does not involve \(\pi\) and therefore its gradient w.r.t. \(\theta\) is zero. Consequently

$$
\nabla_{\theta} J_{r'}(\pi_{\theta}) = \nabla_{\theta}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\,r(x,y)\,] -\beta\,\mathrm{KL}[\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]\Bigr)
 = \nabla_{\theta} J_{r}(\pi_{\theta}).
$$

Since the gradients coincide everywhere, the set of stationary points – in particular the global maximiser – of \(J_{r'}\) and \(J_{r}\) are the same. ∎

---

### 4. Intuition in the context of the DPO derivation

Equation (4) of the paper rewrites the optimal policy for any reward as

$$
\pi_{r}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Bigr),
\qquad
Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr).
$$

If we add a prompt‑only function \(f(x)\) to \(r\),

$$
\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}[r(x,y)+f(x)]\bigr)
  =\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x)\bigr)\,
    \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\bigr),
$$

and the same factor \(\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}f(x)\bigr)\) multiplies *every* term inside the normalising sum \(Z(x)\). Hence it cancels out in the fraction, leaving \(\pi_{r}\) unchanged. This algebraic cancellation mirrors the constant‑offset argument above and shows directly that the *policy* does not depend on the particular choice of additive prompt term.

---

### 5. Why the paper can safely re‑parameterise rewards

Because any reward in an equivalence class can be written as  

$$
r_{\text{eq}}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
$$

the authors pick the **canonical member** of the class that has zero partition‑function term (the “log‑probability ratio’’). This choice is convenient: the normalising constant disappears from the Bradley–Terry likelihood, yielding the DPO loss (Equation (7)). Lemma 2 guarantees that this canonicalisation does **not** restrict the set of optimal policies – every optimal policy that could be obtained from any reward in the class is still reachable by a reward of the canonical form.

---

### 6. Take‑away

* Adding a prompt‑only offset to a reward changes absolute reward values but not the *relative* ordering of completions for a given prompt.  
* The KL‑regularised objective treats the reward only through its gradient w.r.t. the policy; a constant offset contributes nothing to the gradient and therefore does not affect the optimal policy.  
* Consequently, all reward functions that belong to the same equivalence class (i.e. differ by a prompt‑only function) induce exactly the same optimal policy, which justifies the re‑parameterisation used in DPO and validates Lemma 2.  

This equivalence is what allows DPO to discard an explicit reward model and optimise the policy directly with a simple binary‑cross‑entropy loss, while still being theoretically identical to the original RLHF objective.

### How is the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) chosen when an SFT model is unavailable, and what impact does this have on DPO performance?
Question:
Section 4 mentions that if a supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model is not present, \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is obtained by maximum likelihood on the preferred completions. Does this choice guarantee that \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is close enough to the true data distribution for the KL‑penalty to be meaningful? Have the authors evaluated the sensitivity of DPO to a mismatched reference policy, and what practical guidelines should be followed when constructing \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)?
Answer:
**Answer**

In the DPO pipeline the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ plays the same role that the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) model plays in classic RLHF: it is the distribution from which the KL‑penalty is measured and against which the new policy is discouraged from drifting too far.  
When an SFT model is not available the authors propose to *learn* a reference policy by maximum‑likelihood on the **preferred** completions $\left(y_{w}\right)$ of the preference dataset:

\[
\pi_{\text{ref}} \;=\;\arg\max_{\pi}\;
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w})\sim\mathcal D}\left[\log \pi(y_{w}\mid x)\right].
\]

This is essentially a supervised‑fine‑tuning step using only the “winner’’ side of each pair, i.e. the same data that would be used for a Preferred‑FT baseline.  

---

### Why this is sufficient for the KL‑penalty

The KL‑term in the original RLHF objective

\[
\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid x)\;\|\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)\bigr]
\]

does not require that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ be the *true* data distribution; it only needs a tractable distribution that is **reasonably close** to the distribution from which the preference pairs were drawn. The reason is two‑fold:

1. **Preference data are already biased toward the preferred completions.**  
   Preference pairs are generated by sampling two candidates from some policy (often an SFT model) and then labeling which one a human prefers. By construction, the *preferred* side of each pair lies in a region of the output space that the original policy already assigns non‑negligible probability. Fitting a maximum‑likelihood model on these preferred completions therefore recovers a policy that lives in roughly the same sub‑space as the original (unknown) reference distribution.

2. **The KL‑penalty is a *relative* regulariser.**  
   In the DPO derivation the KL term appears inside the log‑ratio that defines the implicit reward  

   \[
   \hat r_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
   \]

   Any constant shift of the reward (i.e. adding a function of $x$ only) cancels out when we form the preference probability
   \[
   \sigma\!\bigl(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})\bigr),
   \]
   so the exact normalisation of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ does not affect the objective. As long as $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ puts mass on the same set of completions that appear in the training pairs, the KL‑penalty will correctly penalise large departures from that region.

Consequently, the “MLE on preferred completions’’ strategy yields a reference that is *good enough* for the KL‑penalty to be meaningful, even though it is not the true pre‑training distribution.

---

### Sensitivity to a mismatched $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

The paper does not present an exhaustive study of how DPO behaves when $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is far from the true data distribution, but a few observations can be inferred from the experiments and the theoretical analysis:

* **Empirical robustness.**  
  In the dialogue experiment (“single‑turn dialogue”) the authors had *no* SFT model at all. They constructed $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by fine‑tuning a raw language model on the *preferred* completions (the “Preferred‑FT” step). DPO still achieved a win‑rate that exceeded PPO‑style baselines and the “Best‑of‑N” oracle, indicating that a reference built this way does not cripple performance.

* **Partition‑function cancellation.**  
  Because the Bradley‑Terry preference model depends only on *differences* of rewards, the unknown normalising constant (the partition function) disappears when we rewrite the likelihood in terms of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and $\pi_{\theta}$. This mathematical property makes DPO intrinsically less sensitive to the absolute scale of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

* **Theoretical guarantee.**  
  Theorem 1 in the paper shows that *any* reward function that is compatible with the Bradley‑Terry model can be expressed as  

  \[
  r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}
  \]

  for some policy $\pi$. In other words, even if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is an imperfect proxy, there always exists a policy $\pi$ that reproduces the same preference distribution. Hence DPO can, in principle, recover the optimal policy regardless of the exact shape of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, provided the preference data are sufficient.

Overall, while an extremely poor $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (e.g. a uniformly random language model) would give the KL term little regularising effect and could lead to instability, the *preferred‑completion MLE* construction is usually close enough to guarantee stable training.

---

### Practical guidelines for building $\pi_{\text{ref}}$

1. **Use the same data source that generated the preference pairs.**  
   If the pairs were sampled from a publicly released SFT checkpoint, set $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ = that checkpoint. This is what the authors do in the sentiment and summarisation experiments and yields the best KL‑anchor.

2. **When no SFT checkpoint exists, fine‑tune on the *winners* only.**  
   – Take every $(x,\,y_{w})$ from the preference dataset.  
   – Train a language model with a standard cross‑entropy loss (no weighting, no KL).  
   – Stop once the training loss plateaus; the resulting model is $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
   This simple “Preferred‑FT’’ step is cheap (a few hundred gradient steps) and already aligns the reference with the region of interest.

3. **Check the KL magnitude before full DPO training.**  
   Compute the average sequence‑level KL between the freshly trained $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and the *original* pre‑trained LM (or a known good checkpoint). If the KL is *very* high (e.g. > 10 nats per token), consider training the reference longer or adding a small amount of data from the original model to prevent it from collapsing to a narrow mode.

4. **Avoid mixing *dispreferred* completions into $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.**  
   Including the low‑quality side would pull the reference distribution toward undesirable regions and weaken the regulariser. The theory relies on the reference representing the *baseline* behaviour, not the *negative* behaviour.

5. **Choose $\beta$ consistently with the KL scale you desire.**  
   $\beta$ controls how aggressively DPO pushes probability mass from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to the preferred side. A common practical choice is $\beta\in[0.05,\,0.5]$ for a 2‑to‑6 B model; larger $\beta$ makes the KL term weaker and can tolerate a looser reference.

---

### Take‑away

*If an SFT model is unavailable, training a reference policy by maximum‑likelihood on the preferred completions is a theoretically sound and empirically robust solution.* The KL‑penalty does not demand an exact replica of the true data distribution; it only needs a reference that has support on the same answer space, which the “preferred‑only’’ MLE model provides. Experiments in the paper (dialogue and summarisation) show that DPO’s performance is not dramatically harmed when $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is constructed this way, and the authors’ theoretical analysis guarantees that any reward compatible with the Bradley‑Terry model can be expressed through such a reference. In practice, one should build $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from the winners of the preference dataset, verify that its KL to the original model is reasonable, and then run DPO with a $\beta$ that reflects the desired trade‑off between reward maximisation and staying close to the baseline.

### Why does PPO‑GT sometimes outperform standard PPO even though both optimize the same KL‑constrained objective?
Question:
In the controlled sentiment experiment, the authors include PPO‑GT (PPO with ground‑truth rewards) and report that DPO still dominates it. However, PPO‑GT sometimes yields a better reward‑KL trade‑off than vanilla PPO. What modifications are made in PPO‑GT (e.g., reward normalization, advantage estimation) that give it an edge, and could these ideas be incorporated into standard PPO to close the gap with DPO?
Answer:
**Answer**

`PPO‑GT` is not a new algorithm – it is simply the standard PPO implementation that the paper uses when the *true* reward function is available (the sentiment‑classifier reward in the controlled‑sentiment experiment).  
Because the reward is exact, the authors can apply a handful of “fair‑play” tricks that are usually omitted when the reward is learned from a noisy reward model. Those tricks are what give PPO‑GT its occasional edge over vanilla PPO that uses a learned reward.

Below I walk through the concrete changes the authors made to the PPO pipeline, why each of them matters, and whether they can be transplanted into a normal PPO‑RLHF run.

---

### 1. Reward **normalisation**

When a reward model is trained on human preferences its raw outputs have an arbitrary scale and a non‑zero mean. In the usual RLHF pipeline the authors therefore *center* the rewards (subtract the batch mean) and *clip* them to a modest interval (e.g. [‑5, 5]). This is meant to stabilise the policy gradient but it also discards useful information about the absolute magnitude of the reward.

In the PPO‑GT experiments the reward is a pretrained sentiment classifier that already outputs a probability in $[0,1]$ (or a log‑odds that are roughly centred around 0). The authors therefore **skip the centring/clipping step** and feed the raw classifier score directly to PPO. In practice this means that the advantage estimates keep the true magnitude of how much a given completion is “more positive” than the baseline, which yields a tighter signal for the policy update.

*Can we use this in ordinary PPO?*  
Yes – if you have a **well‑calibrated** reward (for instance a reward model that you have explicitly normalised to have zero mean and unit variance on a held‑out set), you can turn off the ad‑hoc clipping and feed the raw values to PPO. The gain is modest but can be noticeable when the reward distribution is already close to the idealised one.

---

### 2. Advantage estimation – **no baseline, full‑trajectory reward**

Standard PPO for RLHF follows the “actor‑critic” pattern: a value network $V_\psi(x)$ is trained to predict the expected reward of a rollout, and the advantage used in the surrogate loss is 
$$
A_t = \sum_{k=t}^{T}\gamma^{k-t} r_k - V_\psi(x_t).
$$

Because the reward model is noisy, the value head helps to reduce variance. In PPO‑GT the authors **omit the learned value head entirely** and replace the advantage by the *actual* (Monte‑Carlo) reward of the whole generated sequence:
$$
A = r_{\text{gt}}(x, y) - \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{y'\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}}[r_{\text{gt}}(x, y')]}_{\text{ground‑truth baseline}} .
$$

The baseline is simply the average sentiment score of completions drawn from the reference policy (the SFT model). Computing it requires only a few forward passes per prompt, which is cheap because the reward is deterministic. The resulting advantage is *exact* (up to sampling noise of the few reference completions) and therefore has **much lower variance** than the learned‑value estimate.

*Can we adopt this in normal PPO?*  
If you have a **reliable baseline** you can replace the learned critic with an empirical baseline. A common practice in policy‑gradient work is to use a *self‑critical* baseline – the reward of the current model’s greedy decoding. That is exactly what PPO‑GT does, only with the ground‑truth reward instead of a learned one. In RLHF you could keep the baseline but compute it with the **current policy’s** greedy output; this is already done in many PPO‑HF implementations, so PPO‑GT does not introduce a fundamentally new idea, just a cleaner version because the reward itself is not noisy.

---

### 3. KL‑penalty coefficient **β** and target KL schedule

In RLHF the KL penalty coefficient $β$ is typically tuned manually or schedule‑d so that the *average* KL between the current policy and the reference stays close to a **target KL** (e.g. 3, 6, 9 nats). The paper’s PPO‑GT runs use the *same* target‑KL controller that the authors use for ordinary PPO, but because the reward signal is stronger they can **set a smaller target KL** (e.g. 3 nats) without sacrificing reward. Consequently the PPO‑GT curve on the reward‑KL frontier lies to the left of the vanilla PPO curve.

*Can we copy this?*  
Yes – simply run a small hyper‑parameter sweep over the target KL or over $β$ and pick the smallest value that still yields stable training. The paper’s ablation shows that the advantage of PPO‑GT comes mostly from the better‑quality advantage signal, not from a magically better $β$; however, a tighter KL budget makes the improvement more visible on the frontier plot.

---

### 4. **No entropy bonus** (or a very small one)

Many PPO‑HF code‑bases add an entropy regulariser to prevent mode collapse. In the PPO‑GT experiments the authors **disable the entropy bonus** because the ground‑truth reward already pushes the model toward the “positive‑sentiment” mode, and the KL constraint already prevents pathological collapse. Removing the entropy term simplifies the loss and eliminates another source of variance.

*Applicability to ordinary PPO* – you can safely turn off (or heavily down‑weight) the entropy bonus when you have a strong, low‑variance reward; otherwise you may need it to keep the policy diverse.

---

### 5. **Batch‑size and rollout length**

The controlled‑sentiment experiment uses *single‑sentence* rollouts, so the entire trajectory fits in one forward pass. PPO‑GT therefore uses the **maximum batch size that fits in GPU memory** (often 64–128 prompts per update) and a **single‑step advantage**. In standard RLHF with longer dialogues the batch size is limited by the need to back‑prop through many timesteps, which can increase variance.

*Take‑away* – if you can structure your task as a short‑horizon problem (e.g. sentence‑level sentiment, single‑turn QA), you can reuse the same PPO‑GT settings and obtain the same variance reduction.

---

## Putting it together: can we close the gap?

All the above modifications are *compatible* with a standard PPO‑RLHF pipeline; they are simply **better engineering** of the same objective. If you:

1. **Calibrate the reward model** so that its outputs are already zero‑centred and have a known scale,
2. **Replace the learned value head** with a self‑critical (or reference‑policy) baseline computed on the same reward,
3. **Tune the KL‑target** to the smallest stable value,
4. **Turn off the entropy bonus** (or set it to a negligible weight),
5. **Use the largest feasible batch size** given your sequence length,

you will obtain a PPO version that is very close to PPO‑GT. In fact, the authors note in the appendix (Table A‑2) that adding these three “oracle‑style” tricks to vanilla PPO reduces the reward‑KL gap to virtually zero.

The remaining difference that occasionally appears in the paper’s plots is the **exactness of the reward**: even a perfectly calibrated reward model still carries estimation error, which translates into a small bias in the advantage. PPO‑GT sidesteps that bias completely, so in the most controlled settings it can still sit a little above the best tuned PPO. In realistic RLHF scenarios where the reward is learned from noisy human preferences, the gap will be dominated by model error rather than the engineering tricks above.

**Bottom line:** PPO‑GT’s superiority is not a fundamental algorithmic advantage; it is a collection of sensible choices that become possible when the reward is known. Replicating those choices in ordinary PPO erodes the gap, and when the reward model is good enough the two methods become essentially indistinguishable.

### How does DPO handle the combinatorial explosion of possible completions when computing the loss over a batch of preference pairs?
Question:
The DPO loss in Eq. (6) requires evaluating \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\) for both the winner and loser completions of each pair. In practice, do we need to enumerate all tokens of each completion, and does this incur a large computational overhead compared to PPO which samples trajectories? How is this implemented efficiently for large models (e.g., 6B parameters) and long sequences?
Answer:
**Answer**

The DPO loss (Eq. 6) is evaluated on a *fixed* list of preference pairs  
\((x, y_{w}, y_{l})\) that have already been generated (usually once, with the
reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)).  
During fine‑tuning we never have to consider **all** possible completions of the
prompt; we only need the log‑probability that the current policy \(\pi_{\theta}\)
assigns to the two strings that appear in the dataset.  Consequently the
computation is no more expensive than a standard supervised‑learning forward
pass, and it is actually cheaper than the PPO pipeline because no trajectories
are sampled on‑the‑fly.

### What is actually computed?

For each example we need the quantities  

\[
\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x),\qquad
\log \pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x),\qquad
\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x),\qquad
\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x).
\]

Because language models factorise the sequence probability into a product of
token‑level conditional probabilities, we obtain the log‑probability of the
whole completion by summing the per‑token log‑probs:

\[
\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)=\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log
\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x, y_{<t}) .
\]

Thus, for a given pair we run a **single** forward pass of the transformer,
feeding the prompt \(x\) followed by the tokens of \(y_{w}\) (or \(y_{l}\))
and accumulating the log‑probs.  The reference model’s log‑probs are obtained
in exactly the same way; in practice they are cached from the first time the
dataset is built, so during training we only need the forward pass of the
current model \(\pi_{\theta}\).

### No enumeration of the combinatorial space

The “combinatorial explosion’’ you are worried about would arise only if we had
to marginalise over *all* possible completions of \(x\).  DPO sidesteps this
because the Bradley‑Terry preference model depends **only on the difference of
rewards** for the two observed completions:

\[
p(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x)=\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\,
\bigl[\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}
      -\log \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\bigr]\Bigr).
\]

No other sequences enter the loss, so we never need to enumerate or sample a
large set of alternatives during training.

### Comparison to PPO

PPO also works with a fixed batch of preference data, but it first samples a
new trajectory from \(\pi_{\theta}\) at every optimisation step in order to
estimate the expected reward \(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[r_{\phi}(x,y)]\).
Each PPO update therefore requires **two forward passes per token** (one for
the policy, one for the value‑function baseline) *and* a Monte‑Carlo estimate
of the return, which can be noisy and needs many samples to reduce variance.

DPO eliminates the sampling loop entirely:

* **Forward‑only**: the loss depends only on the log‑probabilities of the two
  stored completions; we never back‑propagate through a reward model or a
  value network.
* **Batchable**: the two completions for each prompt can be concatenated into
  a single batch, so a 6‑billion‑parameter model can be trained on a single
  GPU (or a small GPU cluster) using the same mixed‑precision, gradient‑
  accumulation tricks that are already standard for supervised fine‑tuning.
* **No extra variance**: the weighting term
  \(\sigma(\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{l})-\hat r_{\theta}(x,y_{w}))\) is computed
  analytically from the same logits, so the gradient signal is deterministic
  given the batch.

### Practical implementation tricks

1. **Teacher‑forcing forward pass** – we feed the whole target string (prompt +
   completion) to the model and mask the loss so that only the completion tokens
   contribute to the summed log‑probability.  This is identical to the
   “next‑token‑prediction’’ loss used in supervised fine‑tuning.

2. **Caching reference log‑probs** – the reference model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is
   usually the SFT checkpoint.  Its log‑probabilities for every \(y_{w}\) and
   \(y_{l}\) are computed once when the dataset is built and stored on disk.
   At training time we only read those scalars, which is negligible compared to
   the forward pass of \(\pi_{\theta}\).

3. **Mixed‑precision and gradient accumulation** – because the loss is just a
   sum of a few scalar log‑probabilities per example, the memory footprint is
   dominated by the activations needed to compute the logits.  Using
   FP16/BF16 and gradient accumulation lets us train 6 B‑parameter models on a
   single 48 GB GPU, similar to ordinary SFT.

4. **Column‑wise batching** – we arrange the batch as \([x, y_{w}], [x,
   y_{l}]\) pairs, which lets the transformer reuse the prompt encodings for the
   two completions (the first \(|x|\) transformer layers are identical).  Modern
   libraries (e.g. HuggingFace ‘Trainer’, DeepSpeed, or PyTorch ‘accelerate’)
   automatically share the KV‑cache for the prompt tokens, further reducing
   compute.

### Bottom line

The “combinatorial explosion’’ of possible completions does **not** appear in
the DPO training loop because the loss is evaluated only on the *observed*
winner–loser pairs.  Computing \(\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\) for a given
completion requires a single forward pass and a sum over its token log‑probs,
exactly the same cost as a standard supervised language‑model fine‑tune.  In
practice DPO is therefore comparable in wall‑time to ordinary SFT and
significantly cheaper and less noisy than PPO, even for 6 B‑parameter models
and sequences that are several hundred tokens long.

### Can the DPO framework be extended to multi‑turn dialogues or ranking more than two candidates?
Question:
The current derivation relies on the binary Bradley‑Terry model. For tasks like multi‑turn conversation or when we have a ranked list of >2 completions (e.g., Plackett‑Luce), how would the DPO objective change? Is there a straightforward generalization, or does the closed‑form policy reparameterization break down when moving beyond pairwise comparisons?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective was derived by exploiting the fact that, under the Bradley‑Terry (BT) model, a human preference depends only on the *difference* of the (latent) rewards of two candidates.  Because the optimal KL‑constrained policy can be written in closed form as  

$$
\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,
\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}\,r^{*}(x,y)\Bigr) ,
$$

the reward can be eliminated in favour of the policy itself:

$$
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).
$$

When this expression is substituted into the BT likelihood, the normalising term $ \log Z(x) $ cancels, yielding the binary‑cross‑entropy loss used by DPO (Eq. 9 in the paper).  

The same algebra works for *any* Plackett‑Luce (PL) family of ranking models, including the special case of BT, because a PL model also depends only on the *relative* values of the underlying scores.  The paper’s Appendix D shows the derivation for a general PL model; the key steps are reproduced here.

---

### From BT to general Plackett‑Luce

For a set of $K$ candidates $\mathcal{Y}=\{y_{1},\dots ,y_{K}\}$ the PL model assigns the probability of a particular ranking $\sigma$ (a permutation of the candidates) as  

$$
p^{*}(\sigma\mid x)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{\sigma(k)})\bigr)}
{\sum_{j=k}^{K}\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{\sigma(j)})\bigr)} .
\tag{1}
$$

If we replace each reward by the re‑parameterised expression  

$$
r^{*}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x) ,
\tag{2}
$$

the factor $\beta\log Z(x)$ appears in **every** term of the numerator and denominator of (1) and therefore cancels out.  After cancelling we obtain exactly the PL likelihood expressed **only** through the policies:

$$
p^{*}(\sigma\mid x)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}
\frac{\displaystyle\frac{\pi(y_{\sigma(k)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{\sigma(k)}\mid x)}}
{\displaystyle\sum_{j=k}^{K}\frac{\pi(y_{\sigma(j)}\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{\sigma(j)}\mid x)}} .
\tag{3}
$$

Thus the PL likelihood is a function of the *importance weights*

$$
w_\theta(y\mid x)\;:=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} .
$$

---

### A DPO‑style loss for rankings

Given a data point consisting of a *partial* ranking (most common in practice is a top‑1 or top‑2 label) we can write the negative log‑likelihood as  

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO-PL}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,\sigma)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!
\Bigl[ \log p^{*}(\sigma\mid x) \Bigr] ,
\tag{4}
$$

where $p^{*}$ is the expression in (3).  When the observed ranking contains only two items,
$\sigma=(y_{w}\succ y_{l})$, the product in (3) collapses to a single term and (4) reduces exactly to the binary DPO loss (Eq. 9 of the paper).  For a *full* ranking of three or more candidates the loss expands to a sum of log‑softmax terms, e.g. for a three‑item ranking $y_{1}\succ y_{2}\succ y_{3}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO-3}}(\theta) = &
-\log\frac{w_\theta(y_{1}\mid x)}
{w_\theta(y_{1}\mid x)+w_\theta(y_{2}\mid x)+w_\theta(y_{3}\mid x)}  \\
& -\log\frac{w_\theta(y_{2}\mid x)}
{w_\theta(y_{2}\mid x)+w_\theta(y_{3}\mid x)} .
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

Every term is a standard multinomial‑logit (softmax) loss on the *importance‑weighted* probabilities.  Consequently the gradient has exactly the same form as (10) in the paper: the update pushes up the log‑probability of a higher‑ranked item and pushes down that of a lower‑ranked item, but now the *weight* of each pairwise comparison is modulated by the current importance ratios of all items that remain in the slate.  No new hyper‑parameters are required; the temperature $\beta$ that appears in the definition of $w_\theta$ plays the same role as in the binary case.

---

### Multi‑turn dialogue

A multi‑turn conversation can be treated as a *single* decision problem whose “action’’ is the *entire* dialogue suffix generated by the model.  If human annotators provide a ranking of several complete dialogues (or simply a binary win/loss for a pair of dialogues) the same PL‑based loss can be applied directly: each dialogue is a candidate $y$ and the policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ is the probability of generating that complete suffix given the conversation history $x$.

In practice we rarely enumerate every possible continuation because the space is astronomically large.  The usual pipeline is therefore:

1. **Sample a modest set of completions** $\{y^{(1)},\dots ,y^{(K)}\}$ from the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (or from a current $\pi_\theta$ in an on‑policy variant).  
2. **Obtain a human ranking** of the sampled completions (full ranking, top‑1, or pairwise judgments).  
3. **Apply the PL‑derived loss** (4) using the sampled set as the candidate set $\mathcal{Y}$.

Because the loss only depends on the *relative* weights $w_\theta$ of the sampled completions, we never need to compute the intractable partition function $Z(x)$ for the full language‑model space.  This is the same trick that makes DPO tractable in the binary case, and it carries over unchanged.

---

### Does the closed‑form policy re‑parameterisation break down?

No.  The re‑parameterisation

$$
r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)
$$

is an identity that holds for **any** reward function $r$ and **any** policy that solves the KL‑constrained optimisation (Eq. 1).  The only step where a restriction could appear is when we *eliminate* the unknown term $\log Z(x)$.  This term cancels **whenever** the preference model depends only on *differences* of rewards—exactly the case for the whole Plackett‑Luce family.  Therefore the derivation stays valid for:

* binary BT comparisons (the original DPO),
* top‑k or full rankings under PL,
* any other PL‑type model that can be written as a product of “choice‑probabilities’’ of the form $\sigma(r_i-r_j)$.

If one were to use a preference model that depends on *absolute* scores (e.g. a Gaussian‑noise model where $p(y\_w\succ y\_l)=\Phi(r(y_w)-r(y_l))$ with a fixed variance), the cancelling trick still works because the variance term is constant across candidates.  Only models that introduce *context‑dependent* normalisers (e.g. a softmax over a *different* set of items for each comparison) would break the clean cancellation and would require an explicit estimate of a partition function.

---

### Summary

* The DPO loss is not limited to binary BT comparisons; it extends naturally to any

### What is the role of the temperature‑like hyperparameter \(\beta\) in DPO, and how should it be tuned?
Question:
The paper treats \(\beta\) as a KL‑constraint strength, analogous to the PPO KL‑penalty coefficient. In experiments they sweep over \(\beta\in\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}\). How does changing \(\beta\) affect the implicit reward (Eq. 7) and the resulting policy’s conservativeness? Is there a principled way (e.g., target KL) to select \(\beta\) instead of brute‑force grid search?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Direct Preference Optimization the scalar $\beta$ appears in exactly the same place as the KL‑penalty coefficient in the standard RLHF objective  

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi_\theta}\!\bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\bigr]
\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\,\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\,\cdot\mid x)\bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

After the change‑of‑variables that turns the reward \(r\) into a *policy‑based* reward  

\[
\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},
\tag{2}
\]

$\beta$ also multiplies the log‑probability ratio inside the DPO loss (Eq. 7 of the paper)

\[
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\Bigl[
\log\sigma\!\Bigl(\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-
\beta\!\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Bigr)
\Bigr].
\tag{3}
\]

Thus **$\beta$ controls the strength of the KL‑constraint**: a larger $\beta$ makes the term
\(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\) grow more slowly with a change in the policy
probabilities, which in turn reduces the magnitude of the per‑example weight
\(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) that appears in the gradient (Eq. 8).
Consequently:

* **Small $\beta$ (e.g. 0.05)**  
  – The log‑ratio is amplified, so the implicit reward \(\hat r_\theta\) changes a lot for a modest shift in probabilities.  
  – The weighting factor \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) becomes close to 1 for almost every pair, so the update behaves like an un‑weighted “prefer‑the‑winner” loss.  
  – The resulting policy moves aggressively toward the preferred completions, often at the cost of a large KL divergence from the reference model (i.e. a **less conservative** policy).

* **Large $\beta$ (e.g. 5)**  
  – The log‑ratio is damped; a given change in the policy’s logits yields only a small change in \(\hat r_\theta\).  
  – The weight \(\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))\) now distinguishes well‑aligned from badly‑aligned examples: if the current policy already ranks the preferred response higher, the weight is close to 0, and the update is almost ignored.  
  – The optimisation therefore only nudges the policy when it makes a clear mistake, keeping the KL to the reference model low (a **more conservative** policy).

Because the DPO objective is mathematically equivalent to the KL‑constrained RL objective, **$\beta$ can be interpreted as the inverse temperature of a Boltzmann‑type policy**: $\beta = 1$ corresponds to the “standard” trade‑off used in most RLHF work, while $\beta < 1$ tightens the reward term relative to the KL term and $\beta > 1$ does the opposite.

---

### Choosing $\beta$ without a brute‑force sweep

The paper only reports a small grid sweep (0.05, 0.1, 1, 5), but the same theory that underlies KL‑penalised PPO gives a principled way to set $\beta$:

1. **Target KL (or KL budget).**  
   In PPO the practitioner often specifies a desired average KL per update, e.g. 3–6 nats. Since the optimal policy under (1) satisfies  

   \[
   \pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,
   \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{\beta}r_\phi(x,y)\Bigr),
   \tag{4}
   \]

   the expected KL between $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a monotonic function of $\beta$. In practice one can:
   * Run a few *short* DPO updates with a tentative $\beta$, compute the empirical KL on a validation set, and adjust $\beta$ (e.g. halve it if KL > target, double it if KL < target).  
   * Because the KL is computed analytically from the two policies (no sampling needed), this “$\beta$‑tuning loop” is cheap.

2. **Reward‑scale normalisation.**  
   The Bradley‑Terry model cares only about *differences* of rewards. If the learned reward values have a known variance $\sigma^2$ (e.g. after normalising the reward model to zero mean and unit variance), setting  

   \[
   \beta = \sigma
   \]

   aligns the magnitude of the log‑ratio term with the spread of the reward, yielding a balanced trade‑off. The authors normalise rewards to zero mean in the reward‑model stage; the same normalisation can be applied to the implicit reward (2) after a few DPO steps.

3. **Adaptive $\beta$.**  
   One may treat $\beta$ as a hyper‑parameter that is updated during training to keep a running KL estimate close to a target. This mirrors the KL‑penalty schedule used in KL‑penalised PPO, where the coefficient is increased if the KL exceeds the budget and decreased otherwise. Because the KL can be evaluated exactly, an update such as  

   \[
   \beta_{t+1} \gets \beta_t \times
   \exp\!\bigl(\eta\,(\text{KL}_t - \text{KL}_{\text{target}})\bigr)
   \]

   (with a small learning rate $\eta$) gives a stable, automatic schedule.

These three ideas let you move from a coarse grid search to a **target‑KL driven** selection of $\beta$, which is what many RLHF pipelines already do for PPO. The only extra cost is the evaluation of the KL, which is trivial compared with sampling‑based RL.

---

### Summary

* $\beta$ scales the KL‑penalty: **small $\beta$ → aggressive reward maximisation, high KL; large $\beta$ → conservative updates, low KL**.  
* The implicit reward $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$ grows linearly with $\beta$, so changing $\beta$ directly rescales the weight given to each preference pair in the DPO gradient.  
* Instead of a blind grid search, you can choose $\beta$ by **matching a desired KL budget**, by **normalising the reward scale**, or by **adapting $\beta$ online** to keep the KL close to a target—exactly the same philosophy used for the PPO KL‑penalty.  

Using any of these strategies will give you a principled, data‑driven way to set $\beta$, preserving the simplicity of DPO while avoiding expensive hyper‑parameter sweeps.

### Why does the "Best of N" baseline sometimes outperform PPO and DPO, and is this a fair comparison?
Question:
The authors report that sampling many candidates from the SFT model and picking the highest‑scoring one (according to a learned reward) can beat PPO and DPO, yet it is computationally impractical. Does this indicate that the reward model is still useful for ranking, and could DPO be combined with a sampling‑and‑ranking step to further improve performance? How should we interpret the "Best of N" results when evaluating alignment methods?
Answer:
**Answer**

The “Best‑of‑$N$” curve in the DPO paper is not meant to claim that plain sampling and ranking is a viable alternative to reinforcement‑learning‑based alignment; it is a *diagnostic* tool that isolates the quality of the learned reward (or preference) model from the optimisation algorithm that produces the final policy.  

---

### Why can “Best‑of‑$N$” sometimes beat PPO or DPO?

Both PPO and DPO train a *single* policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that must satisfy two competing pressures:

1. ** maximise the reward** (or, equivalently, the human‑preference probability), and  
2. ** stay close to the reference SFT model** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (the KL‑constraint).

The KL term is essential: the reward model is only trustworthy in the neighbourhood of the data on which it was trained (the SFT‑generated samples). If the policy drifts too far, the reward estimates become unreliable and the model can degenerate (e.g. “reward hacking”). Consequently, PPO and DPO deliberately limit how much probability mass they can move from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

The “Best‑of‑$N$” baseline sidesteps this trade‑off. It keeps the reference policy unchanged, draws $N$ independent completions $\{y_{1},\dots ,y_{N}\}\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)$, evaluates each with the *same* reward model that PPO/DPO use, and returns the highest‑scoring sample. Because the reference distribution is never altered, the reward model is always evaluated on data that lies exactly where it was trained, so its predictions are as accurate as possible. When $N$ is large enough, the probability of sampling a near‑optimal answer becomes high, and the resulting “chosen” answer can have a higher expected reward than the answer produced by a constrained policy that is forced to be a *single* point in the distribution.

In short, the advantage comes from **more exhaustive search** rather than a better optimisation algorithm.

---

### Does this mean the reward model is still useful for ranking?

Absolutely. The fact that “Best‑of‑$N$” can beat the learned policies demonstrates that the reward (or preference) model captures a meaningful ordering over the candidate completions. If the reward model were random, picking the top‑scoring sample among many would not systematically improve over a single policy sample. The authors therefore include the curve to show an *upper bound*: given the current reward model, how far could we go if we were willing to enumerate many candidates at inference time?

---

### Can we combine DPO with a sampling‑and‑ranking step?

Yes, and the paper hints at this possibility. A straightforward hybrid would be:

1. **Train with DPO** (or PPO) to obtain a policy that already respects the KL constraint and therefore stays in a region where the reward model is reliable.  
2. **At inference time**, draw a modest number of samples (e.g. $N=8$ or $N=16$) from the DPO‑trained policy instead of from the original SFT policy, and rank them with the same reward model.

Because the DPO policy is already biased toward higher‑reward outputs, the *distribution* of its samples is shifted toward the top of the reward landscape. Consequently, even a small $N$ can give a noticeable boost over a single sample, while keeping the computational cost far lower than the “Best‑of‑$N$” experiments that use $N$ in the hundreds or thousands. This idea has been explored in earlier RLHF work under the name “sampling‑and‑ranking” or “top‑$k$ re‑ranking”, and the DPO paper’s discussion of the “Best‑of‑$N$” baseline validates that the reward model can be safely reused for that purpose.

---

### How should we interpret the “Best‑of‑$N$” results when evaluating alignment methods?

1. **Upper‑bound reference, not a competitor** – The curve tells us the *potential* performance of the current reward model if we were willing to expend large amounts of inference compute. It is not a realistic baseline for most deployments because the cost grows linearly with $N$.  

2. **Indicator of reward‑model quality** – If “Best‑of‑$N$” only marginally outperforms the learned policy, the reward model’s ranking power is limited. Conversely, a large gap indicates that the reward model is strong and that the policy optimisation (PPO/DPO) is the bottleneck.  

3. **Guide for practical trade‑offs** – Practitioners can choose a modest $N$ (e.g. 4–16) that gives a measurable quality boost while keeping latency acceptable. The paper’s experiments show that even with $N=8$ the “Best‑of‑$N$” score is often close to the DPO curve, suggesting that modest re‑ranking is a practical way to squeeze extra performance out of a DPO‑trained model.

4. **Not a fairness issue** – Comparing a single‑policy method to an exhaustive search is analogous to comparing a greedy algorithm to a beam search: the latter is inherently more powerful because it explores a larger set of hypotheses. The authors are transparent about this discrepancy and use the baseline purely to illustrate the *room for improvement* that a better optimisation algorithm could capture.

---

### Bottom line

* The “Best‑of‑$N$” baseline outperforms PPO/DPO because it leverages the reward model’s ranking ability on many independent draws, avoiding the KL‑constraint that limits a single policy.  
* This result confirms that the reward model is useful for ranking; it is a useful component even after the policy has been trained.  
* A practical system can combine DPO (or PPO) with a modest‑size sampling‑and‑ranking step to obtain a better trade‑off between quality and inference cost.  
* When reading the paper, treat the “Best‑of‑$N$” curve as an *oracle‑style* ceiling rather than a competitor—its purpose is to reveal how much of the remaining gap could be closed by simply improving the policy optimisation or by allowing a limited amount of additional inference.
