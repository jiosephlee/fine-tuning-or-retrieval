\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning with Large Language Models  

Mathematical reasoning occupies a unique position among natural‑language tasks: it demands strict logical consistency, precise symbolic manipulation, and often a multi‑step chain of deductions that must be faithful to the underlying mathematics.  When a large language model (LLM) attempts to solve a problem such as “prove that the sum of the interior angles of a triangle is \(180^\circ\)” or “compute the integral \(\int_0^1 x^2 \,dx\)”, the model must not only generate fluent text, but also sustain a correct sequence of algebraic or geometric transformations.  This combination of linguistic fluency and formal structure makes mathematical reasoning especially challenging for neural networks that were originally designed to predict the next word in a probabilistic language distribution.

In the past few years, the research community has witnessed dramatic progress in this direction.  The advent of scaling laws for LLMs demonstrated that larger models trained on more data tend to exhibit stronger quantitative reasoning capabilities.  Benchmarks such as **MATH** (a competition‑level dataset containing problems from high‑school and early‑college mathematics), **GSM8K** (grade‐school math problems with step‑by‑step solutions), and various geometry‑focused suites have become standard tools for measuring how well an LLM can reason mathematically.  State‑of‑the‑art closed‑source systems—most notably **GPT‑4** and **Gemini‑Ultra**—have achieved scores on MATH that approach or exceed human performance, setting a high bar for any open‑source effort.

Despite these successes, the majority of publicly available models still lag far behind their proprietary counterparts.  Generalist models such as **Mistral‑7B**, **Llama‑2**, and even early instruction‑tuned variants often struggle to maintain logical coherence beyond a few reasoning steps.  Specialized math‑oriented models (e.g., Minerva 540B) demonstrate that sheer parameter count can compensate for data quality, yet their massive size renders them inaccessible to most researchers.  Consequently, there remains a substantial gap: the open‑source community needs models that combine modest parameter budgets with targeted data and training strategies capable of closing the performance gap on the most demanding mathematical benchmarks.

### The Nature of Mathematical Reasoning for AI  

Mathematical problem solving for an LLM can be decomposed into three intertwined components:

1. **Symbolic Understanding** – The model must recognize symbols (variables, operators, Greek letters) and their semantic roles.  This includes parsing LaTeX fragments or plain‑text equations and mapping them onto internal representations that preserve hierarchical structure.

2. **Procedural Reasoning** – Mathematics often proceeds via a deterministic algorithm: apply distributivity, perform substitution, invoke a known theorem, or execute a numerical integration routine.  A competent LLM must emulate these procedures in its token generation process, effectively “thinking out loud” through a chain of thought (CoT) that mirrors how humans write proofs or derivations.

3. **Verification and Consistency** – Errors are easy to propagate; once an incorrect intermediate result appears, downstream steps become invalid.  Robust models therefore need mechanisms—either implicit learned consistency or explicit tool use (e.g., calling Python’s `sympy`)—to validate intermediate calculations and repair mistakes.

The paper “DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models” frames these challenges concretely: it treats mathematical reasoning as a structured task that can be improved both by providing high‑quality domain data during pre‑training and by refining the model’s policy through reinforcement learning (RL).  The authors argue that merely scaling up parameters is not sufficient; rather, *targeted* exposure to mathematical texts plus carefully designed RL objectives yields disproportionate gains.

### Benchmark Landscape: MATH, GSM8K, Geometry Tasks  

To quantify progress, several benchmark suites are used throughout the DeepSeekMath study:

- **MATH** – Consists of competition‑level problems across algebra, geometry, number theory, calculus and combinatorics.  Each item requires a self‑contained solution without external tools; performance is reported as an accuracy percentage over a held‑out test set.

- **GSM8K** – A grade‑school mathematics dataset where each problem is paired with a detailed chain of thought solution.  Because most modern models already possess strong basic arithmetic abilities, GSM8K serves as an indicator of *stepwise reasoning* quality rather than raw numerical skill.

- **Geometry Benchmarks** – Though not enumerated exhaustively in the paper, geometry tasks appear in both English (e.g., OCW Courses) and Chinese (e.g., Gaokao-MathCloze).  These tasks test spatial reasoning, diagram interpretation (implicitly via textual descriptions), and the ability to invoke geometric axioms.

Evaluation methodology is uniform: models receive a prompt describing the problem and are expected to output a *self-contained* textual solution (no external references).  When tool use is permitted—typically by allowing Python code generation—the answer is derived by executing the generated program; correctness is then judged against the ground truth.

Across these suites, DeepSeekMath’s various stages achieve notable milestones:

| Model | GSM8K | MATH | Geometry (selected) |
|------|-------|------|----------------------|
| DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B | 64.2 % | 36.2 % | – |
| DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B | >50 % on MATH without tools | — | Competitive |
| DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B (GRPO) | **88.2 %** | **51.7 %** | Out‑of‑domain gains |

The jump from base to instruction tuning already surpasses many larger open‐source baselines; reinforcement learning via Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) adds another ~5 % absolute improvement on MATH—a benchmark once dominated solely by closed models.

### Limitations of Closed‑Source Models vs. Open‑Source Baselines  

Closed‐source systems such as GPT‑4 and Gemini Ultra have several advantages that are difficult to replicate directly:

- **Massive Scale** – GPT‑4’s parameter count exceeds hundreds of billions; Gemini Ultra employs sophisticated mixture‐of‐experts architectures that dynamically allocate compute per token.
- **Proprietary Data Pipelines** – These companies curate extensive web corpora beyond publicly available Common Crawl snapshots, including high‐quality textbooks, licensed academic repositories, and multi‐modal data.
- **Extensive Human Feedback Loops** – Reinforcement learning from human preference (RLHF) pipelines are run at massive scale with large annotation teams.

Open‐source baselines typically operate under tighter resource constraints: model sizes cap at ≤70 B parameters; training data is limited to publicly released web dumps; human feedback budgets are modest.  Consequently, many open baselines—Mistral 7B, Llama 2 7B/13B after generic instruction tuning—achieve GSM8K scores in the low‐40s% range and struggle to surpass even 30 % on MATH.

The DeepSeekMath project deliberately tackles these gaps by:

1. **Data Engineering** – Constructing a *120 billion token* math corpus (the DeepSeekMath Corpus) drawn exclusively from Common Crawl pages classified as math‐rich via an iterative fastText classifier.
2. **Domain Initialization** – Starting from DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1.5 7B rather than a generic LLM; code pretraining provides structural priors useful for algorithmic reasoning.
3. **Instruction Tuning with CoT/POT/Tool Formats** – Extending typical instruction datasets with chain‐of‐thought and program‐of‐thought exemplars in both English and Chinese.
4. **Efficient RL via GRPO** – Replacing standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)’s heavyweight critic network with a group–relative baseline derived from multiple sampled outputs; this reduces memory consumption while still exploiting comparative reward signals.

By concentrating resources on high‐quality math data and an efficient RL recipe, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that *parameter efficiency* can be dramatically improved: a 7 B model reaches an MATH accuracy of 51.7 %, edging within five points of Gemini Ultra’s reported performance despite being more than ten times smaller.

### Overview of DeepSeekMath Objectives and Contributions  

The paper delineates two primary research thrusts:

#### 1. Scalable Math Pre‑Training  

- **Data Selection Pipeline** – An iterative process that begins with OpenWebMath as seed data, trains a fastText classifier to recall similar pages from deduplicated Common Crawl (≈40 B HTML pages), then refines positive examples through manual annotation of domains exhibiting ≥10 % math page density.
- **Corpus Size & Multilinguality** – After four iterations the pipeline yields *35.5 million* web pages totaling *120 billion tokens*, roughly seven times larger than Minerva’s math web collection and nine times larger than OpenWebMath alone.  The corpus contains both English and Chinese pages; experiments show this multilinguality improves Chinese benchmark scores.
- **Empirical Validation** – Training a modest 1.3 B model on this corpus outperforms identical models trained on MathPile (8.9 B tokens), OpenWebMath (13.6 B tokens), or Proof-Pile-2 (≈40 B tokens) across eight downstream math benchmarks.
- **Insights on Data Types** – Code pretraining prior to math training markedly boosts both toolless reasoning and program–aided solving; conversely, adding arXiv papers contributes little or even harms performance on evaluated tasks.

#### 2. Exploration and Analysis of Reinforcement Learning  

- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – A PPO variant where the baseline is computed from *group averages* of rewards among multiple sampled completions for the same question; no separate critic network is needed.
    - Advantages: lower memory footprint; aligns naturally with pairwise comparison reward models trained on relative judgments.
    - Implementation details include KL regularization performed directly on policy versus reference distributions rather than via per-token penalties.
- **Outcome vs. Process Supervision** – GRPO can be used with outcome supervision (reward only at final answer) or process supervision (reward after each reasoning step). Experiments reveal process supervision yields superior gains.
- **Iterative RL Loop** – After each RL phase the reward model is refreshed using replayed historical data plus newly sampled outputs; this iterative refinement further pushes performance.
- **Unified Paradigm** – The authors place SFT, Rejection Sampling Fine‑tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), PPO, and GRPO within a common gradient formulation
    \[
        \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J} = \mathbb{E}_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\left[\, GC(q,o,t,\pi_{\text{ref}})\,\nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(o_t|q,o_{<t})\right],
    \]
    emphasizing how each method differs in data source (\(\mathcal{D}\)), reward function (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)), and gradient coefficient (\(GC\)). This analysis uncovers why online sampling methods (e.g., GRPO) outperform offline counterparts like RFT: they obtain more informative gradients from evolving policy distributions.

Overall, DeepSeekMath establishes three concrete contributions:

1. **A public large–scale math pretraining corpus**, demonstrating that publicly available web data still hold untapped mathematical knowledge when filtered rigorously.
2. **A new RL algorithm (GRPO)** that achieves state-of-the-art improvements on mathematical reasoning while being computationally lighter than conventional PPO.
3. **A unified analytical framework**, clarifying relationships among recent fine-tuning and RL techniques for LLMs and pointing toward future research directions such as better reward generalization, robust gradient estimators under noisy preferences, and more sophisticated exploration strategies.

By presenting each component—data pipeline design, model initialization choices, instruction tuning formats, reinforcement learning methodology—in detail and grounding them firmly in empirical results from the paper, this chapter equips readers with both intuitive understanding and technical depth needed to engage with contemporary research on mathematical reasoning in language models.

**Building a Large‑Scale Math Corpus from the Web**

---

### Seed Corpus Selection (OpenWebMath) and fastText Classifier Basics  

The first step in constructing a high‑quality mathematical pre‑training dataset is to obtain a reliable seed of math‑rich documents.  The authors of *DeepSeekMath* chose **OpenWebMath** as this seed because it is a curated collection of web pages that already exhibit strong mathematical content.  Using this seed, they trained a lightweight text‑classification model based on **fastText** \citep{joulin2016fasttext}.  

FastText is particularly suited for this task for three reasons.  First, it represents each document as an average of word‑level n‑gram embeddings, allowing rapid inference over billions of pages.  Second, its training procedure is extremely efficient: the authors sampled **500 k** positive examples (randomly drawn from OpenWebMath) and an equal number of negative examples (randomly drawn from the broader Common Crawl).  The model was trained with a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of 0.1, 3‑gram word features, a minimum word count of three, and three epochs of stochastic gradient descent.  After training, the classifier outputs a real‑valued score indicating how “math‑like’’ a page is; higher scores correspond to stronger mathematical relevance.

With this classifier in hand, the authors performed an initial recall over a deduplicated subset of Common Crawl amounting to **40 billion** HTML pages.  By ranking pages according to their fastText scores and keeping only the top‑ranked pages, they obtained a first batch of candidate mathematical documents.  The size of this batch was later calibrated by pre‑training experiments that measured downstream performance when using the top 40 B, 80 B, 120 B and 160 B tokens; for the initial iteration they settled on the top **40 B tokens**, which already yielded noticeable gains on downstream mathematical benchmarks.

---

### Iterative Retrieval, Ranking, and Domain‑Level Expansion  

A single pass of classification is insufficient because the initial positive set (OpenWebMath) does not encompass the full diversity of web‑hosted mathematics.  After the first recall many mathematically relevant pages remained undiscovered.  To address this, the authors introduced an **iterative expansion loop** that gradually enriches the seed and refines the classifier.

1. **Domain grouping** – The entire Common Crawl corpus was partitioned into disjoint domains defined by their base URL (e.g., all pages under `mathoverflow.net`).  
2. **Domain‑level math prevalence** – For each domain the fraction of pages already captured in the previous iteration was computed.  Domains where **more than 10 %** of their pages were retrieved were deemed “math‑related.”  
3. **Manual URL annotation** – Human annotators inspected these math‑related domains and explicitly marked sub‑paths that host mathematical content (for instance `mathoverflow.net/questions`).  
4. **Seed augmentation** – Pages reachable through these newly annotated URLs but not yet collected were added to the positive training set.  

The augmented seed now contains a broader variety of styles—ranging from question–answer forums to lecture notes—so a new fastText model can be trained with improved recall.  This process was repeated **four times**, each time expanding the pool of math pages and retraining the classifier on a richer positive set.

After the fourth iteration, the authors had collected **35.5 million** web pages amounting to roughly **120 billion tokens** of mathematical text.  They observed that the marginal gain between the third and fourth iterations fell below 2 %, indicating that further iterations would be unlikely to bring substantial new material; consequently they stopped data collection at this point.

---

### Deduplication, Token Budgeting, and Stopping Criteria  

Handling billions of web pages requires aggressive deduplication to avoid wasteful training on redundant content.  The authors applied two complementary techniques:

* **URL‑based deduplication** – Pages sharing identical URLs or exact URL prefixes were collapsed, eliminating exact copies produced by different crawl snapshots.
* **Near‑deduplication** – Using similarity thresholds on tokenized text (e.g., shingling or MinHash), near‑identical pages differing only by small boilerplate changes were also merged.

After deduplication the corpus shrank to **40 B HTML pages**, which served as the pool for guided retrieval.  

The overall **token budget**—the amount of text ultimately fed into pre‑training—was managed by progressively increasing the token ceiling in successive experiments (40 B → 80 B → 120 B → 160 B).  The authors monitored downstream performance on a suite of mathematical benchmarks after each pre‑training run; performance improvements plateaued once roughly **120 B tokens** had been consumed, providing an empirical stopping signal for corpus growth.

---

### Decontamination: Preventing Benchmark Leakage  

A critical concern when building a pre‑training corpus from openly available web data is inadvertent inclusion of benchmark test items, which would artificially inflate evaluation scores.  The authors therefore instituted a rigorous **decontamination pipeline** modeled after earlier work on code corpora \citep{deepseek-coder}.

* They compiled all evaluation benchmark texts across English (GSM8K, MATH) and Chinese (CMATH, AGIEval) sources.
* For any web page in their candidate pool they scanned for exact n‑gram overlaps with benchmark texts:
  * If a page contained any **10‑gram** that matched verbatim any substring from any benchmark, that page was discarded.
  * For shorter benchmark excerpts (3–9 grams), an exact match also triggered removal.
* This rule ensured that even partial leakage—such as an entire problem statement appearing in source material—could not survive into training data.

Through this thorough filtering step, the final DeepSeekMath Corpus is guaranteed not to contain test items from any of the benchmarks used later for evaluation.

### Comparative Analysis with MathPile, Proof‑Pile‑2, and OpenWebMath  

To assess whether their newly built corpus offers advantages over existing public math corpora, the authors conducted controlled pre‑training experiments using a **1.3 B parameter language model** (DeepSeek‑LLM 1.3B).  Each model was trained for an equal number of tokens (**150 B**) on one of four corpora:

| Corpus | Origin & Composition | Approx. Size |
|--------|---------------------|--------------|
| **DeepSeekMath Corpus** | FastText filtered Common Crawl + multilingual expansion | **120 B tokens** |
| MathPile | Multi‑source (textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CC, StackExchange, arXiv); >85 % arXiv | 8.9 B tokens |
| OpenWebMath | Directly filtered CC for math | 13.6 B tokens |
| Proof‑Pile‑2 | Mix of OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack (code), arXiv with ratio 2:4:1 | ~42 B tokens |

All models employed identical training hyperparameters: AdamW optimizer with \(\beta_1{=}0.9\), \(\beta_2{=}0.95\), weight decay \(0.1\), peak learning rate \(5.3\times10^{-4}\), batch size \(4\) M tokens and context length \(4\) K.

**Results across eight downstream benchmarks (both English and Chinese)** demonstrated three salient points:

1. **Higher Quality:** Models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus consistently outperformed those trained on MathPile, OpenWebMath or Proof‑Pile‑2 when evaluated via few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting.  Even at relatively low token exposure (≈50 B tokens), DeepSeekMath’s learning curve was steeper than Proof‑Pile‑2’s full‐epoch curve.
   
2. **Multilingual Benefit:** Because the retrieval pipeline did not limit itself to English domains—domains such as Chinese educational portals were also captured—the DeepSeekMath Corpus improved performance on Chinese benchmarks (e.g., CMATH) whereas predominantly English corpora sometimes degraded Chinese results.

3. **Scale Advantage:** The sheer volume (120 B tokens) allowed continued gains beyond what smaller corpora could deliver; baseline corpora quickly saturated after multiple passes over their limited data, producing diminishing returns while DeepSeekMath maintained upward progress throughout its token budget.

These comparative experiments validated that publicly available Common Crawl material harbors abundant high‐quality mathematical text when coupled with a carefully engineered selection pipeline; further, multilingual inclusion yields measurable benefits for non‑English tasks—a property lacking in many prior math corpora.
