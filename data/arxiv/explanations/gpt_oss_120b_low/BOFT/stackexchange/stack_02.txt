\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix reduce expressiveness compared to a dense orthogonal matrix?
Question:
In OFT the orthogonal matrix \(R\) is block‑diagonal, with each block of size \(b\times b\). The authors claim this structure cannot approximate many classic linear transforms and introduces undesirable inductive biases. Could someone clarify:

* How exactly does the block‑diagonal sparsity limit the space of possible rotations?
* Is there a simple example (e.g., a 4‑dimensional rotation) that cannot be represented by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with \(b=2\)?
* How does this limitation affect downstream performance?

Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the trainable matrix  

\[
R\;=\;\operatorname{diag}\!\big(R_{1},R_{2},\dots ,R_{r}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
\qquad 
R_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b},\; br=d,
\]

is forced to be block‑diagonal.  
Each block \(R_{i}\) is an independent orthogonal transformation that mixes only the coordinates belonging to its own block; there is **no coupling** between different blocks.  This simple structural constraint already tells us what can and cannot be represented.

### Why block‑diagonal sparsity limits rotations

An orthogonal matrix \(R\) represents a rotation (or a product of a rotation and a possible reflection) in the whole \(d\)-dimensional space.  Its columns (or rows) form an orthonormal basis, i.e. each basis vector can have components on *all* \(d\) coordinates.  When \(R\) is block‑diagonal, its columns are confined to the sub‑spaces spanned by the blocks:

* The first \(b\) columns have zeros outside the first \(b\) rows,  
* the next \(b\) columns have zeros outside rows \((b+1,\dots ,2b)\), and so on.

Consequently a vector that originally lies in one block can never be rotated into a different block; the transformation is a **direct sum** of independent \(b\)-dimensional rotations
\[
R = R_{1}\oplus R_{2}\oplus \cdots \oplus R_{r}.
\]
The reachable set of orthogonal matrices is therefore the subgroup  

\[
O(b)\times O(b)\times \cdots \times O(b)\subset O(d),
\]

which has dimension \(r\,b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2\).  
The full orthogonal group \(O(d)\) has dimension \(d(d-1)/2\).  
When \(b<d\) we lose \(\frac{d(d-b)}{2}\) degrees of freedom—exactly the degrees of freedom needed to rotate between different blocks.

### A concrete 4‑dimensional example with \(b=2\)

Take \(d=4,\;b=2,\;r=2\).  
A generic rotation in \(\mathbb{R}^{4}\) can be written as a product of *plane* rotations, for instance a rotation by angle \(\theta\) in the plane spanned by the first and third coordinate axes:

\[
R_{\text{mix}}(\theta)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos \theta & 0 & -\sin \theta & 0\\
0           & 1 & 0            & 0\\
\sin \theta & 0 & \cos \theta  & 0\\
0           & 0 & 0            & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The non‑zero entries appear in positions \((1,1),(1,3),(3,1),(3,3)\); they connect **different blocks** (the first block contains rows 1–2, the second block rows 3–4). No choice of two independent \(2\times2\) orthogonal blocks can produce this pattern, because any matrix of the form

\[
R_{\text{blk}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
A & 0\\
0 & B
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A,B\in O(2),
\]

has zeros exactly where \(R_{\text{mix}}(\theta)\) has non‑zeros. Hence the rotation that couples dimensions 1 and 3 (or any other cross‑block pair such as (1,4), (2,3), …) **cannot** be expressed by a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with block size 2.

If we restrict ourselves to rotations that keep each pair \(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{3,4\}\) separate, then any matrix we can represent is of the form  

\[
R_{\text{blk}}=
\begin{pmatrix}
c_{1} & -s_{1} & 0      & 0\\
s_{1} &  c_{1} & 0      & 0\\
0     &   0    & c_{2} & -s_{2}\\
0     &   0    & s_{2} &  c_{2}
\end{pmatrix},
\]

which clearly cannot produce the off‑block mixing shown above.

### Consequences for downstream performance

Because OFT with a block‑diagonal \(R\) can only rotate *within* each pre‑determined coordinate group, it cannot adapt weight directions that require interaction across groups. In practice this limitation manifests as follows:

* **Reduced ability to align pretrained features with task‑specific directions.** Many downstream tasks need linear combinations of neurons that live in different groups (e.g. features extracted from different patches of an image transformer). If those groups are never mixed, the finetuned model may need to compensate by changing later layers or by relying on additional parameters (biases, layernorm etc.), which reduces efficiency.
* **Implicit inductive bias toward “blockwise” invariances.** The model assumes that dimensions that happen to be adjacent in memory are semantically independent—a premise that rarely holds for learned representations.
* **Empirical signal in the paper.** The authors observe that when they replace the block‑diagonal structure by a butterfly factorization—still sparse but with inter‑block connections—the approximation error for random dense orthogonal matrices drops dramatically (Figure 6). This directly reflects the ability of butterfly matrices to realise rotations such as \(R_{\text{mix}}(\theta)\), whereas block‑diagonal OFT cannot.

In short, a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix lives in a low‑dimensional subgroup of \(O(d)\); it cannot express rotations that mix coordinates belonging to different blocks, as illustrated by the simple four‑dimensional example above. This loss of expressiveness limits how well OFT can adapt pretrained weights and explains why more expressive sparse factorizations such as butterfly matrices lead to better finetuning performance while still remaining parameter efficient.
