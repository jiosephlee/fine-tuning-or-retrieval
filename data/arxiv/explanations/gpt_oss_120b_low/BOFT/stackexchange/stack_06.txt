\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### What is multiplicative dropout in BOFT and how does it differ from standard dropout?
Question:
Section 4 mentions a “multiplicative Dropout” where random butterfly components or diagonal blocks are replaced by identity matrices. I am unsure about:

* How is the dropout mask sampled and applied during forward/backward passes?
* Does this dropout preserve the orthogonality constraint automatically?
* How does this technique affect training stability compared to standard dropout used in LoRA?

Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT the trainable orthogonal matrix is not stored as a single dense matrix but as a product of $m$ *butterfly components*  

$$
\mathbf R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},\qquad 
\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d},
$$

each component being a block‑diagonal matrix whose non‑zero blocks are $2b\times 2b$ orthogonal factors (the “butterfly factors’’).  
Because the whole update is multiplicative ($\mathbf R\mathbf W^{0}$), the usual element‑wise dropout used in LoRA cannot be applied – dropping an element would break the orthogonality of the factor.  
Instead the authors introduce **multiplicative dropout**, which works at the *structural* level of the factorisation.

---

### How the mask is sampled and applied  

1. **Sampling**  
   *Two independent Bernoulli masks* are drawn for every training step:  

   * a mask over the *components*: each of the $m$ butterfly components is kept with probability $1-p_{1}$ and replaced by the identity with probability $p_{1}$;  

   * a mask over the *blocks inside a component*: for a given component, its $d/b$ diagonal blocks (each of size $2b\times 2b$) are kept with probability $1-p_{2}$ and replaced by an identity block with probability $p_{2}$.

   The masks are sampled **once per forward pass** (the same mask is used for the backward pass, as in standard dropout).

2. **Application in the forward pass**  
   Let $\mathcal M^{(c)}_{i}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether component $i$ is dropped, and $\mathcal M^{(b)}_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}$ whether block $j$ inside component $i$ is dropped.  
   The effective orthogonal matrix becomes  

   $$
   \widetilde{\mathbf R}
   = \Bigl(\prod_{i=1}^{m}
        \bigl[\, \mathcal M^{(c)}_{i} \,\mathbf I_d
               + (1-\mathcal M^{(c)}_{i})\, 
                 \tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}(\mathcal M^{(b)}_{i}) \bigr]\Bigr),
   $$

   where $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}(\mathcal M^{(b)}_{i})$ has its selected blocks replaced by identity matrices according to the block‑mask.  
   The forward computation of a linear layer therefore uses

   $$
   \mathbf z = (\widetilde{\mathbf R}\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf x .
   $$

3. **Backward pass**  
   Because the mask is fixed during a forward–backward pair, gradients flow through exactly the same set of blocks that were active in the forward pass.  The identity blocks contribute no gradient (they are constant), while the retained blocks receive standard gradients from the loss.  No special treatment beyond ordinary automatic differentiation is required.

---

### Orthogonality preservation  

Each butterfly component is *by construction* an orthogonal matrix (the Cayley parametrisation guarantees $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\mathbf I_d$).  
When we replace an entire component or a block by an **identity matrix**, orthogonality is still satisfied because $\mathbf I_d^{\!\top}\mathbf I_d=\mathbf I_d$.  
Consequently, after applying both masks the product $\widetilde{\mathbf R}$ remains orthogonal **by construction**; there is no need for any post‑hoc projection or re‑normalisation.  This is in stark contrast to standard dropout on weight elements, which destroys orthogonality and would require costly re‑orthogonalisation.

---

### Effect on training stability compared with LoRA’s dropout  

* **Preservation of spectral norm** – Since every factor that survives dropout stays orthogonal, the overall spectral norm of $\widetilde{\mathbf R}$ stays exactly one.  Orthogonal finetuning therefore inherits the stability benefits observed for OFT/BOFT (no amplification of activations, well‑behaved gradients).  LoRA’s additive low‑rank update can change the singular values of the effective weight matrix; applying conventional dropout on its low‑rank part adds additional stochastic variation to those singular values and can occasionally cause spikes in gradient norm.

* **Regularisation at a higher structural level** – Multiplicative dropout hides or reveals whole *information‑exchange pathways* rather than individual scalar weights.  This encourages the model to learn representations that are robust to missing butterfly components, which empirically leads to smoother loss curves and fewer catastrophic spikes during early training stages.

* **Empirical observations reported in the paper** – In Section 4 (the experiments), BOFT with multiplicative dropout (e.g., $p_{1}=0.2,\;p_{2}=0.3$) shows comparable or slightly better validation performance than BOFT without dropout, while LoRA’s standard dropout sometimes degrades performance when the same dropout rates are used because it interferes with the additive low‑rank correction.

Overall, multiplicative dropout maintains the key geometric property (orthogonality) that underlies BOFT’s stability, whereas LoRA’s usual dropout operates on a different parameterisation and does not enjoy that guarantee.

---

### TL;DR  

*The “multiplicative dropout’’ in BOFT randomly zeroes out whole butterfly components and/or whole diagonal blocks inside each component, replacing them by identity matrices.*  
*Because both a butterfly component and an identity matrix are orthogonal, the resulting product stays exactly orthogonal, so no extra projection step is needed.*  
*This structural dropout regularises BOFT without harming its spectral‑norm preservation, leading to training stability that is usually better than applying standard elementwise dropout to LoRA’s additive low‑rank updates.*
