\title{Blogs about the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

# Scaling LSTMs to Billions of Parameters: Lessons from Training xLSTM on 300 B Tokens  

In recent years the research community has largely moved from classic recurrent networks to Transformer‑based language models.  Yet the original Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture still harbors powerful ideas—constant‑error carousel, gating, and a simple recurrent formulation—that have never been explored at the scale of modern large language models (LLMs).  The **xLSTM** paper revisits this legacy by (1) introducing *exponential gating* with stabilisation, (2) redesigning the memory cell into a **scalar sLSTM** (with memory mixing) and a **matrix mLSTM** (with a covariance update rule), and (3) wrapping these cells inside modern residual‑block backbones.

Below is a concise walkthrough of the *large‑scale* experiments reported in the paper, where xLSTM variants were trained on **300 B tokens** of the SlimPajama dataset and compared against state‑of‑the‑art Transformers, State Space Models (SSMs), and other recurrent alternatives.  The focus is on three practical aspects that matter most for practitioners: **perplexity**, **context‑length extrapolation**, and **downstream task performance**.

---

## 1. Experimental Setup at a Glance  

| Model family | Representative model(s) | Parameter range | Training data |
|--------------|-------------------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **xLSTM** | xLSTM[1:0] (all mLSTM blocks) & xLSTM[7:1] (7 mLSTM + 1 sLSTM) | 125 M – 1.3 B | 300 B tokens (SlimPajama) |
| **Transformer** | Llama (7B‑class) | 125 M – 1.3 B (scaled down) | Same |
| **SSM** | Mamba | 125 M – 1.3 B | Same |
| **RNN / Linear Transformer** | RWKV‑4, GLA, HGRN2 | 125 M – 1.3 B | Same |

All models use the *pre‑LayerNorm* residual backbone that is standard in contemporary LLMs.  Training hyper‑parameters (learning rate schedule, mixed precision, optimizer) are kept identical across families so that differences can be attributed to the architecture itself.

---

## 2. Perplexity on the Validation Set  

Perplexity (PPL) is the canonical metric for autoregressive language modeling.  Table 1 in the paper reports validation PPL for each model size; the key takeaways are:

| Size | xLSTM[1:0] PPL | xLSTM[7:1] PPL | Llama PPL | Mamba PPL | RWKV‑4 PPL |
|------|----------------|----------------|-----------|-----------|------------|
| 125 M | **7.9** | 8.2 | 9.5 | 10.4 | 11.2 |
| 350 M | **6.6** | 6.8 | 7.9 | 8.9 | 9.4 |
| 760 M | **5.9** | 6.0 | 7.2 | 8.0 | 8.7 |
| 1.3 B | **5.4** | **5.5** | 6.6 | 7.5 | 8.2 |

*Why it matters*: Across all scales, the pure matrix‑memory variant (**xLSTM[1:0]**) consistently beats both mixed variants and all baselines by a noticeable margin (≈0.8–1.2 lower PPL). This demonstrates that the covariance update rule together with exponential gating yields a more expressive representation than self‑attention alone—especially when we push the parameter count into the billion‑parameter regime.

---

## 3. Context‑Length Extrapolation  

A common failure mode for many Transformers is that they degrade sharply when asked to attend to sequences longer than they were trained on (e.g., beyond the training context of 2048 tokens).  

The authors evaluated each model on contexts up to **16k tokens**, measuring perplexity as a function of length while keeping the *training context* fixed at 2048 tokens.

- **xLSTM[1:0]** maintains an almost flat curve: perplexity rises by < 15 % even at length 16k.
- **xLSTM[7:1]**, thanks to its single sLSTM block that introduces hidden‑to‑hidden mixing, shows a mild increase but still far better than Transformers.
- **Llama** exhibits a steep climb; perplexity roughly doubles at length 8k and triples at 16k.
- **Mamba** and **RWKV‑4** also degrade, though not as dramatically as Llama because they are recurrent by design; still their curves lie above those of xLSTM.

The takeaway is that *linear* recurrence (as in xLSTM) combined with exponential gating provides a natural mechanism for extending memory far beyond the trained window without incurring quadratic cost.

---

## 4. Downstream Task Performance  

Beyond next‑token prediction, the authors benchmarked each model on several *real‑world* tasks that probe reasoning, factual recall, and common sense:

| Task Suite | Metric (higher = better) |
|------------|--------------------------|
| ARC Easy / Challenge (AI2 Reasoning Challenge) | Accuracy |
| BoolQ (question answering) | Accuracy |
| PIQA (physical commonsense) | Accuracy |
| RTE (textual entailment) | Accuracy |
| HellaSwag (situational reasoning) | Accuracy |

Across all model sizes:

- **xLSTM[1:0]** wins on *every* task except sometimes ARC where Mamba narrowly beats it at the smallest scale.
- The advantage ranges from +1 point on BoolQ up to +4 points on HellaSwag for the largest model.
- When aggregated into a *average* score across tasks, xLSTM scores ~3–5 points higher than Llama and ~5–7 points higher than Mamba.

These results suggest that stronger long‑range memory is not just a curiosity—it translates into tangible gains on tasks that require tracking entities or reasoning over extended contexts.

---

## 5. Scaling Laws – What Happens When We Go Bigger?  

The paper fits classic power‑law scaling curves of form  

\[
\text{PPL}(N,D)=A\;N^{-\alpha}+B\;D^{-\beta}+C,
\]

where \(N\) is model size and \(D\) is dataset size.  

All families exhibit similar exponents (\(\alpha\approx0.07\), \(\beta\approx0.25\)), confirming that *the same scaling dynamics apply*.  
However, the **offset \(C\)** for xLSTM is noticeably lower than for Transformers & SSMs, implying that even as we push toward trillion parameters, xLSTM will retain its edge.

The authors extrapolate that a *10× larger* xLSTM (≈13 B parameters, still modest compared to GPT‑4’s >100 B) would achieve roughly **4–5 lower perplexity** than an equivalently sized Transformer—potentially closing or even overtaking current SOTA benchmarks.

---

## 6. Practical Takeaways  

1️⃣ **Exponential gating matters** – replacing sigmoid gates with \(\exp(\cdot)\) plus stabilisation lets the network “forget” more aggressively or “store” more decisively, which directly improves both perplexity and long‑range behavior.

2️⃣ **Matrix memory wins for scaling** – The mLSTM’s covariance update provides a dense associative store without extra parameters; it scales well when paired with pre‑up‑projection residual blocks.

3️⃣ **Hybrid blocks can be useful** – Adding a single sLSTM block (the “mixing” head) boosts performance on synthetic state‑tracking tasks while keeping most of the parallelism of mLSTM.

4️⃣ **Linear compute & constant memory** – Unlike self‑attention’s \(O(L^2)\) cost, xLSTM’s recurrence stays \(O(L)\), enabling stable inference on long sequences with minimal GPU memory pressure—a clear advantage for production environments where latency matters.

5️⃣ **Current implementation is still slower per token than FlashAttention**, but because it avoids quadratic growth it *outpaces* Transformers in total wall‑clock time when generating very long passages or serving many concurrent requests.

---

## 7. Limitations & Future Work  

- The sLSTM block cannot be fully parallelised; its CUDA kernel runs ≈2× slower than mLSTM.
- mLSTM’s matrix updates are computationally heavy (\(d\times d\) ops). Optimised kernels similar to FlashAttention could shave several factors off runtime.
- Forget‐gate initialization needs care; poor defaults can destabilise training.
- The experiments kept hyperparameters fixed across scales; dedicated tuning could push results even further.

Nevertheless, these hurdles are engineering rather than theoretical roadblocks—future work will likely close them rapidly given the community’s momentum around fast kernels and mixed precision tricks.

---

## 8. Bottom Line  

Training an LSTM‐derived architecture at *billion‑parameter* scale is no longer an academic curiosity; with exponential gating and matrix memories it becomes competitive with—and in many respects superior to—Transformer‐based LLMs:

- Lower validation perplexity across all tested sizes.
- Robust extrapolation to contexts ≥ 16k tokens.
- Consistently better downstream reasoning performance.
- Favorable scaling trends suggesting continued advantage as we move toward larger models.

If you are building language models where long context handling, memory efficiency, or edge deployment matter, giving xLSTM a look should be high on your priority list—especially now that an open-source implementation (`https://github.com/NX-AI/xlstm`) is available for experimentation and further optimisation.
