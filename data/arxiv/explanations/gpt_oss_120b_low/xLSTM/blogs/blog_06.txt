# When Does Bigger Mean Better? Decoding the Scaling Laws of xLSTM vs. Transformers  

## Introduction  

Scaling laws have become a go‑to diagnostic for large language models (LLMs). By plotting validation perplexity (or loss) against model size, data volume or compute, researchers repeatedly find a clean power‑law relationship: performance improves roughly as a straight line on a log‑log plot, with a modest offset that depends on the architecture.  

The recent **xLSTM** paper asks the same question that has driven the Transformer boom: *What happens if we take the classic Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM), address its long‑standing weaknesses, and scale it to billions of parameters?* The answer is striking—xLSTM follows a classic scaling law, but its curve sits **below** (i.e., better than) those of state‑of‑the‑art Transformers and State‑Space Models (SSMs). In this post we unpack why the exponent is similar across families while the offset stays favorable for xLSTM, and we explore what this means for the next generation of LLMs.  

---

## Recap: The three ingredients of xLSTM  

Before diving into scaling, let’s recall what makes xLSTM different from a vanilla LSTM:  

1. **Exponential gating** – Input and forget gates can use an exponential activation (instead of a sigmoid) together with a stabilizer state. This lets the model *revise* earlier storage decisions without running into overflow problems.  

2. **New memory structures** –  
   * **sLSTM** keeps a scalar cell but adds a normalizer state and “memory mixing” across multiple heads, allowing richer interaction between cells.  
   * **mLSTM** upgrades the cell to a full matrix **C**, updated by a covariance (outer‑product) rule. The matrix acts like a fast weight memory, storing key–value pairs that can be retrieved with a query vector.  

3. **Residual xLSTM blocks** – The sLSTM or mLSTM cell is embedded in a residual block that either projects up after (sLSTM) or before (mLSTM) the nonlinearity, following the Cover’s theorem intuition that high‑dimensional embeddings make histories easier to separate.

These design choices give xLSTM three practical advantages:  
* **Revisability** (thanks to exponential gating),  
* **Higher storage capacity** (matrix memory), and  
* **Linear time & constant memory cost w.r.t. sequence length**, unlike the quadratic attention in Transformers.

---

## Observed Scaling Behavior  

The authors trained several families on the same 300 B token subset of SlimPajama, varying model size from ≈125 M to 1.3 B parameters. For each family they plotted validation perplexity \(P\) against model size \(N\) in log–log space and fitted

\[
\log P = \alpha \log N + \beta,
\]

where \(\alpha\) is the *scaling exponent* (slope) and \(\beta\) is the *offset* (intercept).  

### What the plots reveal  

| Architecture | Exponent (\(\alpha\)) | Offset (\(\beta\)) |
|--------------|----------------------|--------------------|
| Transformer (Llama) | ≈ ‑0.07 | higher |
| State‑Space Model (Mamba) | ≈ ‑0.07 | medium |
| xLSTM (mixed s/m blocks) | ≈ ‑0.07 | **lowest** |

*All three families share essentially the same exponent.* This means that **doubling model size yields roughly the same percentage improvement in perplexity across architectures**—a universal law governed mainly by data distribution and task difficulty rather than model internals.

What sets xLSTM apart is its **lower offset** (\(\beta\)). On every size point, perplexity is consistently better (by ~5–10 % relative) than Transformers and even stronger than SSMs. In other words, for any given parameter budget, an xLSTM will be more accurate than its Transformer counterpart.

---

## Why Does xLSTM Keep a Favorable Offset?  

### 1. Better Utilization of Parameters  

Exponential gating lets each gate dynamically amplify or suppress signals without being capped at 1. Consequently, *fewer* parameters are needed to achieve strong temporal credit assignment—the network can quickly forget irrelevant past information and strongly retain important signals. This flexibility translates into higher *effective capacity*, meaning each weight contributes more to reducing loss.

### 2. Richer Memory Embedding  

The matrix memory in mLSTM behaves like an associative cache where each key–value pair contributes an outer product \(\mathbf{v}_t\mathbf{k}_t^\top\). This update captures second‑order interactions that would otherwise require many scalar cells and deep stacking in a regular LSTM or Transformer. As a result, even modestly sized mLSTMs store more distinct patterns, improving rare‑token prediction and long‑range recall—two major contributors to overall perplexity.

### 3. Linear Complexity + High Dimensional Embedding  

Because recurrence costs are linear in sequence length, training can afford *longer* contexts without ballooning memory usage. The residual block’s up‑projection creates high‑dimensional hidden states before mixing, which aligns with Cover’s theorem: patterns that were tangled in low dimensions become linearly separable after projection. The combination reduces the burden on later layers, allowing earlier recurrent units to focus on capturing essential dynamics rather than compensating for representational bottlenecks.

### 4. Stabilization Guarantees  

The stabilizer state \(m_t\) ensures that exponential gates do not overflow during long sequences, preserving gradient flow across thousands of timesteps. Stable gradients mean training can converge faster and reach lower minima for a given compute budget—again pulling down the offset.

## Implications for Future LLM Development  

### A Viable Alternative at Scale  

If the scaling exponent remains universal—as these experiments suggest—then any architecture that can push down its offset will dominate at all scales. xLSTM demonstrates exactly this; thus we can expect **billion‑parameter xLSTMs to match or beat contemporary 10+ B Transformers**, especially when hardware favors linear recurrence over attention’s quadratic kernels.

### Compute Efficiency on Edge Devices  

Since memory usage stays constant with sequence length, xLSTMs are naturally suited for on‑device inference where context windows may be long (e.g., real‑time transcription). The lower offset implies fewer parameters are needed for a target quality level, further reducing power consumption.

### Hybrid Models  

The paper shows mixed configurations—xLSTM[7:1] (seven mLSTM blocks per sLSTM block)—perform slightly better than pure mLSTM or pure sLSTM stacks. This suggests future LLM designs could interleave **fast associative matrix cells** with **scalar cells that provide fine‐grained mixing**, yielding architectures that blend the strengths of both worlds.

### Research Directions  

* **Optimized CUDA kernels** – The current implementation is slower than FlashAttention; closing this gap could make xLSTMs even more competitive on GPUs and TPUs.  
* **Deeper scaling experiments** – Extending beyond 1.3 B parameters would verify whether the offset advantage persists or even widens at tens of billions of weights.  
* **Task‑specific fine‑tuning** – Because matrix memory excels at rare token recall, domains with heavy terminology (legal text, scientific literature) may benefit disproportionately from xLSTMs.

---

## Takeaway  

Scaling laws tell us *how* performance improves with size; they don’t dictate *which* architecture sits closest to the optimal curve. xLSTM proves that by revisiting core LSTM ideas—exponential gating and richer memories—we can shift the entire scaling curve downward while preserving the same exponent observed for Transformers and SSMs.

In practical terms: **bigger still means better**, but “bigger” now includes *better organized* parameters. For anyone building tomorrow’s LLMs, keeping an eye on recurrent designs like xLSTM is worthwhile—not merely as a nostalgic nod to early RNNs but as a forward‑looking strategy for efficient, high‑performance language models.
