\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### What is the intuition behind preserving the first \(\hat{n}\) token positions without rescaling during positional interpolation?
Question:
The authors introduce a piecewise function where tokens with position index < \(\hat{n}\) keep their original RoPE angles (i.e., multiplier = 1). Empirically they find performance improves when \(\hat{n}\)>0, especially for longer extensions. Why might early tokens be more sensitive to interpolation? Is there any theoretical analysis linking this choice to attention patterns or gradient flow?
Answer:
**Short answer** –  
The first $\hat{n}$ tokens in a Transformer receive a disproportionate amount of “attention traffic’’ during both pre‑training and downstream inference. Their RoPE angles have therefore been tuned very precisely on the original (e.g. 4‑k) context window. When we stretch the window we inevitably have to *compress* the rotary angles – every dimension $i$ is multiplied by a factor $1/\lambda_i$ – which shortens the effective phase $\Delta \theta_{ij}= (j-i)/\beta^{\, i}$ that the model uses to distinguish two positions $i$ and $j$. For **small** index differences (the case for most pairs that involve an early token) this compression makes the cosine term  

$$
\mathbf{Q}_i^\top \mathbf{K}_j \;\propto\;\sum_{k=0}^{d/2-1}\!\cos!\bigl((j-i)/\beta^{\,k}\bigr)
$$  

almost constant, i.e.\​the model can no longer tell whether two nearby positions differ by one or two steps. In other words, *position crowding* happens most severely where 
the offset $|\,j-i\,|$
is tiny,
and those tiny offsets occur most often around 
the beginning
of
the sequence because every later token attends back
to them.

Keeping
the original RoPE
for
the first $\hat{n}$
positions therefore prevents two related harms:

1. **Loss of fine‑grained relative information** – The high­frequency “fast”
RoPE dimensions ($k$
small,
$\theta_k$
large)
encode short­range relationships exactly
as they were learned
on
the pre­training data
(e.g.\ prompt instructions,
system messages).
By leaving them untouched we preserve
the phase resolution needed for these relationships while still allowing slower dimensions
to be stretched
for distant positions.

2. **Gradient­flow mismatch** – During both pre­training
and any subsequent fine­tuning,
an early token acts as a query for almost every later query
(causal masking means token 
$j>i$
can attend to 
$i$
).
Consequently its contribution appears in
a large fraction
of loss terms
and its gradient magnitude is correspondingly larger than that
of ​a token near​ ​the tail​ ​of​ ​the​ ​sequence.
If we alter its positional encoding with a “one­size­fits­all’’ scaling factor,
we inject​ ​a systematic bias​ ​that conflicts​​ ​with​ ​the​ ​distribution​ ​of​ ​gradients seen​ ​during​ ​pre­training,
slowing convergence​ ​or​​ ​​even destabilising training.
Preserving its original angles removes this source​ ​of bias​ ​​and gives​​ ​​the optimizer​ ​​a smoother landscape​​ .

These observations match what
the authors report as **Finding 2:** “the initial $\hat{n}$
tokens should be extrapolated​ ​with​​ ​less​​ ​interpolation’’—
and they cite related work on *Streaming LLM*
and *LM‐Infinite*
where early tokens are shown empirically
to attract large attention scores​​ .

From a theoretical standpoint one can view this piecewise strategy
as an additional non‐uniformity prior on top of  the NTK‐motivated monotonic scaling across dimensions.
In NTK theory  the kernel entry between positions $i,j$
depends on 
$\cos((\:&#8203;j\-&#8203;i\:))/&#8203;\beta^{&#8203;k})\$;
protecting low–offset entries ($\:&#8203;j\-&#8203;i<&#8203;\widehat{\!\!n}\:$)
simply preserves those kernel entries that dominatethe spectrum at high frequencies.
This selective preservation reduces information loss in exactly those eigen­directionsthat carry most of the signal learned on short contexts。
