\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### Why does linear positional interpolation (PI) cause “crowded” position representations in RoPE?
Question:
The paper explains that PI downscales rotation angles by a factor \(\lambda = s\), where \(s\) is the extension ratio, uniformly across all RoPE dimensions. It then says this makes position information very “crowded”, hindering the model’s ability to distinguish closely positioned tokens. Could someone clarify what “crowded” means mathematically or intuitively in this context? How does uniformly scaling all dimensions lead to reduced discriminability between nearby token positions?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Rotary‐Positional‐Embedding (RoPE) each pair of hidden‐vector components is treated as a complex number  

$$
\mathbf r_{i}(n)=%
\bigr[\cos(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}}),%
      \sin(\tfrac{n}{\beta^{\,i}})\bigr],
      \qquad i=0,\dots ,\frac{d}{2}-1 ,
$$

where $n$ is the token index and $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ controls the frequency of the $i$th “dimension”.  
For a fixed $i$ the vector $\mathbf r_{i}(n)$ rotates on a unit circle with angular step  

$$
\Delta \phi_{i}= \frac{1}{\beta^{\,i}} .
$$

Small–$i$ dimensions have a **large** step (high frequency); large–$i$
dimensions rotate slowly (low frequency).  
The full positional encoding is the concatenation of all those complex pairs. Two distinct positions $n,m$ are distinguished because, for at least one dimension, their complex numbers are far apart on the circle:

$$
\langle \mathbf r_{i}(n), \mathbf r_{i}(m)\rangle
=\cos{\Bigl(\tfrac{n-m}{\beta^{\,i}}\Bigr)} .
\ColumnTag{(1)}
$$

---

### What “crowded’’ means

When we extend a model from its training length $L$ to a longer length $L'=sL$
we cannot simply add fresh angles – otherwise we would introduce completely new values that the model never saw.
Linear Positional Interpolation (PI) therefore **compresses** every angle by a factor $\lambda=s$,

$$
\mathbf r^{\text{PI}}_{i}(n)=%
\bigr[\cos(\tfrac{n}{s\,\beta^{\,i}}),%
      \sin(\tfrac{n}{s\,\beta^{\,i}})\bigr].
\ColumnTag{(2)}
$$

All dimensions are shrunk by exactly the same factor $s>1$.  
If we look at two adjacent tokens $(m=n+1)$ their inner product after PI becomes

$$
%
\bigr<
   \mathbf r^{\text{PI}}_{i}(n),
   \mathbf r^{ {\text{PI}}}_{i}(n+1)
\bigr>
=\cos{\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{s\,        \beta^{\: i}}\Bigr)}
\simeq 1-\frac{
        1}{
        2 s^{2}\,\beta^{\: 2 i}}
\ColumnTag{(3)}
$$

Compared with the original model ($s=1$) the cosine term is **closer to 1**
by roughly a factor $s^{2}$.
Geometrically this means that on every unit circle the points representing consecutive positions have been pulled together; the whole set of possible positions is squeezed into a much smaller arc of the circle.
When we concatenate many such circles, the resulting vectors lie in a very tight region of the high‐dimensional space.
That compression is what the authors call *crowding*: many different token indices map onto vectors that are almost indistinguishable.

From an information‐theoretic point of view, the **minimum Euclidean distance**
between any two distinct positional codes is

$$
d_{\min}^{(s)}=
   \bigl|\!\big|\;
      \mathbf r^{(s)}(n)- 
      \mathbf r^{(s)}(m)
   |\!\big|\;
   \approx 
   \frac{|n-m|}{s}\,
   \sqrt{\sum_{i}
          {\displaystyle 
          \frac{
                 1}{
                 (\beta^{\: i})^{\: 2}}
          }}
   =%
   \frac{
         d_{\min}^{(1)}
        }{
         s},
\ColumnTag{(4)}
$$

which is reduced exactly by $s$.  
If this distance falls below numerical noise or below what downstream linear layers can reliably separate,
two different positions become effectively **aliased**—the model can no longer tell whether it is looking at token~$42$
or token~$43\,00042$.  

---

### Why uniform scaling hurts discriminability

The purpose of having many RoPE dimensions with different frequencies is precisely to give us *multiple resolution scales*:

* low–$i$ (high–frequency) dimensions discriminate very short gaps;
* high–$i$ (low–frequency) dimensions encode long–range order.

Uniformly applying the same factor $s$
destroys this multi–scale property.
All frequencies are divided by $s$, so even the high–frequency axes lose their rapid rotation and become as slow as some low–frequency axes used to be.
Consequently

* Adjacent tokens become almost parallel in **every** subspace;
* The cosine similarity in Eq.~(1) decays much more slowly with $|n-m|$, so attention scores change only slightly across hundreds or thousands of tokens;
* The model receives almost identical query/key pairs for many consecutive positions and cannot assign sharply peaked attention weights.

Non–uniform strategies such as NTK–aware interpolation or YaRN avoid this by choosing a *dimension–dependent* rescaling, e.g.

$$
\sigma_{i}= s^{c_{i}}, 
      \qquad c_{0}<c_{1}<\dots<c_{\tfrac d  2 - 1},
$$

so that low–$i$
(high–frequency) dimensions keep a small rescale ($\sigma_{i}\!\approx 1$)
while only the very low–frequency dims are stretched more aggressively.
That preserves enough angular separation for nearby tokens while still compressing far–away positions enough to fit into the original range.

---

### In short

“Crowded’’ refers to **the geometric concentration of positional vectors**
after linear interpolation: every rotary pair rotates only a fraction $\tfrac 1 s$
of its original speed, so successive tokens occupy almost identical points on their unit circles.
The resulting loss of Euclidean distance or cosine margin makes it hard for attention heads to tell nearby tokens apart,
which manifests as degraded performance when we try large extensions.
Uniformly shrinking all RoPE dimensions therefore compromises the multi–scale encoding that makes RoPE effective,
whereas non–uniform rescaling restores the required separation and alleviates crowding.
