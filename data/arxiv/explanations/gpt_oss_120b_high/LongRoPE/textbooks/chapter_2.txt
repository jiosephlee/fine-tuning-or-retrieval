Chapter 2: Fundamentals of Transformers and Positional Information  

# Self‑attention mechanism and its dependence on token order  

Transformers process an input sequence $x_{1},x_{2},\dots ,x_{L}$ by first mapping each token $x_{t}$ into an embedding vector $\mathbf{e}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. These embeddings are then projected linearly into three families called queries, keys and values:
\[
Q = XW_{Q},\qquad K = XW_{K},\qquad V = XW_{V},
\]
where $X=[\mathbf{e}_{1};\dots ;\mathbf{e}_{L}]$ concatenates the token embeddings and $W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}$ are learnable weight matrices.  
For a single attention head, the interaction between every pair of tokens is measured by the scaled dot‑product
\[
A_{ij}= \frac{\mathbf{q}_{i}\!\cdot \!\mathbf{k}_{j}}{\sqrt{d_k}},
\]
which is turned into a probability distribution through the softmax function:
\[
\alpha_{ij}= \operatorname{softmax}_{j}(A_{ij})=\frac{\exp(A_{ij})}{\sum_{j'=1}^{L}\exp(A_{ij'})}.
\]
The output representation of token $i$ is then obtained by aggregating values according to these attention weights:
\[
\mathbf{o}_{i}= \sum_{j=1}^{L}\alpha_{ij}\,\mathbf{v}_{j}.
\]
Collecting all outputs yields the standard self‑attention operation
\[
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)=
\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\!V .
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) makes it clear that without any additional signal each token treats every other token symmetrically—the computation depends only on pairwise inner products of queries and keys. Consequently, if two input sequences contain exactly the same multiset of tokens but arranged in different orders, their representations would be identical under pure self‑attention. This permutation invariance contradicts natural language where meaning emerges from word order (e.g., “the cat chased the mouse’’ versus “the mouse chased the cat’’). Therefore some form of positional information must be injected so that $(Q,K,V)$ carry a notion of where a token occurs in the sequence. In practice this injection enables language models to predict successive words accurately, perform long‑range reasoning across distant symbols, and exploit contextual cues such as syntax or discourse structure.

Self‑attention is typically performed over many heads simultaneously (“multi‑head” attention). Each head operates on a distinct subspace of dimension $d_h=d/H$, where $H$ denotes the number of heads. By allowing different heads to focus on complementary aspects—some learning local syntactic patterns while others capture coarse semantic relations—the Transformer gains expressive power while retaining an overall computational cost that scales quadratically with sequence length ($O(L^{2})$). Extending $L$ therefore incurs substantial memory/time demands, which motivates research effort dedicated both to efficient attention mechanisms and to extending the usable “context window’’ through better positional encoding strategies—a topic taken up later in this chapter.”

# Absolute vs. relative positional encodings  

The most straightforward way to break permutation invariance is to attach an *absolute* vector that directly tells each token its index in the sequence. Early Transformer designs added a learned or deterministic embedding $\mathbf{p}_{n}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ at position $n$, producing an input representation 
$\mathbf{s}_{n}= \mathbf{e}_{n}+ \mathbf{p}_{n}$.   
A widely cited deterministic choice consists of sinusoidal functions whose frequencies span several octaves:
$\mathbf{p}_{n}[2i]= \sin (n/\beta^{\,i})$,   
$\mathbf{p}_{n}[2i+1]= \cos (n/\beta^{\,i})$
with $\beta>1$. Although convenient because they never require learning parameters beyond those already present for regular embeddings, absolute methods suffer from an inherent limitation: they are defined only up to the maximum index observed during pre‑training. When evaluating a model on sequences longer than those seen during training—as required for tasks involving very long documents or many few‑shot examples—the new indices have no previously learned representation. Empirically this mismatch produces “catastrophic values’’ at unseen positions and makes fine‑tuning unstable, precisely what recent works have reported when trying to reach context windows far beyond several thousand tokens.

*Relative* positional encodings offer an alternative paradigm whereby the relationship between two positions—instead of their raw indices—is incorporated directly into the attention calculation. In such schemes one typically modifies Equation (1) so that
$\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j$ includes an additional term dependent only on $(i-j)$. This idea allows models trained on short sequences still to reason about far‐apart positions because any distance appearing at inference time can be expressed through learned patterns over small offsets together with appropriate functional extrapolation.

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), introduced in recent large language models including LLaMA 2 and Mistral, belongs to this family of *relative* schemes while preserving computational efficiency identical to plain self‑attention~[rope]. Instead of augmenting queries or keys with extra vectors it rotates them inside two‑dimensional subspaces according to sinusoidal angles derived from each token’s absolute index. As shown below, this rotation implicitly endows every inner product with a dependence solely upon the *relative* offset between queries and keys—a property crucial for seamless extrapolation beyond training lengths when combined with careful interpolation strategies such as those explored later in this chapter.”

# Mathematical formulation of Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)  

For a given model dimension $d$, RoPE groups every consecutive pair of scalar components into one complex plane. Let us consider a token located at absolute position $n$. Its rotary angle vector consists of $d/2$ distinct frequencies,
each indexed by $i=0,\dots , d/2{-}1$.  
The angle assigned to dimension $i$ equals $n\,\theta_i$, where
$\displaystyle 
    \theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}
$
and $\theta=10\,000$ constitutes the default base value adopted in modern LLMs~[rope].  
If we write explicitly all cosine–sine pairs associated with these angles, RoPE’s contribution for position $n$ reads
%
$$
[\, 
   \cos (n\,\theta_0),~   \sin (n\,\theta_0),~
   \cos (n\,\theta_1),~   \sin (n\,\theta_1),~
   \dots ,~
   \cos (n\,\theta_{\frac d 2 - 1}),~   \sin (n\,\theta_{\frac d 2 - 1})
] .
$$
%
In practice one multiplies this vector elementwise with both query and key representations before feeding them into Equation (1). Written compactly using complex notation,
the operation amounts simply to applying a rotation matrix
$\mathrm R(\phi)=
    [ [\,cos(\phi),~-sin(\phi);~ sin(\phi),~ cos(\phi)\,] ]$
to each two‑dimensional slice:
%
$$
[\mathbf q_i^{(n)},\, \mathbf q_{i+1}^{(n)}] 
      = 
      \mathrm R( n\,θ_i ) 
      [\, q^{(0)}_i , q^{(0)}_{i+1}\,] ,
$$
and analogously for keys $\mathbf k_j$.  
When taking their dot product,
the rotations combine algebraically:
$\mathbf q_i^{(n)}{}^\!\top 
      \mathbf k_j^{(m)}
      =
      (\textbf q^{(0)} _i{}^\!\top 
        {\textbf k}^{(0)} _j )
         \cos[(m-n)\,\!θ_i] + (\textbf q^{(0)} _{i+1}{}^\!\top 
        {\textbf k}^{(0)} _j )  
        ~~? ~~ $
which reveals that **only** the distance $(m-n)$ survives after simplification—a true *relative* encoding embedded within otherwise static query/key tensors. This mathematical property explains why RoPE can be applied without changing any part of vanilla self‑attention circuitry yet still equip models with information about where each token sits relative to others.



# Frequency spectrum of RoPE dimensions  

The design choice $\displaystyle θ_i=\!θ^{-2i/d}$ yields an exponential decay of rotation frequencies across dimensions. It is convenient also to introduce short notation
$\displaystyle β=\θ^{2/d}$
so that any angle can be expressed compactly as
$\displaystyle n/β^{\, i}.$  
Consequently,
lower indices ($i=0,\!1,\!\dots)$ correspond **to high frequencies** because division by $β^{\, i}$ yields larger angular increments per unit increase in $n$. High‑frequency components therefore oscillate rapidly across consecutive positions; they act like fine gratings capable of distinguishing tokens that appear next to one another—a behavior especially important for capturing syntactic cues such as adjective–noun order or function word placement.

Conversely,
higher indices produce **low frequencies**, changing slowly as the position index grows larger,
and consequently encode coarse‐grained relationships spanning thousands or even millions of tokens. In other words,
while dimension $i=0$ may complete many full cosine cycles within a short context window,
dimension $i=d/2{-}1$ will vary imperceptibly over exactly that same span but will become informative only when evaluating very long separations between tokens.

This non‑uniform spectral distribution lies at the heart of recent advances aimed at stretching Transformer context windows far beyond their original limits (~4 k tokens). Simple linear interpolation---which rescales every angle by a single factor equal to the extension ratio $s=L'/L$---forces **all** dimensions regardless of frequency onto the same compressed range when viewed under longer lengths. The result is “position crowding’’ particularly harmful for high‑frequency slices whose cycles become too dense for effective discrimination among nearby tokens---a phenomenon documented experimentally via sharp perplexity spikes when extending past modest multiples such as 8× or 16× without special care~[pi].

More sophisticated approaches therefore allocate distinct rescaling factors·λ₀,…,$λ_{\! d/2{-}1}$ per dimension,
respecting monotonicity ($λ_0≤λ_1≤…≤λ_{\! d/2{-}1}$) motivated by neural tangent kernel theory~[ntk]. This constraint enforces mild stretching on high‑frequency components while permitting stronger dilation on low‑frequency ones—a strategy shown empirically by NTK‐aware interpolation methods [dyn​amicNTK] to improve extension ratios up through ≈4× without fine‑tuning.~[yarn] further refines this idea through heuristic grouping into three bands (“high”, “mid”, “low”) each receiving its own rule (“extrapolation”, “NTK”, “linear”).  

LongRoPE builds directly upon these observations by treating **both** sources of non‑uniformity—frequency across dimensions **and** early‑position sensitivity—as searchable degrees of freedom rather than fixed hyper­parameters derived from human intuition alone._Through an evolutionary search over massive spaces (>10⁵⁶⁷ candidates even for modest extensions) it learns a set $\{\lambda_i,\hat n\}$ where $\hat n$ dictates how many initial positions retain their original unmodified angles while later ones undergo dimension‐specific scaling._The resulting interpolation preserves key high‑frequency slices where they matter most and respects early‐token importance highlighted by streaming LLM studies~[streamingllm], thereby enabling extensions up to **2048 k** tokens without catastrophic loss in language modeling performance._ 

Understanding this spectral behaviour—how each pair $(θ_i,n)$ contributes sinusoidal cycles at distinct scales—is essential before tackling advanced interpolation techniques such as those presented later in this book. It explains why naïve uniform scaling fails dramatically whereas carefully tailored non‑uniform rescaling succeeds dramatically even when extending contexts by several orders of magnitude._
