Chapter 3: Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) in Detail  

# Derivation of Eq.\,(1) – Cosine/Sine Pair per Dimension  

Transformer architectures require an explicit signal that tells the model where each token occurs in a sequence.  Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) injects this signal directly into the query and key vectors used by the self‑attention mechanism.  
For a token placed at absolute position index \(n\), RoPE constructs a vector whose entries are organized into \(\tfrac{d}{2}\) two‑dimensional “rotating planes”, one plane per rotary dimension.  On plane \(i\) (\(i=0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1\)) the coordinates are  

\[
\bigl[\cos(\!n\theta_{i}),\;\sin(\!n\theta_{i})\bigr] .
\]

Stacking these coordinates across all planes yields a \(d\)-dimensional embedding  

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\bigl[\,
\cos (n\theta_{0}),\, \sin (n\theta_{0}),\, 
\cos (n\theta_{1}),\, \sin (n\theta_{1}),\, 
\dots,\,
\cos \bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr),\, 
\sin \bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr)\,
\bigr] .
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) therefore consists of alternating cosine–sine pairs, one pair for every rotary dimension.  The construction relies only on arithmetic operations; no learned parameters are added beyond those already present in the model’s weight matrices.  Intuitively, as \(n\) grows each pair rotates around the unit circle at a rate determined by its associated angular frequency \(\theta_{i}\).  The collection of all pairs encodes the full absolute position \(n\) through a set of synchronized rotations spanning different speeds.



# Base Value \(\theta\) and Scaling Factor \(\beta=\theta^{2/d}\)  

The angular frequencies \(\{\theta_{i}\}\) are defined geometrically from a single scalar called the **base value**, denoted \(\theta\).  In contemporary LLMs such as LLaMA 2 and Mistral the default choice is  

\[
\theta = 10\,000 .
\]

The frequency for rotary dimension \(i\) follows an exponential decay  

\[
\theta_{i}= \theta^{-\,2 i / d} .
\tag{2}
\]

Because \(\theta_{i}\) appears multiplied by \(n\) inside every sine and cosine term,
it is convenient to rewrite Eq.\,(1) using a **scaling factor**  

\[
\beta = \theta^{\,2/d} .
\]

Substituting Eq.\,(2) into Eq.\,(1),

\[
n\, \theta_{i}= n\, \theta^{-\,2 i / d}
            = \frac{n}{(\,\theta^{\,2/d}\,)^{i}}
            = \frac{n}{\beta^{\,i}} .
\]

Hence each cosine–sine pair can be expressed compactly as  

\[
[\;\cos (n/\beta^{0}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{0}), 
   \cos (n/\beta^{1}),\, \sin (n/\beta^{1}),\, 
   \dots,\,
   \cos (n/\beta^{\,d/2-1}),\, 
   \sin (n/\beta^{\,d/2-1})]\!,
\]

which matches the unified formulation used when discussing positional interpolation later in the work.  
The factor \(\beta>1\); consequently higher rotary indices \(i\) produce larger denominators \(\beta^{\,i}\), thereby reducing the effective angular speed for those dimensions.



# Interpretation of High‑Frequency vs Low‑Frequency Dimensions  

Because \(\beta>1\), the term \(1/\beta^{\,i}\) decays exponentially with increasing \(i\).  
Consequently **low‑index dimensions** (\(i=0,1,\dotsc)\) rotate quickly: their angles change appreciably after only a few tokens.  We refer to these as **high‑frequency dimensions**.  They capture fine‑grained relationships among nearby tokens—small shifts in \(n\) lead to noticeable changes in both cosine and sine components, allowing the model to distinguish closely spaced positions.

Conversely, **high‑index dimensions** rotate slowly because division by a large power of \(\beta\) attenuates the angular increment per token.  These **low‑frequency dimensions** vary only over very long spans of text and therefore encode coarse‑grained positional cues that remain stable across hundreds or thousands of tokens.

Geometrically one may picture every rotary dimension as an independent two‑dimensional coordinate system anchored at the origin.  As we move along the sequence (\(n=0,1,2,\dotsc)\), each point spins around its own unit circle at its specific angular velocity
\( \omega_{i}=1/\beta^{\,i}.\)
Thus early planes spin rapidly while later planes turn sluggishly—a spectrum ranging from rapid local rotations to slow global sweeps.  

This frequency hierarchy explains several empirical observations reported later in LongRoPE:

* During positional interpolation it is beneficial to treat lower dimensions more gently because their high frequencies already furnish dense local information.
* Higher dimensions tolerate stronger scaling (larger rescale factors) without losing critical detail since their contribution mainly concerns distant relationships.
* By respecting this non‑uniformity—assigning distinct rescale factors \(\lambda_{i}\) per dimension—LongRoPE preserves essential information while extending context windows far beyond what uniform scaling can achieve.



# Why RoPE Is Preferred in Modern Large Language Models  

RoPE has become the de facto positional scheme in recent open‑source LLM families such as LLaMA 2~[llama2] and Mistral~[mistral]. Several properties make it especially attractive for models that operate at billion‑parameter scale:

* **Parameter‑free definition** – The embedding is generated on‐the‑fly from a single scalar base value (\(\theta=10^4\)).  No learned lookup tables are required, eliminating memory overhead proportional to maximum sequence length.
* **Seamless integration with self‑attention** – Because RoPE multiplies query/key vectors elementwise before taking their dot product, relative position information emerges automatically from inner products:
  
  \[
     q_n^\top k_m
     = (q_n^\text{raw})^\top R_{\!n}^\top R_{\!m} k_m^\text{raw},
  \]
  
  where \(R_{\!n}\) denotes the rotation matrix defined by Eq.\,(1).  
  The resulting term depends only on \(n-m,\) granting attention an intrinsic bias toward relative distance without introducing separate bias tensors.
* **Robustness under extrapolation** – Since RoPE’s functional form is analytic rather than discrete lookup values, extending beyond lengths observed during pretraining does not instantly produce “catastrophic” out‑of‑distribution activations.  Instead one can interpolate or extrapolate angles (e.g., Position Interpolation~[pi], NTK~[ntk], YaRN~[yarn]), preserving much of the original model behavior even when novel token indices appear.
* **Minimal architectural impact** – All modifications required for context‐window extension amount solely to adjusting rotor scaling factors (\(\lambda_{i}\)) or applying an optional threshold on early positions (\(\hat n.\)) The core transformer layers remain untouched, allowing existing hardware optimizations—such as FlashAttention or quantization pipelines—to stay applicable unchanged.
* **Facilitates efficient long‐context fine‑tuning** – As demonstrated by LongRoPE, once an appropriate set of rescale factors has been located via evolutionary search,
  
  * an initial extension up to \(32\times–64\times,\)
  * followed by modest fine‑tuning (\(<1000\times)\),
  
  yields models capable of handling more than two million tokens while still delivering competitive performance on tasks confined to traditional short windows.
* **Compatibility across architectures** – Any transformer model whose attention adopts rotary embeddings inherits these advantages without additional engineering effort; consequently many publicly released instruction-following models simply adopt RoPE out of convenience and proven efficacy.


In sum, Rotary Position Embedding supplies an elegant mathematical mechanism—rotating planes at exponentially decreasing frequencies—to endow large language models with precise positional awareness while retaining flexibility for extreme context extensions such as those realized by LongRoPE. Its deterministic nature, tight coupling with attention scores, and amenability to fine‐grained interpolation make it uniquely suited for modern LLM deployments spanning billions of parameters and multi‑million token contexts.

Long Context Windows for Large Language Models: Existing Strategies for Extending Context Windows  

# Linear Positional Interpolation (PI) – Uniform Rescaling  

Large language models rely on rotary position embeddings (RoPE) to inject order information into token representations. For a token located at position index $n$, its RoPE vector can be expressed as a sequence of cosine and sine components,
\[
\bigl[\cos (n\theta_{0}),\;\sin(n\theta_{0}),\;\cos(n\theta_{1}),\;\ldots,\;\cos\bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr),\;\sin\bigl(n\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr)\bigr],
\]
where $d$ denotes the hidden dimension of the model and $\theta_{i}= \theta^{-2i/d}$ controls the rotation frequency of the $i^{\text{th}}$ pair of dimensions. In most pretrained models $\theta$ is set to $10\,000$, yielding a dense sampling of angles over the original context window length $L$ (e.g., $L=4096$ tokens for LLaMA‑2).  

When one wishes to increase the maximal sequence length from $L$ to a larger value $L'$ one must decide how to map newly introduced positions $n>L$ back into an angle range that was never observed during pre‑training. Position interpolation (PI) adopts the simplest possible mapping: it rescales every rotary angle by a single scalar factor that equals the extension ratio,
\[
s=\frac{L'}{L}.
\]
Concretely, each cosine–sine pair is recomputed as
\[
\bigl[\cos \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{s}\theta_{0}\bigr),\;\sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{s}\theta_{0}\bigr),\;\ldots,\;\sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{s}\theta_{\frac{d}{2}-1}\bigr)\bigr].
\]
If we write $\beta=\theta^{2/d}$ and denote the actual rescale factor by $\lambda$, we can write this uniformly as  
\[
\operatorname{RoPE}_{n}
=
\bigl[\cos \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{0}}\bigr),\, 
      \sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{0}}\bigr),\, 
      \dots,\,
      \sin \!\bigl(\tfrac{n}{\lambda(\beta)^{d/2-1}}\bigr)\bigr],
\]
with $\lambda=s$ for every dimension when using PI.  

The intuitive appeal of PI lies in its perfect symmetry: every dimension is treated identically and all new token positions are simply compressed into a shorter effective range. However, this uniform compression has a severe side effect when $s$ becomes large. By shrinking all angles by the same factor we “crowd” many distinct token positions into nearly identical angular values. Because attention heads compute inner products of queries and keys that are modulated by these rotated vectors, two tokens that are far apart in absolute position may become indistinguishable after compression. Empirically this manifests as a rapid increase in language‑model perplexity once the extension ratio exceeds roughly $2\times$. In other words, while PI can technically generate embeddings for arbitrarily long sequences, its overly aggressive down‑scaling quickly renders the model incapable of separating neighboring tokens, which explains why prior works report performance degradation well before reaching hundreds of thousands of tokens.

# Neural Tangent Kernel Theory for Non‑Uniform Scaling  

The shortcomings of uniform interpolation prompted researchers to examine RoPE through the lens of information theory and function approximation. The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework provides a principled way to understand how an infinite‑width transformer encodes sinusoidal signals across its layers. Within this theory, each RoPE dimension contributes a specific frequency component to the overall representation; higher‑frequency dimensions capture fine‑grained positional differences while lower‑frequency dimensions encode coarse global structure.  

If we view the mapping from position index $n$ to angular phase as a Fourier basis, then applying a naïve uniform compression corresponds to targeting all frequencies equally—a strategy that harms high‑frequency components disproportionately because they become even higher‑frequency after division by $s$. NTK‑based methods therefore propose a dimension‑wise rescaling,
\[
\lambda_{i}=s^{\,i},
\]
where $i=0,\dots,\tfrac{d}{2}-1$. This formula yields small rescale factors for low‐index (“high‑frequency”) dimensions and larger factors for high‐index (“low‑frequency”) ones. The net effect is twofold: 

1. **Interpolation** is applied more gently in those dimensions that originally carried delicate positional signals, preserving their ability to distinguish nearby tokens.
2. **Extrapolation** occurs in low‑frequency dimensions where compressing angles would otherwise discard much of the global ordering information; expanding those angles counteracts crowding at long distances.

Empirically this non‑uniform scheme reduces perplexity compared with uniform PI at moderate extension ratios (up to roughly $4\times$). Moreover it yields an initialization that is more amenable to later fine‑tuning because fewer “catastrophic” angles are introduced—only those dimensions that require aggressive scaling receive it. Nevertheless, even NTK‑aware interpolations encounter limitations when pushing beyond tens of thousands of tokens. The theoretical justification presumes smooth variation across frequencies but does not account for additional irregularities observed in real transformer architectures, such as varying information entropy across specific heads or layers. Consequently performance still begins to deteriorate noticeably once extensions approach or exceed $8\times$, indicating that further non‑uniformities must be explored.

# YaRN’s Three‑Group Frequency Partitioning  

Building upon NTK insights while aiming for greater practicality, YaRN proposes a three‑group partitioning scheme over RoPE dimensions based on their base frequencies. The algorithm first computes a frequency threshold that divides all $\tfrac{d}{2}$ dimension pairs into:

* **High‑frequency group** – containing the smallest rotation periods;
* **Mid‑frequency group** – occupying an intermediate band;
* **Low‑frequency group** – comprising dimensions with large rotation periods.

Each group receives a distinct treatment:

* High‑frequency dimensions are left untouched ($\lambda=1$), effectively performing *extrapolation* rather than any compression.
* Low‑frequency dimensions undergo *linear* interpolation identical to PI ($\lambda=s$).
* Mid‑frequency dimensions adopt an *NTK-like* scaling where $\lambda_{i}$ grows gradually between $1$ and $s$, thereby distributing interpolation pressure across this band.

The intuition behind this design is straightforward: if one believes that only certain ranges of frequencies truly suffer from crowding under uniform compression, then protecting those ranges while applying softer modifications elsewhere should yield better overall fidelity. By manually assigning each dimension a static rule based on its frequency band, YaRN indeed improves perplexity over plain PI at extensions up to roughly $8\times$.  

However, there are two important drawbacks rooted in its design choices:

1. **Human‐crafted thresholds** – The boundaries separating high-, mid-, and low-frequency groups are derived from empirical heuristics rather than data‐driven optimization. Different model families (e.g., LLaMA vs Mistral) may exhibit distinct internal distributions of information across RoPE dimensions; using a one‑size‐fits‑all cut may therefore be suboptimal.
2. **Lack of positionwise adaptation** – YaRN’s groups apply globally across every token position irrespective of where that token appears within the sequence. Recent analysis shows that initial positions often play an outsized role in attention calculations because early keys receive higher cumulative attention weights (as observed in streaming LLM variants). A uniform treatment across all positions thus discards an opportunity to preserve original angles specifically for early tokens—a fact later leveraged by LongRoPE’s progressive strategy.

These limitations partially explain why YaRN’s perplexity curves tend to flatten then rise sharply once extensions surpass roughly $12\,k$ tokens on datasets such as PG19—a setting where many curated contexts exceed typical short contexts used during pretraining.

# Empirical Shortcomings: Crowded Token Positions and Information Loss  

All three strategies described above share a common empirical vulnerability: when extending beyond modest multiples of the native context length ($2\times$–$8\times$, i.e., up to around $128\,000$ tokens), they either create “crowded” angular spaces or discard essential positional information altogether. In practice this translates into two observable phenomena:

* **Catastrophic new position values** – By introducing large numbers of previously unseen position indices ($>90$\% new when extending from 4 k to more than 1 M tokens), models encounter angles far outside their training distribution unless careful rescales are applied.
* **Attention diffusion** – As attention scores are spread thinly over millions of positions, each individual query–key interaction becomes weaker relative to its contribution within shorter contexts; if positional encodings do not preserve sufficient discriminative variation this diffusion leads directly to higher next‑token prediction loss.
* **Perplexity spikes** – Quantitatively measured perplexities on long-form benchmarks such as PG19 and Proof-pile show abrupt increases at specific lengths: PI typically degrades after about 8 k tokens; NTK remains stable up through roughly 16 k but then rises sharply; YaRN can handle up through 32 k under optimal grouping but often fails dramatically beyond 64 k when extrapolation limits are exceeded.

Furthermore, fine–tuning attempts aimed at mitigating these effects become increasingly expensive as target lengths grow past several hundred thousand tokens because suitable training corpora become scarce and computational costs balloon dramatically. Hence existing methods plateau at around $128\,000$ tokens—not due merely to algorithmic complexity but because their underlying assumptions about uniform frequency scaling or static grouping cannot capture two salient forms of non-uniformity identified later: variable importance across RoPE dimensions *and* differential sensitivity among token positions within a sequence.

In summary, linear positional interpolation provides an easy baseline but suffers from severe crowding under large extensions; NTK theory introduces principled nonuniform scaling yet still fails when extensions become extreme; finally YaRN refines this idea through hand‑crafted grouping but remains limited by its static nature and reliance on heuristic partitions. Understanding these empirical bottlenecks sets the stage for more sophisticated approaches—such as LongRoPE—that explicitly search for dimension‐wise rescaling factors while also allowing early‐token positions to retain their original RoPE angles, thereby unlocking context windows far beyond two million tokens without prohibitive fine–tuning costs.
