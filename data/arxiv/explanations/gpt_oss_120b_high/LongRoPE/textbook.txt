\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

**Foundations of Large Language Models and Their Context Windows**

# Overview of Transformer‑Based Large Language Models  

Large Language Models (LLMs) are neural networks trained on massive text corpora with the objective of predicting the next token given all preceding ones. Modern LLMs adopt the **Transformer** architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}, whose core operation is **self‑attention**. In self‑attention each token produces a *query* vector $q$, a *key* vector $k$, and a *value* vector $v$ through learned linear projections. The attention score between two positions $i$ and $j$ is obtained by a dot product $q_i^\top k_j$, followed by a softmax over all $j$ that lie inside a fixed-size segment called the **context window**. The resulting weighted sum of values creates a new representation for token $i$, allowing information from any other token within that segment to influence it directly.

Because self‑attention treats every pair of positions symmetrically, it cannot infer order unless an explicit notion of position is supplied. Consequently every Transformer Layer adds **positional information** either through absolute embeddings added to token vectors or through **relative encodings** incorporated into attention computations. A widely adopted relative scheme in recent LLMs is **Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)** \cite{rope}. RoPE encodes the index $n$ of a token into sinusoidal rotation angles whose frequencies decay geometrically across dimensions:

\[
\underbrace{\bigl[\cos(n\theta_0), \sin(n\theta_0), \cos(n\theta_1), \dots ,
\cos(n\theta_{d/2-1}), \sin(n\theta_{d/2-1})\bigr]}_{\text{Eq.~\ref{eq:rope}}}
\tag{1}
\]

where $d$ is the hidden dimension, $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ defines frequency $\theta_i$, and $\theta$ defaults to $10\,000$. By rotating queries and keys according to their positions, RoPE makes attention inherently aware of relative distances while preserving computational efficiency—no extra parameters are introduced beyond those already present in standard Transformers \cite{longrope}.


# Definition and Role of the Context Window  

The **context window** $L$ specifies how many tokens can be processed jointly by the model during one forward pass. Formally, an input sequence $\mathbf{x}= (x_1,\dots ,x_{|x|})$ must satisfy $|x|\le L$ before it can be fed into the Transformer stack. This limitation originates from two intertwined reasons:

1. **Positional Indexing** – RoPE defines rotation angles only for integer positions $n \in \{0,\dots ,L-1\}$ that were seen during pre‑training. When a longer sequence requires indices $n \ge L$, the model would encounter *untrained* position values, leading to “catastrophic” out‑of‑distribution behavior \cite{pi}.  
2. **Computational Constraints** – Self‑attention scales quadratically with $L$ ($O(L^2)$ operations). Even with efficient kernels, hardware memory caps how many tokens can be materialized simultaneously during training or inference.

The practical consequence is that an LLM’s ability to reason over textual relationships is bounded by its current $L$. For example, when answering questions that require recalling facts introduced several thousand tokens earlier, an LLM whose $L=4096$ may completely lose access once those facts fall outside its active window.


# Typical Context Sizes in Public Models  

The community has converged on **a few thousand tokens** as the default capability for most open‑source checkpoints:

* **LLaMA 2** possesses a hard limit at **4096 tokens** \cite{llama2}.  
* Other contemporary families such as **GPT‑4**, **Mistral**, and various Code‑LLM releases also adopt windows ranging roughly between $4\,000$–$8\,000$ tokens unless explicitly enlarged during post‑training modifications.

Research focusing on *context extension* demonstrates that naïve scaling quickly reaches diminishing returns:

* Linear interpolation (“PI”) \cite{pi} can push an original $L=4096$ up modestly but suffers severe degradation when trying ratios larger than $2\times$.  
* More sophisticated schemes based on the Neural Tangent Kernel (“NTK”) \cite{ntk,dynamicntk} or frequency‑grouping (“YaRN”) \cite{yarn} stretch windows further yet still plateau near **128 k** tokens after costly fine‑tuning \cite{longlora,pi,yarn,zhang2024soaring,liu2023scaling}.

Thus while conventional releases sit at a few kilobytes, dedicated engineering efforts have managed extensions into the **tens–hundreds of kilotokens**, revealing both feasibility and remaining bottlenecks.


# Impact of Limited Context on Applications  

A constrained window introduces systematic failures across diverse downstream scenarios:

* **In‑Context Learning with Many Demonstrations** – Algorithms that embed dozens of examples within the prompt demand space far exceeding $4\,000$ tokens ~\cite{Huang2023FewerIM}. When demonstrations collapse outside the active window, model performance drops sharply.
* **LLM Agents & Autoregressive Planning** – Agents that maintain iterative reasoning traces or tool‑use logs quickly exceed native capacities ~\cite{park2023generative,madaan2023selfrefine}, causing loss of earlier plan steps.
* **Long Document Summarization / Retrieval** – Summaries must ingest entire books or legal contracts ($>100\,000$ tokens). If only their first segment fits inside $L$, critical later passages never influence generation.
* **Synthetic Retrieval Tasks** – The “passkey retrieval” benchmark places a hidden five‑digit code somewhere deep inside a long text ~\cite{passkey}. Baselines employing PI/NTK/YaRN see accuracy fall to zero once evaluation reaches $\approx128\,{\rm k}$ tokens because they cannot locate distant clues.
* **Attention Dispersion Across Vast Tokens** – As highlighted by Pi et al., spreading attention thinly over hundreds of thousands of positions dilutes signal strength for short‐range dependencies that dominate natural language understanding ~\cite{pi}.

Collectively these observations make it clear that current windows restrict both *breadth* (amount of text visible) and *depth* (quality of reasoning even within visible range).


# Motivation for Extending Beyond Standard Limits  

The desire to surpass existing caps is motivated by three concrete challenges identified in recent literature:

1. **Catastrophic New Position Values** – Introducing millions of fresh indices yields severe out‑of‑distribution effects because RoPE angles $\frac{n}{\beta^i}$ become far outside their training distribution ~\cite{pi}. Simple uniform rescaling therefore harms convergence during any subsequent fine‑tuning phase.
2. **Data Scarcity & Training Cost at Extreme Lengths** – Texts longer than $1\,000\,{\rm k}$ tokens are rare ~(the “long‐text scarcity” problem). Training directly on such sequences would demand prohibitive GPU hours due both to quadratic attention costs and massive batch sizes required for stable optimization.
3. **Attention Crowding Degrades Short‑Context Performance** – When extending indiscriminately—e.g., applying a single scaling factor $\lambda=s$ across all RoPE dimensions—the encoded angles become densely packed within their original interval ~(Fig.~1). This “crowding” reduces discriminability among nearby positions and leads to noticeable perplexity spikes even at original lengths < $L$ ~\cite{pi}.

Addressing these challenges calls for *non‑uniform* handling both across RoPE dimensions (low frequencies vs.\ high frequencies) and across token positions (the first few hundred tokens receive disproportionately large attention). The proposed solution—LongRoPE—leverages an evolutionary search to discover dimension‑wise rescaling factors $\lambda_i$ together with a threshold $\hat n$ that leaves early positions untouched (Eq.~\ref{eq:problem}). By doing so it achieves two crucial outcomes:

* Provides a well‑behaved initialization that dramatically reduces information loss before any fine‑tuning.
* Enables an eightfold extension without any additional gradient updates (\(4\,{\rm k}\rightarrow32\,{\rm k}\)), thereby bridging part of the gap between cheap engineering tricks and expensive full‑scale training.

These insights motivate researchers now to pursue *progressive extension*: first grow modestly to \(256\,{\rm k}\) using modest fine‑tuning budget (\(~1{k}\) steps), then invoke another non‑uniform interpolation step that lifts the model all the way past **2048 k** tokens—a scale previously unattainable without massive computational investment.

In summary, understanding how Transformers encode position—and why their default windows become bottlenecks—sets the stage for advanced techniques such as LongRoPE which systematically exploit hidden non‑uniformities within RoPE. The following chapters will delve into those technical contributions, presenting evolutionary search strategies, progressive fine‑tuning pipelines, and empirical evaluations demonstrating near‑original performance even at two million token contexts.

Positional Information in Transformers  

# Why self‑attention needs positional cues  

Self‑attention computes pairwise interactions between all token representations independently of their order in the input sequence. In mathematical terms, given token vectors $\mathbf{x}_0,\dots,\mathbf{x}_{L-1}$, multi‑head attention forms queries $\mathbf{q}_i$, keys $\mathbf{k}_j$, and values $\mathbf{v}_j$, and then evaluates attention scores through inner products $\mathbf{q}_i^\top \mathbf{k}_j$. Because inner products are invariant under any permutation of the indices $i$ and $j$, a vanilla self‑attention layer cannot distinguish whether a sentence reads “the cat chased the mouse” or “the mouse chased the cat”. Consequently, without an external signal indicating where each token occurs, a Transformer would treat any reordering of tokens as identical, leading inevitably to ambiguity and poor language modeling performance.  

The necessity of explicit order information becomes especially apparent when a model processes very long documents: once it receives more tokens than it was ever exposed to during pre‑training, many token positions have never been seen before (“untrained new position indices”), causing out‑of‑distribution behavior and a rapid decline in downstream task accuracy – a phenomenon repeatedly observed across large language models (LLMs) whose context windows are limited (e.g., LLaMA 2’s 4096‑token window). Hence every Transformer requires an auxiliary mechanism that injects positional cues into its token representations before self‑attention is applied.  

# Absolute learned positional vectors  

The earliest remedy adopted by Transformer architectures augments each token embedding with a *learned* vector that depends solely on its absolute index $n$. Formally, one introduces a table of parameters $\{\mathbf{p}_0,\mathbf{p}_1,\dots,\mathbf{p}_{L_{\max}-1}\}$ where $\mathbf{p}_n\in\mathbb R^{d}$ ($d$ being the hidden dimension). For an input token at position $n$, its hidden representation becomes  
$$\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_n = \mathbf{x}_n + \mathbf{p}_n.$$  
During pre‑training these vectors are optimized jointly with all other model weights so that the network learns how information should be arranged along a fixed maximum length $L_{\max}$. This approach works well when the maximum length matches the range encountered during training (e.g., 4 k tokens). However, when one wishes to evaluate at a longer length $L'>L_{\max}$, there simply exist no entries $\mathbf{p}_{L_{\max}},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{L'-1}$; naive extrapolation would assign arbitrary values or repeat existing ones, creating “catastrophic values’’ that break attention patterns. Consequently pure absolute learned embeddings cap extensibility unless paired with costly fine‑tuning on longer sequences – an expensive operation both because very long texts are scarce and because GPU memory requirements grow quadratically with sequence length.  

# Sinusoidal positional embeddings  

A complementary family of embeddings replaces discrete learnable parameters with deterministic functions of $n$. The classic sinusoidal scheme defines each component $i$ of a position vector by two sinusoids whose frequencies span several octaves:
$$
p_{n}^{(2i)} = \sin\!\bigl(n\,\theta_i\bigr), 
\qquad 
p_{n}^{(2i+1)} = \cos\!\bigl(n\,\theta_i\bigr),
$$
where the angular frequency satisfies  
$$\theta_i = \theta^{-\,2i/d}.$$  
Here $\theta$ denotes a base constant (typically set to $10^4$); higher dimensions correspond to higher frequencies while lower dimensions encode slowly varying patterns. Because sinusoidal functions are defined analytically for any real argument $n$, this scheme naturally extends beyond any pre‑training horizon without introducing undefined entries – one simply evaluates the same formulas for larger $n$. Moreover, inner products between rotated query–key pairs retain a simple dependence on **relative** distances $(m-n)$ rather than on absolute indices—a property exploited by rotary-based techniques discussed next. Nevertheless, despite this theoretical ability to extrapolate arbitrarily far, naïve use of sinusoidal embeddings suffers from *crowding*: when a sequence becomes much longer than it was trained on, many tokens acquire nearly identical angle values especially in high‑frequency dimensions, making it difficult for attention heads to discriminate nearby tokens. This phenomenon motivates more sophisticated interpolation strategies when extending context windows drastically.  

# Relative position representations  

An alternative line of research sidesteps absolute coordinates entirely by letting attention heads receive explicit *relative* distance information between two tokens $i$ and $j$. Instead of adding an absolute vector $\mathbf p_n$, one introduces bias terms or auxiliary matrices that depend on $(j-i)$:
$$
e_{ij}= f(j-i) ,
$$ 
where $f(\cdot)$ can be a learned embedding table or a parameterized function. When incorporated into attention calculation,
$$
\alpha_{ij}= \frac{\exp(\mathbf q_i^\top \mathbf k_j + e_{ij})}{\sum_{k}\exp(\mathbf q_i^\top \mathbf k_k + e_{ik})},
$$ 
the model directly perceives how far apart two words lie regardless of their global location inside the sequence. Relative mechanisms have demonstrated better generalization across lengths because they do not require explicit entries for unseen absolute indices; nevertheless they usually demand architectural modifications—additional bias addition steps or extra computation per attention head—which complicates deployment in existing LLM pipelines that heavily rely on fast matrix multiplications optimized for standard absolute embeddings. Consequently many production systems retain simpler additive schemas despite their limitations when faced with extreme context lengths exceeding millions of tokens.  

# Transition towards rotational encodings  

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) unifies several advantages of sinusoidal and relative schemes while preserving computational simplicity identical to vanilla Transformers – no extra bias terms nor auxiliary networks are required after initialization. For token at index $n$, RoPE represents its position through pairs of cosine–sine terms:
$$
[\;\cos(n\,\theta_0),\,\sin(n\,\theta_0),\, 
   \cos(n\,\theta_1),\,\sin(n\,\theta_1),\, 
   \dots,
   \cos(n\,\theta_{d/2-1}),\,\sin(n\,\theta_{d/2-1})]\!.   \tag{1}
$$
Each dimension $i$ rotates query and key vectors by an angle proportional to $n\,\theta_i$, where again $\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}$ shares exactly the same frequency schedule introduced for sinusoidal embeddings (\S Sinusoidal Positional Embeddings). Crucially, after applying these rotations (“twisting” queries and keys) before dot‑product computation,
$$
(\operatorname{Rot}_{n}\,\mathbf q)\!^{\!\top}\!(\operatorname{Rot}_{m}\,\mathbf k)
=
    \mathbf q^{\!\top}
    R(\theta)^{\,m-n}
    \mathbf k,
$$
the resulting scalar depends **only** on their relative offset $(m-n)$ via an orthogonal matrix power $R(\theta)^{m-n}$. In other words, RoPE automatically confers *relative* awareness while remaining expressed entirely through an additive modification of token embeddings—no extra parameters or special attention masks are required during inference.

Because RoPE’s angles grow linearly with index $n$, extending a model’s context window forces us to evaluate cosine/sine at indices far larger than those encountered during pre‑training (~4 k). Directly feeding such large arguments yields “catastrophic values’’ —the model’s internal statistics have never seen those angles’ distributional regime, leading again to performance collapse when processing extremely long texts (> 128 k tokens). To mitigate this mismatch researchers have proposed *positional interpolation*: rescaling every angle by a factor $\lambda$ so that effective arguments stay within a familiar range:
$$
[\;\cos\tfrac{n}{\lambda},\, 
   \sin\tfrac{n}{\lambda},\, 
   \dots]\!.   \tag{2}
$$  
When extending by an overall ratio $s=L'/L$, simple linear interpolation adopts $\lambda=s$, shrinking all angles uniformly (“PI”). More nuanced ways distribute compression differently across dimensions; NTK‑based techniques set $\lambda=s^{\,i}$ so low‑frequency dimensions endure stronger compression while high‑frequency ones change little (“extrapolation” versus “interpolation”). YaRN further groups dimensions into three frequency bands—high frequencies unchanged ($\lambda=1$), middle frequencies follow NTK scaling, low frequencies undergo linear compression—yet determines group boundaries manually via empirical inspection.

Empirical analyses reveal two overlooked *non‑uniformities* governing how best to compress angles:

1️⃣ **Dimension‐wise non‑uniformity** – Lower‑frequency dimensions tolerate larger compression factors because they change slowly over long spans; conversely high‐frequency dimensions should remain nearly untouched ($\lambda\simeq 1$) lest nearby tokens become indistinguishable.
2️⃣ **Position‐wise non‑uniformity** – Tokens near the start of a sequence receive disproportionately high attention scores during early layers (“initial tokens”), making precise positioning more critical there than farther downstream positions.

By jointly optimizing per‑dimension scalars $\lambda_i$ together with an initial‑token threshold $\hat n$ (so that for all $n<\hat n$, no compression occurs), one can preserve key angular information while still stretching sequences far beyond their original limits—a finding central to the LongRoPE method introduced later in this work. Evolutionary search across this enormous space identifies rescale configurations satisfying monotonicity constraints ($\lambda_0 \leqslant \lambda_1 \leqslant\dots$), reflecting theoretical expectations derived from Neural Tangent Kernel analyses which predict smoother mapping for higher‐frequency components.

These insights explain why modern LLM extensions now favour *rotary* techniques equipped with sophisticated interpolation instead of raw additive absolute tables or handcrafted relative biases: rotary encodings inherently support scalable relative reasoning; refined non‑uniform rescaling mitigates crowding effects; and all adjustments remain confined within existing classically fast matrix multiplications—making them compatible with large production deployments while still enabling unprecedented context lengths up to two million tokens once coupled with progressive fine‑tuning strategies such as those embodied by LongRoPE.

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE): Theory and Mechanics  
===================================================================  

In modern large language models (LLMs) such as LLaMA 2 and Mistral, the order of tokens is communicated through a *rotary* positional encoding rather than an ordinary additive sinusoid or learned absolute table.  Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) modifies the inner‑product computation performed by self‑attention so that positional information becomes an intrinsic part of every query–key interaction.  This chapter presents a detailed derivation of RoPE from first principles, explains how its frequency spectrum is governed by a single base constant $\theta$, shows how the rotated embeddings are fused into the attention matrix, and finally discusses why this design enjoys practical benefits over traditional encodings—especially when it comes to extending context windows far beyond those seen during pre‑training, as demonstrated by the LongRoPE method.  

-------------------------------------------------------------------  
## Formal definition of RoPE angles  

Consider a Transformer layer whose hidden size is $d$.  For each token at discrete position $n\in\{0,\dots,L\!-\!1\}$ we construct a $d$‑dimensional positional vector by alternating cosine and sine terms:

\[
\underbrace{\bigl[\,\cos (n\theta_0),\, \sin (n\theta_0),\, 
\cos (n\theta_1),\, \sin (n\theta_1),\,\dots,\,
\cos (n\theta_{d/2-1}),\, \sin (n\theta_{d/2-1}) \bigr]}_{\text{RoPE}(n)} .
\tag{1}
\]

The scalar $\theta_i$ denotes a *rotation frequency* specific to the $i$‑th pair of dimensions.  Following the original rotary formulation \cite{rope}, these frequencies follow a geometric progression controlled by a single base constant $\theta$:

\[
\theta_i \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\;\theta^{-\,2i/d},
\qquad 
\text{with default }\theta = 10\,000 .
\tag{2}
\]

Thus lower indices $i$ correspond to **high‑frequency** components (larger $\theta_i$) while higher indices represent **low‑frequency** components (smaller $\theta_i$).  Equation (1) captures every token’s absolute location solely through trigonometric modulation; no learnable table is required.  

When extending an existing model’s context window from its original length $L$ to a larger target length $L'$, we denote the *extension ratio* by  

\[
s \;\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\;\frac{L'}{L}.
\tag{3}
\]

All existing extensions manipulate Eq.\,(1) by scaling the argument $n$ before feeding it into the sine/cosine functions—a process commonly called *positional interpolation*.  In its most general form there exists a per‑dimension **rescale factor** $\lambda_i$ such that

\[
\mathrm{RoPE}_{\,\lambda}(n)_i
=
\bigl[\cos(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i}\,\beta^{-i}),~ 
       \sin(\tfrac{n}{\lambda_i}\,\beta^{-i}) \bigr],
\qquad
\beta=\theta^{2/d},
\tag{4}
\]

which reproduces Eq.\,(1) when every $\lambda_i=1$.  The family of methods discussed later—linear interpolation (PI), NTK‑based scaling, YaRN’s grouped scaling, and finally LongRoPE’s learned non‑uniform scaling—all amount to different choices of $\lambda_i$.  



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
## Derivation using complex numbers  

Equation (1) naturally groups every two consecutive entries into a **complex number**:

```math
c_{i}(n)= \cos(n \theta_i)+j\,\sin(n \theta_i)= e^{j\, n \theta_i},
```

where $j=\sqrt{-1}$ denotes the imaginary unit.  The full $d$‑dimensional embedding can therefore be seen as concatenating $d/2$ independent complex rotors

\[
C(n)= \bigl[ e^{j\, n \theta_0}, ~ e^{j\, n \theta_1}, ~ \dots ,~
e^{j\, n \theta_{d/2-1}} \bigr].
\tag{5}
\]

Let $x_n\in \mathbb R^{d}$ be the token’s raw hidden vector prior to any positional operation,
and let us split it analogously into $d/2$ complex components
$X_{i}(n)=a_{i}(n)+jb_{i}(n)$,
where $(a_{i},b_{i})$ are precisely the two real coordinates associated with head dimension~$i$.   
Applying Eq.\,(5) amounts mathematically to multiplying each component pointwise:

```math
X'_{i}(n) = X_{i}(n)\,\cdot e^{j\, n \theta_i}.
```

Because multiplication by a unit‑magnitude complex exponential simply **rotates** a vector in its own two‑dimensional plane, RoPE adds an angle proportional to both position $n$ and frequency $\theta_i$.  

Now consider two tokens at positions $p$ and $q$. Their rotated representations become  

```math
X'_{i}(p)=X_{i}(p)\,e^{j p \theta_i},
     ~~   X'_{i}(q)=X_{i}(q)\,e^{j q \theta_i}.
``` 

When computing their inner product inside self‑attention,
the phase factors combine as  

```math
X'_{i}(p)\,\overline{X'_{i}(q)}
   = X_{i}(p)\,\overline{X_{i}(q)}\,e^{j(p-q)\theta_i},
```

where $\overline{\cdot}$ denotes complex conjugation.
Consequently **the only residual dependence on absolute positions** is through their **difference** $(p-q)$ multiplied by $\theta_i$.  
Summing over all dimensions yields exactly  

```math
Q_p^\top K_q 
   = \sum_{i=0}^{d/2-1}
     {\bigl(a_{i}(p)a_{i}(q)+b_{i}(p)b_{i}(q)\bigr)} 
     \cos((p-q)\,\theta_i)
   + j\times (\dots),
```

which shows that RoPE turns an ordinary dot product into one whose magnitude already carries relative positional bias.  
Thus there is no need for an auxiliary bias table—instead rotational geometry supplies what would otherwise have been added explicitly.


-------------------------------------------------------------------  
## Frequency spectrum controlled by base $\boldsymbol{\theta}$  

The set $\{\theta_i=\theta^{-2 i/d}\}_{i=0}^{d/2-1}$ determines how quickly angular phases change across token positions.  
From Eq.\,(2) one sees that **the single scalar $\theta$ governs an exponential ladder**:

* **Large $\boldsymbol{\theta}$** → every $\theta_i$ becomes small → rotations progress slowly → low frequencies dominate → longer “wavelengths” allow discrimination of far‑apart tokens without rapidly wrapping around.
* **Small $\boldsymbol{\theta}$** → frequencies become high → rapid oscillations → useful for distinguishing nearby tokens but quickly become ambiguous for distant positions due to periodicity (“aliasing”).

Because both training data and inference workloads typically contain diverse distances between relevant tokens, an LLM benefits from simultaneously preserving high frequencies (for fine granularity near the beginning of a sequence) and low frequencies (for coarse alignment far away).  The geometric progression encoded by Eq.\,(2) accomplishes exactly this balance without hand‑crafted tuning.  

When extending context windows far beyond those seen during pre‑training—as LongRoPE does—the effective “range” of permissible positions grows dramatically.  Simple linear interpolation would uniformly shrink all angular arguments by $s$, i.e., replace $n$ with $n/s$, which forces all frequencies onto an overly cramped segment of their cycles (“crowded” positions).  By contrast, allowing **dimension‑specific rescaling** $\lambda_i$ lets higher‑frequency dimensions receive *less* compression while lower‑frequency ones absorb more—mirroring exactly what Eq.\,(2) already prescribes but now adapting dynamically according to an extension ratio $s$.  

LongRoPE’s evolutionary search discovers optimal $\lambda_i$ values such that  

```math
4~{\leq}\lambda_0 < \lambda_1 < \dots < 
   \lambda_{\frac d 2 - 1}\le s\times 1.25,
``` 

a monotonic schedule inspired by NTK theory (\cite{ntk}).  
Empirically Section 3 demonstrates that respecting this non‑uniform spectrum improves perplexity at long lengths dramatically compared with naïve uniform compression.


-------------------------------------------------------------------  
## Integration with the attention matrix  

Self‑attention computes scores between every query–key pair:

```math
A(p,q)=
    \frac{
        Q_p^\top K_q 
    }%
    {\sqrt d},
```

followed by softmax normalization across all keys~$q$.  
In vanilla Transformers without rotary encoding one would add an *absolute* sinusoidal vector or learnable bias after linear projection:

```math
Q_p = x_pW_Q + e(p), 
    ~~K_q = x_qW_K + e(q).
```

With RoPE we instead **rotate** each projected vector before performing the inner product:
let  

```math
Q'_p = R(p)\,[x_pW_Q], 
~~~~K'_q = R(q)\,[x_qW_K],
```

where \(R(n)\in {\mathbb R}^{d\times d}\) implements block–diagonal rotations determined exactly by Eq.\,(5).   
Because \(R(p)\) consists solely of cosines and sines arranged in $2\times2$ blocks

```math
R(p)_{{(2k-1):(2k)}}=
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \cos(p\!\times!\!\beta^{-k}) & -\,\sin(p\times!\beta^{-k})\\[4pt]
        \sin(p\times!\beta^{-k}) & ~~\,   \cos(p\times!\beta^{-k})
    \end{pmatrix},
```

the attention score becomes

```math
A(p,q)= 
    \frac{
        [x_pW_Q]^\top R(p)^\top R(q)[x_qW_K]
    }{\sqrt d}
    =
    \frac{
        [x_pW_Q]^\top R(q-p)[x_qW_K]
    }{\sqrt d}.
```

The crucial observation is that \(R(p)^\top R(q)=R(q-p)\); thus **the relative displacement $(q-p)$ alone determines the rotational offset** appearing inside every dot product.
Consequently roaming through distant positions does not increase computational cost nor parameter count—only an extra trigonometric evaluation per head dimension is required—and there is no need for learned relative‐position tables.

When extending contexts beyond what was originally trained on, one merely changes how much angles are scaled for large indices:
the rescaling function embedded in Eq.\,(4),

```math
I(\hat{\lambda}_i,\hat n)
=
   {\begin{cases}
       1                 & n<\hat n\\[4pt]
       {1}/{\lambda_i}   & n'\geqslant {\hat n}
     }\end{cases}},
```

decides whether token `$n`’s angle should keep its pretrained value (`$\hat n` leading tokens`) or undergo compression (`$\lambda_i` applied afterward).  
During inference LongRoPE evaluates three regimes—original short window (< 8 k tokens), intermediate extensions (< 256 k tokens), and ultra‑long runs (< 2048 k tokens)—by swapping among pre‑computed $\{\lambda_i,\hat n\}` sets without altering model weights.

## Practical advantages over traditional encodings  

Traditional position representations fall into two families:

| Style | Mechanism | Typical drawbacks |
|------|-----------|------------------|
| Absolute sinusoid addendum | Add vectors $e(n)$ elementwise after projection | Requires separate lookup or recomputation for every new token index; never extrapolates gracefully beyond seen indices |
| Learnable absolute table | Store one embedding per index up to some maximum | Memory scales linearly with max length; new indices introduce *catastrophic values* because they have never been observed during training (\cite{pi}) |

Rotary embeddings avoid both issues because they are defined analytically rather than stored discretely.  The consequences documented throughout this work include:

* **Minor architectural changes:** Extending LLaMA 2 or Mistral merely means substituting a tiny set of scaling factors (`$\lambda_i$, `$\hat n`); all weight matrices stay untouched.
* **Low fine‑tuning cost:** Since only positional parameters move during adaptation, LongRoPE needs merely ~\(10^3\) gradient steps on modestly long sequences (~256 k tokens), whereas naïve fine‑tuning for > 1000 k tokens would demand prohibitive GPU hours (\cite{longlora}, Sec.~1).
* **Robustness to sparsely available ultra‑long data:** Because LongRoPE first learns good initializations through non‑uniform interpolation before any data exposure, it circumvents “data scarcity” at multi‑million token lengths—a primary obstacle highlighted in Sec.~1.
* **Better preservation of original performance:** Section 4 reports only marginal perplexity increase on short sequences after extending up to 2048 k tokens; this contrasts sharply with PI or Nyquist‐limited NTK methods whose performance collapses once more than twice the original length is requested.
* **Scalable extrapolation:** By allowing different $\lambda_i$ per dimension—as confirmed empirically in Finding 1 (§3)—LongRoPE achieves an eightfold (`8×`) extension even without any fine‐tuning at all—a feat impossible for uniform methods such as PI whose linear compression makes distant angles indistinguishable.
* **Compatibility with downstream optimizations:** Since rotary embeddings are purely functional transformations applied before dot products, existing flash‐attention kernels, quantization schemes, or pruning pipelines remain applicable unchanged (\cite{LongRoPE}).

Collectively these benefits explain why many state-of-the-art LLMs now adopt rotary positioning as their default choice instead of static absolute tables—and why LongRoPE can push this choice further toward two million token contexts while preserving both language modeling quality (`perplexity`) and downstream task accuracy (`passkey retrieval`, `MMLU`, `TruthfulQA`).

### Summary  

Rotary Position Embedding converts token indices into per‑dimension rotations defined analytically by Eq.\,(1).  Interpreting each cosine/sine pair as a complex exponential reveals why rotating queries and keys embeds *relative* distance directly into their dot product—a core operation inside self‑attention.  The laddered frequency spectrum generated from a single base constant $\theta=10^4$ provides both high resolution near sequence start and low resolution far away; manipulating those frequencies with dimension‑specific rescale factors enables smooth extrapolation far beyond training limits.  When blended into attention scores through simple block rotations ($R(n)$), RoPE incurs virtually no extra memory overhead yet yields powerful relative positioning properties.  Finally, because it requires only numeric transformations rather than additional learned parameters, RoPE permits lightweight extensions such as LongRoPE’s evolutionary search over $\{\lambda_i,\hat n,\}$ achieving unprecedented context windows up to \(2048~k\) tokens while keeping short‑window performance intact—a combination unattainable for conventional absolute or learned encodings.

**Challenges When Extending the Context Window**

Large language models (LLMs) such as LLaMA 2 and Mistral have demonstrated impressive capabilities across a multitude of tasks, yet they are fundamentally limited by the size of their *context window*—the maximum number of tokens that can be processed simultaneously.  In practice, most contemporary LLMs operate with a context window of a few thousand tokens (e.g., 4 k for LLaMA 2).  Extending this window to hundreds of thousands or even millions of tokens promises new applications in long‑document reasoning, extensive in‑context learning, and autonomous agents that must retain a long history of interaction.  However, the research presented in **LongRoPE** identifies three intertwined obstacles that have so far prevented such dramatic scaling: (1) catastrophic values arising from untrained position indices, (2) a severe shortage of ultra‑long training data together with prohibitive compute and memory demands, and (3) attention dispersion across millions of positions that erodes the model’s ability to focus on relevant information.  

The sections below unpack each obstacle in detail, grounding the discussion in empirical observations and theoretical arguments made by the authors of LongRoPE.  The goal is to give readers an intuitive sense of why naïvely increasing a model’s context window is far more difficult than simply feeding it longer sequences.

---

## Catastrophic Values from Untrained Position Indices  

In transformer architectures, positional information is injected into token embeddings so that the model can distinguish “the first word” from “the thousandth word.”  Modern LLMs typically rely on **RoPE** (Rotary Positional Embedding), which encodes a token at absolute position \(n\) as a series of sinusoidal rotations whose frequencies are determined by a base \(\theta\) (usually \(10\,000\)).  Formally, the RoPE vector for dimension \(i\) contains the pair \(\bigl[\cos(n\theta_i),\sin(n\theta_i)\bigr]\), where \(\theta_i=\theta^{-2i/d}\) and \(d\) denotes the hidden dimension.

During pre‑training, only positions up to the original context length \(L\) (e.g., \(4{\,}096\)) appear in the data.  Consequently, the model never sees rotation angles for indices larger than \(L-1\).  When one attempts to feed a sequence longer than \(L\), new position indices are introduced that have never been trained on.  The rotary angles associated with these indices can take on **catastrophic values**—numerical patterns dramatically different from those encountered during training.  From an optimization viewpoint these values act as out‑of‑distribution inputs; gradients computed on them are noisy or even divergent because the learned weight matrices have never adapted to such rotations.

Empirically, LongRoPE reports that “untrained new position indices introduce many catastrophic values,” causing fine‑tuning to become unstable or to converge very slowly.  This problem becomes especially acute when extending from a few thousand tokens to over one million tokens: more than ninety percent of the positional indices become novel, amplifying the risk that hidden states receive malformed directional cues from RoPE.  The effect is analogous to handing a student a textbook written in an unknown alphabet; without exposure during learning, comprehension collapses.

---

## Distribution Shift Between Trained and Extended Positions  

Closely related to catastrophic values is a **distribution shift** between the set of positions seen during pre‑training and those encountered after extension.  In statistical learning theory, models assume that training and test data share the same underlying distribution.  For positional embeddings this assumption translates into “the distribution over rotation angles at inference matches that seen during pre‑training.”  

When extending to an enlarged context window by factor \(s = \frac{L'}{L} > 1\), existing methods (e.g., linear Position Interpolation (PI)) multiply all rotation angles by \(\frac{1}{s}\).  While this rescales angles into the familiar range, it also **crowds** distinct token positions into overlapping regions of angle space.  As more tokens occupy narrower angular intervals, their representations become less discriminable—a hallmark of distribution shift.  

LongRoPE observes that naïve scaling leads to “out‑of‑distribution issues” because the rescaled angles no longer preserve the original *information entropy* embedded across RoPE dimensions.  In other words, high‑frequency dimensions that originally captured fine‑grained relative ordering become too compressed when uniformly shrunk by \(s\), while low‑frequency dimensions may be over‑stretched.  This mismatch explains why earlier approaches such as PI or even more sophisticated schemes like NTK‐aware interpolation still suffer steep performance drops once extension ratios exceed roughly fourfold.

---

## Data Scarcity for > 1 000 k Token Documents  

Even if one could resolve positional interpolation issues theoretically, extending an LLM’s context window still demands **training data** whose individual documents exceed the target length \(L'\).  Most public text corpora contain documents measured in kilobytes or at most low hundreds of kilobytes; truly massive texts—books spanning multiple millions of tokens or concatenated web archives exceeding one million tokens—are exceedingly rare.

LongRoPE emphasizes three consequences of this scarcity:

1. **Limited Supervision** – Fine‑tuning an LLM on longer sequences relies on next‑token prediction loss across all positions. If only a handful of examples reach beyond \(1{\,}000{\,}000\) tokens, stochastic gradient descent cannot reliably estimate gradients for those extreme positions.

2. **Sampling Bias** – The few available ultra‑long documents tend to be homogeneous (e.g., large code bases or specific literary works), which may bias the model toward niche patterns rather than general long‑range dependencies.

3. **Training Instability** – Because most sampled batches will contain far shorter sequences mixed with occasional ultra‑long ones, gradient magnitudes can fluctuate wildly across steps; this instability compounds with the positional issues described earlier.

The authors therefore argue that “fine‑tuning usually requires texts of corresponding lengths,” yet such texts are “limited” in current datasets. The paucity of megabyte‑scale narratives compels researchers either to synthesize artificial documents (which may not reflect natural language structure) or accept suboptimal fine‑tuning regimes that cannot fully exploit an enlarged context window.

---

## GPU/Memory Requirements for Ultra‑Long Training  

Training transformer models scales quadratically with sequence length because self‑attention attends every token pair \((i,j)\).  The memory footprint required to store attention matrices alone grows as \(\mathcal{O}(L'^2)\).  When target lengths reach hundreds of thousands—or even two million tokens—the raw memory needed far exceeds what modern GPUs can hold even after model parallelism or activation checkpointing.

LongRoPE quantifies these constraints: extending from \(4{\,}096\) to \(256{\,}k\) already demands “16 A100 GPUs” under their fine‑tuning pipeline; reaching \(2048{\,}k\) would naïvely require orders of magnitude more hardware if trained end‑to‑end at that length.  Moreover, larger batches are necessary to maintain statistical efficiency; however increasing batch size further inflates GPU memory consumption due to redundant storage of key/value tensors for each token.

The authors therefore note three intertwined cost factors:

* **Compute Hours** – Each forward–backward pass must process an astronomically larger tensor; training epochs multiply accordingly.
* **GPU Memory** – Even offloading strategies cannot accommodate attention maps for multi‑million token windows.
* **Infrastructure Complexity** – Distributed training across dozens or hundreds of high‑end GPUs introduces communication overheads that dominate runtime when sequence lengths explode.

These practical barriers explain why most prior work has capped extended contexts at roughly `128k` tokens—beyond which resource requirements become “prohibitively extensive.”

---

## Attention Density Dilution Across Very Long Sequences  

Finally, extending context windows fundamentally alters how attention operates throughout a transformer layer. In standard settings with modest lengths (\(L = 4{\,}k\)), each token can attend relatively densely: every key/value pair contributes appreciably to any given query’s softmax weighting because there are only a few thousand competing terms.

When sequence length grows by orders of magnitude, attention scores become *diluted*. The softmax denominator sums over millions of exponentiated dot products; consequently any single token’s contribution shrinks dramatically unless its similarity score is exceptionally high. This phenomenon is referred to as **attention density dilution**, and it manifests empirically as degraded performance on tasks that rely heavily on short‑range dependencies—even within the original window—because attention weights become flatter and less discriminative.

The paper cites prior work showing that when extending beyond “2 k” tokens without careful positional redesign, “attention becomes dispersed as it's spread thinly across numerous token positions,” which leads directly to “performance degradation on the original short context.” In practice this means that even if a model receives an early passage it cares about (e.g., crucial instructions within the first few hundred tokens), those signals can be drowned out by overwhelming noise from faraway positions whose cumulative influence overwhelms local attentional focus.

Addressing this requires either novel attention mechanisms—such as sparse or hierarchical attention patterns—or careful repositioning (as LongRoPE does) so that early tokens retain stronger relative representations through less aggressive interpolation (“extrapolating less” on initial positions). This mitigates dilution but does not fully solve it; thus attention density remains one of the core challenges when pushing context windows toward multi‑million token scales.

---

### Synthesis  

Collectively, these five challenges constitute a tightly coupled system: catastrophic positional values trigger distribution shift; both are aggravated by insufficient training data; alleviating them would normally require exhaustive fine‑tuning on ultra‑long sequences—a process made infeasible by GPU/memory constraints; finally, even if one could overcome these hurdles computationally and data-wise, attention dilution threatens any gains by blurring signal relevance across massive contexts. 

Recognizing this landscape is essential before attempting any engineering solution such as LongRoPE’s evolutionary search for non‑uniform rescaling factors and progressive fine‑tuning strategy. Only by systematically addressing each obstacle—reducing positional distortion while respecting hardware limits and preserving attentional focus—can researchers hope to reliably expand LLMs’ effective context windows beyond two million tokens.
