### How is the Cayley parameterization $\mathbf R = (I+\mathbf Q)(I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ derived and why does it guarantee orthogonality?
Question:
$\mathbf Q$ must be skew‑symmetric ($\mathbf Q=-\mathbf Q^T$). I understand that this yields an orthogonal $\mathbf R$, but I am unclear on:
* A step‑by‑step derivation showing $\mathbf R^T \mathbf R = I$.

### 3. Why use the Cayley parametrisation instead of Householder reflections?

Both constructions generate orthogonal matrices, yet they differ considerably in parameter efficiency and gradient flow.

* **Parameter count & expressivity**   
  An arbitrary $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix lives on a manifold of dimension $d(d-½)$––the number of independent entries in a skew–symmetric generator.
  Using a single generator ${\:}{\:}$ automatically supplies all those degrees of freedom;
  only its upper–triangular part needs learnable parameters.
  By contrast, representing a generic rotation as a product of Householder reflectors requires roughly $O(d)$ reflectors,
  each contributing $d$ parameters,
  yielding considerably more bookkeeping and higher variance in gradients because each reflector involves normalising its defining vector ($v/\|v⟩$).

* **Differentiability & computational cost**   
  The Cayley map $( ){ }( ){ }^{‑¹}$ is a smooth rational function of ${\:}{\:}$.
  Modern autodiff frameworks compute its Jacobian efficiently via saved factorizations or directly through back–propagation through the linear solve performed by $( ){ }^{ ־¹}$.
  No explicit re–orthogonalisation such as Gram–Schmidt or SVD is required;
  solving a linear system costs $O(d³)$ once per forward pass and benefits from highly optimised GPU kernels.

* **Determinant constraint**   
  The Cayley transform maps ${\:}{\:}$ onto all orthogonal matrices **except**
  those having an eigenvalue equal to $-1$ (i.e.\ rotations by exactly π).
  This tiny restriction does not affect finetuning—
  an exact π–rotation seldom appears as an optimal update for deep diffusion models,
  and any missing direction can be approximated arbitrarily closely by nearby angles.

* **Stability in optimisation**   
  Because every update stays on the manifold automatically,
  optimiser steps never drift off–manifold and never require costly projection operations.
  The authors observed empirically (§ 3.2)
  that removing the orthogonality constraint makes training diverge;
  the smoothness provided by the Cayley map thus contributes directly to finetuning stability.

### Summary

Starting from a skew–symmetric generator ${\:}{\:}$,
the Cayley transform $( ){ }( ){ }^{  ־¹}$ produces an exact orthogonal matrix because $( ){ }$ and $( ){ }$ are transposes of one another,
so their product collapses to the identity after inversion.
For any real skew–symmetric ${\:}{\:}$,
$( ){ }$ is guaranteed nonsingular—the eigenvalues are never zero—
so no extra norm bound on ${\:}{\:}$ is required beyond numerical prudence.
Compared with alternatives such as Householder reflectors,
the Cayley parametrisation supplies all degrees of freedom through a single dense set of unconstrained parameters,
offers a differentiable closed form that avoids expensive re–orthogonalisation,
and empirically yields far more stable finetuning when applied inside diffusion models (see § 3.2–§ 3.4).

Hence, within *Orthogonal Finetuning* the authors choose Equation (1)
because it gives them an easy‑to‑optimise, memory‑efficient way of forcing each trainable layer‑wise transformation ${\:}{\:}$
to stay on the orthogonal manifold—exactly what is needed to preserve hyperspherical energy while still allowing rich directional updates during text‑to‑image diffusion finetuning.
