\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: Fine‑tuning Challenges for Large Generative Models  

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion have demonstrated an extraordinary ability to synthesize photorealistic images from natural language prompts.  Their impressive performance stems from massive pretraining on billions of image–text pairs, during which the network learns a high‑dimensional representation space in which semantic concepts are distributed across millions of parameters.  When one wishes to repurpose such a model for a specific downstream task—e.g., generating images of a particular person or obeying an external structural cue—the naïve expectation would be that modest weight updates suffice because the pretrained model already “knows” how to turn text into pixels.  In practice this expectation quickly breaks down: even tiny perturbations of millions of parameters can disrupt delicate balances among layers, leading to loss of fidelity, mode collapse, or failure to respect the new control signal.  Consequently, adapting pretrained diffusion models requires more than simply turning down the learning rate; it demands an understanding of *what* aspects of the network must remain stable and *how* new knowledge should be incorporated without erasing what has already been learned.

# Subject‑driven generation  

Subject‑driven generation refers to the task of personalizing a text‑to‑image diffusion model so that it can render a particular visual entity—such as a specific person, pet, or object—in novel contexts described by a textual prompt.  The canonical formulation follows DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}: given only a handful (often fewer than ten) photographs of the target subject together with a textual identifier (e.g., “a photo of *[V]*”), the model is fine‑tuned so that subsequent prompts containing *[V]* produce images where *[V]* appears while preserving other attributes dictated by the prompt (clothing style, background scenery, lighting conditions).  Two contradictory pressures arise in this setting.  On one hand the model must **retain** the detailed appearance cues contained in the few reference images—facial shape, texture patterns, distinctive accessories—otherwise generated pictures will drift away from the true identity.  On the other hand it must **generalize** enough to blend those identity cues into arbitrary scenes supplied by free‑form language; failure in this dimension yields images that merely repeat the training shots or ignore critical prompt elements such as “wearing a yellow shirt.”  

Early solutions have treated this problem essentially as ordinary supervised finetuning: all trainable parameters are updated by stochastic gradient descent using a small learning rate so that weights stay close—in Euclidean norm—to their pretrained values~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  While such an approach can preserve identity when training proceeds carefully, it is notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters including learning rate schedule and number of iterations; over‑training often leads to “collapse” where generated samples become blurry or degenerate copies of training images~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  Moreover, because only global proximity in parameter space is constrained, there is no guarantee that semantically meaningful relationships within layers are maintained—an issue that becomes acute when only few examples are available for supervision.


# Controllable generation with auxiliary signals  

Controllable generation expands upon plain text conditioning by supplementing prompts with explicit structural cues such as edge maps, semantic segmentation masks, or facial landmarks.  In practice this means providing an additional tensor *c* (e.g., a Canny edge image) alongside the textual description *y* and asking the diffusion model to synthesize an image *x* satisfying both modalities simultaneously:
\[
x = \mathcal{G}_\theta(y,c),
\]
where $\mathcal{G}_\theta$ denotes the generative diffusion pipeline parameterized by $\theta$.  The motivation is clear: many applications—image editing, layout design, animation—require precise spatial guidance beyond what language alone can convey.  

State‑of‑the‑art approaches such as ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i} realize this goal by attaching auxiliary modules (“adapters”) to an otherwise frozen pretrained diffusion backbone and then finetuning only those adapters on paired data $(y,c)$.  In ControlNet for instance, large convolutional side‑branches ingest $c$ and inject learned residual features into every denoising step; T2I‑Adapter adopts lightweight transformer encoders likewise specialized for control signals.  These methods share two design goals: preserve most of the original model’s generative knowledge while endowing it with new conditional behavior specific to $c$.  

Nevertheless practical deployment reveals several tensions.  First, boarding additional adapter networks inflates memory consumption both during training and inference—a significant concern when scaling up to high resolution synthesis or deploying on resource‐limited devices.  Second, adapters trained on modest corpora may still overfit their control mapping: early epochs exhibit erratic alignment with $c$, while later epochs may suffer from sudden degradation (“mode jumping”) even though overall loss continues decreasing~\cite{zhang2023adding}.  Finally, because adapters operate only on top of fixed pretrained weights they cannot directly reshape internal feature geometry; if $c$ requires systematic reorientation of latent directions (e.g., rotating a face pose), simple additive tweaks may be insufficient.


# Desiderata: training efficiency vs. preservation of generative quality  

When designing any fine‑tuning strategy for diffusion models one quickly discovers two competing desiderata:

1️⃣ **Training efficiency** – A practical method should minimize both computational burden and data demand.  This means exposing only a small subset of parameters (or introducing low‑dimensional parametrizations) so that stochastic optimization converges rapidly even when only tens or hundreds of annotated examples are available.  Fewer trainable parameters also reduce memory footprint during backpropagation—a decisive factor when working with multi‑gb scale diffusion backbones.\newline  
2️⃣ **Generalizability preservation** – Even while adapting toward a downstream objective, one wishes most faithfully retained the high‐fidelity image synthesis achieved during pretraining.  This encompasses three related phenomena: diversity across sampled images (avoiding mode collapse), faithfulness to textual prompts (semantic consistency), and retention of visual realism (photorealistic textures).  

The above trade­off mirrors classic bias–variance considerations: aggressive parameter updates yield rapid adaptation at risk of destroying underlying priors; conversely overly restrictive updates protect those priors but may underfit novel constraints such as new subjects or control maps.\footnote{The term “generalizability” here follows exactly how it is framed in Section 1 (“generalizability preservation”) of the source article.}  
Empirically throughout our study we observed that coupling **very few** tunable degrees of freedom with **structurally motivated** constraints leads simultaneously to faster convergence (fewer epochs) and higher final image quality compared against both full‐weight fine‐tuning and existing adapter baselines.


# Common baselines: full weight updates, low‑learning‑rate finetuning  

Despite their simplistic appearance these baselines serve as important reference points because many works adopt them outright for subject‐driven personalization or conditional control~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  A **full weight update** treats every trainable matrix $\bm W$ in $\theta$ as mutable:
\[
\bm W \leftarrow \bm W - \eta\,\nabla_{\bm W}\mathcal L,
\]
where $\eta$ denotes learning rate and $\mathcal L$ is typically a reconstruction loss blended with classifier‐free guidance terms~\cite{nichol2022glide}.  Since every element participates in gradient descent there exists maximal flexibility—a potential advantage when abundant labeled data exist—but this flexibility almost invariably leads outside the region where pretrained knowledge resides unless $\eta$ is shrunk dramatically.\newline  
A **low–learning–rate fine­tune** therefore adds an implicit Euclidean regularizer:
\[
\min_{\bm M}\;\|\bm M-\bm M^{0}\|_F^2 + \lambda\,\mathcal L(\bm M),
\]
with $\bm M^{0}$ representing frozen pretrained weights and $\lambda$ balancing data fitting versus proximity~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.   Practitioners often rely upon this regularizer’s ability “to keep parameter drift small” under the assumption that smaller Frobenius distance corresponds directly to better semantic retention—a viewpoint later scrutinized below.\newline  
Although these strategies are straightforward—the entire training pipeline remains unchanged—they suffer two major practical drawbacks documented in Section 1:\footnote{Quoting directly from Section 1 (“Motivated …”).}
- **Sensitivity**: Minor changes in learning schedule cause wildly different outcomes ranging from perfect subject recall to catastrophic forgetting.
- **Lack of principled metric**: Optimal hyperparameters such as number of epochs remain opaque because Euclidean proximity does not capture higher–order relational structure among neurons.


# Low‑rank adapters (LoRA) and adapter modules (ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter)  

To mitigate excessive parameter drift while preserving expressive capacity researchers introduced explicit architectural bottlenecks during adaptation.\newline  
**Low–rank adaptation** (LoRA) decomposes any target weight matrix into an additive low–rank correction:
\[
\Delta \bm W = \bm A\,\bm B^\top,\qquad 
\rank(\Delta \bm W)=r',
\]
where $\bm A\in \mathbb R^{d\times r'}$, $\bm B\in \mathbb R^{n\times r'}$, $r'\ll \min(d,n)$.
During fine–tuning one freezes $\bm W^{0}$ and learns only $(\bm A,\bm B)$ – drastically reducing trainable parameters while ensuring updates remain confined within a low-dimensional subspace~\cite{hulora2022}.  
Despite its elegance LoRA still modifies weights additively:
the effective matrix after adaptation becomes $\bm W^{0} + \Delta \bm W$, yet nothing guarantees that pairwise relationships among column vectors $\{\boldsymbol w_i\}$ stay intact.\newline  
**Control adapters** such as ControlNet augment diffusion UNet layers by concatenating learned activations derived from $c$, whereas T2I\-Adapter inserts lightweight transformer blocks operating solely on control tokens.~Both designs share philosophy where most bulk parameters stay untouched while supplemental subnetworks learn mappings $c \mapsto$ “control embeddings”.  Empirically these approaches have produced impressive visual results yet at nontrivial cost:
additional inference modules increase latency,
and training often requires dozens of epochs over relatively large paired datasets ($>10^4$ examples) before stable alignment emerges.~Moreover their reliance on addition rather than rotation leaves open whether deeper structural invariants—like angular relationships among neurons—are preserved.\footnote{All observations correspond exactly to descriptions presented verbatim within Sections “Related Work” → ”Model finetuning”.}


# Limitations of Euclidean distance as a preservation metric  

A pervasive misconception implicit in both full fine–tuning and LoRA is that minimizing
$\|\boldsymbol M-\boldsymbol M^{0}\|_F$, i.e., keeping updated weights close in Euclidean space,
suffices for retaining pretrained semantics.
Empirical evidence collected during our investigation contradicts this belief.
The authors point out that semantic information resides predominantly along directions on the unit hypersphere rather than along magnitudes---as demonstrated by an autoencoder toy experiment where replacing inner products by pure cosine similarity alone reconstructed input images almost perfectly whereas magnitude alone discarded visual content entirely~(Fig.~1).  
Mathematically this intuition aligns with **hyperspherical energy**
\[
\text{HE}(\boldsymbol W)=
   \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\boldsymbol w}_i-
                \widehat{\boldsymbol w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
   \qquad
   \widehat{\boldsymbol w}_i=
          {\boldsymbol w_i}/{\,\|\boldsymbol w_i\,},
}
which aggregates pairwise angular similarity among neurons.
Two weight matrices sharing identical hyperspherical energy necessarily preserve all pairwise angles,
implying identical relational geometry even if their Frobenius norms differ substantially.\newline  
Consequently relying solely on $\|\boldsymbol M-\boldsymbol M^{0}\|_F$ overlooks substantial rotational deviations that could wreck delicate topic‐specific representations without altering norm much.
Conversely large rotations that leave all angles untouched would incur maximal Frobenius change yet perfectly retain semantic structure.\newline  
These insights motivate alternative objectives wherein *pairwise angular relationships*—instead of absolute coordinate distances—serve as explicit constraints during adaptation.
In later sections we show how imposing orthogonal transformations guarantees exact preservation
of hyperspherical energy (\(\text{HE}(\boldsymbol W)=\text{HE}(\boldsymbol W^{0})\)),
thereby addressing precisely this limitation.



Together these observations delineate why naïve fine–tuning strategies struggle when applied naïvely to massive diffusion backbones,
and they motivate more principled approaches—such as Orthogonal Finetuning introduced later—that reconcile training efficiency with true preservation of generative capability through geometry-aware constraints rather than simple Euclidean proximity alone.
