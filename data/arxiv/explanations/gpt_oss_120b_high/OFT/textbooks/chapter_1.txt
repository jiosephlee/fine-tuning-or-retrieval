\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 1: # Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

In recent years **text‑to‑image diffusion models** have become the dominant technology for generating high‑fidelity photographs from natural language prompts.  Pioneering systems such as **GLIDE** \cite{saharia2022photorealistic}, **Imagen** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} and **Stable Diffusion** \cite{rombach2022high} demonstrate that a single pretrained neural network can learn a remarkably rich joint distribution over images and their textual descriptions.  Understanding how these systems are built—and why they differ fundamentally from earlier generative families—is essential before studying downstream adaptation techniques such as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).  

This chapter surveys the historical progression from autoregressive and generative‑adversarial networks (GANs) toward modern diffusion approaches, explains how a *forward* noise‑adding process is paired with a *reverse* denoising sampler, contrasts *pixel‑space* versus *latent‑space* formulations, describes the architectural building blocks that recur in today’s flagship models, discusses how large vision–language encoders such as **CLIP** enable cross‑modal conditioning, and finally outlines the training objectives that make diffusion feasible (most notably score matching). Throughout we emphasize intuition rather than formal derivations, keeping mathematical notation confined to simple LaTeX expressions.

---

## From Autoregressive and GAN‑Based Generators to Diffusion Models  

Early attempts at synthesizing images conditioned on language relied on two dominant frameworks:

1. **Autoregressive transformers** modeled image generation as a sequence prediction problem \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical}.  By factorising an image into a chain of discrete tokens (often obtained via vector quantisation), an autoregressive network predicts each token conditioned on previously generated ones together with a textual prompt.  This paradigm mirrors language modelling but suffers from quadratic decoding cost when images become large because tokens must be generated one after another.

2. **Generative adversarial networks (GANs)** learned a mapping from random latent vectors to realistic images via a two‑player minimax game \cite{zhang2017stackgan}.  Conditional GANs were extended with textual conditioning \cite{reed2016generative}, yet training instability and mode collapse limited their ability to capture the full diversity required by open‑ended prompts.

Both families succeed when paired with powerful language backbones (e.g., large transformer encoders) but encounter intrinsic bottlenecks—slow sampling for autoregression and unstable optimisation for GANs.  

Diffusion modelling emerged as an appealing alternative precisely because it reframes generation as *iterative refinement* rather than direct one‑shot synthesis \cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score}. By starting from pure noise and repeatedly applying learned denoising transforms, a diffusion model circumvents adversarial dynamics while preserving high sample quality. The empirical breakthroughs reported by GLIDE \cite{saharia2022photorealistic}, Imagen \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} and Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} confirm that this paradigm scales gracefully even when conditioned on long free‑form texts.

## The Forward Diffusion Process and Reverse Denoising Samplers  

A diffusion model defines a *forward* stochastic process \(q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})\) that gradually corrupts an image \(x_{0}\) by adding Gaussian noise over discrete timesteps \(t=1,\dots,T\).  After sufficiently many steps (\(t=T\)) the distribution becomes close to an isotropic standard normal distribution \( \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})\).  Intuitively one may think of watching an initially sharp photograph deteriorate into static through repeated blurring operations—the final noisy frame contains virtually no perceptible structure.

Training proceeds by learning a *reverse* Markov chain \(p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})\) that aims to undo each noisy transition.  Practically this reverse step is implemented by a deep neural network \(\epsilon_{\theta}\) which receives three inputs:
* the noisy image at timestep \(t\),
* a timestep embedding encoding how much noise has been added,
* an optional conditioning signal derived from the textual prompt.  

The network predicts either the added noise itself or equivalently the **score function**
\( \nabla_{x_{t}} \log p(x_{t})\), i.e., the gradient of the log‐density at \(x_{t}\).  
During inference one starts from pure Gaussian noise \(x_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf I)\) and repeatedly applies \(p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})\) moving backwards until reaching \(x_{0}\), which is then decoded as an image aligned with the conditioning signal.

Because every reverse step resembles a small denoising operation, many iterations produce smooth trajectories across image space—a property exploited heavily when adapting pretrained models for downstream tasks (the focus of later chapters).

## Latent‑Space Diffusion Versus Pixel‑Space Diffusion  

Even though all modern systems share the same mathematical backbone described above, they differ markedly in *where* they perform denoising:

| Model family | Working domain | Key auxiliary component | Typical conditioning |
|--------------|----------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **GLIDE** \cite{saharia2022photorealistic} | **Pixel space** | Trains directly on RGB images | Text encoder learned jointly with diffusion prior |
| **Imagen** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} | **Pixel space** | Uses frozen pretrained text encoder | Text encoder frozen during training |
| **Stable Diffusion** \cite{rombach2022high} | **Latent space** | Learns a visual codebook via VQ‑GAN \cite{esser2021taming} | Text encoder typically CLIP‑style |
| **DALL·E 2** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} | **Latent space** | Constructs joint image–text embeddings using CLIP \cite{radford2021learning} | Conditioning mediated through shared embedding |

Pixel‐space methods operate directly on full resolution images throughout the forward–reverse chain; consequently they must allocate substantial memory during training but benefit from avoiding additional compression artifacts.  

Latent‐space methods first compress each photograph into a compact representation \(z=\mathcal{E}(x)\) using an autoencoder (often VQ‑GAN). The diffusion process then evolves \(z_t\) rather than raw pixels, dramatically reducing computational cost while preserving most perceptual information because decoding \(\mathcal{D}(z_T)\) reconstructs realistic textures once denoised back to \(z_0\).  

Both design choices affect how subsequent finetuning strategies interact with pretrained weights—an observation central to Orthogonal Finetuning where preserving structural relationships among latent neurons becomes crucial.

## Key Components in Popular Models  

### 1. Noise Predictor Network (U‑Net Backbone)

All aforementioned systems share a U‑Net–style architecture for \(\epsilon_{\theta}\).  This network consists of:
* **Encoder blocks** compressing spatial resolution while increasing channel dimensionality,
* A bottleneck where global context aggregates,
* **Decoder blocks** gradually restoring spatial size,
* Skip connections linking encoder–decoder pairs so low‑level detail bypasses deep transformations.

Within each block reside linear layers (or convolutions) whose weight matrices we denote generically by \(\bm W^{0}\).  During finetuning these matrices may be altered—but respecting certain geometric invariants makes it possible to retain pretrained generative abilities (see Sections 4–5).

### 2. Cross‑Attention Between Text and Visual Features  

Conditioning on language relies heavily on *cross‐attention*: query vectors derived from visual features attend over key/value vectors produced by a textual encoder.  
In practice, large transformer‐based language encoders generate token embeddings \(\{\mathbf h_j^{\text{text}}\}_{j=1}^{L}\); these are projected into query/key/value spaces inside each attention head so that visual representations can be modulated proportionally to semantic relevance indicated by words such as “red”, “mountain”, or “portrait”.  

Cross‐attention thus implements an adaptive weighting scheme analogous to manually crafted masks used in older subject‐driven pipelines \cite{avrahami2022blended}, yet it is learned end‑to‑end through backpropagation over millions of captioned images.

### 3. Text Encoders Inspired by Vision–Language Pretraining  

A pivotal development facilitating robust multimodal alignment has been large contrastive pretraining frameworks exemplified by **CLIP** \cite{radford2021learning}.  
These systems learn paired image/text embeddings whose inner products reflect semantic similarity—a property leveraged both:
* Directly during inference where textual tokens are fed into cross‐attention,
* Indirectly during training when loss functions encourage consistency between generated samples and their prompts (e.g., class‐preservation losses used in DreamBooth).

Models such as Stable Diffusion inherit a CLIP‐style encoder directly; Imagen adopts an external frozen language model; GLIDE opts instead for jointly learned tokenisation coupled tightly with its own prior network—a design choice reflected back onto performance trade­offs observed across benchmarks \cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical}.

## Training Objectives: Score Matching and Guidance  

### Score Matching  

The central learning signal underlying all modern diffusers is **score matching**, first formalised in continuous‐time settings \cite{song2021denoising}. Given noisy data pair \((x_t,\! t)\), the neural predictor \(\epsilon_{\theta}\) minimizes an expected squared error between its output and either:
* The true added Gaussian noise,
* Or equivalently—the score vector \(\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t|x_0)\).

Mathematically one minimizes  
\[
    \mathbb E_{x_0,t,\epsilon}\!\Bigl[\,\bigl\|\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t)-\epsilon\bigr\|^{\,2}\Bigr],
\]
where \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathbf I)\) denotes random noise injected during forward corruption.
Because this loss directly aligns predicted gradients with those derived analytically from the forward Markov chain, optimization proceeds stably even when hundreds of timesteps are involved—a decisive advantage over adversarial criteria traditionally used in GAN training.

### Conditional Guidance Without Explicit Classifiers  

When generating conditioned samples one needs a way to bias the reverse dynamics toward fulfilling some external signal (e.g., a textual prompt). A widely adopted technique eliminates an auxiliary classifier altogether: during sampling one evaluates two versions of \(\epsilon_{\theta}\)—one conditioned on text (\(c=1\)) and one unconditional (\(c=0\)). A scalar weight \(w>0\) amplifies the conditional component:
\[
    \tilde{\epsilon}_{\theta}(x_t,t,c)=
        \epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t,c=0)
        + w\,\bigl[\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t,c=1)-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t,c=0)\bigr].
\]
This *classifier‑free guidance* effectively steers samples toward higher likelihood under the conditioned distribution while preserving diversity—a mechanism explicitly exploited in many downstream control tasks such as ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} or T2I‑Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}. Although our primary focus remains orthogonal finetuning rather than sampling tricks, it is important to recognize that stability during finetuning often hinges upon preserving this delicate balance between unconditional realism and conditional fidelity.

## Connecting Foundations to Orthogonal Finetuning  

Having outlined how modern generative pipelines manipulate high dimensional weight tensors inside U-Net blocks (\(\bm W^{0}\)), how textual cues modulate those tensors via cross‐attention, and how score matching guarantees reliable backward dynamics, we arrive at a crucial observation made throughout recent work: **the geometric relationships among neurons encode much of their semantic power**.  

In particular,
* Normalising neurons onto a unit hypersphere reveals pairwise angular similarities (“hyperspherical similarity”) that correlate strongly with semantic behaviour \cite{liu2017deep}.
* Preserving these pairwise angles during adaptation ensures that *hyperspherical energy* remains unchanged—a property rigorously guaranteed when each layer’s weights are transformed by an orthogonal matrix \(\bm R^\top \bm R = \mathbf I.\)

Consequently Orthogonal Finetuning (\( \bm W = \bm R\, \bm W^{0}\); see Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_general}) adapts pre‑trained generators *without destroying* their learned relational structure.
Later chapters will detail how learning layer–shared orthogonal matrices yields faster convergence​ (see Fig.~4), greater sample efficiency​ (see Fig.~5), and eliminates additional test–time overhead because after finetuning one simply multiplies \( \bm R\,\bm W^{0}\) once offline.

### Summary  

This chapter traced the evolution from early autoregressive/GAN generators toward today’s high–performance text–to–image diffusion models, unpacked how stochastic forward corruption pairs with learned reverse denoisers, contrasted pixel versus latent domains, highlighted recurring architectural motifs such as U-Net backbones together with CLIP‐driven cross‐attention modules, and clarified why score matching together with classifier‐free guidance constitute reliable training objectives.  
Understanding these foundations equips readers with the intuition required to appreciate why imposing *orthogonal constraints* during finetuning preserves hyperspherical energy—and ultimately why Orthogonal Finetuning becomes an effective tool for steering massive multimodal generators toward new downstream capabilities such as subject-specific personalization or fine-grained controllable synthesis.
