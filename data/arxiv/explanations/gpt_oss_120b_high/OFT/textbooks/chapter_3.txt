# Vectors on the Unit Hypersphere and Cosine Similarity  

In a diffusion model each linear layer stores a collection of weight vectors, often called *neurons*.  
If a weight vector is denoted by $\bm w\in\mathbb R^{d}$, its Euclidean norm is $\|\bm w\|=\sqrt{\bm w^{\!\top}\bm w}$.  
The **unit hypersphere** $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ is the set of all vectors whose norm equals one:
\[
\mathbb S^{d-1}= \bigl\{\hat{\bm w}\in\mathbb R^{d}\mid \|\hat{\bm w}\|=1\bigr\}.
\]
Normalizing every neuron,
\[
\hat{\bm w}_{i}= \frac{\bm w_{i}}{\|\bm w_{i}\|},
\]
projects it onto $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ while preserving its direction.  

The most common way to quantify directional similarity between two unit‑norm vectors $\hat{\bm u}$ and $\hat{\bm v}$ is the **cosine similarity**
\[
\operatorname{cos}(\hat{\bm u},\hat{\bm v}) \;=\;\hat{\bm u}^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm v}
   \;\in[-1,\,1].
\]
Because both vectors lie on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, their inner product equals the cosine of the geometric angle $\theta$ between them:
\[
\theta(\hat{\bm u},\hat{\bm v})=\arccos(\hat{\bm u}^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm v}),
\qquad 
0 \le \theta \le \pi.
\]
Consequently cosine similarity encodes **angular distance** – two neurons whose directions differ little have a cosine close to $+1$, while nearly opposite directions yield a cosine close to $-1$.  

In the context of the paper this angular relationship underlies **hyperspherical energy**,
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)
    := 
    \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\;\hat{\bm w}_{i}-\hat{\bm w}_{j}\;\bigr\|^{-1},
    \tag{H}
\]
where $\mathbf W=[\,\bm w_{1},\,…,\,\bm w_{n}\,]\in \mathbb R^{\,d\times n}$ collects all neurons in a layer.
Because each $\hat{\bm w}_{i}$ has unit norm,
$\|\;\hat{\bm w}_{i}-\hat{\bm w}_{j}\;\|^{2}
   =2(1-\operatorname{cos}(\hat{\bm w}_{i},\hat{\bm w}_{j})),$
so $\operatorname{HE}$ is completely governed by pairwise cosine similarities.
The empirical observation reported in the paper—that preserving hyperspherical energy correlates with retaining semantic generation ability—therefore amounts to preserving these angles among neurons.

# Orthogonal Matrices: Definitions and Basic Properties  

A square matrix $\mathbf R\in \mathbb R^{\,d\times d}$ is called **orthogonal** if it satisfies  
\[
\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I,
    \qquad
    \mathbf R\,\mathbf R^{\!\top}= \mathbf I ,
    \tag{O}
\]
where $\mathbf I$ denotes the $d\times d$ identity matrix.
Equation (O) says that columns (and rows) form an *orthonormal* basis:
each column has unit length and any two distinct columns are mutually perpendicular.
From $(O)$ one immediately obtains two key properties:

* **Norm preservation**  
  For any vector $\bm x$,  
  $\|\mathbf Rx\|^2 = (\mathbf Rx)^{\!\top}(\mathbf Rx)
      = \bm x^{\!\top}\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf Rx
      = \|\bm x\|^2$.  
  Hence an orthogonal transformation does not change Euclidean lengths.
* **Inner‑product preservation**  
  For any pair $(\bm x,\bm y)$,
  $(\mathbf Rx)^{\!\top}(\mathbf Ry)=
     \bm x^{\!\top}\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf Ry
     = \bm x^{\!\top}\bm y$.
  Therefore both dot products and cosines between pairs of vectors remain exactly unchanged after multiplying by $\mathbf R$.
  
Preserving inner products means that *pairwise angles* are invariant under an orthogonal map.
Consequently **hyperspherical energy**, which depends solely on those angles through Eq (H), stays identical when every neuron inside a layer is multiplied on the left by an orthogonal matrix:
if $\mathbf W_{\text{new}}=\mathbf RW_{\text{old}}$, then 
$\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W_{\text{new}})
   = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W_{\text{old}})$.
This invariance lies at the heart of *Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT): finetuning consists precisely of learning such an $\mathbf R$ per layer so that semantic structure measured by angles remains untouched while still allowing directional modifications essential for downstream tasks.

Geometrically an orthogonal matrix either rotates space or reflects it across some hyperplane:
the **determinant** distinguishes these cases.
If 
$\det(\mathbf R)=+1$, $\mathbf R$ corresponds purely to a rotation;
if 
$\det(\mathbf R)=-1$, it also contains a reflection component.
Both types preserve angles, which explains why either may be admitted during finetuning unless one wishes to exclude reflections explicitly.

# Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$ versus General Orthogonal Group $O(d)$  

All $d\times d$ orthogonal matrices together form a *group* under matrix multiplication,
denoted $O(d)=\bigllbrace\, \mathbf R~|~ \mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I\,\bigr\rbrace$.
It satisfies closure, associativity, contains the identity $\mathbf I$, and each element has an inverse equal to its transpose ($\, \mathbf R^{-1}=\mathbf R^\top$).

Within $O(d)$ lies the **special orthogonal group**
\(SO(d)=
   \bigllbrace\, 
   \mathbf R~|~ 
   \mathbf R^\top \!	extbf R=\textbf I,\,
   \det(\mathbf	R)=+1 
  	\bigr\rbrace.\)
These are precisely the pure rotations without any reflecting component.
The complementary subset
\(O(d)\setminus SO(d)=
      \bigllbrace\, 
      0U:\det(\cdot )=-1 
     	\bigr\rbrace.\)

The paper makes two practical observations concerning these groups:

* The **Cayley parametrization** described later always yields elements in $SO(d)$ because it cannot generate a determinant $-1$ matrix; nevertheless experiments show this limitation does not degrade performance.
* For OFT only *preservation* of pairwise angles matters; whether an extra reflection occurs does not affect hyperspherical energy or downstream generative quality. Thus either $SO(d)$ or full $O(d)$ can serve as admissible sets of finetuning transformations, with $SO(d)$ often preferred for computational convenience.

# Skew‑Symmetric Matrices as Generators of Orthogonal Transforms  

A square matrix $\mathbf Q$ is **skew‑symmetric** when  
$\displaystyle
   \mathbf Q^\top=-\,\mathbf Q .
   \tag{S}
$
All diagonal entries must be zero, while off‑diagonal entries satisfy $q_{ij}= -q_{ji}$.
Skew‑symmetry implies that every eigenvalue of $\mathbf Q$ is either zero or purely imaginary—a fact exploited when building rotations through exponential maps or rational transforms such as Cayley’s.

In differential geometry terms,
the set of all skew‑symmetric matrices coincides with the **tangent space** at the identity element $\mathbf I$ of $O(d)$:
\( T_{\! I}O(d)=
     \bigllbrace\, 
     		\, 	extbf A~|~    
	        {\bf A}^{T}= - {\bf A}
	       	\bigr\rbrace .$
Thus any infinitesimal motion away from $\bf I$ inside $O(d)$ can be expressed as $\, {\bf I}+t {\bf Q}+ o(t)$ with ${\bf Q}$ skew‑symmetric.  

The OFT framework leverages this relationship by *parameterizing* every trainable orthogonal matrix through a skew‑symmetric generator $\bf Q$:
once $\bf Q$ is chosen,
the corresponding finite rotation/reflection entering Eq (O)
is produced by a closed‑form mapping – namely the **Cayley transform**,
which guarantees exact orthogonality without invoking iterative procedures such as Gram–Schmidt.
Because optimisation occurs over entries of ${bf Q}$ rather than directly over ${bf R}$,
the number of independent trainable parameters reduces from $d^2$ raw entries to only $d(d-1)/2$, precisely matching the dimensionality of $O(d)$’s manifold structure.
Moreover initializing ${bf Q}= {\bf 0}$ yields ${bf R}= {\bf I}$,
ensuring OFT starts from exactly the pretrained weights before any directional update takes place.

# Manifold Perspective on $O(d)$: Tangent Space and Retraction Operations  

Viewing $O(d)$ as a smooth manifold equips us with tools familiar from optimisation on manifolds.
Each point ${bf X}\in O(d)$ possesses its own tangent space
\( T_{\! X}O(d)=
      {\bf X}\cdot T_{\! I}O(d)
  	  =
      {\bf X}\cdot 
      \bigllbrace\, {\bf A}: {\bf A}^{T}= - {\bf A}
	    	\bigr\rbrace .\normalsize)
Any tangent vector ${bf Z}$ satisfies ${bf X}^{T}{bf Z}+ {\bf Z}^{T}{bf X}=0$,
expressing “infinitesimal” orthogonality preservation.

When performing gradient descent on such a manifold,
a naïve Euclidean step will typically leave the manifold;
hence we must *retract* back onto $O(d)$ after each update.
A retraction \(R_{\!X}({ bf Z}) : T_X O(d)\to O(d)\cap U,\)
where \(U⊂ℝ^{d×d}\) is some neighbourhood,
maps a tangent vector onto a nearby point on the manifold while locally mimicking the exponential map.

The **Cayley transform** provides exactly such a retraction for $O(d)$:
for any skew‑symmetric ${bf Q}$ sufficiently small so that $(I-\! {bf Q})$ is invertible,
the map
\( {\cal C}:{ bf Q}\mapsto ({ I}+ { bf Q})( { I}-{ bf Q})^{-1}
          := {\bf R}
            ,                           `
produces an element ${ bf R }\in O(d)$ with ${ bf R}^{ T}{ bf	R}= { bf	I }.$
In geometric language,
${ bf	Q }$ lives in \(T_I O( d)\);
${ bf	R }={cal C}( { bf	Q })$ retracts it back onto \(O( d)\).
Because retractions are computationally cheap—only requiring one matrix inverse—Cayley’s map constitutes an attractive alternative to numerically expensive projections or full exponential maps during training.

The paper exploits this retraction twice:

* To impose *exact* orthogonality during parametric updates (`ODE`).
* To enforce an additional *radius constraint* about identity when building **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning** (COFT):
the permissible deviation becomes a bound on ‖${ bf	R}- { bf	I }‖$, which after applying Cayley’s first–order expansion reduces simply to controlling ‖${ bf	Q }‖$. This conversion enables projected gradient descent within the Euclidean space of skew‑symmetric generators while still guaranteeing resulting ${ bf	R }$ lies on \(O( d)\).
