# Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion Models  

In recent years **text‑to‑image diffusion models** have become the dominant technology for generating high‑fidelity photographs from natural language prompts.  Pioneering systems such as **GLIDE** \cite{saharia2022photorealistic}, **Imagen** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} and **Stable Diffusion** \cite{rombach2022high} demonstrate that a single pretrained neural network can learn a remarkably rich joint distribution over images and their textual descriptions.  Understanding how these systems are built—and why they differ fundamentally from earlier generative families—is essential before studying downstream adaptation techniques such as Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT).  

This chapter surveys the historical progression from autoregressive and generative‑adversarial networks (GANs) toward modern diffusion approaches, explains how a *forward* noise‑adding process is paired with a *reverse* denoising sampler, contrasts *pixel‑space* versus *latent‑space* formulations, describes the architectural building blocks that recur in today’s flagship models, discusses how large vision–language encoders such as **CLIP** enable cross‑modal conditioning, and finally outlines the training objectives that make diffusion feasible (most notably score matching). Throughout we emphasize intuition rather than formal derivations, keeping mathematical notation confined to simple LaTeX expressions.

---

## From Autoregressive and GAN‑Based Generators to Diffusion Models  

Early attempts at synthesizing images conditioned on language relied on two dominant frameworks:

1. **Autoregressive transformers** modeled image generation as a sequence prediction problem \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical}.  By factorising an image into a chain of discrete tokens (often obtained via vector quantisation), an autoregressive network predicts each token conditioned on previously generated ones together with a textual prompt.  This paradigm mirrors language modelling but suffers from quadratic decoding cost when images become large because tokens must be generated one after another.

2. **Generative adversarial networks (GANs)** learned a mapping from random latent vectors to realistic images via a two‑player minimax game \cite{zhang2017stackgan}.  Conditional GANs were extended with textual conditioning \cite{reed2016generative}, yet training instability and mode collapse limited their ability to capture the full diversity required by open‑ended prompts.

Both families succeed when paired with powerful language backbones (e.g., large transformer encoders) but encounter intrinsic bottlenecks—slow sampling for autoregression and unstable optimisation for GANs.  

Diffusion modelling emerged as an appealing alternative precisely because it reframes generation as *iterative refinement* rather than direct one‑shot synthesis \cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score}. By starting from pure noise and repeatedly applying learned denoising transforms, a diffusion model circumvents adversarial dynamics while preserving high sample quality. The empirical breakthroughs reported by GLIDE \cite{saharia2022photorealistic}, Imagen \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} and Stable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} confirm that this paradigm scales gracefully even when conditioned on long free‑form texts.

---

## The Forward Diffusion Process and Reverse Denoising Samplers  

A diffusion model defines a *forward* stochastic process \(q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})\) that gradually corrupts an image \(x_{0}\) by adding Gaussian noise over discrete timesteps \(t=1,\dots,T\).  After sufficiently many steps (\(t=T\)) the distribution becomes close to an isotropic standard normal distribution \( \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})\).  Intuitively one may think of watching an initially sharp photograph deteriorate into static through repeated blurring operations—the final noisy frame contains virtually no perceptible structure.

Training proceeds by learning a *reverse* Markov chain \(p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})\) that aims to undo each noisy transition.  Practically this reverse step is implemented by a deep neural network \(\epsilon_{\theta}\) which receives three inputs:
* the noisy image at timestep \(t\),
* a timestep embedding encoding how much noise has been added,
* an optional conditioning signal derived from the textual prompt.  

The network predicts either the added noise itself or equivalently the **score function**
\( \nabla_{x_{t}} \log p(x_{t})\), i.e., the gradient of the log‐density at \(x_{t}\).  
During inference one starts from pure Gaussian noise \(x_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf I)\) and repeatedly applies \(p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})\) moving backwards until reaching \(x_{0}\), which is then decoded as an image aligned with the conditioning signal.

Because every reverse step resembles a small denoising operation, many iterations produce smooth trajectories across image space—a property exploited heavily when adapting pretrained models for downstream tasks (the focus of later chapters).

## Latent‑Space Diffusion Versus Pixel‑Space Diffusion  

Even though all modern systems share the same mathematical backbone described above, they differ markedly in *where* they perform denoising:

| Model family | Working domain | Key auxiliary component | Typical conditioning |
|--------------|----------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| **GLIDE** \cite{saharia2022photorealistic} | **Pixel space** | Trains directly on RGB images | Text encoder learned jointly with diffusion prior |
| **Imagen** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} | **Pixel space** | Uses frozen pretrained text encoder | Text encoder frozen during training |
| **Stable Diffusion** \cite{rombach2022high} | **Latent space** | Learns a visual codebook via VQ‑GAN \cite{esser2021taming} | Text encoder typically CLIP‑style |
| **DALL·E 2** \cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} | **Latent space** | Constructs joint image–text embeddings using CLIP \cite{radford2021learning} | Conditioning mediated through shared embedding |

Pixel‐space methods operate directly on full resolution images throughout the forward–reverse chain; consequently they must allocate substantial memory during training but benefit from avoiding additional compression artifacts.  

Latent‐space methods first compress each photograph into a compact representation \(z=\mathcal{E}(x)\) using an autoencoder (often VQ‑GAN). The diffusion process then evolves \(z_t\) rather than raw pixels, dramatically reducing computational cost while preserving most perceptual information because decoding \(\mathcal{D}(z_T)\) reconstructs realistic textures once denoised back to \(z_0\).  

Both design choices affect how subsequent finetuning strategies interact with pretrained weights—an observation central to Orthogonal Finetuning where preserving structural relationships among latent neurons becomes crucial.

## Key Components in Popular Models  

### 1. Noise Predictor Network (U‑Net Backbone)

All aforementioned systems share a U‑Net–style architecture for \(\epsilon_{\theta}\).  This network consists of:
* **Encoder blocks** compressing spatial resolution while increasing channel dimensionality,
* A bottleneck where global context aggregates,
* **Decoder blocks** gradually restoring spatial size,
* Skip connections linking encoder–decoder pairs so low‑level detail bypasses deep transformations.

Within each block reside linear layers (or convolutions) whose weight matrices we denote generically by \(\bm W^{0}\).  During finetuning these matrices may be altered—but respecting certain geometric invariants makes it possible to retain pretrained generative abilities (see Sections 4–5).

### 2. Cross‑Attention Between Text and Visual Features  

Conditioning on language relies heavily on *cross‐attention*: query vectors derived from visual features attend over key/value vectors produced by a textual encoder.  
In practice, large transformer‐based language encoders generate token embeddings \(\{\mathbf h_j^{\text{text}}\}_{j=1}^{L}\); these are projected into query/key/value spaces inside each attention head so that visual representations can be modulated proportionally to semantic relevance indicated by words such as “red”, “mountain”, or “portrait”.  

Cross‐attention thus implements an adaptive weighting scheme analogous to manually crafted masks used in older subject‐driven pipelines \cite{avrahami2022blended}, yet it is learned end‑to‑end through backpropagation over millions of captioned images.

### 3. Text Encoders Inspired by Vision–Language Pretraining  

A pivotal development facilitating robust multimodal alignment has been large contrastive pretraining frameworks exemplified by **CLIP** \cite{radford2021learning}.  
These systems learn paired image/text embeddings whose inner products reflect semantic similarity—a property leveraged both:
* Directly during inference where textual tokens are fed into cross‐attention,
* Indirectly during training when loss functions encourage consistency between generated samples and their prompts (e.g., class‐preservation losses used in DreamBooth).

Models such as Stable Diffusion inherit a CLIP‐style encoder directly; Imagen adopts an external frozen language model; GLIDE opts instead for jointly learned tokenisation coupled tightly with its own prior network—a design choice reflected back onto performance trade­offs observed across benchmarks \cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical}.

## Training Objectives: Score Matching and Guidance  

### Score Matching  

The central learning signal underlying all modern diffusers is **score matching**, first formalised in continuous‐time settings \cite{song2021denoising}. Given noisy data pair \((x_t,\! t)\), the neural predictor \(\epsilon_{\theta}\) minimizes an expected squared error between its output and either:
* The true added Gaussian noise,
* Or equivalently—the score vector \(\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t|x_0)\).

Mathematically one minimizes  
\[
    \mathbb E_{x_0,t,\epsilon}\!\Bigl[\,\bigl\|\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t)-\epsilon\bigr\|^{\,2}\Bigr],
\]
where \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathbf I)\) denotes random noise injected during forward corruption.
Because this loss directly aligns predicted gradients with those derived analytically from the forward Markov chain, optimization proceeds stably even when hundreds of timesteps are involved—a decisive advantage over adversarial criteria traditionally used in GAN training.

### Conditional Guidance Without Explicit Classifiers  

When generating conditioned samples one needs a way to bias the reverse dynamics toward fulfilling some external signal (e.g., a textual prompt). A widely adopted technique eliminates an auxiliary classifier altogether: during sampling one evaluates two versions of \(\epsilon_{\theta}\)—one conditioned on text (\(c=1\)) and one unconditional (\(c=0\)). A scalar weight \(w>0\) amplifies the conditional component:
\[
    \tilde{\epsilon}_{\theta}(x_t,t,c)=
        \epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t,c=0)
        + w\,\bigl[\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t,c=1)-\epsilon_{\theta}(x_t,t,c=0)\bigr].
\]
This *classifier‑free guidance* effectively steers samples toward higher likelihood under the conditioned distribution while preserving diversity—a mechanism explicitly exploited in many downstream control tasks such as ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} or T2I‑Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}. Although our primary focus remains orthogonal finetuning rather than sampling tricks, it is important to recognize that stability during finetuning often hinges upon preserving this delicate balance between unconditional realism and conditional fidelity.

## Connecting Foundations to Orthogonal Finetuning  

Having outlined how modern generative pipelines manipulate high dimensional weight tensors inside U-Net blocks (\(\bm W^{0}\)), how textual cues modulate those tensors via cross‐attention, and how score matching guarantees reliable backward dynamics, we arrive at a crucial observation made throughout recent work: **the geometric relationships among neurons encode much of their semantic power**.  

In particular,
* Normalising neurons onto a unit hypersphere reveals pairwise angular similarities (“hyperspherical similarity”) that correlate strongly with semantic behaviour \cite{liu2017deep}.
* Preserving these pairwise angles during adaptation ensures that *hyperspherical energy* remains unchanged—a property rigorously guaranteed when each layer’s weights are transformed by an orthogonal matrix \(\bm R^\top \bm R = \mathbf I.\)

Consequently Orthogonal Finetuning (\( \bm W = \bm R\, \bm W^{0}\); see Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_general}) adapts pre‑trained generators *without destroying* their learned relational structure.
Later chapters will detail how learning layer–shared orthogonal matrices yields faster convergence​ (see Fig.~4), greater sample efficiency​ (see Fig.~5), and eliminates additional test–time overhead because after finetuning one simply multiplies \( \bm R\,\bm W^{0}\) once offline.

### Summary  

This chapter traced the evolution from early autoregressive/GAN generators toward today’s high–performance text–to–image diffusion models, unpacked how stochastic forward corruption pairs with learned reverse denoisers, contrasted pixel versus latent domains, highlighted recurring architectural motifs such as U-Net backbones together with CLIP‐driven cross‐attention modules, and clarified why score matching together with classifier‐free guidance constitute reliable training objectives.  
Understanding these foundations equips readers with the intuition required to appreciate why imposing *orthogonal constraints* during finetuning preserves hyperspherical energy—and ultimately why Orthogonal Finetuning becomes an effective tool for steering massive multimodal generators toward new downstream capabilities such as subject-specific personalization or fine-grained controllable synthesis.

Fine‑tuning Challenges for Large Generative Models  

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion have demonstrated an extraordinary ability to synthesize photorealistic images from natural language prompts.  Their impressive performance stems from massive pretraining on billions of image–text pairs, during which the network learns a high‑dimensional representation space in which semantic concepts are distributed across millions of parameters.  When one wishes to repurpose such a model for a specific downstream task—e.g., generating images of a particular person or obeying an external structural cue—the naïve expectation would be that modest weight updates suffice because the pretrained model already “knows” how to turn text into pixels.  In practice this expectation quickly breaks down: even tiny perturbations of millions of parameters can disrupt delicate balances among layers, leading to loss of fidelity, mode collapse, or failure to respect the new control signal.  Consequently, adapting pretrained diffusion models requires more than simply turning down the learning rate; it demands an understanding of *what* aspects of the network must remain stable and *how* new knowledge should be incorporated without erasing what has already been learned.

# Subject‑driven generation  

Subject‑driven generation refers to the task of personalizing a text‑to‑image diffusion model so that it can render a particular visual entity—such as a specific person, pet, or object—in novel contexts described by a textual prompt.  The canonical formulation follows DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}: given only a handful (often fewer than ten) photographs of the target subject together with a textual identifier (e.g., “a photo of *[V]*”), the model is fine‑tuned so that subsequent prompts containing *[V]* produce images where *[V]* appears while preserving other attributes dictated by the prompt (clothing style, background scenery, lighting conditions).  Two contradictory pressures arise in this setting.  On one hand the model must **retain** the detailed appearance cues contained in the few reference images—facial shape, texture patterns, distinctive accessories—otherwise generated pictures will drift away from the true identity.  On the other hand it must **generalize** enough to blend those identity cues into arbitrary scenes supplied by free‑form language; failure in this dimension yields images that merely repeat the training shots or ignore critical prompt elements such as “wearing a yellow shirt.”  

Early solutions have treated this problem essentially as ordinary supervised finetuning: all trainable parameters are updated by stochastic gradient descent using a small learning rate so that weights stay close—in Euclidean norm—to their pretrained values~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  While such an approach can preserve identity when training proceeds carefully, it is notoriously sensitive to hyperparameters including learning rate schedule and number of iterations; over‑training often leads to “collapse” where generated samples become blurry or degenerate copies of training images~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  Moreover, because only global proximity in parameter space is constrained, there is no guarantee that semantically meaningful relationships within layers are maintained—an issue that becomes acute when only few examples are available for supervision.


# Controllable generation with auxiliary signals  

Controllable generation expands upon plain text conditioning by supplementing prompts with explicit structural cues such as edge maps, semantic segmentation masks, or facial landmarks.  In practice this means providing an additional tensor *c* (e.g., a Canny edge image) alongside the textual description *y* and asking the diffusion model to synthesize an image *x* satisfying both modalities simultaneously:
\[
x = \mathcal{G}_\theta(y,c),
\]
where $\mathcal{G}_\theta$ denotes the generative diffusion pipeline parameterized by $\theta$.  The motivation is clear: many applications—image editing, layout design, animation—require precise spatial guidance beyond what language alone can convey.  

State‑of‑the‑art approaches such as ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I‑Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i} realize this goal by attaching auxiliary modules (“adapters”) to an otherwise frozen pretrained diffusion backbone and then finetuning only those adapters on paired data $(y,c)$.  In ControlNet for instance, large convolutional side‑branches ingest $c$ and inject learned residual features into every denoising step; T2I‑Adapter adopts lightweight transformer encoders likewise specialized for control signals.  These methods share two design goals: preserve most of the original model’s generative knowledge while endowing it with new conditional behavior specific to $c$.  

Nevertheless practical deployment reveals several tensions.  First, boarding additional adapter networks inflates memory consumption both during training and inference—a significant concern when scaling up to high resolution synthesis or deploying on resource‐limited devices.  Second, adapters trained on modest corpora may still overfit their control mapping: early epochs exhibit erratic alignment with $c$, while later epochs may suffer from sudden degradation (“mode jumping”) even though overall loss continues decreasing~\cite{zhang2023adding}.  Finally, because adapters operate only on top of fixed pretrained weights they cannot directly reshape internal feature geometry; if $c$ requires systematic reorientation of latent directions (e.g., rotating a face pose), simple additive tweaks may be insufficient.


# Desiderata: training efficiency vs. preservation of generative quality  

When designing any fine‑tuning strategy for diffusion models one quickly discovers two competing desiderata:

1️⃣ **Training efficiency** – A practical method should minimize both computational burden and data demand.  This means exposing only a small subset of parameters (or introducing low‑dimensional parametrizations) so that stochastic optimization converges rapidly even when only tens or hundreds of annotated examples are available.  Fewer trainable parameters also reduce memory footprint during backpropagation—a decisive factor when working with multi‑gb scale diffusion backbones.\newline  
2️⃣ **Generalizability preservation** – Even while adapting toward a downstream objective, one wishes most faithfully retained the high‐fidelity image synthesis achieved during pretraining.  This encompasses three related phenomena: diversity across sampled images (avoiding mode collapse), faithfulness to textual prompts (semantic consistency), and retention of visual realism (photorealistic textures).  

The above trade­off mirrors classic bias–variance considerations: aggressive parameter updates yield rapid adaptation at risk of destroying underlying priors; conversely overly restrictive updates protect those priors but may underfit novel constraints such as new subjects or control maps.\footnote{The term “generalizability” here follows exactly how it is framed in Section 1 (“generalizability preservation”) of the source article.}  
Empirically throughout our study we observed that coupling **very few** tunable degrees of freedom with **structurally motivated** constraints leads simultaneously to faster convergence (fewer epochs) and higher final image quality compared against both full‐weight fine‐tuning and existing adapter baselines.


# Common baselines: full weight updates, low‑learning‑rate finetuning  

Despite their simplistic appearance these baselines serve as important reference points because many works adopt them outright for subject‐driven personalization or conditional control~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.  A **full weight update** treats every trainable matrix $\bm W$ in $\theta$ as mutable:
\[
\bm W \leftarrow \bm W - \eta\,\nabla_{\bm W}\mathcal L,
\]
where $\eta$ denotes learning rate and $\mathcal L$ is typically a reconstruction loss blended with classifier‐free guidance terms~\cite{nichol2022glide}.  Since every element participates in gradient descent there exists maximal flexibility—a potential advantage when abundant labeled data exist—but this flexibility almost invariably leads outside the region where pretrained knowledge resides unless $\eta$ is shrunk dramatically.\newline  
A **low–learning–rate fine­tune** therefore adds an implicit Euclidean regularizer:
\[
\min_{\bm M}\;\|\bm M-\bm M^{0}\|_F^2 + \lambda\,\mathcal L(\bm M),
\]
with $\bm M^{0}$ representing frozen pretrained weights and $\lambda$ balancing data fitting versus proximity~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}.   Practitioners often rely upon this regularizer’s ability “to keep parameter drift small” under the assumption that smaller Frobenius distance corresponds directly to better semantic retention—a viewpoint later scrutinized below.\newline  
Although these strategies are straightforward—the entire training pipeline remains unchanged—they suffer two major practical drawbacks documented in Section 1:\footnote{Quoting directly from Section 1 (“Motivated …”).}
- **Sensitivity**: Minor changes in learning schedule cause wildly different outcomes ranging from perfect subject recall to catastrophic forgetting.
- **Lack of principled metric**: Optimal hyperparameters such as number of epochs remain opaque because Euclidean proximity does not capture higher–order relational structure among neurons.


# Low‑rank adapters (LoRA) and adapter modules (ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter)  

To mitigate excessive parameter drift while preserving expressive capacity researchers introduced explicit architectural bottlenecks during adaptation.\newline  
**Low–rank adaptation** (LoRA) decomposes any target weight matrix into an additive low–rank correction:
\[
\Delta \bm W = \bm A\,\bm B^\top,\qquad 
\rank(\Delta \bm W)=r',
\]
where $\bm A\in \mathbb R^{d\times r'}$, $\bm B\in \mathbb R^{n\times r'}$, $r'\ll \min(d,n)$.
During fine–tuning one freezes $\bm W^{0}$ and learns only $(\bm A,\bm B)$ – drastically reducing trainable parameters while ensuring updates remain confined within a low-dimensional subspace~\cite{hulora2022}.  
Despite its elegance LoRA still modifies weights additively:
the effective matrix after adaptation becomes $\bm W^{0} + \Delta \bm W$, yet nothing guarantees that pairwise relationships among column vectors $\{\boldsymbol w_i\}$ stay intact.\newline  
**Control adapters** such as ControlNet augment diffusion UNet layers by concatenating learned activations derived from $c$, whereas T2I\-Adapter inserts lightweight transformer blocks operating solely on control tokens.~Both designs share philosophy where most bulk parameters stay untouched while supplemental subnetworks learn mappings $c \mapsto$ “control embeddings”.  Empirically these approaches have produced impressive visual results yet at nontrivial cost:
additional inference modules increase latency,
and training often requires dozens of epochs over relatively large paired datasets ($>10^4$ examples) before stable alignment emerges.~Moreover their reliance on addition rather than rotation leaves open whether deeper structural invariants—like angular relationships among neurons—are preserved.\footnote{All observations correspond exactly to descriptions presented verbatim within Sections “Related Work” → ”Model finetuning”.}


# Limitations of Euclidean distance as a preservation metric  

A pervasive misconception implicit in both full fine–tuning and LoRA is that minimizing
$\|\boldsymbol M-\boldsymbol M^{0}\|_F$, i.e., keeping updated weights close in Euclidean space,
suffices for retaining pretrained semantics.
Empirical evidence collected during our investigation contradicts this belief.
The authors point out that semantic information resides predominantly along directions on the unit hypersphere rather than along magnitudes---as demonstrated by an autoencoder toy experiment where replacing inner products by pure cosine similarity alone reconstructed input images almost perfectly whereas magnitude alone discarded visual content entirely~(Fig.~1).  
Mathematically this intuition aligns with **hyperspherical energy**
\[
\text{HE}(\boldsymbol W)=
   \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\widehat{\boldsymbol w}_i-
                \widehat{\boldsymbol w}_j\bigr\|^{-1},
   \qquad
   \widehat{\boldsymbol w}_i=
          {\boldsymbol w_i}/{\,\|\boldsymbol w_i\,},
}
which aggregates pairwise angular similarity among neurons.
Two weight matrices sharing identical hyperspherical energy necessarily preserve all pairwise angles,
implying identical relational geometry even if their Frobenius norms differ substantially.\newline  
Consequently relying solely on $\|\boldsymbol M-\boldsymbol M^{0}\|_F$ overlooks substantial rotational deviations that could wreck delicate topic‐specific representations without altering norm much.
Conversely large rotations that leave all angles untouched would incur maximal Frobenius change yet perfectly retain semantic structure.\newline  
These insights motivate alternative objectives wherein *pairwise angular relationships*—instead of absolute coordinate distances—serve as explicit constraints during adaptation.
In later sections we show how imposing orthogonal transformations guarantees exact preservation
of hyperspherical energy (\(\text{HE}(\boldsymbol W)=\text{HE}(\boldsymbol W^{0})\)),
thereby addressing precisely this limitation.



Together these observations delineate why naïve fine–tuning strategies struggle when applied naïvely to massive diffusion backbones,
and they motivate more principled approaches—such as Orthogonal Finetuning introduced later—that reconcile training efficiency with true preservation of generative capability through geometry-aware constraints rather than simple Euclidean proximity alone.

Mathematical Foundations: Linear Algebra and Hyperspherical Geometry  

# Vectors on the Unit Hypersphere and Cosine Similarity  

In a diffusion model each linear layer stores a collection of weight vectors, often called *neurons*.  
If a weight vector is denoted by $\bm w\in\mathbb R^{d}$, its Euclidean norm is $\|\bm w\|=\sqrt{\bm w^{\!\top}\bm w}$.  
The **unit hypersphere** $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ is the set of all vectors whose norm equals one:
\[
\mathbb S^{d-1}= \bigl\{\hat{\bm w}\in\mathbb R^{d}\mid \|\hat{\bm w}\|=1\bigr\}.
\]
Normalizing every neuron,
\[
\hat{\bm w}_{i}= \frac{\bm w_{i}}{\|\bm w_{i}\|},
\]
projects it onto $\mathbb S^{d-1}$ while preserving its direction.  

The most common way to quantify directional similarity between two unit‑norm vectors $\hat{\bm u}$ and $\hat{\bm v}$ is the **cosine similarity**
\[
\operatorname{cos}(\hat{\bm u},\hat{\bm v}) \;=\;\hat{\bm u}^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm v}
   \;\in[-1,\,1].
\]
Because both vectors lie on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$, their inner product equals the cosine of the geometric angle $\theta$ between them:
\[
\theta(\hat{\bm u},\hat{\bm v})=\arccos(\hat{\bm u}^{\!\top}\!\hat{\bm v}),
\qquad 
0 \le \theta \le \pi.
\]
Consequently cosine similarity encodes **angular distance** – two neurons whose directions differ little have a cosine close to $+1$, while nearly opposite directions yield a cosine close to $-1$.  

In the context of the paper this angular relationship underlies **hyperspherical energy**,
\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)
    := 
    \sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\;\hat{\bm w}_{i}-\hat{\bm w}_{j}\;\bigr\|^{-1},
    \tag{H}
\]
where $\mathbf W=[\,\bm w_{1},\,…,\,\bm w_{n}\,]\in \mathbb R^{\,d\times n}$ collects all neurons in a layer.
Because each $\hat{\bm w}_{i}$ has unit norm,
$\|\;\hat{\bm w}_{i}-\hat{\bm w}_{j}\;\|^{2}
   =2(1-\operatorname{cos}(\hat{\bm w}_{i},\hat{\bm w}_{j})),$
so $\operatorname{HE}$ is completely governed by pairwise cosine similarities.
The empirical observation reported in the paper—that preserving hyperspherical energy correlates with retaining semantic generation ability—therefore amounts to preserving these angles among neurons.

# Orthogonal Matrices: Definitions and Basic Properties  

A square matrix $\mathbf R\in \mathbb R^{\,d\times d}$ is called **orthogonal** if it satisfies  
\[
\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf R=\mathbf I,
    \qquad
    \mathbf R\,\mathbf R^{\!\top}= \mathbf I ,
    \tag{O}
\]
where $\mathbf I$ denotes the $d\times d$ identity matrix.
Equation (O) says that columns (and rows) form an *orthonormal* basis:
each column has unit length and any two distinct columns are mutually perpendicular.
From $(O)$ one immediately obtains two key properties:

* **Norm preservation**  
  For any vector $\bm x$,  
  $\|\mathbf Rx\|^2 = (\mathbf Rx)^{\!\top}(\mathbf Rx)
      = \bm x^{\!\top}\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf Rx
      = \|\bm x\|^2$.  
  Hence an orthogonal transformation does not change Euclidean lengths.
* **Inner‑product preservation**  
  For any pair $(\bm x,\bm y)$,
  $(\mathbf Rx)^{\!\top}(\mathbf Ry)=
     \bm x^{\!\top}\mathbf R^{\!\top}\mathbf Ry
     = \bm x^{\!\top}\bm y$.
  Therefore both dot products and cosines between pairs of vectors remain exactly unchanged after multiplying by $\mathbf R$.
  
Preserving inner products means that *pairwise angles* are invariant under an orthogonal map.
Consequently **hyperspherical energy**, which depends solely on those angles through Eq (H), stays identical when every neuron inside a layer is multiplied on the left by an orthogonal matrix:
if $\mathbf W_{\text{new}}=\mathbf RW_{\text{old}}$, then 
$\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W_{\text{new}})
   = \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W_{\text{old}})$.
This invariance lies at the heart of *Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT): finetuning consists precisely of learning such an $\mathbf R$ per layer so that semantic structure measured by angles remains untouched while still allowing directional modifications essential for downstream tasks.

Geometrically an orthogonal matrix either rotates space or reflects it across some hyperplane:
the **determinant** distinguishes these cases.
If 
$\det(\mathbf R)=+1$, $\mathbf R$ corresponds purely to a rotation;
if 
$\det(\mathbf R)=-1$, it also contains a reflection component.
Both types preserve angles, which explains why either may be admitted during finetuning unless one wishes to exclude reflections explicitly.

---

# Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$ versus General Orthogonal Group $O(d)$  

All $d\times d$ orthogonal matrices together form a *group* under matrix multiplication,
denoted $O(d)=\bigllbrace\, \mathbf R~|~ \mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I\,\bigr\rbrace$.
It satisfies closure, associativity, contains the identity $\mathbf I$, and each element has an inverse equal to its transpose ($\, \mathbf R^{-1}=\mathbf R^\top$).

Within $O(d)$ lies the **special orthogonal group**
\(SO(d)=
   \bigllbrace\, 
   \mathbf R~|~ 
   \mathbf R^\top \!	extbf R=\textbf I,\,
   \det(\mathbf	R)=+1 
  	\bigr\rbrace.\)
These are precisely the pure rotations without any reflecting component.
The complementary subset
\(O(d)\setminus SO(d)=
      \bigllbrace\, 
      0U:\det(\cdot )=-1 
     	\bigr\rbrace.\)

The paper makes two practical observations concerning these groups:

* The **Cayley parametrization** described later always yields elements in $SO(d)$ because it cannot generate a determinant $-1$ matrix; nevertheless experiments show this limitation does not degrade performance.
* For OFT only *preservation* of pairwise angles matters; whether an extra reflection occurs does not affect hyperspherical energy or downstream generative quality. Thus either $SO(d)$ or full $O(d)$ can serve as admissible sets of finetuning transformations, with $SO(d)$ often preferred for computational convenience.

# Skew‑Symmetric Matrices as Generators of Orthogonal Transforms  

A square matrix $\mathbf Q$ is **skew‑symmetric** when  
$\displaystyle
   \mathbf Q^\top=-\,\mathbf Q .
   \tag{S}
$
All diagonal entries must be zero, while off‑diagonal entries satisfy $q_{ij}= -q_{ji}$.
Skew‑symmetry implies that every eigenvalue of $\mathbf Q$ is either zero or purely imaginary—a fact exploited when building rotations through exponential maps or rational transforms such as Cayley’s.

In differential geometry terms,
the set of all skew‑symmetric matrices coincides with the **tangent space** at the identity element $\mathbf I$ of $O(d)$:
\( T_{\! I}O(d)=
     \bigllbrace\, 
     		\, 	extbf A~|~    
	        {\bf A}^{T}= - {\bf A}
	       	\bigr\rbrace .$
Thus any infinitesimal motion away from $\bf I$ inside $O(d)$ can be expressed as $\, {\bf I}+t {\bf Q}+ o(t)$ with ${\bf Q}$ skew‑symmetric.  

The OFT framework leverages this relationship by *parameterizing* every trainable orthogonal matrix through a skew‑symmetric generator $\bf Q$:
once $\bf Q$ is chosen,
the corresponding finite rotation/reflection entering Eq (O)
is produced by a closed‑form mapping – namely the **Cayley transform**,
which guarantees exact orthogonality without invoking iterative procedures such as Gram–Schmidt.
Because optimisation occurs over entries of ${bf Q}$ rather than directly over ${bf R}$,

# Hyperspherical Energy as a Semantic Metric  

In modern text‑to‑image diffusion models the parameters of each linear layer consist of many high‑dimensional “neurons’’ – weight vectors that project an input activation onto a scalar response.  Although these vectors live in Euclidean space, recent work has shown that most of their expressive power resides in their **directions** rather than their magnitudes.  A natural way to quantify the relational structure among directions is *hyperspherical energy* (HE), a scalar summarizing how uniformly the neurons are spread over the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$.  This chapter introduces hyperspherical energy formally, interprets its geometric meaning, proves its invariance under orthogonal transformations, and connects its stability to semantic preservation in vision models.  We conclude with a toy experiment that explicitly demonstrates why angular information dominates over magnitude when neural representations encode visual semantics.

---  

## Definition of Hyperspherical Energy  

Consider a fully‑connected layer with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf W=\{\mathbf w_{1},\dots,\mathbf w_{n}\}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n},
\]

where each column $\mathbf w_{i}$ represents a neuron of dimension $d$.  To focus solely on direction we first normalize every neuron:

\[
\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}\;\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\;\frac{\mathbf w_{i}}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|}\;\in S^{d-1},
\]

so $\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}$ lies on the unit hypersphere $S^{d-1}$ centered at the origin.  
The **hyperspherical energy** of this layer is defined as the sum over all unordered pairs of neurons of the inverse Euclidean distance between their normalized vectors:

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)\;\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\;
\sum_{i\neq j}\Bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\Bigr\|^{-1}
}
\tag{1}
\]

The term $\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\|$ measures how far apart two neurons lie on $S^{d-1}$; its reciprocal therefore penalizes *closeness*: nearby neurons contribute a large amount to $\operatorname{HE}$ while well‑separated neurons contribute only a little.  The overall quantity aggregates these pairwise interactions into a single scalar that reflects how *uniformly* or *clustered* the set of directions is distributed across the sphere.



---  

## “Uniformity’’ versus “Clustering’’ on the Unit Sphere  

Because every norm‐one point lives on $S^{d-1}$, two extreme configurations illustrate the behavior of $\operatorname{HE}$:

1. **Uniform configuration** – Suppose $n$ neurons are arranged so that they are nearly equidistant from one another (e.g., vertices of a regular simplex when $n=d+1$).  In this case each pairwise distance $\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\|$ is close to its maximal possible value $2$, yielding an inverse close to $0.5$.  Since all pairs contribute roughly the same modest amount, the total $\operatorname{HE}$ remains relatively **low**.

2. **Clustered configuration** – Imagine many neurons collapsing toward a common direction $\mathbf u$.  Then for many pairs $(i,j)$ we have $\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\|\approx 0$, making $ \|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\|^{-1}$ explode toward infinity.  Consequently $\operatorname{HE}$ becomes **high**, reflecting strong redundancy among directions.

Thus *low* hyperspherical energy signals that neuron directions are spread out uniformly across $S^{d-1}$, whereas *high* energy indicates substantial clustering or redundancy.  In practice pretrained diffusion models tend to occupy an intermediate regime where directions encode rich semantic relationships without excessive redundancy.  Maintaining this regime during fine‑tuning is conjectured to protect the original generative knowledge.



---  

## Orthogonal Invariance of Hyperspherical Energy  

A fundamental property of $\operatorname{HE}$ is its invariance under any common orthogonal transformation applied to all neurons in a layer.  
Let $\mathbf R\in O(d)$ be an orthogonal matrix satisfying $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I$.  Define a transformed weight matrix

\[
\widetilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\, \mathbf W .
\]

Because orthogonal matrices preserve Euclidean norms ($\|\mathbf R\,\mathbf v\|=\|\mathbf v\|$) and inner products ($(\mathbf R\,\mathbf v)^\top (\mathbf R\,\mathbf u)= \mathbf v^\top \mathbf u$), we have

\[
\widetilde{\widehat{\mathbf w}}_{i}
    = \frac{\widetilde{\mathbf w}_i}{\|\widetilde{\mathbf w}_i|}
    = \frac{\mathbf R\, \mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf R\, \mathbf w_i|}
    = \frac{\mathbf R\, \mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i|}
    = \mathbf R\, \widehat{\mathbf w}_i .
\]

Consequently for any pair $(i,j)$,

\[
\bigl\|\widetilde{\widehat{\mathbf w}}_i-\widetilde{\widehat{\mathbf w}}_j\bigr\|
   = \bigl\|\, \mathbf R(\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\mathbf w}_j)\,\bigr\|
   = \bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_i-\widehat{\bf w}_j\bigr\|,
\]

where we used again norm preservation by $\mathbf R$.  Substituting this equality into Eq.\;(1) yields

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\widetilde {\bf W}) 
   = 
   \sum_{i\neq j}
   \bigl
      \|\widetilde {\widehat{{w}}}_{i}
                - 
              \widetilde {\widehat{{w}}}_{j}
      \bigr
      ^{-1}
   =
   \sum_{i\nobreakneq j}
   \bigl
      {\|\widehat{{w}}_{i}
                - 
              {\widê}_{j}
      }\bigr
      ^{-1}
   =
   \operatorname{HE}({ {\bf W}}).
\]

Thus any orthogonal matrix applied uniformly across all neurons leaves hyperspherical energy exactly unchanged.  In other words, **pairwise angular relationships—precisely what $\operatorname{HE}$ measures—are invariant under rotations and reflections**.



---  

## Why Stability of Hyperspherical Energy Correlates with Semantic Preservation  

The primary goal when adapting large text‑to‑image diffusion models to downstream tasks (such as subject‑driven synthesis or controllable generation) is to augment their behavior without erasing the rich semantic knowledge gathered during pretraining.  Conventional fine‑tuning approaches measure closeness solely by Euclidean distance between weight matrices ($‖ \M - \M^{0}‖$) or by imposing low‑rank constraints (e.g., LoRA).  However such constraints do not directly control *how* relative neuron directions evolve.

Hyperspherical energy offers a *structural* metric: it quantifies exactly those pairwise angles whose empirical studies have shown strong correspondence with semantics.  
Prior works cited in the source material observe that:

* Angular similarity between feature vectors aligns closely with class or object identity (Liu et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2020).  
* On classification tasks smaller hyperspherical energy correlates with better generalisation because it forces neurons apart; conversely for generative fine‑tuning one wishes **not** to alter these angles drastically so as not to disturb learned semantic manifolds.

Therefore an intuitive desideratum emerges: a good fine‑tuned diffusion model should exhibit **minimal change** in $\operatorname{HE}$ relative to its pretrained counterpart:

```
‖ HE(𝑾) − HE(𝑾⁰) ‖ ≈ 0 .
```

In practice this requirement is met by learning per‑layer orthogonal matrices $\bf R$ (the Orthogonal Fine‑tuning or OFT scheme). Because each such transformation preserves all pairwise distances on $S^{d−1}$ by construction, it guarantees exact equality  

$\operatorname{HE}(𝑾)=  $\operatorname{HE}(𝑾⁰)$ .  

Empirically, OFT (and its constrained variant COFT) produce markedly higher scores on subject fidelity metrics such as DINO and CLIP‑I compared with naïve DreamBooth fine‑tuning or LoRA updates—indicating better retention of identity information.  Moreover, qualitative inspection shows more faithful preservation of visual details when only directional changes are allowed while magnitudes remain untouched.



---  

## Toy Experiments Demonstrating Angular Dominance over Magnitude  

To illuminate why direction alone can encode virtually all image information, the authors performed an illustrative autoencoder study on flower images:

* **Training setup** – A conventional convolutional autoencoder was trained end‑to‑end using the standard inner product between input patches $\bf x$ and convolution kernels $\bf w$, i.e., $z=\bf w^{⊤}\bf x$.  
* **Test‑time variants** – After training, three alternative ways of computing feature maps were evaluated on previously unseen images:
  1️⃣ Using exactly the inner product employed during training (the baseline).  
  2️⃣ Using only *magnitudes*: replace each inner product by $\|\,z\,\|$, discarding any angle information between $\bf x$ and $\bf w$.  
  3️⃣ Using only *angular* information: compute cosine similarity $(\, {\bf x}^{⊤}{\bf w} )/(‖x‖‖w‖)$ which isolates direction while normalizing away magnitude.
* **Results** – Reconstructions obtained via variant (c) recovered the original flowers almost perfectly—pixel‑wise structures and colors remained intact—whereas variant (b) produced blurry or indistinguishable outputs failing to convey recognizable content. Notably, this result emerged even though cosine activation was never used during training; thus directionality alone already stored sufficient semantic detail in learned filters.

The experiment corroborates two crucial insights exploited by OFT:

1. **Semantic richness resides predominantly in angles**, confirming observations from spectral analysis where phase (angular component) carries most image semantics while magnitude bears little discriminative power.
2. **Altering neuron directions selectively changes output semantics**, suggesting that fine‑tuning through pure rotations can steer generation without dismantling existing knowledge—a principle formalized by enforcing orthogonality.

Consequently hyperspherical energy—a global measure built from precisely these pairwise angles—serves as an elegant proxy for monitoring whether fine‐tuning respects or disrupts semantic structure.



---  

### Concluding Remarks on Hyperspherical Energy  

Hyperspherical energy condenses every pairwise angular relationship among neurons into a single scalar that reflects how uniformly those directions populate $S^{d-1}$. Its invariance under orthogonal transformations gives rise to Orthogonal Fine‑tuning: by learning only an orthogonal matrix per layer one can rotate every neuron consistently, thereby preserving `HE` exactly while still allowing flexible adaptation needed for downstream generation tasks. Empirical evidence—from toy autoencoders illustrating angular supremacy to full diffusion model fine‑tunes achieving superior subject fidelity—demonstrates that keeping `HE` stable correlates strongly with preserving learned semantics in vision models.



--- 

> **Key Takeaway:** When adapting powerful generative networks, protecting the delicate web of directional relationships encoded in weight vectors—captured succinctly by hyperspherical energy—is far more effective than merely limiting Euclidean parameter drift. Orthogonal transformations provide exactly this protection while granting sufficient expressiveness for controlled image synthesis.

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Theory and Objective Formulation  

In this chapter we develop the mathematical foundations underlying **Orthogonal Finetuning** (OFT), the core finetuning paradigm proposed for adapting large text‑to‑image diffusion models. We begin by formulating a principled objective that captures the desire to preserve the relational structure among neurons after finetuning. Then we show how minimizing this objective forces the finetuned weight matrix to be an **orthogonal transformation** of its pretrained counterpart. The derivation naturally leads us to discuss why the transformation is applied **layer‑shared** rather than independently per neuron, how this operation can be interpreted as a change of canonical coordinates rather than a modification of magnitudes, and finally we connect these ideas to an intuitive analogy from Fourier analysis—phase versus magnitude—highlighting why angular information dominates semantic content.

---

## Formal Problem Statement  

Consider a single linear layer of a diffusion model with weight matrix  

\[
\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n},
\]

where \(d\) denotes the dimensionality of the input feature vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) and \(n\) denotes the number of output neurons (or channels). Let \(\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\) be the \(i\)-th column of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\); this column corresponds precisely to one neuron’s set of incoming weights.  

The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) of a weight matrix quantifies the uniformity—or lack thereof—of its normalized columns on the unit hypersphere \(\mathbb{S}^{\,d-1}\). Formally,

\[
\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf{W}) 
:= 
\sum_{i\neq j} 
\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}:=
\frac{\mathbf w_{i}}{\|\mathbf w_{i}\|}.
\tag{1}
\]

The term \(\bigl\|\widehat{\mathbf w}_{i}-\widehat{\mathbf w}_{j}\bigr\|^{-1}\)
is larger when two normalized neurons are close on the sphere,
and smaller when they are far apart.
Consequently, lower HE reflects more uniformly spread directions,
while higher HE indicates clustering or redundancy among directions.
Empirically, **hyperspherical similarity**—the cosine similarity
between pairs of neurons—has been shown to encode rich semantic structure
in vision models \citep{liu2017deep,chen2020angular}.


When adapting a pretrained model \(\mathbf W^{0}\) to a downstream task,
the naive approach is simply to update each entry via gradient descent.
However such unrestricted updates may dramatically alter pairwise angular
relations among columns, thereby destroying the very structure that makes
the pretrained model effective at generating diverse visual content.
A more faithful desideratum is therefore:

> **Preserve hyperspherical energy:**  
> After finetuning we would like  
> \(\operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W)\approx
>   \operatorname{HE}(\mathbf W^{0})\).

Mathematically this reads

\[

        HE}(\mathbf W)
        - 
        \operatorname{
        HE}(\mathbf W^{0})
   \Bigr\|.

Because hypothesis spaces are high‑dimensional,

A common transform across an entire layer gives us precisely:
*the desired preservation* while *drastically reducing* trainable parameters—
we only need one orthogonal matrix per layer instead of one per neuron.
This property makes OFT both mathematically sound and practically efficient.

## Linear‑Algebra Interpretation: Rotating Coordinates Instead of Scaling Magnitudes  

Let us reinterpret Eq.(3)

\(  
   {\bf W}= {\bf RW}^{0},
   
$
as an operation on linear maps rather than on individual scalar weights.
A fully–connected layer realizes the map 

\( f_{\! 0}:~x↦ {\bigl({W}^{0}}\bigr)^T x .                              \
\
Applying an orthogonal matrix on its left yields  

\( f(x)=({RW}^{0})^Tx
     ={W}^{0\,T}{R}^Tx .                                   \
\
Since an orthogonal matrix satisfies  
${R}^T={R^{-1}},$
this can be read as **pre‑multiplying** the input feature vector by ${R}^{-1}$ before passing it through the original pretrained map $f_{\! 0}$:

\fbox{$ f(x)= f_{\! 0}( {R^{-1}}\!x ) . $}

Thus OFT does not alter how ${W}^{0}$ combines input components;
instead it **changes the basis** in which those inputs are expressed.
From a geometric perspective,
${R}$ rotates or reflects the entire input space,
so every neuron’s coordinate representation changes coherently while their relative angles stay invariant.
Because Euclidean norms are untouched (orthogonality guarantees length preservation),
the magnitude information carried by ${W}^{0}$ remains intact:
only orientations—the “directions” crucial for encoding semantics—are shifted.  

Contrast this with naive additive updates such as  
${W}= {W}^{~Ø}+Δ,$ where $Δ$ may arbitrarily stretch some directions,
or even expand norm values independently across neurons.
Such unconstrained alterations typically perturb both magnitudes *and* angles,
breaking hyperspherical uniformity and consequently demolishing pretraining knowledge.
The OFT formulation avoids both pitfalls simultaneously:
it respects original magnitudes indirectly‐through normalisation inherent in HE,
and guarantees exact preservation of inter‑neuron angles via a rigid rotation.

## □ Intuition from Fourier Phase versus Magnitude Analogy  

A classic result from signal processing states that **the phase spectrum carries most visual semantics**, whereas magnitude mainly determines low‑level intensity contrast.
Given an image $I$, its two‑dimensional Fourier transform yields:

```
F(u,v)= M(u,v)\,\exp(j·φ(u,v)),
```

where $M$ denotes magnitude and $φ$ denotes phase.
If one reconstructs an image using $M$ from one picture but $φ$ from another,
the resulting visual content nearly matches that of *the image providing φ*, despite having completely unrelated magnitudes.
Intuitively:
*• The amplitude tells “how much” frequency exists;*
*• The phase tells “where” those frequencies line up.*

Consider now a neural network layer whose normalized weight vectors lie on $\mathbb S^{d-1}$.
Each vector’s direction encodes *relative alignment* among features—exactly analogous
to Fourier phase—while its norm corresponds loosely to amplitude.
When we multiply all column vectors simultaneously by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$,
we preserve every pairwise angle:
this operation mirrors rotating *all phases together* while leaving amplitudes untouched.
Consequently,
just as swapping phase spectra enables radical yet semantics‑preserving image manipulation,
applying a shared orthogonal rotation allows us to steer a diffusion model toward new tasks (e.g., injecting a subject identity or adhering to edge maps) **without discarding** its pretrained semantic knowledge.
Hence OFT harnesses exactly what Fourier intuition foretells:
*semantically meaningful control ≈ manipulation of angular structure*, while *preserving generation quality ≈ holding magnitude constant*.

## Summary  

We have formally grounded Orthogonal Finetuning within a rigorous optimisation framework:

1. **Objective formulation:** Minimising hyperspherical‑energy discrepancy forces invariance
   under any admissible transform;
2. **Derived condition:** Zero discrepancy occurs iff there exists an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$ such that $\mathbf W=\mathbf RW^{0}$;
3. **Layer sharing:** The orthogonal map must be identical for all neurons within a layer – otherwise pairwise angular relations collapse;
4. **Geometric view:** Multiplying by $\mathbf R$ merely rotates the canonical coordinate system while preserving norms;
5. **Fourier analogy:** Like preserving phase while discarding magnitude in frequency domain synthesis, OFT preserves angular relationships—the principal carriers of semantics—in neural representations.

Through these lenses OFT emerges not merely as a heuristically chosen finetuning trick but as a theoretically justified method that maintains structural integrity at every step. This foundation informs subsequent chapters where practical parameterizations (Cayley transform, block‐diagonal factorisation), constrained variants (COFT), and empirical applications (subject‑driven generation, controllable generation) will be explored.
