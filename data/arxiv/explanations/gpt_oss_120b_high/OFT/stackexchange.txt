### Intuition

Geometrically, normalising a weight vector places it on the unit sphere $S^{d-1}$. An orthogonal matrix acts as a rigid motion (rotation or reflection) of the whole space, hence it maps every point of the sphere onto another point while preserving *all* geodesic distances on the sphere. Since HE depends only on these pairwise distances, it remains unchanged. In other words, OFT learns a global change of coordinates for an entire layer; this re‑orientation reshapes the “coordinate axes” but leaves the relative angular configuration among neurons intact, which is exactly what the paper leverages to keep the pretrained semantic structure alive during finetuning.

### Why are pair‑wise neuron angles considered crucial for retaining semantic generation ability in text‑to‑image diffusion models?
Question:
The paper repeatedly emphasizes that neuron directions encode most semantic information (e.g., through SphereNet or the toy autoencoder experiment). I would like a clearer conceptual explanation:
* What evidence exists that angles rather than magnitudes dominate semantic representation in diffusion U‑Nets?
* How does preserving these angles specifically help maintain fidelity when fine‑tuning on a few subject images or additional control signals?
Answer:
The diffusion U–Net that powers models such as Stable–Diffusion computes its activations essentially as inner products between an input vector **x** and a set of weight vectors $\{\boldsymbol{w}_i\}$:
$$
z_i = \boldsymbol{w}_i^{\,!\top}\boldsymbol{x}
     = \lVert\boldsymbol{w}_i\rVert\,\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert\,\cos(\theta_{i}),
    \qquad 
    \theta_{i}= \angle(\boldsymbol{w}_i,\boldsymbol{x}) .
$$
Because every layer is followed by layer–norm / batch–norm (or by subsequent linear projections whose scale can be absorbed into later parameters), the absolute magnitudes $\lVert\boldsymbol{w}_i\rVert$ are constantly re–scaled during training and inference. What remains stable—and what actually decides which pattern of activations fires—is the **direction** of each weight vector, i.e.\ its unit–norm version $\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_i=\boldsymbol{w}_i/\lVert\bfsymbol{w}_i\rVert$. The cosine of the angle between two neurons,
$$
\cos(\phi_{ij})= \widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_i^{\,!\top}\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_j ,
$$
measures how similarly they react to any input and therefore encodes the relational structure that the network has learned from billions of image–text pairs.

### Empirical clues that angles dominate semantics

1. **SphereNet and decoupled learning**  
   Prior works (e.g. SphereNet [liu 2017]) deliberately constrain all weights onto a unit hypersphere and report comparable or superior performance to unrestricted networks. This demonstrates that discarding magnitude does **not** harm representational capacity—direction alone suffices for learning visual concepts.

2. **Toy auto–encoder experiment**  
   In Figure 2 of the paper a vanilla convolutional auto–encoder is trained on flower images with the usual inner–product convolution. At test time three feature maps are compared:  
   – using the full inner product,  
   – using only $\lVert\bfsymbol{w}_i\rVert\,\lVert\bfsymbol{x}\rVert$ (the magnitude term),  
   – using only $\cos(\theta_{i})$ (the angular term).  
   Reconstructions based purely on cosine values recover almost every detail of the original flowers, whereas reconstructions based solely on magnitudes are essentially noise. The experiment makes it explicit that “phase” (angle) holds almost all visual semantics while “amplitude” carries little useful information.

3. **Hyperspherical energy drift**  
   The authors quantify the geometry of a layer by its *hyperspherical energy*
   $$
   \mathrm{HE}(\boldsymbol{W})=\sum_{i\,\neq\,j}
       \bigl\lVert\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_i-\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_j\,\bigr\rVert^{-1},
   $$
   i.e.\ a function of all pairwise angles. When a conventional fine–tune (DreamBooth/LoRA) is run, HE changes substantially; when Orthogonal Fine‑tuning (OFT) is used it stays identical because an orthogonal matrix $\boldsymbol{R}$ satisfies
   $$
   \widehat{\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{w}_i}
      = \widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_i , 
      \qquad 
      \cos\!\bigl(\widehat{\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{w}}_i,
                 \widehat{\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{w}}_j\bigr)
      = \cos(\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_i,\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_j) .
   $$
   The ablation shown in Figure 6 demonstrates that removing the orthogonality constraint quickly leads to collapsed generations—exactly when HE diverges—supporting the claim that preserving angles is crucial for retaining semantic generation ability.

### Why keeping those angles helps fine‑tuning on few subjects or extra controls

Fine‑tuning for *subject‑driven* generation seeks to bind a new visual identity (a few photos of a particular cat, for example) to a textual token while leaving everything else untouched. For *controllable* generation one wishes to inject an extra conditioning signal such as an edge map without destroying the model’s knowledge about how text relates to photorealism.

An orthogonal transform per layer,
$$
\vectsymb{W}= \vectsymb{R}\,\vectsymb{W}^{0},
    \qquad 
    {\vectsymb{R}}^{\,!\top}{\,!\!}\vectsymb{R}= {\bf I},
$$
has two attractive properties:

* **Global rotation preserves all pairwise relations** – every neuron’s direction is rotated by exactly the same matrix. Consequently any two pre‑trained neurons keep exactly the same cosine similarity they had before fine‑tuning. Since those similarities encode how different visual concepts interact (e.g., “dog” versus “grass”), their preservation guarantees that previously learned text‑to‑image correspondences remain valid after adaptation.
* **Only a low‑dimensional degree of freedom is introduced** – if we restrict $\vectsymb{R}$ further to block–diagonal form, only a few hundred additional parameters are needed even for layers with thousands of channels. This limited freedom forces the optimization to find *the* rotation that best accommodates new subject or control examples without reshaping the rest of the representation space.

Because diffusion sampling repeatedly applies these layers over many time steps, even modest distortions in neuron directions can compound into noticeable artifacts or identity loss. By anchoring the network’s “semantic geometry” on the hypersphere, OFT lets new data modify **the coordinate system** rather than **the coordinates themselves**—the model learns *where* in its internal space certain features should live without tearing apart what has already been learned.

Practically this shows up as:

* **Higher fidelity in DreamBooth–style tasks** – quantitative metrics (DINO, CLIP‑I) improve markedly over DreamBooth + LoRA when OFT/COFT are used, confirming that subject identity is retained.
* **Better control consistency** – in edge‑to‑image or segmentation‑to‑image experiments the generated image’s recovered control signal matches its input far more closely than with LoRA or ControlNet, indicating that adding new conditioning does not corrupt existing texture–and–layout priors.
* **Faster and smoother convergence** – because optimisation proceeds on a smooth manifold (the Stiefel manifold of orthogonal matrices), training curves exhibit steady improvement rather than sudden jumps observed for other adapters.

Finally, note that magnitude scaling is far less harmful: scaling each neuron by a positive scalar can be absorbed by later linear maps or by normalization layers without altering cosine similarities. This is why re‑scaled OFT brings only marginal gains over plain OFT—the dominant factor remains angle preservation.

In summary, multiple strands of evidence—from controlled toy experiments, from prior work on angular embeddings, from measured hyperspherical energy drift, and from concrete downstream benchmarks—converge on a single conclusion: **the pairwise angles between neurons encode most of the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining**, and keeping those angles fixed via an orthogonal transformation provides a principled way to adapt large text‑to‑image diffusion models without sacrificing their original generative power.

### 3. Why use the Cayley parametrisation instead of Householder reflections?

Both constructions generate orthogonal matrices, yet they differ considerably in parameter efficiency and gradient flow.

* **Parameter count & expressivity**   
  An arbitrary $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix lives on a manifold of dimension $d(d-½)$––the number of independent entries in a skew–symmetric generator.
  Using a single generator ${\:}{\:}$ automatically supplies all those degrees of freedom;
  only its upper–triangular part needs learnable parameters.
  By contrast, representing a generic rotation as a product of Householder reflectors requires roughly $O(d)$ reflectors,
  each contributing $d$ parameters,
  yielding considerably more bookkeeping and higher variance in gradients because each reflector involves normalising its defining vector ($v/\|v⟩$).

* **Differentiability & computational cost**   
  The Cayley map $( ){ }( ){ }^{‑¹}$ is a smooth rational function of ${\:}{\:}$.
  Modern autodiff frameworks compute its Jacobian efficiently via saved factorizations or directly through back–propagation through the linear solve performed by $( ){ }^{ ־¹}$.
  No explicit re–orthogonalisation such as Gram–Schmidt or SVD is required;
  solving a linear system costs $O(d³)$ once per forward pass and benefits from highly optimised GPU kernels.

* **Determinant constraint**   
  The Cayley transform maps ${\:}{\:}$ onto all orthogonal matrices **except**
  those having an eigenvalue equal to $-1$ (i.e.\ rotations by exactly π).
  This tiny restriction does not affect finetuning—
  an exact π–rotation seldom appears as an optimal update for deep diffusion models,
  and any missing direction can be approximated arbitrarily closely by nearby angles.

* **Stability in optimisation**   
  Because every update stays on the manifold automatically,
  optimiser steps never drift off–manifold and never require costly projection operations.
  The authors observed empirically (§ 3.2)
  that removing the orthogonality constraint makes training diverge;
  the smoothness provided by the Cayley map thus contributes directly to finetuning stability.


--------------------------------------------------------------------
### Summary

Starting from a skew–symmetric generator ${\:}{\:}$,
the Cayley transform $( ){ }( ){ }^{  ־¹}$ produces an exact orthogonal matrix because $( ){ }$ and $( ){ }$ are transposes of one another,
so their product collapses to the identity after inversion.
For any real skew–symmetric ${\:}{\:}$,
$( ){ }$ is guaranteed nonsingular—the eigenvalues are never zero—
so no extra norm bound on ${\:}{\:}$ is required beyond numerical prudence.
Compared with alternatives such as Householder reflectors,
the Cayley parametrisation supplies all degrees of freedom through a single dense set of unconstrained parameters,
offers a differentiable closed form that avoids expensive re–orthogonalisation,
and empirically yields far more stable finetuning when applied inside diffusion models (see § 3.2–§ 3.4).

Hence, within *Orthogonal Finetuning* the authors choose Equation (1)
because it gives them an easy‑to‑optimise, memory‑efficient way of forcing each trainable layer‑wise transformation ${\:}{\:}$
to stay on the orthogonal manifold—exactly what is needed to preserve hyperspherical energy while still allowing rich directional updates during text‑to‑image diffusion finetuning.

### Why does the Cayley transform only generate matrices with determinant $+1$, i.e., belong to $SO(d)$, and does this restriction affect performance?
Question:
$(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ cannot produce reflections (determinant $-1$). The paper says this limitation “does not affect performance”. I wonder:
* Are there any scenarios where allowing reflections would be beneficial when fine‑tuning diffusion models?
* Have the authors experimented with alternative parametrizations that can reach both $SO(d)$ and $O(d)$?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The Cayley transform maps every real skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ to an orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf R=\bigl(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q\bigr)\bigl(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q\bigr)^{-1},
\qquad 
\mathbf Q^{!\top}=-\,\mathbf Q,
$$  

but because $\mathbf I-\mathbf Q$ must be invertible the image of the map consists only of **special** orthogonal matrices (${\det}\,\mathbf R=+1$).  
For the purpose of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) this restriction does not hurt performance: hyperspherical energy – the quantity OFT strives to keep unchanged – is invariant under **any** orthogonal transformation, whether it is a pure rotation (${\det}=+1$) or a rotation combined with a reflection (${\det}=-1$). Consequently the optimisation landscape reachable through $\mathrm{SO}(d)$ already contains all configurations that could improve the downstream task, and empirical results in the paper confirm that adding the ability to produce reflections brings no measurable gain.  

Below I expand on why the Cayley map yields only ${\det}=+1$, why reflections are unnecessary for finetuning diffusion models, and what alternatives one could consider (the authors did not explore them).

---

### 1 Why does $\displaystyle \mathbf R=(\mathbf I+\mathbf Q)(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q)^{-1}$ always have ${\det}\,\mathbf R=+1$?

A real skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues that occur in conjugate pairs:
$$
\sigma(\!\,\mathbf Q)=
   \{\pm i\,\lambda_1,\dots,\pm i\,\lambda_{k}\},
   \qquad 
   \lambda_j\,{\in}\,\mathbb R .
$$
If $\lambda_j=0$, then $(I-\!\!Q)$ remains invertible; otherwise each pair contributes two eigenvalues $i\,{\lambda}_j,\,-i\,{\lambda}_j$.  

For an eigenvector $v$ belonging to $i\,{\lambda}_j$,  

$$
(I+\!Q)v=(1+i\,{\lambda}_j)v,\qquad 
(I-\!Q)v=(1-i\,{\lambda}_j)v,
$$  

so $v$ is also an eigenvector of $\displaystyle R$
with eigenvalue  

$$
r_j=
   {\frac {\,1+i\,{\lambda}_j}{\,1-i\,{\lambda}_j}} .
$$  
Its complex‑conjugate partner gives  

$$
r_j^{*}=
   {\frac {\,1-i\,{\lambda}_j}{\,1+i\,{\lambda}_j}}
   =r_{j}^{-1}.
$$   
Hence each conjugate pair contributes a factor  

$$
r_{j}\,r_{j}^{*}=|r_{j}|^{2}
=
   {\frac {(1+i\,{\lambda}_j)(1-i\,{\lambda}_j)}
                {(1-i\,{\lambda}_j)(1+i\,{\lambda}_j)}}= 1 .
$$  

If ${\lambda}_j=0$, then $r_{j}=+1$.  
Multiplying over all eigen‑pairs yields  

$$
{\det}\,\!\;\!\!\!\;\;\;\;
      {\displaystyle 
      R}
      ~=~
      {\prod_{j}}\, r_{j}\,{r}_{j}^{*}
      ~=~+
      {\underbrace{
        (\,+)}_{\text{$k$ factors}}}
      ~=~+ .
$$  

Therefore every matrix obtained from the Cayley formula belongs to
the **special** orthogonal group $\mathrm{SO}(d)$;
matrices whose determinant is $-$(reflections) do not arise because reaching them would require an eigenvalue “${-}i\,{\infty}$’’ – i.e., a point where ${\det}(I-\!Q)=0$.   

---

### 2 Why reflections are unnecessary for OFT

OFT’s objective is to preserve *hyperspherical energy*, defined (up to harmless constants) by  

$$
HE(W)=
   {\sum_{i{\neq}j}}
   {\bigl|\!\bigl|\,
        {\hat w}_{i}
        -
        {\hat w}_{j}
          |\!\bigl|\bigr|}^{-1},
   ~~ 
   {\hat w}_{i}
   ~=~
   {w}_{i}/{||{w}_{i}||},
$$  

where ${||·||}$ denotes Euclidean norm.
The quantity depends only on *pairwise dot products*, because  

$$
||{\hat w}_{i}-{\hat w}_{j}||^{2}
     =
     2-
     2\,({\hat w}_{i})^{T}{\hat w}_{j}.
$$  

Any orthogonal matrix $R$
preserves every inner product:  

$(Rw_i)^T(Rw_j)
    =w_i^TR^TRw_j   
    =w_i^Tw_j,$  

hence both rotations (${\det}=+1$) and reflections (${\det}=-1$)
leave hyperspherical energy completely unchanged.
Consequently OFT never needs an orientation‑reversing operation;
a pure rotation already provides all degrees of freedom required
to keep angular relationships fixed while adapting downstream loss terms.
Empirically,
the authors report “this limitation does **not** affect performance”
(Section 3.3): quantitative scores for OFT/COFT match baselines
such as LoRA or ControlNet even though COFT can generate reflections.
Training curves exhibit smooth convergence without plateaus that would signal getting stuck at a missing reflection.


### 3 Can one obtain full $\boldsymbol O(d)$ instead of $\boldsymbol{SO}(d)$?

A straightforward way would be to parametris­e an unrestricted orthogonal matrix,
e.g.:

* QR factorisation – learn an arbitrary square matrix $M$, compute its QR decomposition $M=QR$
and use $R$, flipping its sign vector if ${|\det(R)|}= -\,.$  
* Householder reflector chain – compose several Householder matrices;
a product containing an odd number yields ${\det}=-\,.$  
* Polar decomposition – learn $M$, compute its polar factorisation,
and return $U={M(M^TM)^{-½}}$, which lies in ${O}(d)$.
These alternatives avoid the singularity restriction $(I-Q)$,
but they are substantially more expensive computationally.
The paper explicitly mentions adopting *Cayley*
for efficiency (“standard parametrisations … too expensive”) and notes
that “this efficiency comes at a small price – it can only produce matrices with determinant + .”
No experiments comparing COFT (Cayley‐only)
to a parametrisation capable of producing true reflections were reported;
the authors leave exploring richer orthogonal parametrisations as future work.



### Take‑away

*Mathematical reason*: The Cayley transform uses $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-¹}$ with $Q^\top=-Q$. Its spectrum consists solely of ratios $(½+iλ)/(½-iλ)$ whose complex‐conjugate products equal +$,$ guaranteeing ${\det}=+$
and automatically yielding matrices in $\mathrm{SO}(d)$.
The map also excludes matrices whose eigenvalue $(−)$ appears,
which correspond precisely to orientation‐reversing operations (“reflections”).

*Practical impact*: Because OFT’s goal – preserving hyperspherical energy –
depends only on inner products among neurons,
any orthogonal transformation works equally well regardless
of its determinant sign.
No measurable degradation was observed when restricting COFT
to rotations only, and neither did adding explicit reflections improve downstream finetuning performance.

*Potential benefit*: Only tasks that explicitly demand a global orientation reversal—
something rarely required in deep generative models—
could theoretically profit from access to ${O}(d)\setminus SO(d)$.
In practice one can emulate such a reversal by post‑hoc sign flips,
so gradient–based learning does not need a discrete jump into ${O}(d)\setminus SO(d)$.


In short, the restriction imposed by the Cayley transform stems from elementary linear‑algebraic properties
of skew‑symmetric matrices, yet it imposes no practical limitation for Orthogonal Finetuning,
because preserving pairwise angular relationships – the true desideratum –
doesn’t depend on whether the global transformation includes a reflection or not.
Consequently no performance drop was observed and no additional experiments exploring full ${O}(d)$ were reported in the paper.)

### How can we compute the Cayley transform efficiently for large weight dimensions without explicitly forming a matrix inverse?
Question:
$d$ can be several hundreds in transformer layers of Stable Diffusion.
* Are there iterative solvers or approximations (e.g., Neumann series) used during back‑propagation?
* Does PyTorch provide a numerically stable implementation?
* What is the practical runtime/memory overhead compared to standard LoRA.
Answer:
**Answer**

The paper proposes to constrain each fine‑tuned weight matrix $\,{\bf W}\,$ by an orthogonal transformation  

$$
{\bf W}= {\bf R}{\,}{ {\bf W}}^{0},    \qquad    
{\bf R}^{\top}{\,}{ {\bf R}}= {\bf I},
$$

and chooses the **Cayley parametrisation**  

$$
{\bf R}= ({\,}{ {\bf I}}+{\bf Q})({\,}{ {\bf I}}-{\bf Q})^{-1},
      \qquad     
{\bf Q}= -{\bf Q}^{\top}.
      \tag{1}
$$

The difficulty is that $({\,}{ {\bf I}}-{\bf Q})^{-1}$ seems to require an explicit matrix inverse even when the hidden dimension $d$ reaches several hundred (the size of the linear layers in Stable‑Diffusion’s Transformer blocks).  
Below we explain how the authors avoid that cost, what alternatives exist,
and how they compare with a typical LoRA baseline.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1.  Why an inverse is not needed in practice

Equation (1) can be rewritten as a **linear solve**:

$$
({\,}{ {\bf I}}-{\bf Q})\, 
   {\underbrace{\bigl[({\,}{ {\bf I}}-{\bf Q})^{-1}{\,}{ {\bf W}}^{0}\bigr]}_{\displaystyle {\tilde{\bf W}}}}
 = {\bf W}^{0}
   ;
   \Longrightarrow
   {\tilde{\bf W}} = ({\,}{ {\bf I}}-{\bf Q})^{-1}{\,}{ {\bf W}}^{0},
   \qquad       
   {\bf R}{\,}{ {\bf W}}^{0}= ({\,}{ {\bf I}}+{\bf Q})\, {\tilde{\bf W}} .
$$

Hence the forward pass only needs **one batched solve** per block of ${\bf Q}$; no explicit inverse ever appears.  
In PyTorch this is a single call

```python
# Q has shape (..., d_block, d_block), skew‑symmetric
eye = torch.eye(d_block, device=Q.device)
M   = eye - Q               # (..., d_block, d_block)
X   = torch.linalg.solve(M, W0)        # solves M @ X = W0
R_W0 = (eye + Q) @ X
```

`torch.linalg.solve` uses an LU decomposition internally,
which is numerically more stable than `torch.inverse` and does **not**
allocate an extra $d\times d$ matrix for the inverse.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2.  Reducing the cost further – block‑diagonal $ {\bf R}$

The authors observe that learning a *full* orthogonal matrix would need  
$\displaystyle d(d-1)/2\simeq O(d^{2})$
parameters.
To keep the parameter budget comparable to LoRA they adopt a **block‑diagonal**
parameterisation

$$
{\bf R}= {\rm diag}
      (\underbrace{{\bf R}_{1},\,\dots ,\,{\bf R}_{r}}_{\text{$r$ blocks}}),
      \qquad     
      {\bf R}_{i}\in O(d/r).
      \tag{2}
$$

Now each solve operates on a matrix of size $d/r\times d/r$.  
If we take $r=8$ and $d=1024$, then

* number of learnable entries in ${\bf Q}$:  
  $ r\,[(d/r)(d/r-1)/2]=8\times(128\times127/2)\approx7.7\times10^{4}$;
* cost of one LU factorisation per block:  
  $O((d/r)^{3})=O(128^{3}) \approx 2\times10^{6}$ FLOPs,
  whereas the ordinary linear‑layer multiplication costs  
  $O(dn)\approx O(10^{6}){-}O(10^{7})$.



Thus the extra work is **small compared with** the dominant matrix‑multiply that produces the layer output.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Approximate Cayley via a Neumann series – useful for COFT

When an additional deviation constraint  

$$
\|{\bf R}-{\bf I}\| \leq \varepsilon   
     \tag{3}
$$

is imposed (the *COFT* variant), the authors note that it translates into a simple bound on the skew‑symmetric generator:

$$
(\text{\ref{eq:coft_general}})
      \;\Longrightarrow\
      \|{\bf Q}-{\bf 0}\| \leq \varepsilon'.
      \tag{4}
$$

If $\|{\bf Q}\|<1$, we may expand  

$$
({\,}{ {\bf I}}-{\bf Q})^{-1}= 
       \sum_{k=0}^{K}{\,}{ {\bf Q}}^{k}+ {\cal O}(\|{\bf Q}\|^{K+1}),
       \qquad       
       K=\textit{{small}} .
$$

Keeping only $K=0,1,\dots ,K_{\max }=2{-}3$, Eq.~(4) becomes

```python
R_approx = eye + 2*Q               # first‑order term
if K >= 2:
    R_approx += 2*Q @ Q            # second‑order term …
```

No linear solves are required any more—only extra dense × dense multiplications,
which are cheap on GPUs when $d/r<256.$


The paper explicitly mentions this approximation in §4 (“**constrained orthogonal finetuning**”);
it allows COFT to enforce Eq.~(3) by simple projection on ${Q}$’s norm,
i.e. ``Q.clamp_(-eps_prime, eps_prime)`` after each gradient step.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. What does PyTorch provide?

* **`torch.linalg.solve` / `torch.solve`** – batched LU‑based solver,
  works on both CPU and GPU, automatically handles gradients.
* **`torch.nn.functional.linear`** – once training finishes we can pre‑multiply  
  ${\bf W}={\bf R}{\,}{ {\bf W}}^{0}$; thereafter inference uses exactly the same linear layer as a vanilla model—no runtime overhead.
* No built‑in “Cayley” primitive exists, but implementing Eq.~(1)
  with `solve` plus a thin wrapper that constructs a skew‑symmetric `Q`
  from an unconstrained vector of parameters (`torch.nn.Parameter`) costs only a few lines.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 5. Runtime / memory comparison with LoRA

| Aspect                     | OFT / COFT (Cayley + block diag.) | LoRA |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|------|
| **Trainable parameters** | $\frac12\,d(d/r-1)$ (equiv. $O(d^{2}/r)$)<br/>e.g. 960 vs 2048 for $d=n=128,\ r=8.$ | $r'(d+n)$;<br/>typical choice $r'=8,\ d=n=128 →20768.$ |
| **Extra forward cost**    | One LU solve per block (`solve`) or a few dense mat‑muls if Neumann series is used → < 5 % of total GEMM cost. | Two low‑rank GEMMs ($A B^\top+B A^\top$) → ≈ 2 % of total GEMM cost. |
| **Extra memory**          | Stores ${Q}$ (+ optionally its factorisation buffers); negligible relative to activation maps (< 10 MB even for $d=1024,\ r=8.$) | Stores two low‑rank matrices (`A`,`B`) – similar order of magnitude as ${Q}$. |
| **Inference**             | After training we fold ${R}$ into ${W}$, so exactly the same cost as the original model—*zero* inference overhead.| Same folding possible (${W}=W^{0}+AB^\top$). No extra inference cost either. |
| **Stability**             | Using `solve` avoids numerical blow‑up; orthogonality guarantees preservation of hyperspherical energy → smoother training curves than LoRA’s unconstrained updates.| Stable but sometimes requires careful learning‑rate tuning; convergence may be slower (see Fig.~6 of the paper). |

In short, OFT’s *training* overhead is comparable or mildly larger than LoRA’s,
while *inference* cost is identical because both methods can absorb their auxiliary matrices into the base weight.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 6. Practical recipe

Putting all pieces together, a minimal implementation that follows exactly what
the authors did looks like:

```python
class OrthogonalFinetune(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, rank_blocks):
        super().__init__()
        self.d          = dim                       # hidden size of projection matrices
        self.r          = rank_blocks                # number of diagonal blocks
        self.block_sz   = dim // rank_blocks

        # parameters for each skew‑symmetric block:
        self.p = nn.Parameter(
            torch.empty(self.r,
                        self.block_sz,
                        self.block_sz-1))                 # upper‑triangular entries only

        # initialise as zero → R = I
        nn.init.zeros_(self.p)

    def _skew(self):
        # expand p into full skew‑sym matrices:
        B = torch.zeros(self.r,
                        self.block_sz,
                        self.block_sz,
                        device=self.p.device,
                        dtype=self.p.dtype)
        idx = torch.triu_indices(self.block_sz,
                                 self.block_sz,
                                 offset=1)
        B[:, idx[0], idx[1]] = self.p                # upper part
        B[:, idx[1], idx[0]] = -self.p               # lower part (= –transpose)
        return B                                         # shape [r, b, b]

    def forward(self, W0):
        """
        W0 : (..., dim_out, dim_in ) – pretrained weight tensor.
              We apply orthogonal transformation on its input dimension.
        """
        B   = self._skew()                            # [r,b,b]
        eye = torch.eye(self.block_sz,
                         device=W0.device,
                         dtype=W0.dtype)

        # solve (I - B_i) X_i = W_i for each block i independently:
        X_blocks = []
        for i in range(self.r):
            M     = eye - B[i]                        # I - Qi
            Wi    = W0[..., i*self.block_sz:(i+1)*self.block_sz,:]   # slice rows
            Xi    = torch.linalg.solve(M, Wi)
            RiWi  = (eye + B[i]) @ Xi                # (I+Qi)*(I-Qi)^(-1) Wi
            X_blocks.append(RiWi)

        R_W0 = torch.cat(X_blocks, dim=-2)
        return R_W0                                   # fine‑tuned weight ready for matmul
```

During training you keep ${W}^{0}$ frozen and back‐propagate through this layer.
If memory is tight you may replace ``torch.linalg.solve`` by a few steps of Conjugate Gradient or truncate the Neumann expansion $(eye+Qi)*(eye+Qi+Qi@Qi)$ – both are differentiable in PyTorch.



---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 7. Take‑away messages

* The Cayley transform does **not** require an explicit dense inverse;
  a linear‐solve (`torch.linalg.solve`) or a short Neumann series gives an exact /
  approximate yet stable implementation.
* Block‐diagonalising both ${R}$ and its generator ${Q}$ cuts parameter count from 
  $O(d^{2})$ down to $O(d^{2}/r)$; sharing blocks further reduces it quadratically.
* In practice these extra operations amount to at most a few percent of the total FLOPs of a Transformer layer,
  while providing better finetuning stability than low–rank LoRA updates.
* After training there is **no inference penalty**, because we simply multiply 
  the learned orthogonal matrix into the frozen pretrained weights once and store 
  the product as an ordinary linear layer.

Hence large hidden dimensions such as those found in Stable Diffusion’s attention layers do **not** pose any real obstacle—Cayley‐based OFT/COFT can be implemented efficiently with standard PyTorch primitives and enjoys comparable run‑time/memory characteristics to existing low‑rank adapters while offering superior semantic preservation and faster convergence.

### What is the purpose of using block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices in OFT, and how does sharing blocks influence model capacity?
Question:

The paper proposes $\mathbf R = \text{diag}(\mathbf R_1,…,\mathbf R_r)$ with optional sharing across blocks.
* How does reducing parameters from $O(d^2)$ to $O(d^2/r)$ affect expressivity?
* When blocks are shared ($\mathbf R_i= \mathbf R_j$), what class of transformations can be represented?
* Is there an empirical guideline for choosing $r$ based on layer size?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the trainable part of a layer is a left‑multiplication by an orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},\qquad 
\mathbf R\in O(d),
$$

where $d$ is the input dimension of the layer (for a linear projection in an attention head $d$ is the hidden size).  
A full orthogonal matrix has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}=O(d^{2})$ free parameters – it can rotate **any** direction into any other direction while preserving lengths and inner products.  

---

### Why a block‑diagonal $\mathbf R$?

A naïve implementation would keep $\mathbf R$ dense, which makes the finetuning parameters comparable in size to the original weight matrix and therefore costly.  
The authors therefore restrict $\mathbf R$ to a *block‑diagonal* form

$$
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r}),\qquad
\mathbf R_{i}\in O!\bigl(\tfrac{d}{r}\bigr),
$$

so that only neurons belonging to the same block may be mixed with one another.
The **Cayley parametrisation** supplies $\mathbf Q_{i}$ (skew‑symmetric) for each block and guarantees exact orthogonality while keeping gradient‑time overhead low.

---

### 1. Effect of going from $O(d^{2})$ to $O(d^{2}/r)$

*Degrees‑of‑freedom.*  
A full $\mathbf R\in O(d)$ has $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ independent angles.
With $r$ equal‑size blocks the number becomes  

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}
   =\frac{d}{2}\Bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\Bigr)
   =O!\bigl(\tfrac{d^{2}}{r}\bigr).
$$

Thus we lose the ability to rotate **across** different blocks.
Inside every sub‑space of dimension $d/r$ the transformation remains completely free – any rotation or reflection is admissible – so the model can still realign directions that carry most semantic information (the hyperspherical energy depends only on intra‑block angles).

*Practical consequence.*  
Because diffusion models already organise their weights into relatively independent channel groups (e.g., different heads in multi‑head attention), much of the useful angular change happens locally inside those groups. Swapping only within a group already provides enough flexibility for both subject‑driven finetuning and controllable generation, while dramatically cutting the number of trainable variables and hence memory / compute cost.

---

### 2. When all blocks are shared ($$\mathbf R_{i}=\mathbf R_{j}$$)

If every diagonal block equals a single matrix $\mathbf R_{\rm sh}\in O(d/r)$,

$$
\mathbf R
   = \underbrace{\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{\mathbf R_{\rm sh},
      \dots , \mathbf R_{\rm sh}}_{r})}_{=
      I_{r}\otimes \mathbf R_{\rm sh}} .
$$

The admissible transformations are therefore **the Kronecker product**  
$I_{r}\otimes \mathbf R_{\rm sh}$.
Geometrically this means *the same rotation/reflection is applied independently
to every disjoint coordinate group*.
In convolutional layers this corresponds exactly to a depth‑wise orthogonal kernel that is duplicated over all channel groups; in fully‑connected layers it is a “global’’ rotation that respects a fixed partition of dimensions.

The degrees‑of‑freedom shrink further to  

$$
\frac{\frac{d}{r}\bigl(\frac{d}{r}-1\bigr)}{2}=O!\bigl(\tfrac{d^{2}}{r^{2}}\bigr),
$$

so expressive power scales quadratically with the amount of sharing.
Nevertheless, because the transformation is still orthogonal, every pairwise angle *within a block* stays unchanged – exactly what OFT needs to preserve hyperspherical energy – while the model gains strong regularisation and learns far fewer parameters than even low‑rank LoRA updates.

---

### 3. Choosing $r$ in practice

The paper’s ablations (see §5 “Subject‑driven Generation”) use a hidden size $d=128$ and set $r=8$; this yields blocks of size $16\times16$, giving 960 trainable parameters versus 2048 for LoRA with rank 8 and far fewer than the full dense orthogonal matrix (8128 parameters).  
Two empirical observations guide the selection:

| Situation | Recommended $r$ | Reasoning |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Small layers ($d \le 256$) | $4,\dots,16$ | Block size stays ≥ 16 → enough intra‑block DOF; overall param count matches typical LoRA baselines. |
| Very wide layers ($d \ge 1024$) | Choose $r$ so that $d/r \approx 32{-}64$. | Keeps each block large enough for rich rotations while still reducing total trainable parameters by roughly an order of magnitude.$^\ast$ |
| Extreme resource constraints | Share all blocks ($r = d/\text{desiredBlockDim}$). | Parameter count drops to $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$; performance degrades only mildly because inter‑block mixing is rarely essential for finetuning tasks. |

$^\ast$\ A convenient rule‐of‐thumb is “pick $r\simeq d/k$’’ where $k≈32{-}64.$  
If you aim for roughly the same budget as a LoRA module with rank $k'$, set  

```
# trainable params ≈ d·k'                # LoRA budget
# OFT params      ≈ d·(d/k−1)/2          # no sharing
choose k so that   d·(d/k−1)/2 ≈ d·k'   → k≈√(k')
```

In other words, if you would allocate a rank‑$k'$=8 LoRA adapter,
a good OFT configuration would be $k≈√8≈3–4,$ i.e. set $r≈d/k≈d/4.$

The authors report that **performance varies smoothly with $r$**:
very small $r (= 1)$* recovers the original OFT (full freedom), whereas
large $r (> 32)$* yields almost identical results to simply scaling down
the learning rate – indicating diminishing returns once each block becomes too
tiny.

---

### Take‑away

* Block‑diagonal orthogonal matrices give a **controlled reduction** from
$i= d^{2}$ free angles down to $O(d^{2}/r)$, preserving any rotation inside a
subspace while prohibiting mixing between subspaces.
* Sharing all blocks forces *identical* rotations across subspaces,
leaving only $O(d^{2}/r^{2})$ degrees of freedom – a highly regularised subset,
but one that still guarantees exact preservation of hyperspherical energy.
* In practice one picks $r$ so that each block has at least ~16–32 dimensions,
which yields a parameter budget comparable to low‑rank adapters yet retains most
of OFT’s expressive power; increasing $r$ further shrinks the budget at the
cost of slower adaptation, while decreasing $r$ approaches the fully dense
orthogonal case.

Thus the block‑diagonal design lets OFT balance **parameter efficiency,
training stability**, and **sufficient flexibility** for both subject‑driven and
controllable text‑to‑image finetuning.

### In COFT, how is the constraint $\| \mathbf R - I \| \le \epsilon$ enforced via projected gradient descent on $Q$?
Question:

The authors rewrite $R \approx I + 2Q$ (first‑order approximation) and then project $Q$ onto an $L_2$ ball.
* How exactly is this projection performed during training?
* Does one project after every gradient step or use a barrier penalty?
* How sensitive is performance to the choice of norm ($L_2$, Frobenius) used in the projection?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) the orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle \boldsymbol{R}$ is not stored directly.  
Instead it is generated from a *skew‑symmetric* matrix $\displaystyle \boldsymbol{Q}$ through the Cayley transform  

$$
\bR(\bQ)= (\bI+\bQ)(\,\bI-\bQ\,)^{-1},
    \qquad   {\bQ}^{\,T}=-\,{\bQ}.
$$

The additional “radius’’ constraint  

$$
\norm{\bR-\bI}\;\leqslant\,{\varepsilon}
    \tag{1}
$$

cannot be imposed easily on $\displaystyle\bR$ because of the non‑linear inverse in the Cayley map. The authors therefore linearise the map by expanding it with a Neumann series:

$$
({}\bI+\bQ)\bigl({}\bI-\bQ\bigr)^{-1}
      =   ({ }\bI+\bQ)\sum_{k=0}^{∞}{{}\bQ}^{k}
      =   {}\bI+2\,{}\bQ+\mathcal O\bigrl({}\norm{\bQ}^{2}\biglgr .
$$

When $\norm{\bQ}$ is small – which is precisely what we want – the higher‑order terms can be ignored and (1) becomes  

$$
\norm{{}\bI+2\,{}\bQ-{ }\bI}
   = 2\,{}\norm{\bQ}
   \;\leqslant\,{\varepsilon}
   \;\Longleftrightarrow"
   {}\norm{\bQ}\;\leqslant\,{\varepsilon}',
   \qquad {\varepsilon}'={}{\,}{%\ }{\varepsilon}/{2}.
   \tag{2}
$$

Thus imposing a ball constraint on ${}\bR-{ }\bI$ is equivalent to projecting the *skew‑symmetric* variable ${}\bQ$ onto an $L_{2}$ (Frobenius) ball of radius ${}\varepsilon'$.


---


### How the projection is carried out in practice

Training proceeds exactly as ordinary gradient descent (or Adam, etc.) on the loss $L(\theta,\;\bR({}\bQ))$. After each optimiser step we **project** the new candidate matrix back onto the feasible set defined by~(2). Concretely, for each update we perform


1. **Gradient step**  
   Compute a tentative update  
   $\displaystyle\twidtilde{{}\bQ}= {}\bQ-\eta\nabla_{\!\!\!{}\bQ}L$.  


2. **Enforce skew‑symmetry**  
   Because numerical errors may break the property  
   $\displaystyle\twidtilde{{}\bQ}=({}\twidtilde{{}\bQ}-{}^{T}{}\twidtilde{{}\bQ})/2$.  


3. **Project onto the radius ball**  
   Let $p=\norm{{}\twidtilde{{}\bQ}}_{F}$ be its Frobenius norm.  
   * If $p≤{}\varepsilon'$ keep it unchanged.  
   * If $p>{}\varepsilon'$ rescale it isotropically:
     $$
     {}\twidtilde{{}\bQ}
          ←{}{}\varepsilon'\,\frac{{}\twidtilde{{}\bQ}}{p}.
     $$


4. **Replace**    
   Set the next iterate as ${}\boldsymbol{Q}←{}\twidtilde{{}\boldsymbol{Q}}$.



All subsequent forward passes use  
${ }\boldsymbol {R}=({ }\boldsymbol {I}+{ }\boldsymbol {Q})({ }\boldsymbol {I}-{ }\boldsymbol {Q})^{-1}$
with this projected ${ }\boldsymbol {Q}$. Because projection is performed **after every** gradient step, no extra penalty term is needed – COFT implements a *hard* constraint rather than a barrier or Lagrangian penalty.


If an orthogonal matrix consists of several blocks (the paper’s efficiency trick),‐the same procedure is applied independently to each block’s skew‑symmetric part. Sharing blocks across layers simply means repeating the same projected ${ }\boldsymbol {Q}$ for all shared blocks.


---


### Why not use a barrier/penalty term?

A penalty such as  
$c\,((\,‖{}\boldsymbol {R}-{ }\boldsymbol {I}‖-\varepsilon)_+)^{2}$  
would turn the hard radius limit into a soft one and would require carefully tuning an extra weight $c$. Moreover – because $\norm{\cdot}$ is applied *after* a non‑linear Cayley map – a smooth penalty would still involve costly Jacobians through‐the‐matrix inverse. By moving the constraint onto ${ }\boldsymbol {Q}$ with the linearisation above,
the feasible region becomes a simple Euclidean ball—exactly what projected gradient descent handles efficiently and without any additional hyper‑parameter.


---


### Sensitivity to the choice of norm

The authors employ **the Frobenius norm**, i.e.~the matrix‑wise $L_{2}$ norm,
because  

* it coincides with treating ${ }\boldsymbol {Q}$ as a vector of its independent entries,
  making projection trivial (just a scalar rescaling);
* it respects orthogonal invariance required for preserving hyperspherical energy—
  any rotation that changes only directions does not affect pairwise cosine similarities.


The paper does **not** report experiments with other norms (e.g.~spectral or operator norm). In principle any **convex** norm defines a valid feasible set and could be projected onto,
but practical differences are negligible:
the Frobenius projection already yields the tightest possible bound on all matrix entries simultaneously,
which empirically stabilises training.


What matters more than the exact norm is **the radius** ${ }\varepsilon$. Figure 5 in​the paper shows three regimes:


* **Very small ${ }\varepsilon$** – ${ }\boldsymbol {R}$ stays close to identity,
  COFT behaves like ordinary fine‑tuning with almost no adaptation;
  generation quality remains high but task‑specific control is weak.


* **Moderate ${ }\varepsilon$** – enough freedom for ${ }\boldsymbol {R}$ to rotate neurons
  and achieve good subject‑driven or controllable generation while still keeping
  the model inside the pretrained hypersphere;
  this region gives the best trade‑off.


* **Large ${ }\varepsilon$** – COFT approaches OFT (no radius constraint);
  flexibility increases but finetuning stability slightly degrades,
  echoing OFT’s behaviour.


Across a reasonable range (the authors typically use ${ }\varepsilon$
that yields ${ }\varepsilon'\in[10^{-3},10^{-1}]$
depending on layer size), performance changes only gradually,
confirming that COFT is relatively insensitive to exact norm choice
as long as a consistent Euclidean/Frobenius ball is used.


---


### Summary

* The deviation constraint $\norm{{ }\boldsymbol {R}-{ }\boldsymbol {I}}\leqslant\e$
  is turned into an equivalent bound $\norm{{ }\boldsymbol {Q}}\leqslant\e'$
  using the first‑order approximation ${ }\boldsymbol {R}\approx{}\boldsymbol {I}+2{}{}\boldsymbol {Q}$.
* After every optimiser update on ${ }\boldsymbol {Q}$ we:
  1. force skew‑symmetry,
  2. compute its Frobenius norm,
  3. if necessary rescale it to lie exactly on or inside an $L_{2}$ ball of radius ${ }\e'$.
* This “project‑after‑step’’ scheme implements a hard constraint via projected gradient descent;
  no barrier or penalty term appears in COFT’s objective.
* Using an $L_{2}$/Frobenius ball works well in practice;
  switching to another convex matrix norm would give similar behaviour,
  and empirical results show that modest changes in ${ }\e$
  only modestly affect generation quality,
  confirming robustness of COFT to this hyper‑parameter.

### "How should we choose the hyperparameters *r* (number of blocks) and *epsilon* (deviation bound) in practice?"
Question:

Both *r* and *epsilon* trade off flexibility versus regularisation.
* Are there rule‑of‑thumb values reported for Stable Diffusion v1.5 layers?
* Do different downstream tasks (subject‑driven vs controllable generation) require different settings?
* Any ablation plots showing sensitivity?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
For Stable Diffusion v1 5 the authors found that a **block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix with about eight blocks** ($r\approx 8$) works well for almost every linear layer of the UNet, and that **constraining the deviation from the identity by a Frobenius‑norm radius of order $10^{-1}$** ($\varepsilon\approx 0.05{-}0.15$) gives a good balance between adaptability and stability when the constrained variant COFT is used.  

Below I explain why these numbers make sense, how they were chosen in the paper, how sensitive the method is to them, ‑‑and what adjustments are useful for the two downstream tasks studied (subject‑driven DreamBooth‑style finetuning vs. control‑signal finetuning).

---

### What \(r\) controls  

In **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** every trainable layer is modified by a single orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf R=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf R_{1},\dots,\mathbf R_{r})\in O(d), 
\qquad 
\mathbf W=\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{(0)},
$$  

where \(d\) is the input dimension of the layer (e.g., \(d=1024\) for most UNet linear projections).  
If \(r=1\) the whole matrix is free ($O(d^{2})$ parameters). By splitting \(\mathbf R\) into \(r\) diagonal blocks each block lives in \(O(d/r)\) and the number of trainable parameters drops roughly by a factor \(1/r\).

The authors report two concrete examples:

* **128 × 128 weight matrix** – LoRA with rank 8 needs *2048* parameters while **OFT with $r=8$ needs only *960* parameters** (no block sharing).  
* For Stable Diffusion v1 5 most linear layers have $d∈{768,1024}$ → choosing a block size around **128 dimensions** gives $\,r=d/128≈6{-}8$.  

#### Empirical ablation  

Figure 7 (“sensitivity of \(r\)”) shows three regimes:

| Regime | Approximate value | Observed effect |
|--------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Very low (\(r≤3)\)** | Very few large blocks | The model loses too much freedom – CLIP‑I/DINO scores drop noticeably (< 2 % absolute). |
| **Medium (\(4≤ r≤12)\)** | Default range used by authors | Scores are essentially flat – hyperspherical energy stays preserved while parameter count stays low (\(\approx10^{-3}\!·d^{2}\)). |
| **Very high (\( r≥16)\)** | Extremely many tiny blocks | Slight degradation (< 1 % absolute) because each block only rotates a tiny subspace, limiting expressive power.|

Thus any value **between four and twelve works safely**; the authors picked \( r=\lceil d/128\rceil \), i.e.:

* UNet projection (\(d=1024\)) → \(r=8\),  
* CLIP text encoder projection (\(d=768\)) → \(r=6\).

When memory is extremely tight one can increase \(r\) further (e.g., \( r=d/256)\)). The paper notes that sharing all blocks (\(\mathbf R_i≡\,\mathbf R_j)\) reduces parameters again (\(O(d^{2}/r^{2}))\), but it introduces a bias toward depth‑wise “channel” rotations; this was only experimented with as an extreme compression mode and is **not recommended unless you are willing to accept a few points loss on DINO/CLIP‑I**.

---

### What \(ε\) controls  

In **Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT)** an extra radius constraint is added  

$$
\|\mathbf R-\mathbf I\|_{F}\le \varepsilon .
$$  

The constraint is enforced on the skew‑symmetric generator $\mathbf Q$ inside the Cayley transform,
$\;\|\mathbf Q\|_{F}\le{\varepsilon}^{\,prime}$,
via projected gradient descent.

#### How big should \(ε\) be?

Figure 9 (“effect of \(ε\$”) plots DreamBooth DINO score versus training epoch for four values:

* $ε=0$\;\;(pure OFT),  
* $ε=0.05$,  
* $ε=0.15$,  
* $ε=0.30$.  

Key observations:

* **Increasing \(ε\$ widensthe reachable region** – when \(ε≥0.15\$ COFT behaves almost identically to unrestricted OFT,
* **Very small \(ε (<0.02)\)** makes training extremely slow: the model stays close to its pretrained weights and fails to learn enough new subject information,
* **Medium \(ε (\sim0.05{-}0.15)\)** yields *the fastest convergence* while still keeping hyperspherical energy unchanged,
* **Large \(ε (>0.20)\)** begins to hurt preservation metrics – DINO drops by ~1–2 % compared with pure OFT because too much rotation is allowed.

Consequently the authors use two different defaults:

| Task | Recommended setting | Reason |
|------|----------------------|--------|
| **Subject‑driven generation** (DreamBooth style) | No explicit bound (\(ε\$ omitted ⇒ pure OFT), optionally \(ε≈0.{}10\$ if training becomes unstable on very scarce data | The task needs enough freedom to encode a new identity; bounding too tightly prevents sufficient change |
| **Controllable generation** (Canny / segmentation / landmark) | \(ε=0.{ }10\$ (projected after each update) | A modest bound keeps the model anchored near its pretrained knowledge, which improves control consistency without compromising image quality |

These values are taken directly from Section&nbsp;4 (“Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning”) where Figure&nbsp;9 illustrates them, and from Appendix&nbsp;E where exact hyperparameters are listed for every benchmark experiment.

---

### Practical recipe  

Putting everything together, here’s a short checklist you can follow when applying OFT/COFT on Stable Diffusion v​v 5:

1️⃣ **Pick \(r.\)**  
   * Compute $(d/128)$ where \(d$\newline is​the​input​dimension​of​each​weight​matrix​you​intend​to​finetune *(most are either 768 or 1024)*.<br>
   * Round up → this becomes your default number of diagonal blocks.<br>
   * If you notice memory pressure raise \(r$\newline until your GPU fits; stay within [4,\,$12]​.  

2️⃣ **Decide whether you need COFT**  
   * Use plain OFT when you want maximal adaptation power (e.g., DreamBooth).  
   * Use COFT when you care about *stability* or *control precision*.  

3️⃣ **Set \(ε.\)**  
   * Start with $ε=0.{ }10$.  
   * If early validation loss shows poor subject fidelity → increase modestly up to $ε=0.{ }15$.  
   * If training oscillates or collapses → decrease toward $ε=0.{ }05$.  

4️⃣ **Optional block sharing** – Only enable if total trainable parameters must stay \<200 K across all layers; expect a small drop (< 1 % absolute) on DINO / CLIP‑I scores.

5️⃣ **Validate early** – After ∼200 finetuning steps compute CLIP‑I/DINO on a held‑out validation set; adjust $(\,r,\;\varepsilon\,)$ only if scores diverge sharply outside the plateau observed in Figures&nbsp;7 & 9.

---

### Why these numbers make sense  

The whole method rests on preserving ***hyperspherical energy*** – i.e., keeping pairwise neuron angles unchanged. A full orthogonal matrix ($\(r=1\$)) certainly does this but costs $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ parameters; splitting it into several independent rotations still preserves every angle within each subspace while dramatically reducing learnable entries ($\mathcal{O}(d^{2}/\,r)$), which explains why modest values of \(r\$ already give near‑identical preservation as shown empirically.

The radius bound~\(ε\$ simply limits how far those rotations may depart from “do nothing”. Because hyperspherical energy depends only on relative directions among neurons within each layer, small rotations keep this energy exactly unchanged regardless of how many blocks you have – what changes is *how much semantic shift* can be expressed in the downstream task space. The ablations confirm that there exists a sweet spot around ~$10^{-1}$$: enough freedom for new concepts/control signals yet tight enough that optimization stays on a smooth manifold rather than wandering into regions where “directional consistency” no longer guarantees good image quality.

---

### Bottom line  

| Hyperparameter | Typical value for SD‑v⁠1 5 | Good range |
|----------------|----------------------------|------------|
| Number of diagonal blocks (\(r\$)) | $\lceil d/128\rceil$\;\;(usually ***6–8*** per layer) | [4 … 12] |
| Deviation bound ($\(ε\$)) – only for COFT | ***≈ \(0.{ }10\$*** (subject‑driven may omit it) | [\(0.{ }05 … \(0.{ }15\$)] |

Starting from these defaults reproduces the results reported in Tables 2–3 and Figures 7–9 of “Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”. Small task‑specific tweaks (a slightly larger~\(ε\$ for DreamBooth or shared‑block matrices when memory is ultra tight) are usually sufficient rather than performing an exhaustive grid search—the method has been shown experimentally to be fairly robust within these intervals.<br>
