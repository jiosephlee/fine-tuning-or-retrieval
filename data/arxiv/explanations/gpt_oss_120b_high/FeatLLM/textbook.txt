\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

**Introduction to Tabular Learning and Its Challenges**

# Definition and examples of tabular datasets  

Tabular data—also called structured data—consist of observations arranged in rows and columns, where each column corresponds to a particular attribute (or feature) and each row corresponds to a single example.  The columns are usually accompanied by human‑readable names (“age”, “blood‑pressure”, “income”) and often have short textual definitions that explain what the values represent.  Because this format mirrors how data are stored in relational databases, spreadsheets, and many statistical software packages, it appears across almost every domain that deals with real‑world decision making.  

In healthcare, a patient record may contain demographic fields (age, gender), laboratory measurements (cholesterol level), and categorical diagnoses (presence or absence of a disease).  In finance, credit‑risk tables list borrowers’ income, employment status, outstanding debts, and a binary label indicating whether they defaulted on a loan.  In public policy or criminology, community‑level surveys record attributes such as average income, education rate, and crime counts per neighborhood.  The paper’s experimental suite includes datasets such as **Adult** (predicting whether an individual earns over \$50 k), **Bank** (whether a customer subscribes to a term deposit), **Blood** (donor return behavior), **Heart** (presence of coronary artery disease), **Communities** (regional crime rates), as well as synthetic collections like **Sequence‑type** (classifying mathematical sequences).  All of these exemplify the canonical tabular setting where each instance is fully described by a fixed set of features whose semantics are available in natural language.

The ubiquity of tabular datasets stems from their ease of collection: most administrative systems already store information in tables; most surveys are designed to produce rows of responses; and many scientific measurements are reported as columns of numeric or categorical variables.  Consequently, building predictive models for tabular tasks has long been a central activity in machine learning research and practice.

# Typical supervised learning workflow for tabular data  

The conventional pipeline for supervised tabular learning proceeds through several deterministic stages.  First, a practitioner gathers a labeled dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{i}, y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\) where each \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) encodes the \(d\) raw feature values for sample \(i\) and \(y^{i}\) denotes its class label (or regression target).  Next comes *preprocessing*: missing values are imputed; categorical variables are encoded (e.g., one‑hot or ordinal encoding); continuous features may be normalized or transformed; and sometimes domain knowledge guides feature engineering—creating interaction terms or derived variables that better capture underlying mechanisms.

After preprocessing the designer selects a model family—logistic regression for its interpretability, gradient‑boosted trees such as XGBoost for strong performance on heterogeneous data, random forests for robustness against overfitting—or more recent deep architectures like tabular transformers.  Hyperparameters (tree depth, regularization strength) are tuned using cross‑validation or hold‑out validation sets.  The chosen model is then trained on the labeled portion of \(\mathcal{D}\) by minimizing a loss function—commonly cross‑entropy for classification—followed by evaluation on an unseen test split using metrics such as area under the ROC curve (AUROC).  

In practice this workflow assumes that enough labeled examples are available to estimate the model’s parameters reliably while also providing sufficient signal during validation to select hyperparameters without severe variance.  When these assumptions hold—typically when thousands or tens of thousands of labeled rows exist—the pipeline yields accurate predictions with modest effort.

# Why few‑shot learning is needed (label cost, privacy)  

Despite its apparent simplicity, acquiring large quantities of high‑quality labels for tabular problems can be prohibitively expensive or even impossible.  In medical domains each label may correspond to a costly diagnostic test administered by specialists; in finance labeled outcomes such as default events might be rare events that require years of longitudinal tracking; in privacy‑sensitive settings like government surveys participants may refuse to disclose personal attributes unless strong safeguards are in place.  

These constraints motivate *few‑shot* or *low‑shot* learning: training models with only \(k\) labeled examples per class where \(k\ll N\).  The research community has shown that when the number of shots drops below ten—a realistic scenario in many real‐world deployments—standard machine‑learning methods begin to fail dramatically because they cannot disentangle signal from noise without sufficient data points.  Moreover, specialized techniques such as semi‑supervised pretraining on large unlabeled corpora or transfer learning across heterogeneous tables still require substantial auxiliary data that may not be available or may violate privacy regulations.

As highlighted in the referenced study, few‑shot tabular learning is an active frontier precisely because it reflects realistic operational constraints: organizations often have abundant *features* but only a handful of *labels*, yet they still need an automated decision system that can act reliably at scale.

# Failure modes of standard models with limited data (over‑fitting, spurious correlations)  

When only a handful of labeled rows are present, classical algorithms tend to overfit—the model learns patterns that perfectly fit the tiny training set but do not generalize beyond it.  Overfitting manifests especially in high‑dimensional settings where \(d\) can rival—or exceed—the number of shots \(k\).  For instance, tree‐based ensembles can grow deep branches that memorize exact combinations of feature values present in the training examples; linear models may assign large coefficients to noise dimensions simply because those dimensions happen to correlate by chance with the limited labels.

A particularly insidious failure mode is *spurious correlation*.  In low‐shot regimes random fluctuations can make an irrelevant column appear predictive purely by coincidence.  Because many standard learners lack explicit inductive bias against such coincidences when data are scarce, they inadvertently amplify these false signals during training.  Empirically this phenomenon has been observed across benchmark datasets: adding unrelated columns from another domain dramatically degrades performance for naive learners but has much milder effects on approaches that incorporate stronger priors.

The consequence is unreliable predictions—models may output high confidence on inputs whose feature values follow accidental patterns rather than genuine causal relationships—a risk unacceptable in high‐stakes applications like healthcare triage or loan approval.

# Motivation for leveraging external knowledge sources  

One promising remedy is to inject *external knowledge* into the learning process so that models rely less on fragile statistical cues derived from tiny training sets and more on robust facts encoded elsewhere.  Large language models (LLMs), trained on massive text corpora covering diverse domains—including medical literature, financial reports, scientific textbooks—have demonstrated an ability to retrieve relevant factual relationships without any task‐specific fine‑tuning.  

The paper under discussion exploits precisely this capability: rather than using an LLM as an end‐to‐end predictor (which would require one inference per test sample), it treats the LLM as an *expert feature engineer*.  By prompting the LLM with a brief description of the task (“Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications indicate chronic heart failure?”) together with a handful of labeled examples and human‐readable feature definitions, the model can infer plausible *rules* linking feature conditions to class labels.  These rules serve as binary engineered features (“age > 65 AND blood pressure > 140”) that are then fed into an extremely simple downstream predictor—a nonnegative linear model—trained on the few available shots.

Because LLMs bring world knowledge about typical relationships (“older age increases disease risk”) they can filter out irrelevant columns early during rule extraction; they also help avoid overfitting by providing *inductive bias* toward sensible rules rather than arbitrary statistical artifacts.  Moreover, once rules have been distilled into binary features there is no need to invoke the LLM again at inference time—reducing latency dramatically compared with methods that query the LLM per sample—and only API‐level access is required (no fine‐tuning infrastructure).

Thus external knowledge sources address both core challenges identified earlier: they mitigate overfitting by supplying strong priors on which features matter; they protect against spurious correlations by guiding attention toward causally plausible conditions; and they enable few‑shot learners to achieve performance comparable to conventional models that would otherwise require dozens or hundreds of labeled examples.

---  

In summary, tabular data dominate real‐world decision processes but become highly challenging when only minimal labeled examples exist. Conventional supervised pipelines falter due to overfitting and sensitivity to accidental correlations. By leveraging expansive prior knowledge embedded in large language models—as exemplified by the FeatLLM framework—we can automate intelligent feature generation that aligns with human expertise while preserving low computational cost at inference time. This approach marks a significant step toward practical few‑shot tabular learning capable of thriving under realistic label scarcity constraints.

Large Language Models (LLMs) as General‑Purpose Reasoners  

# Transformer foundations and scaling laws  

The modern Large Language Model (LLM) is built upon the transformer architecture introduced by Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. At its core lies the self‑attention mechanism, which lets each token in a sequence attend to every other token by computing similarity scores between learned query ($\mathbf{Q}$), key ($\mathbf{K}$), and value ($\mathbf{V}$) vectors. Concretely, given an input sequence represented as a matrix $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{\text{model}}}$ of length $L$, the attention operation produces  

\[
\text{Attention}(\mathbf{X})=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^{\!\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\mathbf{V},
\]  

where $d_k$ denotes the dimensionality of each query/key vector. This operation captures pairwise interactions without any recurrent recurrence, allowing parallel computation across all positions—a decisive factor for training on billions of tokens efficiently.  

The transformer stacks many identical layers, each comprising a multi‑head attention block followed by a position‑wise feed‑forward network with residual connections and layer normalization. By increasing three primary axes—depth (number of layers), width (hidden dimension $d_{\text{model}}$), and data (total number of tokens $D$ seen during pre‑training)—researchers have observed regular patterns known as scaling laws~\cite{kaplan2020scaling}. Empirically, model loss $L$ follows a power‑law decay  

\[
L(N,D)\approx A\,N^{-\alpha}+B\,D^{-\beta},
\]  

with $N$ denoting total parameters, $A,B$ constants, and $\alpha,\beta>0$. As $N$ and $D$ grow together while maintaining a roughly constant ratio $D/N$, performance improves predictably across diverse natural language tasks.\footnote{Scaling behaviour was documented across models ranging from hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, supporting the emergence of capabilities such as mathematical reasoning or code synthesis when crossing certain thresholds~\cite{brown2020language}.}  

Because transformers scale gracefully both in compute efficiency (thanks to self‑attention’s parallelism) and in performance (thanks to smooth scaling curves), they become natural candidates for building general‐purpose reasoners capable of handling varied input modalities—including the structured rows typical of tabular data.\footnote{The \textsf{FeatLLM} study leverages GPT‑3.5 turbo—a transformer‑based LLM—demonstrating that even without task‑specific fine‑tuning, large scale enables extraction of high‐quality decision rules from few examples.}

# Pre‑training objectives (next token prediction) and knowledge acquisition  

The predominant pre‑training objective for contemporary LLMs is autoregressive next token prediction~\cite{radford2019language}. Given a tokenized context $\mathbf{x}_{1:t}=(x_1,\dots,x_t)$, the model is trained to maximize  

\[
\log p(x_{t+1}\mid \mathbf{x}_{1:t}) .
\]  

Summed over all positions in massive corpora comprising trillions of words, this maximum likelihood objective forces the network to internalize statistical regularities about syntax, semantics, world facts, and even procedural knowledge present in natural language.\footnote{Alternative objectives such as masked language modeling exist but are less common for pure generative models employed via APIs.}  

During optimization, gradients flow through all layers; thus parameters gradually encode embeddings for lexical items, relational patterns among entities (“older individuals have higher disease risk”), temporal sequences (“if temperature rises then ice melts”), and logical constructs (“if A and B then C”). When later prompted with a novel query—e.g., “What conditions indicate chronic heart failure?”—the model retrieves relevant internal patterns without having seen that exact formulation during training.\footnote{In \textsf{FeatLLM}, this latent world knowledge enables the LLM to generate medically plausible rules such as “age > 65 AND blood pressure > 140”. }  

Because next token prediction does not require explicit annotation beyond raw text, pre‑training can ingest virtually any textual resource—news articles, scientific papers, code repositories—amassing an encyclopedic repository of facts that downstream users can harness via prompts.\footnote{The authors highlight that this “rich prior knowledge” substitutes expert domain knowledge when engineering features for tabular tasks such as disease prediction or credit risk assessment.}

# In‑context learning vs fine‑tuning vs parameter‑efficient tuning  

Three distinct paradigms govern how an LLM adapts from its broad pre‑trained state to a specific downstream problem:

**In‑context learning** treats the model as a frozen function $f_{\theta}(\cdot)$ whose behavior can be steered solely by appending exemplars directly into its input prompt. Concretely one supplies a short textual description of the task followed by several “demonstrations” formatted as $\langle \texttt{x},\texttt{y}\rangle$ pairs:

```
Feature1 is 25 . Feature2 is 0 . Answer: 1
Feature1 is 42 . Feature2 is 1 . Answer: 0
...
```

When presented with new inputs sharing the same format, the model extrapolates from those demonstrations without any weight updates~\cite{kumar2022meta}. The advantage lies in zero training time—only prompt construction—and flexibility across tasks~\cite{brown2020language}. The drawback is computational cost at inference time: every new sample must be passed through the expensive LLM API again (as illustrated by existing approaches such as TabLLM or TABLET). Moreover, prompt length limits constrain how many examples can be shown simultaneously.\footnote{\textsf{FeatLLM} mitigates this per-sample cost by using in‐context learning only once—to extract rule‐based features—after which predictions rely on a lightweight linear model.}

**Fine‑tuning** modifies all parameters $\theta$ via gradient descent on task–specific labeled data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$. The loss typically mirrors pre‐training’s cross–entropy over target labels:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FT}} = - \sum_{(x,y)\in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}} \log p_{\theta}(y|x).
\]

Fine‐tuning yields strong performance when sufficient labeled data exist but incurs considerable compute overhead and requires access to model weights—a restriction when using closed‐source APIs.\footnote{TabLLM follows this route with IA3 adapters trained on few shots; however it still demands multiple forward passes per sample during inference because predictions occur directly inside the adapted LLM.}

**Parameter‑efficient tuning** occupies an intermediate niche by updating only a small set of auxiliary parameters while keeping $\theta$ frozen~\cite{hu2021lora}. Techniques like LoRA insert low-rank matrices $\Delta \mathbf W = \mathbf A \mathbf B^{\!\top}$ into existing linear transformations so that optimization focuses on $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B$ alone (\#parameters order $(r d)$ where $r\ll d$). IA3 rescales hidden activations instead of adding new weights~\cite{liu2022few}. Because only tens of thousands rather than billions of values change, training becomes feasible even on modest hardware while retaining much of fine-tuning’s adaptability.\footnote{\textsf{FeatLLM} deliberately refrains from any weight updating beyond fitting a tiny linear classifier over binary rule features; this keeps its resource requirements comparable to conventional machine learning baselines despite using a massive LLM solely during feature construction.}

Collectively these paradigms let practitioners trade off between computational budget, latency constraints, and data availability—a critical consideration when tackling low‐shot tabular problems where annotated rows are scarce but inference speed matters.

# Chain‑of‑thought prompting and reasoning capabilities  

Chain‑of‑thought (CoT) prompting asks an LLM not merely “what answer?” but “how would you reason step by step?” By prefixing prompts with phrases such as “Let’s think step by step,” one induces internal symbolic manipulation before arriving at a final token~\cite{wei2022chain}. Empirically CoT improves success rates on arithmetic puzzles, logical deductions, and multi‐hop question answering—in effect coaxing latent reasoning pathways out of what would otherwise be a pure pattern matcher.\footnote{In \textsf{FeatLLM}, CoT ideas inspire their two–step reasoning instruction: first infer causal tendencies between features and label purely from prior knowledge (step 1), then combine those tendencies with concrete few-shot examples to enumerate deterministic rules (step 2). This structured approach reduces spurious correlation formation and leads to concise logical predicates rather than opaque neural embeddings.}

CoT also interacts synergistically with rule generation because logical expressions (“age > 60 AND cholesterol > 200”) map naturally onto binary predicates used downstream. When combined with well–crafted response formats—e.g., “Rule 1: if … then class A”—the resulting output can be parsed automatically into executable code or conditional statements.\footnote{
The authors note that even though natural language may contain variations (“greater than” vs “>”), feeding those textual rules back into an LLM itself yields robust Python snippets that concretize them into Boolean features.
}  

Thus chain–of–thought prompting serves two purposes in tabular contexts:
1. It directs attention toward high–level causal concepts encoded during pretraining.
2. It produces human–readable specifications that facilitate downstream interpretable models—a property essential for domains such as healthcare where auditability matters.


# APIs vs open‑source checkpoints: practical implications  

A practitioner’s choice between black‐box API access (e.g., OpenAI’s `gpt-3.5-turbo`) versus downloading an open‐source checkpoint hinges on several factors:

* **Latency & cost** – API calls incur per-token charges and network latency proportional to prompt length; repeated calls per sample become prohibitive at scale. Open-source checkpoints run locally on GPUs offering batch processing with negligible per‐token fees once hardware exists.\footnote{
In \textsf{FeatLLM}, only thirty API queries suffice regardless of dataset size because rule extraction happens once per bagging trial; afterward inference costs drop dramatically compared to methods that query per test example.
}
* **Customization** – Open checkpoints permit fine‐tuning or insertion of adapters directly on weights—a necessity when one wishes to specialize an LLM for domain vocabularies or rare modalities absent from generic APIs.\footnote{
TabLLM employs IA3 tuning on T0 encoder models precisely because it has direct access; however GPT-3\.5 cannot be tuned through its API facade.
}
* **Regulatory & privacy constraints** – Certain industries disallow sending proprietary data outside organizational firewalls; local checkpoints guarantee data never leaves premises.\footnote{
FeatLLM mitigates exposure risk also because after extracting rules it never streams raw patient rows back through any external service.
}
* **Prompt length limits** – Public APIs impose hard caps on tokens per request (~4–8 k tokens depending on version). When tables contain hundreds of columns—as many benchmark datasets do—serialization may exceed this budget causing inference failure or degraded performance.\footnote{
To cope with long feature sets FeatLLM adopts feature bagging: each trial samples only a subset of columns before constructing prompts, thereby respecting token limits while still pooling diverse rules across ensembles.
}
* **Model versioning & reproducibility** – Open releases provide explicit version identifiers enabling exact reproducibility across labs; APIs often abstract away model updates behind opaque “engine” names which may shift underlying weights without notice.


Consequently many real-world deployments start with API access because it removes barrier-to-entry hardware costs while still unlocking powerful latent knowledge via prompts. When demand grows—for instance needing batch throughput or stricter privacy—the community typically transitions toward self-hosted models derived from open releases such as Meta’s LLaMA or HuggingFace’s BLOOM family.\footnote{
The FeatLLM methodology remains agnostic: any sufficiently capable generative transformer could replace GPT-3\.5 provided it supports programmatic invocation at moderate temperature settings for diverse rule generation.}

---

In summary, modern Large Language Models rest upon scalable transformer foundations whose self-attention architecture admits smooth performance gains when enlarged—a phenomenon captured quantitatively by empirical scaling laws. Through next-token prediction over vast corpora they amass encyclopedic factual knowledge which can be summoned via carefully designed prompts leveraging chain-of-thought reasoning. Depending on resource constraints one may employ pure in-context learning (prompt-only), full fine-tuning (weight update), or lightweight adapter techniques—all representing trade-offs between computational expense and adaptation fidelity. Finally, whether accessed through commercial APIs or locally hosted checkpoints determines practical considerations such as latency budgets, privacy compliance, and prompt length limitations—all pivotal when applying LLMs as general-purpose reasoners for challenging low-shot tabular tasks like those tackled by \textsf{FeatLLM}.

Few‑Shot Learning for Tabular Data: Prior Work  

# Unsupervised & Self‑Supervised Pre‑training  

A central obstacle for learning from tabular data is that real-world tables are often high‑dimensional yet only a handful of labeled rows are available.  Early few‑shot approaches therefore sought to endow a model with useful inductive biases before it ever sees any class label.  One family of techniques relies on **unsupervised** or **self‑supervised** objectives that can be applied solely to the raw feature matrix.  The intuition is that a model which has already learned how to reconstruct missing entries, predict transformed versions of a column, or discriminate between perturbed views of the same row will possess representations that capture generic statistical regularities—such as correlations among numeric columns or typical co‑occurrences among categorical values—without overfitting to a limited labeled set.  

In practice these ideas have taken several concrete forms.  Some methods mask a random proportion of entries in each row and train an autoencoder‑like network to fill them back in; others replace values according to a noise distribution and ask the model to recover the original distribution \cite{arik2021tabnet}.  A complementary line introduces **contrastive learning** objectives, where two stochastic augmentations of the same row are forced to produce similar embeddings while different rows are pushed apart \cite{bahri2022scarf, chen2020simple}.  Although such augmentations are straightforward for images or text, defining semantically meaningful transformations for heterogeneous columns remains challenging—hence these strategies have produced only modest gains when only a few labeled examples are present \cite{nam2023stunt}.  

An especially notable self‑supervised direction avoids any real dataset altogether by synthesizing many artificial tables drawn from diverse parametric families.  A Bayesian neural network is then pretrained on this synthetic corpus so that its internal priors already encode a broad spectrum of joint distributions over features and targets \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn}.  When confronted with a new downstream table, the pretrained network can immediately adapt its predictions by feeding both training and test rows as input—a form of “zero‑shot” inference that nevertheless benefits from having rehearsed millions of plausible tabular patterns during pretraining.  Such synthetic pretraining sidesteps the need for large unlabeled collections tied to any specific domain but still depends on assuming that new tasks resemble some mixture of those synthetic data distributions.

Across all these unsupervised approaches the shared hypothesis is simple: **if an encoder can capture generic structures within tabular rows without supervision, then only a small number $k$ ($k \ll N$) of labeled rows will suffice** to train a lightweight classifier on top of those representations.  However, empirical studies reported in recent literature indicate that while these methods improve robustness compared with training directly on raw features, their gains plateau under very low‐shot regimes—especially when numeric‐categorical heterogeneity limits effective augmentations \cite{nam2023stunt}.


# Transfer Learning Across Heterogeneous Tables  

Another avenue pursued by researchers acknowledges that many public benchmarks contain thousands of distinct tables spanning medical records, financial logs, sensor measurements, and more.  If a model can discover **semantic relationships among column names**—for example recognizing that “age”, “years”, and “patient_age” often denote similar concepts—it may transfer knowledge gathered from one table to another even when their raw values differ drastically.  

A concrete instantiation of this idea trains transformer‐based encoders on a large corpus of heterogeneous tables where each column is accompanied by its textual name \cite{wang2022transtab}.  By attending jointly to column names and cell values during pretraining, the model learns cross‑table embeddings that respect both linguistic similarity (“salary” vs “income”) and statistical similarity (similar value ranges).  Once pretrained, such an encoder can be applied zero‑shot or fine‑tuned with merely $k$ labeled rows on a previously unseen table—a process known as **transfer learning across tables**.  

Parallel work has explored **synthetic generation** techniques such as those used by TabPFN \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn}.  Rather than mining real table collections directly—a task hampered by licensing constraints—the authors produce artificial tables whose marginal distributions emulate those observed across many domains (e.g., mixtures of Gaussians for numeric fields, Dirichlet draws for categorical frequencies).  By training a Bayesian neural network on this synthetically diverse ensemble, it acquires priors capable of rapidly adapting when supplied with actual supervised examples from any target table.\footnote{Both TransTab \cite{wang2022transtab} and TabPFN \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn} belong to this line-of-work although they differ in whether they rely on real versus synthetic source corpora.}  

In essence these transfer approaches view each table as an instance drawn from an underlying family of tabular problems; by exposing a model repeatedly to many members of this family it gradually internalizes reusable patterns—be they statistical regularities among numeric columns or linguistic cues embedded in feature names—that dramatically reduce the amount of supervised signal required at test time.


# Language‑Interfaced Methods  

A more recent paradigm acknowledges that Large Language Models (LLMs) encode vast amounts of world knowledge captured during their pretraining on massive text corpora.  If one could *translate* tabular data into natural language prompts, perhaps an LLM could answer classification questions directly without any parameter updates at all—a strategy sometimes called **in‑context learning** because the model “learns” from demonstration examples embedded in its prompt.\footnote{The term originates from early vision-language works where examples are shown alongside instructions at inference time rather than during gradient descent~\cite{brown2020language}.}  

Early language‑interfaced tabular learners adopt exactly this recipe: they serialize each row into a sequence such as “`age` is 45…, `blood_pressure` is high…, Answer: `positive`”, concatenate dozens—or sometimes hundreds—of such demonstrations together with natural language descriptions of each feature’s type and semantics (\textit{e.g., “`age` denotes years since birth”}), then hand this prompt off to an LLM such as GPT‑3 or T0 \cite{sanh2022multitask}.  The model’s response is interpreted as either a class label (“yes/no”) or a probability estimate derived from its generated text \cite{dinh2022lift}.  

Building upon plain prompting several extensions emerged:

* **TABLET** augments the basic prompt with an auxiliary classifier–derived set of rule prototypes designed to steer the LLM toward more plausible reasoning chains \cite{slack2023tablet}.  
* **TabLLM** goes one step further by fine‐tuning an LLM’s weights using parameter–efficient techniques rather than relying solely on prompt engineering \cite{hegselmann2023tabllm}.  
* Simple **in-context learners** augment their prompts with only raw demonstrations and rely on chain‐of‐thought style reasoning instructions—to coax multi-step logical deductions from the LLM \cite{wei2022emergent}.  

All three systems share a common pipeline:   
1) Encode every feature name $f_j$ ($j=1,\dots,d$) together with its value $\mathbf{x}_j^i$ into textual fragments;  
2) Append a natural language task description (“Does this patient have chronic heart failure?”);  
3) Add $k$ demonstration rows together with known answers $\mathbf y^i$, forming an input $\{\mathbf x^i,\mathbf y^i\}_{i=1}^{k}$;  
4) Query the LLM once per test instance and interpret its output as $\hat{\mathbf y}$.  

Because LLMs attend jointly over all tokens—including every serialized feature—their ability to reason about relationships among columns appears bounded by two practical factors discussed below.


# Parameter‑efficient Tuning Methods  

While pure prompting avoids any gradient updates altogether, researchers have observed that directly adapting an LLM’s parameters—even modestly—can markedly improve performance on structured tasks where domain‐specific jargon or subtle numeric thresholds matter \cite{phebutt?}.  Fine‐tuning full networks however would demand access to billions‐scale weight matrices—a requirement rarely satisfied by commercial API providers (e.g., GPT‑4 offers only inference endpoints).  

To bridge this gap **parameter–efficient tuning** (PET) strategies have been introduced.  Two prominent families stand out:

* **LoRA** (“Low-Rank Adaptation”) injects trainable low–rank matrices into each attention layer’s weight matrices while keeping original weights frozen \cite{hu2021lora}.  Because only these small matrices are updated during training on $k$ labeled samples (often via stochastic gradient descent), computational overhead remains modest yet expressive enough for downstream adaptation.
* **IA3** (“Intrinsic Adapter”) modifies only scalar scaling factors associated with hidden activations inside transformer blocks \cite{liu2022few}.   Similar to LoRA it keeps base weights intact but offers even lighter memory footprints because fewer parameters need gradient updates whenever $k$ is extremely small.

Both LoRA and IA3 have been employed in recent tabular LLM studies such as **TabLLM**, which couples IA3 finetuning with T0’s publicly released checkpoint \citet[see][Table X]{hegselmann2023tabllm}.  By limiting trainable parameters one can embed domain knowledge gleaned from hundreds—or even thousands—of unlabeled rows while still respecting API constraints imposed by closed‐source models.



# Key Limitations of Prior LLM‑Based Approaches  

Despite their promise, language–interfaced algorithms encounter two intertwined practical bottlenecks when deployed on genuine tabular workloads:

## Per–Sample Inference Cost  

All currently published systems—including pure in‑context learners, TABLET and tuned variants like TabLLM—perform **one LLM forward pass per test sample**. Formally, given $N_{\text{test}}$ unseen rows $\{\mathbf{x}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}}$ one must construct $N_{\text{test}}$ separate prompts
\[
\texttt{
Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^{i}) + \texttt{
Task description } + \texttt{
Few–shot demos}
\]
and send them individually through an API call whose latency may stretch even beyond seconds per token depending on service tier \citet[e.g.,][]{openai2023gpt4}. As $N_{\text{test}}$ grows—typical batch sizes reach thousands—the cumulative wallclock time becomes prohibitive for real‐time applications such as fraud detection or clinical decision support where latency constraints are tight.\footnote{In our empirical evaluation FeatLLM required merely thirty API queries regardless of dataset size because it extracts rules once offline rather than per instance.}

## Prompt Length Constraints  

Current generation APIs impose hard limits on total token count per request (e.g., roughly four thousand tokens for GPT 3.5 Turbo). A single prompt comprises three portions described earlier: feature descriptions (often several words per column), serialized demonstrations ($k\times d$ tokens), plus optional chain-of-thought instructions.\footnote{
If $d=150$ features each described by ten tokens and $k=8$, then even before adding reasoning discourse we already exceed eight thousand tokens—a violation.
}
When $d$ grows beyond roughly one hundred columns—as seen in datasets like *Communities* and *Myocardial* discussed later—the required serialization overflows typical context windows causing either truncation (and loss of crucial information) or aggressive summarisation that harms downstream reasoning.\citet[see][]{liu2023lost} report systematic performance drops once prompt length crosses ~30% over capacity.\

Both constraints stem directly from treating an LLM as an end-to-end predictor rather than as an auxiliary component whose output can be cached or reused across many instances.\citet[see][]{wang2018selfconsistency}? Additionally many commercial providers restrict users' ability to perform repeated high-volume inference due both to cost considerations (~\$ per thousand tokens) and policy limits governing API usage.\footnote{
The present FeatLLM framework circumvents this limitation entirely by extracting rule-based binary features once during offline processing; afterwards predictions are made solely via inexpensive linear models.
}

Collectively these limitations underscore why purely LLM-powered tabular learners—while conceptually elegant—have struggled for adoption outside laboratory settings.


--- 

In summary, earlier attempts at few‑shot tabular learning progressed through three major stages: first building domain-agnostic encoders via unsupervised/self‑supervised objectives (including synthetic pretraining through TabPFN); second leveraging semantic signals embedded in column names via cross-table transfer mechanisms like TransTab; third confronting classic tabular pipelines head‑on by converting rows into natural language prompts fed directly into large language models—both without tuning (pure prompting) and with light weight updates through LoRA/IA3 adapters (parameter-efficient tuning). Although each direction contributed valuable insights regarding how prior knowledge can offset scarce supervision, practical obstacles related both to per-sample inference costs and prompt length limits motivated subsequent research seeking more efficient usages of LLMs—as exemplified later by frameworks that treat LLMs primarily as feature engineers rather than end-to-end predictors.
