\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: **Introduction and Motivation**

Few‑shot tabular learning sits at the intersection of two demanding constraints: real‑world problems often present data in a structured table format, yet acquiring large labeled datasets is frequently infeasible because of cost, privacy, or rarity of events.  In such settings a model must extract useful predictive signals from a handful of annotated examples while resisting the temptation to latch onto accidental patterns that happen to appear in the limited data.  Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have revealed powerful reasoning capabilities that can be harnessed as a source of external knowledge, opening a promising avenue for addressing these challenges without costly model retraining or extensive manual feature engineering.



# Real‑world importance of tabular data

Tabular datasets are ubiquitous across domains where decision making depends on heterogeneous attributes recorded in rows and columns.  Finance relies on credit histories and transaction logs; healthcare projects patient outcomes from laboratory results, demographics, and diagnostic codes; manufacturing monitors sensor readings alongside operational parameters; public policy evaluates socio‑economic indicators together with geographic information.  In each case the raw table encodes a compact representation of complex phenomena that practitioners have historically extracted manually using domain expertise.  

Because these fields often involve high stakes—such as approving loans or diagnosing disease—the reliability of predictions matters as much as raw accuracy.  Moreover, many applied settings lack large pools of annotated records: a new medical test may initially have only dozens of confirmed cases, or a nascent credit product may be rolled out to a limited pilot group before broader adoption.  Consequently, methodologies that can operate effectively on modest numbers of labeled rows while still leveraging the rich information already embedded in existing tables are crucial for translating machine‑learning advances into tangible societal impact.



# The low‑shot regime and overfitting risk

Formally, let a tabular dataset be denoted by  
\[
\mathcal{D}= \{(\mathbf{x}^{i},\mathbf{y}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]  
where each input vector $\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ comprises $d$ heterogeneous features and $\mathbf{y}^{i}$ belongs to a finite set of classes $\{1,\dots,c\}$.  In $k$‑shot learning only $k\ll N$ labeled examples are made available for training—typically $k\in\{2,4,8\}$ per class.  

When $k$ is small, standard supervised learners tend to memorize idiosyncrasies rather than discover robust relationships.  Empirically this manifests as **spurious correlations**: patterns that hold only within the tiny training subset but vanish on new data \cite{bartlett2021deep}.  For example, an irrelevant demographic attribute might by chance coincide with positive outcomes in four labelled patients; a classifier trained on those four points would incorrectly assign predictive weight to that attribute, leading to severe performance degradation when faced with realistic test samples.  

The heightened variance inherent to low‑shot regimes also hampers reliable hyperparameter tuning or model selection because cross‑validation folds contain too few samples per class to yield stable estimates.  As a result, classical tabular learners such as logistic regression or tree ensembles either underfit (due to strong regularization needed for stability) or overfit (when regularization is insufficient), making it difficult to achieve acceptable performance without injecting additional inductive bias.



# Limitations of conventional feature engineering

Traditional approaches mitigate low‑shot overfitting by manually crafting informative features – e.g., aggregating raw measurements into ratios, encoding domain‐specific thresholds, or deriving interaction terms believed a priori to influence outcomes.  This process rests on three fragile pillars:

1. **Domain expertise requirement** – Engineers must possess deep subject matter knowledge (medical guidelines, financial regulations) to identify which transformations are meaningful.  When datasets span novel domains or when experts are scarce, this step becomes a bottleneck.
2. **Scalability constraints** – Human designers cannot feasibly generate bespoke features for each new table encountered in practice.  A pipeline that works for one dataset may need entirely different transformations for another whose columns have distinct semantics.
3. **Static nature** – Manually engineered features are fixed once defined; they cannot adapt dynamically to subtle cues present in even a few labelled examples unless re‑engineered manually.

Consequently expert feature construction does not fully solve the low‑shot problem: it reduces reliance on noisy statistical patterns but replaces it with another source of potential bias—human misinterpretation or omission—and it cannot be readily deployed at scale across heterogeneous tables.



# Opportunity offered by LLMs’ reasoning abilities

Large language models trained on massive corpora encapsulate extensive factual knowledge (“older individuals have higher disease risk”, “high credit utilization predicts default”) and possess sophisticated pattern‑matching capacities acquired through self‑supervised learning on billions of tokens \cite{brown2020language}.  When presented with concise task descriptions and a handful of exemplars—a process known as **in‑context learning**—an LLM can infer underlying causal relationships without any parameter updates \cite{wei2022emergent}.  

The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework exploits precisely this capability by turning an LLM into an *automatic feature engineer*.  Its prompting strategy decomposes the reasoning process into two stages:

1. **Problem understanding** – The model first reflects on generic knowledge about how each column type (numeric, categorical) generally relates to the target task (`Step 1`).  This isolates informative columns while discarding irrelevant ones based solely on world knowledge.
2. **Rule extraction** – Leveraging both this prior understanding and the $k$ labelled demonstrations (`Step 2`), the LLM synthesizes explicit decision rules (“if age > 65 AND cholesterol > 240 then class = high risk”).  

These rules are parsed back into executable code that yields *binary* features indicating rule satisfaction for any sample.  Because rule generation occurs only once per dataset (rather than per test instance), inference thereafter reduces to evaluating inexpensive linear combinations—a stark contrast to prior LLM-based tabular approaches that required an LLM query for every test point \cite{dinh2022lift,hegselmann2023tabllm}.  

Crucially, embedding domain knowledge within rules mitigates spurious correlations: by forcing the model’s attention toward plausible causal pathways derived from its pretraining corpus, \textsf{FeatLLM} prevents accidental reliance on unrelated columns even when those columns appear correlated within the tiny training set (as demonstrated empirically in Section 5).  Moreover, because rule extraction is phrased as natural‑language generation constrained by structured response formats, it scales automatically across diverse tables without hand‑crafting new descriptors for each column set.

In sum, LLMs furnish an *external inductive bias* that complements scant labelled data while remaining agnostic to any particular downstream model architecture.  By translating this bias into concrete engineered features—rather than treating the LLM itself as an end‑to‑end predictor—researchers obtain both computational efficiency (no per‑sample API calls) and interpretability (rules are human readable).  These advantages position LLM-powered feature engineering as a compelling solution for modern low‑shot tabular learning scenarios across finance, healthcare, manufacturing, and beyond.
