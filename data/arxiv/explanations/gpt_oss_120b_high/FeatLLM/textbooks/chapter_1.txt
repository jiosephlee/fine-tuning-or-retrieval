\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

Chapter 1: **Introduction to Tabular Learning and Its Challenges**

# Definition and examples of tabular datasets  

Tabular data—also called structured data—consist of observations arranged in rows and columns, where each column corresponds to a particular attribute (or feature) and each row corresponds to a single example.  The columns are usually accompanied by human‑readable names (“age”, “blood‑pressure”, “income”) and often have short textual definitions that explain what the values represent.  Because this format mirrors how data are stored in relational databases, spreadsheets, and many statistical software packages, it appears across almost every domain that deals with real‑world decision making.  

In healthcare, a patient record may contain demographic fields (age, gender), laboratory measurements (cholesterol level), and categorical diagnoses (presence or absence of a disease).  In finance, credit‑risk tables list borrowers’ income, employment status, outstanding debts, and a binary label indicating whether they defaulted on a loan.  In public policy or criminology, community‑level surveys record attributes such as average income, education rate, and crime counts per neighborhood.  The paper’s experimental suite includes datasets such as **Adult** (predicting whether an individual earns over \$50 k), **Bank** (whether a customer subscribes to a term deposit), **Blood** (donor return behavior), **Heart** (presence of coronary artery disease), **Communities** (regional crime rates), as well as synthetic collections like **Sequence‑type** (classifying mathematical sequences).  All of these exemplify the canonical tabular setting where each instance is fully described by a fixed set of features whose semantics are available in natural language.

The ubiquity of tabular datasets stems from their ease of collection: most administrative systems already store information in tables; most surveys are designed to produce rows of responses; and many scientific measurements are reported as columns of numeric or categorical variables.  Consequently, building predictive models for tabular tasks has long been a central activity in machine learning research and practice.

# Typical supervised learning workflow for tabular data  

The conventional pipeline for supervised tabular learning proceeds through several deterministic stages.  First, a practitioner gathers a labeled dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{i}, y^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\) where each \(\mathbf{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\) encodes the \(d\) raw feature values for sample \(i\) and \(y^{i}\) denotes its class label (or regression target).  Next comes *preprocessing*: missing values are imputed; categorical variables are encoded (e.g., one‑hot or ordinal encoding); continuous features may be normalized or transformed; and sometimes domain knowledge guides feature engineering—creating interaction terms or derived variables that better capture underlying mechanisms.

After preprocessing the designer selects a model family—logistic regression for its interpretability, gradient‑boosted trees such as XGBoost for strong performance on heterogeneous data, random forests for robustness against overfitting—or more recent deep architectures like tabular transformers.  Hyperparameters (tree depth, regularization strength) are tuned using cross‑validation or hold‑out validation sets.  The chosen model is then trained on the labeled portion of \(\mathcal{D}\) by minimizing a loss function—commonly cross‑entropy for classification—followed by evaluation on an unseen test split using metrics such as area under the ROC curve (AUROC).  

In practice this workflow assumes that enough labeled examples are available to estimate the model’s parameters reliably while also providing sufficient signal during validation to select hyperparameters without severe variance.  When these assumptions hold—typically when thousands or tens of thousands of labeled rows exist—the pipeline yields accurate predictions with modest effort.

# Why few‑shot learning is needed (label cost, privacy)  

Despite its apparent simplicity, acquiring large quantities of high‑quality labels for tabular problems can be prohibitively expensive or even impossible.  In medical domains each label may correspond to a costly diagnostic test administered by specialists; in finance labeled outcomes such as default events might be rare events that require years of longitudinal tracking; in privacy‑sensitive settings like government surveys participants may refuse to disclose personal attributes unless strong safeguards are in place.  

These constraints motivate *few‑shot* or *low‑shot* learning: training models with only \(k\) labeled examples per class where \(k\ll N\).  The research community has shown that when the number of shots drops below ten—a realistic scenario in many real‐world deployments—standard machine‑learning methods begin to fail dramatically because they cannot disentangle signal from noise without sufficient data points.  Moreover, specialized techniques such as semi‑supervised pretraining on large unlabeled corpora or transfer learning across heterogeneous tables still require substantial auxiliary data that may not be available or may violate privacy regulations.

As highlighted in the referenced study, few‑shot tabular learning is an active frontier precisely because it reflects realistic operational constraints: organizations often have abundant *features* but only a handful of *labels*, yet they still need an automated decision system that can act reliably at scale.

# Failure modes of standard models with limited data (over‑fitting, spurious correlations)  

When only a handful of labeled rows are present, classical algorithms tend to overfit—the model learns patterns that perfectly fit the tiny training set but do not generalize beyond it.  Overfitting manifests especially in high‑dimensional settings where \(d\) can rival—or exceed—the number of shots \(k\).  For instance, tree‐based ensembles can grow deep branches that memorize exact combinations of feature values present in the training examples; linear models may assign large coefficients to noise dimensions simply because those dimensions happen to correlate by chance with the limited labels.

A particularly insidious failure mode is *spurious correlation*.  In low‐shot regimes random fluctuations can make an irrelevant column appear predictive purely by coincidence.  Because many standard learners lack explicit inductive bias against such coincidences when data are scarce, they inadvertently amplify these false signals during training.  Empirically this phenomenon has been observed across benchmark datasets: adding unrelated columns from another domain dramatically degrades performance for naive learners but has much milder effects on approaches that incorporate stronger priors.

The consequence is unreliable predictions—models may output high confidence on inputs whose feature values follow accidental patterns rather than genuine causal relationships—a risk unacceptable in high‐stakes applications like healthcare triage or loan approval.

# Motivation for leveraging external knowledge sources  

One promising remedy is to inject *external knowledge* into the learning process so that models rely less on fragile statistical cues derived from tiny training sets and more on robust facts encoded elsewhere.  Large language models (LLMs), trained on massive text corpora covering diverse domains—including medical literature, financial reports, scientific textbooks—have demonstrated an ability to retrieve relevant factual relationships without any task‐specific fine‑tuning.  

The paper under discussion exploits precisely this capability: rather than using an LLM as an end‐to‐end predictor (which would require one inference per test sample), it treats the LLM as an *expert feature engineer*.  By prompting the LLM with a brief description of the task (“Does this patient’s myocardial infarction complications indicate chronic heart failure?”) together with a handful of labeled examples and human‐readable feature definitions, the model can infer plausible *rules* linking feature conditions to class labels.  These rules serve as binary engineered features (“age > 65 AND blood pressure > 140”) that are then fed into an extremely simple downstream predictor—a nonnegative linear model—trained on the few available shots.

Because LLMs bring world knowledge about typical relationships (“older age increases disease risk”) they can filter out irrelevant columns early during rule extraction; they also help avoid overfitting by providing *inductive bias* toward sensible rules rather than arbitrary statistical artifacts.  Moreover, once rules have been distilled into binary features there is no need to invoke the LLM again at inference time—reducing latency dramatically compared with methods that query the LLM per sample—and only API‐level access is required (no fine‐tuning infrastructure).

Thus external knowledge sources address both core challenges identified earlier: they mitigate overfitting by supplying strong priors on which features matter; they protect against spurious correlations by guiding attention toward causally plausible conditions; and they enable few‑shot learners to achieve performance comparable to conventional models that would otherwise require dozens or hundreds of labeled examples.

---  

In summary, tabular data dominate real‐world decision processes but become highly challenging when only minimal labeled examples exist. Conventional supervised pipelines falter due to overfitting and sensitivity to accidental correlations. By leveraging expansive prior knowledge embedded in large language models—as exemplified by the FeatLLM framework—we can automate intelligent feature generation that aligns with human expertise while preserving low computational cost at inference time. This approach marks a significant step toward practical few‑shot tabular learning capable of thriving under realistic label scarcity constraints.
