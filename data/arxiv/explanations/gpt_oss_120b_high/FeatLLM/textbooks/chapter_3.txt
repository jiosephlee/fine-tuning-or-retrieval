Chapter 3: Few‑Shot Learning for Tabular Data: Prior Work  

# Unsupervised & Self‑Supervised Pre‑training  

A central obstacle for learning from tabular data is that real-world tables are often high‑dimensional yet only a handful of labeled rows are available.  Early few‑shot approaches therefore sought to endow a model with useful inductive biases before it ever sees any class label.  One family of techniques relies on **unsupervised** or **self‑supervised** objectives that can be applied solely to the raw feature matrix.  The intuition is that a model which has already learned how to reconstruct missing entries, predict transformed versions of a column, or discriminate between perturbed views of the same row will possess representations that capture generic statistical regularities—such as correlations among numeric columns or typical co‑occurrences among categorical values—without overfitting to a limited labeled set.  

In practice these ideas have taken several concrete forms.  Some methods mask a random proportion of entries in each row and train an autoencoder‑like network to fill them back in; others replace values according to a noise distribution and ask the model to recover the original distribution \cite{arik2021tabnet}.  A complementary line introduces **contrastive learning** objectives, where two stochastic augmentations of the same row are forced to produce similar embeddings while different rows are pushed apart \cite{bahri2022scarf, chen2020simple}.  Although such augmentations are straightforward for images or text, defining semantically meaningful transformations for heterogeneous columns remains challenging—hence these strategies have produced only modest gains when only a few labeled examples are present \cite{nam2023stunt}.  

An especially notable self‑supervised direction avoids any real dataset altogether by synthesizing many artificial tables drawn from diverse parametric families.  A Bayesian neural network is then pretrained on this synthetic corpus so that its internal priors already encode a broad spectrum of joint distributions over features and targets \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn}.  When confronted with a new downstream table, the pretrained network can immediately adapt its predictions by feeding both training and test rows as input—a form of “zero‑shot” inference that nevertheless benefits from having rehearsed millions of plausible tabular patterns during pretraining.  Such synthetic pretraining sidesteps the need for large unlabeled collections tied to any specific domain but still depends on assuming that new tasks resemble some mixture of those synthetic data distributions.

Across all these unsupervised approaches the shared hypothesis is simple: **if an encoder can capture generic structures within tabular rows without supervision, then only a small number $k$ ($k \ll N$) of labeled rows will suffice** to train a lightweight classifier on top of those representations.  However, empirical studies reported in recent literature indicate that while these methods improve robustness compared with training directly on raw features, their gains plateau under very low‐shot regimes—especially when numeric‐categorical heterogeneity limits effective augmentations \cite{nam2023stunt}.


# Transfer Learning Across Heterogeneous Tables  

Another avenue pursued by researchers acknowledges that many public benchmarks contain thousands of distinct tables spanning medical records, financial logs, sensor measurements, and more.  If a model can discover **semantic relationships among column names**—for example recognizing that “age”, “years”, and “patient_age” often denote similar concepts—it may transfer knowledge gathered from one table to another even when their raw values differ drastically.  

A concrete instantiation of this idea trains transformer‐based encoders on a large corpus of heterogeneous tables where each column is accompanied by its textual name \cite{wang2022transtab}.  By attending jointly to column names and cell values during pretraining, the model learns cross‑table embeddings that respect both linguistic similarity (“salary” vs “income”) and statistical similarity (similar value ranges).  Once pretrained, such an encoder can be applied zero‑shot or fine‑tuned with merely $k$ labeled rows on a previously unseen table—a process known as **transfer learning across tables**.  

Parallel work has explored **synthetic generation** techniques such as those used by TabPFN \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn}.  Rather than mining real table collections directly—a task hampered by licensing constraints—the authors produce artificial tables whose marginal distributions emulate those observed across many domains (e.g., mixtures of Gaussians for numeric fields, Dirichlet draws for categorical frequencies).  By training a Bayesian neural network on this synthetically diverse ensemble, it acquires priors capable of rapidly adapting when supplied with actual supervised examples from any target table.\footnote{Both TransTab \cite{wang2022transtab} and TabPFN \cite{hollmann2022tabpfn} belong to this line-of-work although they differ in whether they rely on real versus synthetic source corpora.}  

In essence these transfer approaches view each table as an instance drawn from an underlying family of tabular problems; by exposing a model repeatedly to many members of this family it gradually internalizes reusable patterns—be they statistical regularities among numeric columns or linguistic cues embedded in feature names—that dramatically reduce the amount of supervised signal required at test time.


# Language‑Interfaced Methods  

A more recent paradigm acknowledges that Large Language Models (LLMs) encode vast amounts of world knowledge captured during their pretraining on massive text corpora.  If one could *translate* tabular data into natural language prompts, perhaps an LLM could answer classification questions directly without any parameter updates at all—a strategy sometimes called **in‑context learning** because the model “learns” from demonstration examples embedded in its prompt.\footnote{The term originates from early vision-language works where examples are shown alongside instructions at inference time rather than during gradient descent~\cite{brown2020language}.}  

Early language‑interfaced tabular learners adopt exactly this recipe: they serialize each row into a sequence such as “`age` is 45…, `blood_pressure` is high…, Answer: `positive`”, concatenate dozens—or sometimes hundreds—of such demonstrations together with natural language descriptions of each feature’s type and semantics (\textit{e.g., “`age` denotes years since birth”}), then hand this prompt off to an LLM such as GPT‑3 or T0 \cite{sanh2022multitask}.  The model’s response is interpreted as either a class label (“yes/no”) or a probability estimate derived from its generated text \cite{dinh2022lift}.  

Building upon plain prompting several extensions emerged:

* **TABLET** augments the basic prompt with an auxiliary classifier–derived set of rule prototypes designed to steer the LLM toward more plausible reasoning chains \cite{slack2023tablet}.  
* **TabLLM** goes one step further by fine‐tuning an LLM’s weights using parameter–efficient techniques rather than relying solely on prompt engineering \cite{hegselmann2023tabllm}.  
* Simple **in-context learners** augment their prompts with only raw demonstrations and rely on chain‐of‐thought style reasoning instructions—to coax multi-step logical deductions from the LLM \cite{wei2022emergent}.  

All three systems share a common pipeline:   
1) Encode every feature name $f_j$ ($j=1,\dots,d$) together with its value $\mathbf{x}_j^i$ into textual fragments;  
2) Append a natural language task description (“Does this patient have chronic heart failure?”);  
3) Add $k$ demonstration rows together with known answers $\mathbf y^i$, forming an input $\{\mathbf x^i,\mathbf y^i\}_{i=1}^{k}$;  
4) Query the LLM once per test instance and interpret its output as $\hat{\mathbf y}$.  

Because LLMs attend jointly over all tokens—including every serialized feature—their ability to reason about relationships among columns appears bounded by two practical factors discussed below.


# Parameter‑efficient Tuning Methods  

While pure prompting avoids any gradient updates altogether, researchers have observed that directly adapting an LLM’s parameters—even modestly—can markedly improve performance on structured tasks where domain‐specific jargon or subtle numeric thresholds matter \cite{phebutt?}.  Fine‐tuning full networks however would demand access to billions‐scale weight matrices—a requirement rarely satisfied by commercial API providers (e.g., GPT‑4 offers only inference endpoints).  

To bridge this gap **parameter–efficient tuning** (PET) strategies have been introduced.  Two prominent families stand out:

* **LoRA** (“Low-Rank Adaptation”) injects trainable low–rank matrices into each attention layer’s weight matrices while keeping original weights frozen \cite{hu2021lora}.  Because only these small matrices are updated during training on $k$ labeled samples (often via stochastic gradient descent), computational overhead remains modest yet expressive enough for downstream adaptation.
* **IA3** (“Intrinsic Adapter”) modifies only scalar scaling factors associated with hidden activations inside transformer blocks \cite{liu2022few}.   Similar to LoRA it keeps base weights intact but offers even lighter memory footprints because fewer parameters need gradient updates whenever $k$ is extremely small.

Both LoRA and IA3 have been employed in recent tabular LLM studies such as **TabLLM**, which couples IA3 finetuning with T0’s publicly released checkpoint \citet[see][Table X]{hegselmann2023tabllm}.  By limiting trainable parameters one can embed domain knowledge gleaned from hundreds—or even thousands—of unlabeled rows while still respecting API constraints imposed by closed‐source models.



# Key Limitations of Prior LLM‑Based Approaches  

Despite their promise, language–interfaced algorithms encounter two intertwined practical bottlenecks when deployed on genuine tabular workloads:

## Per–Sample Inference Cost  

All currently published systems—including pure in‑context learners, TABLET and tuned variants like TabLLM—perform **one LLM forward pass per test sample**. Formally, given $N_{\text{test}}$ unseen rows $\{\mathbf{x}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}}$ one must construct $N_{\text{test}}$ separate prompts
\[
\texttt{
Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^{i}) + \texttt{
Task description } + \texttt{
Few–shot demos}
\]
and send them individually through an API call whose latency may stretch even beyond seconds per token depending on service tier \citet[e.g.,][]{openai2023gpt4}. As $N_{\text{test}}$ grows—typical batch sizes reach thousands—the cumulative wallclock time becomes prohibitive for real‐time applications such as fraud detection or clinical decision support where latency constraints are tight.\footnote{In our empirical evaluation FeatLLM required merely thirty API queries regardless of dataset size because it extracts rules once offline rather than per instance.}

## Prompt Length Constraints  

Current generation APIs impose hard limits on total token count per request (e.g., roughly four thousand tokens for GPT 3.5 Turbo). A single prompt comprises three portions described earlier: feature descriptions (often several words per column), serialized demonstrations ($k\times d$ tokens), plus optional chain-of-thought instructions.\footnote{
If $d=150$ features each described by ten tokens and $k=8$, then even before adding reasoning discourse we already exceed eight thousand tokens—a violation.
}
When $d$ grows beyond roughly one hundred columns—as seen in datasets like *Communities* and *Myocardial* discussed later—the required serialization overflows typical context windows causing either truncation (and loss of crucial information) or aggressive summarisation that harms downstream reasoning.\citet[see][]{liu2023lost} report systematic performance drops once prompt length crosses ~30% over capacity.\

Both constraints stem directly from treating an LLM as an end-to-end predictor rather than as an auxiliary component whose output can be cached or reused across many instances.\citet[see][]{wang2018selfconsistency}? Additionally many commercial providers restrict users' ability to perform repeated high-volume inference due both to cost considerations (~\$ per thousand tokens) and policy limits governing API usage.\footnote{
The present FeatLLM framework circumvents this limitation entirely by extracting rule-based binary features once during offline processing; afterwards predictions are made solely via inexpensive linear models.
}

Collectively these limitations underscore why purely LLM-powered tabular learners—while conceptually elegant—have struggled for adoption outside laboratory settings.


--- 

In summary, earlier attempts at few‑shot tabular learning progressed through three major stages: first building domain-agnostic encoders via unsupervised/self‑supervised objectives (including synthetic pretraining through TabPFN); second leveraging semantic signals embedded in column names via cross-table transfer mechanisms like TransTab; third confronting classic tabular pipelines head‑on by converting rows into natural language prompts fed directly into large language models—both without tuning (pure prompting) and with light weight updates through LoRA/IA3 adapters (parameter-efficient tuning). Although each direction contributed valuable insights regarding how prior knowledge can offset scarce supervision, practical obstacles related both to per-sample inference costs and prompt length limits motivated subsequent research seeking more efficient usages of LLMs—as exemplified later by frameworks that treat LLMs primarily as feature engineers rather than end-to-end predictors.
