### How does “incoherent processing” using a random orthogonal matrix M reduce quantization error in FP8 attention?
Question:
Section 3.4 describes multiplying Q and K by an orthogonal matrix M before quantizing to FP8, claiming it spreads out outliers. 

- Why does applying an orthogonal transformation not change the final attention output?
- What properties must M have (e.g., Hadamard × diagonal ±1) to be efficiently applied?
- Does this transformation affect gradient computation in the backward pass?

I’m looking for an intuitive explanation backed by mathematical reasoning.

Answer:
**Answer**

In FP8 FlashAttention the authors first apply an *incoherent* linear map  

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf Q'&=\mathbf Q\,\mathbf M,\qquad &
\mathbf K'&=\mathbf K\,\mathbf M,
\\[2pt]
%
& (\text{and later }\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\!\!\!
      \displaystyle \textstyle 
      {\bf V}\text{'s rotation is performed analogously})
.\end{aligned}
$$

and only then quantise $\displaystyle
      {\bf Q}',{\bf K}'$
(and later ${\bf V}$) to FP8.  
The trick exploits two facts:

* an orthogonal matrix preserves inner products, so the attention scores are unchanged;
* a random rotation spreads occasional very‑large (“outlier”) entries over many coordinates,
  thereby shrinking their dynamic range before rounding to an 8‑bit mantissa.

### 1. Why the output does not change  

The usual multi‑head attention score matrix is  

$$
    {\bf S}= \alpha\,{\bf Q}{\bf K}^{\!\top}, 
    \qquad 
    \alpha=\frac 1{\sqrt {d}} .
$$

If we replace ${\bf Q},{\bf K}$ by their rotated versions we obtain  

$$
\begin{aligned}
   {\tilde{\bf S}} &=
     \alpha\,({\bf Q}{\bf M})({\bf K}{\bf M})^{\!\top}          \\[2pt]
                  &=   
     \alpha\,({\bf Q}{\bf M})({\bf M}^{\!\top}{\bf K}^{!\top}) \\[2pt]
                  &=   
     \alpha\,{\underbrace{
           (\;{\bf Q}({\bf M}{\bf M}^{!\top})\:)}_
           {\displaystyle =\,{\bf Q}} }\,{\bf K}^{!\top}
       =   \alpha\,{\bf Q}{\bf K}^{!\top}
       =   {\bf S}.
   .
