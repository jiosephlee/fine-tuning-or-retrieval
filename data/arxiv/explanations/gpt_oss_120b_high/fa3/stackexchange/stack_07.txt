\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### Why do exponential special functions on H100 have such low throughput compared to Tensor‑Core matmuls?
Question:
The paper mentions that H100 provides ~989 TFLOPs for FP16 matmuls but only ~3.9 TFLOPs for exponentials used in softmax. 

- Which hardware unit executes these functions (e.g., Multi‑Function Unit) and why is it bandwidth limited?
- Is there any way to accelerate exponentials using Tensor Cores or other instructions?
- Does overlapping softmax with GEMM fully hide this bottleneck, or are there remaining limits?

Understanding this hardware imbalance explains why overlapping matters.

Answer:
The huge gap between the \$989 \\,\mathrm{{TFLOP/s}}\,$ peak for FP16 matrix‑multiplication and the \$3\.9 \\,\mathrm{{TFLOP/s}}\,$ peak quoted for the exponential in the H100 comes from the way NVIDIA has partitioned the silicon.  

All dense GEMMs are carried out by the **Tensor Cores** (exposed as the WGMMA instruction on Hopper). A Tensor Core operates on \$8\times8\$‑element tiles at every clock cycle and every streaming multiprocessor (SM) contains many such units; together they give almost a teraflop of FP16 MACs per SM. By contrast, transcendental operations such as \$\exp\$,\ \$\log\$ or \$\sin\$ are not part of a matrix‑multiply‑friendly datum path. They are executed by a separate **Multi‑Function Unit (MFU)** — the modern descendant of the older Special‑Function Unit (SFU). Each SM houses only a handful of MFU pipelines (the paper notes “16 special‑function operations per SM per clock”), so even if all 132 SMs fire at the boost clock of \$1\.83\,\\,\mathrm{{GHz}}\,$ the total work‑rate is only  

\[
16\,\\,\mathrm{{ops/SM/cycle}}\times132\,\\,\mathrm{{SM}}\times1\.83\,\\,\mathrm{{GHz}}\approx3\.9\,\\,\mathrm{{TFLOP/s}}\!,
\]

which matches the figure reported in §2\.2 of FlashAttention‑3\. Because this ceiling is set by **compute resources**, not by DRAM bandwidth, we call it “compute‑limited” rather than “bandwidth‑limited”.  

Why can’t we simply ask a Tensor Core to evaluate \$\exp\$? A Tensor Core implements a fixed macro‑operation \$\sum_{i}a_{i}b_{i}\,$ plus optional bias addition and scaling — its datapath has no provision for nonlinear arithmetic beyond multiply‑add. NVIDIA has not published an instruction that maps an elementwise \$\exp\$ onto WGMMA, nor any fused “exp‑plus‑MAC” primitive. Consequently any attempt to speed up \$\exp\$ must fall back on regular CUDA cores: either use an approximation based on a short polynomial or piecewise‑linear table lookup written with ordinary arithmetic instructions, or rely on the library implementation that already runs on the MFU. Neither approach can benefit from massive parallelism of Tensor Cores without substantial algorithmic redesign; doing so would also increase register pressure and likely hurt occupancy.  

The practical consequence for attention kernels is that an entire row of scores \$\mathbf S_{i}\$
must be transformed by  
\$ \widetilde{\mathbf P}_{i}= \exp(\mathbf S_{i}-m_{i}) \$.
For head dimension \$d\$ typical values (\$/64\/,\ /\128\/,\ /\256\/)
this means thousands of exponentials per token pair — far more than what
a handful of MFUs can supply if they run alone.
The authors therefore overlap these cheap operations with expensive GEMMs
using **warp specialization**: one warpgroup continuously issues WGMMA while
another warpgroup proceeds through the softmax pipeline  
(max reduction $\rightarrow{}$
subtraction $\rightarrow{}$
$\exp$
 $\rightarrow{}$
row sum).
Because WGMMA is asynchronous thanks to Hopper’s TMA/Tensor Core pipeline,
both units can progress simultaneously without incurring stalls — this is what
they call “ping pong” scheduling.  

Does this overlap completely hide the bottleneck? Empirically it does not erase
it entirely but it reduces its impact dramatically.
The paper reports a rise from roughly \$570\,\\,\mathrm{{TFLOP/s}}\$
(softmax serialized after GEMM) to \$620\$–\$640\,\\,\mathrm{{TFLOP/s}}\$
when softmax is pipelined underneath WGMMA — a relative improvement
of about \$10\,%\-$12\,%\$.  
The residual gap persists because several factors limit full masking:

* **Data dependencies** — the row maximum \$m_{i}\$
  must be known before any exponentials in that block can start,
  so each block still incurs at least one cycle where both units idle waiting
  for \$m_{i}\$.  
* **Barrier overhead** — the producer/consumer warpgroups synchronize via
  `bar.sync`; although lightweight,
  these barriers prevent perfect back-to-back issuing when block sizes are small
  or when causal masking cuts half the work.
* **Register pressure** — the two-stage pipeline keeps both
  the current score tile \$\mathbf S_{\mathrm{{cur}}}\$
  and the next tile \$\mathbf S_{\mathrm{{next}}}\$
  alive in registers;
  if we pushed for deeper pipelines or larger tiles we would run out
  of registers and have to spill,
  which would lower overall throughput.

In long‐sequence regimes where \$N^{2}\$
dominates over these fixed latencies,
overlapping pushes softmax cost down to well below \$5\,%\$
of total runtime,
achieving close to \$75\,%\$
utilisation of H100’s FP16 peak as shown in Figure 4 of FlashAttention 3.
For shorter sequences or heavily masked causal attention there remain enough idle
cycles that further software tricks —
e.g., tighter vectorized polynomial approximations executed on CUDA cores —
might shave off another percent or two,
but they cannot reach
the same order‐of‐magnitude speedup that tensor cores already provide for GEMM.

In summary,
exponentials on H100 are executed by a small MFU whose per‐SM throughput caps at roughly
\$3\.9\,\\,\mathrm{{TFLOP/s}}\$
— orders of magnitude lower than tensor‐core matmul capacity because those units are deliberately tiny compared with MAC arrays.
There is no direct way to route an \$\exp\$ through a tensor core;
any acceleration must rely on software approximations running on regular cores.
Overlapping softmax with GEMM through warp specialization effectively hides most —
but not all —
of this hardware imbalance,
making attention kernels bottlenecked primarily by matrix multiplication again rather than
by transcendental evaluation.
This explains why FlashAttention 3’s main performance gain stems from carefully interleaving these two disparate compute streams rather than from trying to squeeze more FLOPs out<|endoftext|>
