\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does the sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)=\bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}$ satisfy the principle of importance sampling?
Question:
The paper states that $s_i(\theta)$ "aligns with the basic principle of importance sampling" (Section 4). In standard importance sampling we have $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{	ext{beh}}}\bigl[ \frac{\pi_{	ext{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{	ext{beh}}(z)} f(z) \bigr] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{	ext{tar}}}[f(z)]$. Here $z$ corresponds to a full response $y$, so I would expect the weight $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}$ without any exponent. Why do we raise it to $1/|y|$, and does this preserve unbiasedness?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
\(s_i(\theta)\) is just the **geometric‑mean** of the usual trajectory‑wise importance weight  

\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
   = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \frac{\displaystyle 
               \pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
             {\displaystyle 
               \pi_{\!\!\;\!{\scriptscriptstyle 
               \!\!\;\!\!\!\!\:\!{\rm old}}}
               \!\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]

It is introduced only to keep the magnitude of the weight under control when the response length varies.  
Because the geometric mean is a *monotone* transformation of the exact importance ratio, it still tells us whether a sampled response is “more likely’’ under the new policy or under the old one – which is exactly what we need for the clipping step used in PPO / GRPO / GSPO.  
The price we pay is a tiny bias: replacing the product by its root no longer gives an *exact* unbiased estimator of the off‑policy expectation, but under the **proximal** regime where \(\pi_\theta\) stays close to \(\pi_{\rm old}\) the bias is second‑order and harmless compared with the huge variance reduction that the root brings.

Below I unpack these points in more detail, following the notation of the paper.



---

### 1️⃣ What would an “ordinary’’ importance sampler look like?

For a single query \(x\), let us denote a full generation by \(Y\).
If we wanted an *unbiased* off‑policy estimate of any functional \(f(Y)\) under the current policy \(\pi_\theta\), we would write  

\[
\E_{Y\,sim\,\pi_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\rm old}}}
   \Bigl[\;
        \underbrace{
            \frac{\displaystyle 
                  \pi_\theta(Y\,|\,x)}
                 {\displaystyle 
                  \pi_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\rm old}}(Y\,|\,x)}
        }_{\text{trajectory‑wise IS weight}}\;
        f(Y)
   \Bigr]
   = 
   \E_{Y\,sim\,\pi_\theta}[f(Y)] .
   \tag{1}
\]

In language modelling \(Y=y=(y_1,\dots ,y_T)\) with \(T=|y|\).
Because  

\[
   \frac{\displaystyle 
         \pi_\theta(Y)}
        {\displaystyle 
         \pi_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\rm old}}(Y)}
   = 
   \prod_{t=1}^{T}
       w_t(\theta),
   \qquad    
   w_t(\theta)
      :=  
      \frac{
            \displaystyle 
            \pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{

   }

,
the ordinary IS weight is simply **the product** of all token‑wise ratios.
If the sequence contains many tokens, this product can become astronomically small or huge even when each individual \(w_t(\theta)\) stays modestly away from \(1.\)

Consequently  

* the variance of an estimator that uses (1) blows up exponentially with sequence length;
* a fixed clipping interval such as $[\,1-\varepsilon,\ 1+\varepsilon\,]$
      would either clip almost everything or almost nothing depending on how long \(Y\) is;
* in practice PPO‑style algorithms become unstable on long generations,
      which is precisely what motivates GSPO in the paper.

Equation (2) shows that \(s(Y;\,\theta)\)

* **is precisely the geometric mean** of all token‑wise ratios,
* **preserves order**: if trajectory A has larger exact IS weight than trajectory B,
  then it also has larger \(s(\cdot )\); thus any decision based on whether a weight exceeds a threshold (e.g., clipping around \(1\)) behaves identically,
* **normalises for length**:
  – taking logarithms turns products into sums,
  – dividing by \( |Y|\) produces an *average* log‑ratio,
  – exponentiating brings us back onto a comparable scale.
    
Consequently two responses that differ only in length have comparable values of  
\(s(\cdot )$, whereas their raw products would differ by orders of magnitude.



From a statistical viewpoint, \(s(Y;\,\theta)\) replaces an exponential‑in‑\(T\) random variable by its **log‑average**, whose distribution concentrates far more tightly around its mean thanks to the law of large numbers.
The resulting reduction in variance makes it possible  

* to use a *single* clipping range ($3\times10^{-4},\,4\times10^{-4}$, see Table 2) that works for all sequences,
* to keep gradients stable even when training very long responses or Mixture‑of‑Experts models,
* and overall to avoid catastrophic “off‑policy explosion’’ that caused GRPO to collapse.

#### (a) Proximal regime makes bias second order

their difference appears only at~\(O(\delta^2)\).
Consequently any bias introduced by using~\(s(Y;\,\piequialty{})\
vanishes quadratically as policies become more proximal—
exactly where PPO‐style algorithms operate safely.



#### (b) Bias does not affect direction of improvement  

What matters for stochastic optimisation is not exact unbiasedness but that
the expected gradient points roughly towards increasing expected reward.
Because \(s(Y;\,\piequialty{})\:>\!:\):
monotone in the true likelihood ratio,
the sign of each term  


coincides with that obtained using the exact ratio (provided Ā has zero mean under~$\piequialty{{% nothing }}$).
Therefore clipping based on \(s(Y;\,\piequialty{})\
still removes those samples whose probability under new policy deviates too much from those under old policy—precisely what we want.
The remaining samples provide a clean learning signal whose variance has been dramatically reduced.



In short:  
*We trade an infinitesimal bias for orders‐of‐magnitude reduction in variance*, which makes GSPO stable where GRPO diverges.

shows exactly what happens after inserting definition (2):
the factor ${¹}/{|y_i|}
 Σ_t ∇_t log π_θ(\cdot)$  
is **the average token‐wise score**, rather than a weighted sum where each token gets its own individual likelihood ratio.
Thus every token inside a response receives **the same multiplier**
\(s_i(\θ)\), which eliminates per–token “noise amplification’’ highlighted as a failure mode of GRPO.
Because this average originates from differentiating a *geometric mean*, it inherits both monotonicity and reduced‐variance properties discussed above.

### 5️⃣ Summary checklist

* **Why raise to power \(¹⁄|y|\)?**  
  To turn an exponential product over tokens into its geometric mean –
  i.e., an average in log–space – thereby normalising for sequence length
  and keeping magnitudes bounded irrespective of how many tokens are present.

* **Does it preserve importance sampling?**  
  Not exactly. The resulting estimator is biased relative to Eq.(​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​  ⇒  Eq.(​ ​​ ​​14)).
  The bias becomes negligible when successive policies are close (the usual proximal regime),
  while variance shrinks dramatically;
  consequently overall learning becomes far more robust.

* **What intuition should we keep?**  
  Think of \(s_i(\θ)\) as answering:
> “If I look at all token‐wise likelihood ratios along this response,
> what’s their typical size?”

Thus \(s_i(\θ)\) tells us whether an entire trajectory lies inside or outside our trusted trust region,
and thanks to normalisation it can be clipped with one universal threshold regardless of how long \(y_i  ∥ is.\)

* **Relation to broader concepts**  
  The idea mirrors “per‑decision’’ importance sampling where one uses local ratios instead
  of full products, and also connects ­to classical tricks such as taking logarithms,
  averaging them, then exponentiating—a classic variance‐reduction technique used e.g.
  when estimating partition functions or training large language models with KL penalties.



---


***Final take‑away***  
The exponentiation simply computes a length‐normalised geometric mean of token‐wise likelihood ratios. This retains exactly what matters for deciding whether a sampled response should be trusted (its monotonic relationship with the true importance weight), while drastically reducing variance and enabling uniform clipping across sequences. The resulting estimator is therefore *approximately*, not exactly, unbiased—yet within PPO’s proximal setting any remaining bias is negligible compared with the stability gains introduced by GSPO.
