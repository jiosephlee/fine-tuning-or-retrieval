\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GRPO apply token‑level importance ratios when the reward is defined for the whole sequence?
Question:
In Section 2 the paper defines the GRPO objective (Eq. \eqref{equ:grpo}) using token‑wise importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$, yet the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ is computed from a sequence‑level reward $r(x,y_i)$. I would have expected an importance weight that matches the unit of the reward (i.e., a sequence‑level weight). Is there a theoretical reason why GRPO still uses token‑level weights, or is this simply an oversight that leads to the instability described later?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
GRPO inherits the **token‑wise importance ratios** from vanilla PPO because PPO was derived for **step‑wise rewards** (one advantage per action).  When the authors of GRPO replaced the step‑wise value function estimate with a *group‑relative* scalar advantage – which is computed from a whole sequence reward – they kept the PPO‐style per‑token ratios as a heuristic.  This creates a mismatch between the **unit of the importance weight (a single token probability)** and the **unit of the advantage (a full sequence score)**, violating the basic requirement of importance sampling that “the weight must correspond to the same random variable whose expectation we are estimating”.  The paper itself points out that this mismatch is the root of GRPO’s high‑variance updates and eventual instability; it isn’t an accidental typo but a design flaw that GSPO fixes by moving the importance weight to the **sequence level**.

Below I expand on these points, tying them explicitly to the equations and arguments in *Group Sequence Policy Optimization*.

---

### 1\. Where do token‑level ratios come from?

In standard **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** for sequential decision making we have

$$

    \mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\frac1{|y|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
        \min\bigl(
            w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\,
            {\rm clip}\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_t
       \bigr)
    \Bigr].
$$

with  

$$
w_t(\theta)=
    {\displaystyle 
        {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}{%
               \pi_{\;\!\!\;\!\,\!{\rm old}}
               _{\!\!\;\!
               }(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t})}}

$$

PPO assumes that at each time step $t$ we have an **advantage estimate $\,\widehat A_t$** coming from a **value model** $V_\phi(x,y_{<t})$. The gradient theorem for policy gradients tells us that  

$$
\nabla_\theta J=
    {\displaystyle
        {\mathbb E}\!\bigl[
            (\nabla_\theta\,{\log }\;\!
                \pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}))
                ~ {\widehat A}_t

$$  

So it makes sense to weight every **log‑probability gradient** with its own importance ratio $w_t(\boldsymbol{\!\!\!\!\;\;\;\;\;\;} )$.

### 4\. Is it merely an oversight?

It is not a typographical slip; rather it reflects a ***design decision*** carried over from PPO without rederiving the objective under a scalar–reward setting.  
The authors themselves label this mismatch “the fundamental misapplication … which … leads to high‑variance training noise”.

If you ignore clipping altogether and use only a scalar reward,

Writing this sum explicitly shows every *token’s* log–gradient receives exactly THE SAME SCALAR MULTIPLIER—no per‐token importance ratio appears at all.  
GRPE tried to borrow PP­O’s clipping benefits while keeping *that single global multiplier*, but mistakenly inserted per-token likelihood ratios inside ​the ‟min/clip” operator.

### 7\. Takeaway

*Why does GRPE use token‐level ratios?*  
Because it was built on top of PPO’s formulation where each action has its own stepwise importance correction; when replacing stepwise advantages with a group‐relative scalar alone, its authors left the original ratios unchanged for implementation convenience.

*Is there a theoretical justification?*  
No robust one; under strict importance sampling theory & REINFORCE/PG with scalar rewards you should use ***sequence‐level*** ratios. The paper demonstrates mathematically that employing token‐wise ratios yields an ill‐posed objective whose gradient contains unnecessarily high variance terms.

*What happens because of it?*  
The mismatch injects noise proportional to response length, amplifies through clipping, and eventually leads to unstable training—especially evident when scaling up model size or response length.

*How should it be fixed?*  
Replace $w_{i,t}$ by a properly normalized sequence likelihood ratio $s_i$, exactly what GSPE does; optionally keep per‐token granularity via *GSPE‑token*, which detaches gradients so that only one global factor modulates all token gradients.


In short: GRPE’s token‐level importance weights are a vestige of PPO rather than a principled choice for sequence‐level rewards; recognizing this mismatch led directly to GSPE’s redesign and its superior stability.
