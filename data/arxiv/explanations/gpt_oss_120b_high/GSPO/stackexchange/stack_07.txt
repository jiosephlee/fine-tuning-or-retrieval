\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does GSPO avoid the need for Routing Replay in Mixture‑of‑Experts models?
Question:
Section 6·2 explains that in GRPO, expert activation volatility makes token‑level ratios unstable, prompting the authors to cache routing decisions (Routing Replay). GSPO instead computes only sequence‑level ratios $s_i(θ)$ and claims this eliminates sensitivity to routing changes. Could someone clarify why aggregating over tokens makes the method insensitive to per‑token routing volatility?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language model each token is routed through a small subset of “experts”.  
When the policy parameters change during RL updates the gating network often selects a different set of experts for the same token. Consequently the conditional probability $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t})$ may change **dramatically** even though the underlying language model has not fundamentally altered its knowledge about that word.  

GRPO computes an *individual* importance weight  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\displaystyle \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t})}
        {\displaystyle \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y {i <t}|\,x,\ y{i < t})},
$$  

and multiplies this scalar into the gradient of every token:  

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
   \;\propto\;
   \sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
          \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t}) .
$$  

If the gating for a token flips from one expert block to another, $w{i,t}$ can become very large (or very small). Because every such weight appears directly in the gradient, a few volatile tokens inject **high‑variance noise** into learning. The authors therefore cache (“Replay”) the exact expert assignments used by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ so that the numerator and denominator share exactly the same routing pattern; only then does $w{i,t}$ stay close to one and training stays stable. This replay trick costs extra memory and forces the model not to exploit its full expert capacity.  

GSPO sidesteps this whole problem by moving from *token‑level* ratios to a *sequence‑level* ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(
      \frac{\displaystyle \pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}
           {\displaystyle \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
   \Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   =\exp
     \Bigl(
        \frac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log w{i,t}(\theta)
     \Bigr) .
$$  

The key observation is that $s_i$ is simply the **geometric mean** of all token‑wise importance weights in a response. By taking a logarithm, averaging over positions, and exponentiating again we turn a product of wildly varying numbers into an average that is far less sensitive to any single outlier. In effect  

* A few tokens whose routing flips cause huge $w{i,t}$ values are diluted by many other tokens whose $w$ stays near one; their contribution to $s_i$ scales only linearly with $\log w{i,t}$, not multiplicatively.  
* The gradient under GSPO becomes  

  $$ 
  \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
      \;\propto\;
      s_i(\theta)\,
      \sum_t\nabla_\theta
               \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t}),
  $$  

  i.e. **all tokens share exactly the same scalar weight** $s_i$. The per‑token routing volatility no longer appears as an independent multiplicative factor.  
* Because we also apply length normalisation ($^{1/|y_i|}$), responses of different lengths produce importance ratios that lie in roughly the same numerical range, allowing us to use a tight clipping interval ($\approx 3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$) without worrying about occasional extremely large values.  

Intuitively, GSPO treats an entire response as one Monte‑Carlo sample from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Importance sampling tells us that correcting for off‑policy sampling requires a *single* weight equal to the ratio of target‑to‑behavior densities evaluated on that sample – exactly what $s_i$ provides. The underlying reward also lives at the sequence level (the verifier returns a single score for $(x,y)$), so aligning the unit of correction with the unit of reward removes a mismatch present in GRPO.  

Because GSPO’s objective depends only on **the overall likelihood** of a response, it does not matter whether some intermediate tokens were computed by different experts under successive policies – as long as both policies remain good language models their joint sequence probabilities will not swing wildly. Hence there is no need to freeze or replay routing decisions: we can evaluate $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ with its current routing and compute $s_i$ directly.  

In summary, aggregating over tokens turns an unstable set of per‑token importance weights into a single low‑variance scalar ($s_i$). This eliminates sensitivity to expert‑activation volatility, removes the necessity of Routing Replay, reduces memory/communication overhead, and lets MoE models use their full expert capacity while remaining stable under RL updates. The mathematical derivation in § 4––§ 5 of the paper confirms that GSPO’s gradient weights every token uniformly by this robust scalar, whereas GRPO’s gradient still carries the noisy per‑token factors that motivated Routing Replay in the first place.
