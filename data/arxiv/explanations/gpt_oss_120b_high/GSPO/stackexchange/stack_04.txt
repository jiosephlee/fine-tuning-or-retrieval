\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why are the clipping ranges for GSPO ($3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$) three orders of magnitude smaller than those for GRPO (0.2–0.27)?
Question:
Both algorithms use a clip function $\mathrm{clip}(r,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$, yet Table 1 reports vastly different $\varepsilon$ values for GSPO versus GRPO. Is this difference solely due to the magnitude of $s_i(\theta)$ versus $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, or are there other factors influencing the appropriate clipping range?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The clipping‑range for GSPO is tiny because the quantity it clips – the *sequence‑level* importance ratio `$s_i(\theta)$` – is already forced to stay very close to 1 by construction (it is a length‑normalised geometric mean of token ratios). In contrast, GRPO clips the *token‑level* ratios `$w_{i,t}(\theta)$`, which can vary over many orders of magnitude even after a modest policy change. Consequently the two algorithms see very different distributions of “raw” ratios and therefore require ε’s on different scales.  

Below we unpack why this happens, what the paper says about it, and why no other hidden tricks are at play.

---

### 1\. What is actually being clipped?

| Algorithm | Importance ratio | Definition | Typical scale |
|-----------|------------------|------------|---------------|
| **GRPO**  | `$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$` | Token‑wise likelihood ratio (Eq.​ (2)) | Can be far from 1 (sometimes > 2 or < 0.5) |
| **GSPO**  | `$s_i(\theta)=\Bigl[\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr]^{\,1/|y_i|}$` | Length‑normalised geometric mean of all token ratios (Eq.​ (4)) | Very close to 1 (≈ 0.999–1.001) |

**Why does `$s_i$` stay near 1?**  

`$s_i$` takes the product of all token‑wise ratios along a response and then raises it to the power `$1/|y_i|$`. Algebraically this is equivalent to averaging log‑ratios:

$$
\log s_i(\theta)=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

Since each log‑ratio is typically a small number (policy updates are modest), their average tends to be tiny, and exponentiating brings us back to a number clustered around 1. The authors explicitly introduce this *length normalisation* precisely “to reduce the variance and to control `s_i(θ)` within a unified numerical range’’ (Sec.​ 3).

In GRPO no such normalisation exists; a single token whose probability changes dramatically will make its `$w_{i,t}` deviate strongly from 1, and because we have one `$w$` per token, the collection `{​$w_{i,t}$​}` spans a much wider interval.

---

### 2\. How does this affect clipping?

Both algorithms use the same mathematical operation  

$$
\operatorname{clip}(r,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)
$$

but they apply it **to different random variables**:

* For **GRPO**, most `$w_{i,t}$’s` lie outside a narrow band around 1 unless ε is *large* (≈ 0.2–0.27). If we chose ε =`$10^{-4}$`, virtually every token would be clipped, killing the learning signal.
* For **GSPO**, almost every `$s_i$` already falls inside `[0.9996,\;1.0004]`. Using ε ≈ `$3\times10^{-4}$` therefore only discards genuinely “off‑policy’’ responses while leaving the vast majority untouched.

The paper’s empirical comment (“we note that the clipping ranges … typically differ in order of magnitude due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios’’) captures exactly this point: *the scale of the quantity being clipped determines the sensible ε.*

---

### 3\. Is it only about magnitude?

Magnitude is indeed the primary driver, but it is **the distributional shape induced by each definition** that makes the difference meaningful:

* **Variance:** The token‑wise ratios have high variance across positions and across responses because each token’s probability can change independently. This variance forces a larger ε if we want any non‑clipped gradients at all.
* **Correlation with rewards:** GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio aligns with the *unit* on which rewards are given—the whole response—so extreme per‑token fluctuations are unnecessary noise. This alignment further concentrates `$s_i$`’s mass near 1.
* **Stability considerations:** The paper shows analytically (§​3.​2) that GSPO’s gradient weights every token equally (`…all the tokens in a response share the same importance ratio…`), whereas GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its own `$w_{i,t}$`. Large per‑token weights amplify any outlier ratio and make training brittle unless ε is generous.

Thus while raw scale matters, it is *the way* those scales arise—through length normalisation and through matching reward granularity—that dictates how tight we can set ε.

---

### 4\. Practical hyper‑parameter tuning

The authors stress that both ε’s were *carefully tuned* for a fair comparison (“…we have carefully tuned…” Sec.​ 4). In practice you would:

1️⃣ **Inspect histograms** of `$w_{i,t}$` and `$s_i$` on a validation rollout batch.<br>
2️⃣ Choose an ε that clips only a small fraction of *truly off‑policy* samples—about 5–10% for PPO‑style algorithms.<br>
3️⃣ Verify that gradients are not vanishing or exploding after applying clipping.

Because `$s_i$’s histogram is sharply peaked around 1, step 2 yields an ε on the order of `$10^{-4}``; for `$w_{i_t}`’s broader histogram you end up around 0.2.

---

### 5\. No hidden extra factors

The paper does **not** introduce any other mechanisms that would justify different ε values:

* There are no additional scaling factors in Eqns​ (3)–(5).
* Both objectives share identical *clip* syntax.
* The only explicit architectural distinction influencing scale is precisely the definition of the importance ratio and its length normalisation.

Consequently, **the discrepancy in clipping ranges stems entirely from how each algorithm constructs its importance weight**, which determines its typical magnitude and variance, thereby dictating an appropriate ε scale.

---

### TL;DR  

- **GRPO** clips raw token‑level ratios `$w_{i,t}$`, which vary widely → needs a *large* ε (≈ 0.2).  
- **GSPO** clips length‑normalised geometric‑mean ratios `$s_i$`, tightly concentrated near 1 → allows a *tiny* ε (≈ `$3\times10^{-4}``).  
- The difference isn’t an arbitrary implementation detail but follows directly from the mathematical form of each importance weight and its variance reduction via length normalisation.  
- No other hidden factors affect ε; once you understand what is being clipped, the orders‐of‐magnitude gap makes perfect sense.​
