\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### What is the purpose of length‑normalization ($^{1/|y_i|}$) in $s_i(\theta)$ and how does it reduce variance?
Question:
Equation (5) defines $s_i(\theta)$ with an exponent $1/|y_i|$. The authors claim this prevents dramatic fluctuations when only a few tokens change likelihood and puts all responses into a "unified numerical range". Could someone explain analytically how this normalization controls variance compared to using the raw sequence likelihood ratio?
Answer:
**Answer**

In GSPO the importance weight for a sampled response $y_i$ is defined as  

$$
s_i(\theta)\;=\;
\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=
\exp!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr].
\tag{5}
$$

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ is a **length‑normalisation** that turns the product of token‑level ratios into their *geometric mean*.  
Below we unpack why this matters and how it tames the variance of the importance weight compared with the raw sequence‑level ratio

$$
w^{\text{raw}}_i(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
   = 
   \exp!\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr].
\tag{A}
$$

---

### 1. From a product to an average on the log‑scale  

Every token contributes a factor  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{%
         \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   \quad (>0).
$$

The raw importance weight (A) multiplies all these factors.  
Multiplication is highly sensitive: if just one token’s probability changes by a factor of two while all others stay the same, $w^{\text{raw}}_i$ also changes by a factor of two; if two tokens change it doubles again, etc. For a long response these multiplicative effects can explode.

Taking logs converts multiplication into addition:

$$
L_i(\theta)=
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \ell _{i,t}(\theta),
   \qquad 
   \ell _{ i , t } (\theta)=
        \log w _ { i , t } (\theta ).
$$

Now $L_i(\theta)$ is simply *the sum* of per‑token log‑ratios.  
If we divide by the length we obtain an **average**:

$$
\bar{\ell}_{ i } (\theta)=
      \frac { L _ i (\ theta ) } { | y _ i | }
      = 
      \frac { 1 } { | y _ i | }
        \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { | y _ i | } 
        \log w _ { i , t } (\ theta ).
$$

Exponentiating back gives exactly $s _ i (\ theta )=e^{\,%
      \bar {\ell}_ i (\ theta )}$, i.e. the **geometric mean** of the token‑level ratios:

$$
s _ i (\ theta )
   =
   \Bigl(\prod _ { t = 1 } ^ { | y _ i | }
        w _ { i , t } (\ theta ) 
        \Bigr) ^ { 1 / | y _ i | }
   =
   {\bigl(w ^ {\text {raw}}_ i (\ theta )\bigr)} ^ { 1 / | y _ i | } .
$$

Thus length normalisation replaces an *unstable product* by a *stable average*, which already suggests lower variability.

* Without normalisation you compute ap​​roduct​ofmany ratios – a quantity that can swing dramatically if just afew tokens change.
* By taking logs you turnthatproductintoasum; dividingbylength makesit an average.
* Exponentiating bringsyoubacktoprobability spacebutnowyouhavethe geometric meanofper‑tokenratios.
* The averaging shrinksthe variance roughly like $1/| 	y |$; longer sentences become more stableinsteadoftheirstochastic‐product counterpart.
* Consequently all responses liveinroughly thesame numeric neighbourhoodaround 1,
 which lets youuse tight clipping intervalsandprevents catastrophicupdates.
* In gradientterms every token receives equalweightandlong responsesdo not overshootbecausetheircontributionisdividedbytheirlength.
