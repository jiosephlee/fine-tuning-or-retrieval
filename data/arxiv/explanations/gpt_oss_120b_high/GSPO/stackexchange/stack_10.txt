\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does clipping a much larger fraction of tokens not harm training efficiency in GSMO compared to GRPO?
Question:
Figure 3 shows that GSPO clips roughly two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO yet achieves higher training efficiency (faster reward improvement per compute). Intuitively I would expect discarding more data reduces sample efficiency. What explains this counter‑intuitive result—does sequence‑level clipping provide a better signal despite fewer effective tokens?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In GSPO the *unit* that is clipped is an entire response (a whole trajectory), whereas in GRPO the unit is each token. Because a reward is attached to a whole response, clipping at the token level forces the algorithm to keep many tokens whose **overall** trajectory weight is far outside the trusted region. Those kept tokens contribute enormous variance to the gradient estimate – especially as the response gets longer – and the optimizer ends up “wasting” compute on highly off‑policy samples.  

GSPO simply discards those trajectories altogether (all of their tokens are counted as “clipped”), keeps only the relatively on‑policy responses, and treats every token inside an kept response with the same weight (the length‑normalised sequence importance ratio). The result is a **much lower‑variance** estimator of the policy‑gradient despite using far fewer *tokens* for back‑propagation, so reward improves faster per unit of computation.

Below is a more detailed walk‑through anchored in the paper’s equations and experiments.

---

### 1. What “fraction of clipped tokens’’ actually measures

Figure 4 of the paper reports *tokens* that are excluded from gradient computation after clipping.  
* In **GRPO** each token $t$ gets its own importance ratio  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$  

and it is clipped independently of all other tokens in the same response.  
* In **GSPO** an entire response $y_i$ gets a single importance ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr]^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
$$  

If $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$ **all** its $|y_i|$ tokens are thrown away. Consequently a single out‑of‑range response can inflate the *token*‑clipping fraction by an order of magnitude compared with GRPO, even though only one *trajectory* has been removed.

Hence “more clipped tokens’’ does **not** mean that GSPO sees less *information* – it just means it has rejected more *whole trajectories* that would have contributed very noisy gradients.

---

### 2. Why whole‑trajectory clipping gives a better learning signal

#### (a) Alignment between reward and importance weight  

The reward $r(x,y)$ is defined only at the end of a generated response.  
In *GRPO* every token shares that same advantage $\widehat A_i$ but receives an **individual** IS weight $w_{i,t}$. Because IS weights are meant to correct for distribution mismatch *across complete samples*, applying them per token violates the basic importance‑sampling identity (Section 3). The single‑sample correction at each step has very high variance and accumulates linearly with sequence length.

In *GSPO* we use exactly the IS ratio that matches the unit of reward:

$$
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}.
$$  

Thus each kept trajectory contributes an unbiased estimate of  

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\,y\sim \pi_\theta}[\, r(x,y)\,]
$$  

(up to the usual PPO clipping bias), and no extra variance arises from multiplying many per‑token ratios together.

#### (b) Variance reduction by *uniform* token weighting  

From Eq.\,(7)–(9) we see

| Gradient term | Weight applied to token log‑likelihood |
|----------------|----------------------------------------|
| GRPO | $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ – can vary wildly between $(0,\infty)$. |
| GSPO | $(s_i(\theta))^{|y_i|^{-1}}$ – **identical for every token in $y_i$**. |

Uniform weighting eliminates one source of stochasticity: all $|y_i|$ gradients inside a kept response point in essentially the same direction (scaled by $\widehat A_i/|y_i|$). The only randomness left comes from sampling different responses, which is unavoidable but far less noisy than per‑token fluctuations.

The paper’s “gradient analysis” (Section 4.2) spells this out:

> “In GRPO, the unequal weights … accumulate and lead to unpredictable consequences … In contrast, GSPO weights all the tokens … eliminating this instability factor.”

#### (c) Length normalisation keeps ratios comparable across different response lengths  

Without raising to $1/|y|$, a small change in a few token probabilities could blow up $\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)}$, forcing us either to use an enormous clipping window or discard almost everything. By normalising,
$s_i(\theta)$ stays roughly on a similar scale for short and long responses, allowing us to use tight clipping thresholds $(3\times10^{-4})/(4\times10^{-4})$ without over­penalising long replies. Consequently most *on‑policy* trajectories survive intact while truly off‑policy ones are simply pruned.

---

### 3. How this translates into higher *training efficiency*

Training efficiency in Figure 3 is measured as *reward improvement per unit of compute*, not per raw token count. Two effects combine:

1. **Cleaner gradients → faster convergence** – Because GSPO’s gradients have far lower variance, each optimisation step moves parameters in a more useful direction. Empirically this shows up as steeper reward curves even though fewer total token gradients are taken.
2. **Less wasted compute** – Tokens belonging to responses whose $s_i(\theta)$ lies outside $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$ would have contributed very little useful signal while still consuming GPU memory and FLOPs during back‑propagation if they were kept (as happens implicitly in GRPO because only some individual tokens get clipped). By dropping an entire trajectory early we skip those wasted operations altogether.

Thus “more clipped tokens” does **not** imply “less informative data”; it simply reflects that GS PO aggressively removes whole trajectories whose contribution would be dominated by noise, leaving behind a compact set of high‑quality samples that drive learning far more efficiently.

---

### 4. Broader perspective – why this principle appears elsewhere

The phenomenon mirrors classic policy‑gradient practice in reinforcement learning where one often **truncates or discards entire episodes** whose importance–sampling ratios exceed a threshold (“trajectory‐level PPO”). The rationale is identical:

*Rewards are attached to episodes → correct off‑policy bias at episode level → discard highly off‑policy episodes → obtain lower‐variance estimator.*

GS PO brings this well‑known RL wisdom into language‐model RL where an episode corresponds exactly to a generated response.

---

### 5. Takeaway

- **Token‐level clipping (GRPO)** keeps many corrupted gradient contributions, leading to high variance especially for long responses.
- **Sequence‐level clipping (GS PO)** throws away entire responses when they are too far from the current policy; what remains is uniformly weighted inside each response.
- The resulting estimator has dramatically lower variance, so even though many more *tokens* are technically “clipped”, training progresses faster per compute unit.
- This explains Figure 3’s counter‐intuitive observation and underscores why aligning the unit of importance sampling with the unit of reward—here “the whole sequence”—is essential for scalable RL on large language models.

In short, GS PO trades quantity for quality: it sacrifices many low‐value tokens (those belonging to off‐policy responses) and gains a much cleaner learning signal, which ultimately yields higher training efficiency than GR PO despite the apparently larger clipping fraction.
