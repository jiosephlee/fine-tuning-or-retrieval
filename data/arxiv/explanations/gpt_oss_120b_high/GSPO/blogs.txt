# Reinforcement Learning Meets Large Language Models: A Primer for Everyday Readers  

*Why RL is becoming essential for scaling language models, how it works at a high level, and what obstacles appear when we try to train truly gigantic models.*

---

## Why Reinforcement Learning Is the Next Big Thing for LLMs  

Large language models (LLMs) have already shown they can generate fluent text, answer questions, and even write code.  Yet many of the most impressive capabilities—solving competition‑level math problems, planning multi‑step reasoning chains, or following nuanced human preferences—still require **learning from feedback** rather than just from raw text.  

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides exactly this feedback loop:

1. **Ask** the model a query (the *environment*).  
2. **Let** the model produce a response token by token (the *action*).  
3. **Score** the whole response with a **reward model** that reflects how well it meets the desired behavior (correctness, helpfulness, safety, …).  
4. **Update** the model so that future responses earn higher rewards.

When we repeat this cycle millions of times, the model gradually shifts from merely copying patterns in its pre‑training corpus to actively optimizing for the reward we care about. Recent work on Qwen‑3 and similar systems shows that large‑scale RL can push language models from “good” to “superhuman” on specialized benchmarks.

---

## A High‑Level Sketch of How RL Trains a Language Model  

Below is a simplified version of the loop most RL‑for‑LLM pipelines follow:

| Component | What It Does | How It Appears in the Paper |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------------------|
| **Policy** ($\pi_\theta$) | The autoregressive language model parameterized by $\theta$. It tells us the probability of each next token given the past context. | “An autoregressive language model … is defined as a policy $\pi_\theta$.” |
| **Reward** ($r(x,y) \in [0,1]$) | After seeing a query $x$ and a generated response $y$, a verifier returns a scalar score $r(x,y)$. | “A query‑response pair … can be scored by a verifier $r$, resulting in a reward $r(x,y)$.” |
| **Advantage / Relative Advantage** | The reward is turned into an advantage term that tells us how much better (or worse) one response is relative to its peers in the same batch. | GRPO and GSPO define $\widehat A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\text{mean}}{\text{std}}$. |
| **Importance Ratio** | Because we update an *old* policy ($\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) with samples drawn from it while optimizing a *new* policy ($\pi_\theta$), we need a correction factor – an *importance weight* – that measures how far the new policy has moved away from the old one. | PPO uses token‑level weight $w_t(\theta)$; GSPO uses sequence‑level weight $s_i(\theta) = \bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}$. |
| **Clipping** | To keep updates from being too aggressive on out‑of‑distribution samples, RL algorithms clip the importance ratio within a narrow band around 1 (e.g., $[1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]$). | Both PPO/GRPO and GSPO have “clip” terms in their objectives. |
| **Gradient Update** | The final objective combines clipped importance ratios with advantages and backpropagates through the policy network to adjust $\theta$. | Equations (2), (5), (7) in the paper illustrate this mathematically. |

In practice you might hear two classic algorithms:

* **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** – uses per‑token importance ratios *and* a separate value network that predicts expected rewards.
* **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)** – drops the value network by using relative advantages across a *group* of sampled responses (“group size” $G$).

Both aim to stay close enough to the old policy while nudging it toward higher rewards.

---

## The Trouble With Scaling Up: What Goes Wrong on Giant Models?  

When you move from a 7B parameter backbone to 30B+, add sparsity via Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE), or generate very long answers (hundreds of tokens), several hidden pitfalls surface:

### 1️⃣ Token‑Level Importance Ratios Become Extremely Noisy  

GRPO’s importance weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$ is computed **per single token** using only one sample from each next‑token distribution. Importance sampling theory tells us such ratios are reliable only when averaged over many samples from the behavior distribution. With only one sample per token, the ratio injects high variance into gradients—variance that compounds across long sequences. Empirically this manifests as “catastrophic model collapse”: after a few updates, loss spikes dramatically and recovery becomes impossible even after checkpoint rollback or hyperparameter tweaks.

### 2️⃣ Value Networks Are Heavyweight And Hard To Scale  

PPO’s reliance on an auxiliary value model means you effectively double your memory footprint (the value head often matches the size of the policy head). For massive models this is prohibitive, especially when you want to sample many rollouts per GPU step.

### 3️⃣ MoE Routing Volatility Breaks Token‑Level Corrections  

MoE models dynamically activate only a handful of experts per token. During training those activations can shift dramatically after each gradient step—up to ≈10 % of experts change for the same rollout after one update in Qwen3‑30B‐A3B experiments. Since GRPO’s importance ratio compares *different* activated subnetworks between old and new policies ($w_{i,t}$ mixes two distinct routing paths), its magnitude fluctuates wildly and no longer correctly measures off‑policy deviation.

#### A Workaround That Still Costs Much  

The authors previously introduced **Routing Replay**, which caches which experts were used by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and forces $\pi_\theta$ to reuse them when computing $w_{i,t}$. While this stabilizes GRPO on MoE models, it adds memory overhead and limits how freely experts can adapt—a concrete example of engineering hacks required to make token‑level RL work at scale.

---

## Lessons From Recent Research: Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

The paper *Group Sequence Policy Optimization* proposes an elegant fix that directly addresses the three pain points above.

### Core Insight – Align Reward Granularity With Optimization Granularity  

The reward we care about ($r(x,y)$) evaluates an **entire response**, not individual tokens. Therefore it makes sense for our off‑policy correction also to operate at the **sequence level**, rather than sprinkling corrections across each token independently.

### Sequence‐Level Importance Ratio  

GSPO replaces $w_{i,t}$ with  
\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
     = \exp\!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                 {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
       \Bigr].
\]
All tokens inside a response share exactly the same ratio $s_i$, so gradient contributions are weighted uniformly across the sequence—a dramatic reduction in variance compared with GRPO’s per‑token weighting.

### Sequence‐Level Clipping  

GSPO clips *whole responses*:
\[
\min\!\Bigl(s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,
           \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta), 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i\Bigr).
\]
Because $s_i$ already incorporates length normalization ($^{1/|y_i|}$), clipping thresholds are tighter (e.g., $3\times10^{-4}$ vs.\ $0.2$ used by GRPO). Yet despite clipping many more tokens overall—as shown in Figure 2 of the paper—training efficiency actually improves because each retained sample carries a cleaner learning signal.

### No Need For Routing Replay  

Since $s_i$ depends only on *sequence likelihood*, it stays stable even when individual token routing changes dramatically inside an MoE layer. Empirically GSPO converges on MoE models without any extra caching tricks—a huge simplification for both memory usage and engineering effort.

### Simpler Infrastructure  

Practically speaking, you can use inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang) that already compute per-sequence log probabilities during generation as your importance ratios. There’s no longer a need to recompute token-by-token log probs with heavyweight training kernels—a win for distributed training pipelines where inference and training run on different hardware stacks.

### Real‐World Payoff  

When applied to Qwen3‐30B models:

* Training curves remained smooth throughout long runs.
* Performance continued improving as compute increased—unlike GRPO where instability often halted progress.
* Benchmarks such as AIME’24 Pass@1, LiveCodeBench Pass@1, and Codeforces Elo all saw higher scores under identical compute budgets.
* Crucially, these gains fed directly into the “remarkable improvements” observed in Qwen3’s latest releases.

---

## Takeaways For Everyday Readers  

* **Reinforcement learning lets massive language models learn from feedback**, opening doors to reasoning abilities far beyond pure next-token prediction.
* Traditional RL recipes (PPO & GRPO) hit roadblocks once you scale up—high variance from token-level corrections and instability in sparsely activated MoE networks.
* **Group Sequence Policy Optimization** solves these problems by switching from token‐wise corrections to whole‐sequence corrections—a move that respects how rewards are actually given.
* The result is *more stable training*, *better use of compute*, *simpler engineering*, and ultimately *stronger language models*.  
* If you see headlines about “RLHF” or “fine-tuning LLMs with reinforcement learning”, know that behind those buzzwords sits careful mathematics about importance sampling and clipping—and innovations like GSPO are what make those methods work at billions of parameters scale.

---

**Bottom line:** Reinforcement learning is no longer just an academic add-on; it’s fast becoming indispensable for squeezing out top-tier performance from today’s gigantic language models. New algorithmic ideas such as GSPO demonstrate that by aligning our mathematical tools with how we evaluate model behavior—at the level of entire responses—we can finally train these giants reliably and efficiently. As research continues down this path, expect even more impressive capabilities emerging from future generations of LLMs—all powered by smarter ways to teach them what we truly want them to do.



# From Tokens to Whole Sentences: Why Sequence‑Level Thinking Saves Giant LLMs from Collapse  

*An intuitive deep‑dive into why applying importance weights to whole responses—rather than individual tokens—keeps massive language models stable during reinforcement learning.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why RL for Large Language Models Is Hard  

Reinforcement learning (RL) lets us teach language models to solve math puzzles, write code, or reason over long passages by rewarding *entire* generated answers. The bigger the model (think billions of parameters) and the longer the responses, the more sensitive the training becomes. In practice we often see **catastrophic model collapse**—a sudden drop in quality that cannot be recovered even after rolling back checkpoints or tweaking hyper‑parameters.

The culprit is not just “more data = more instability.” It lies deeper in how we **correct for off‑policy samples** when we reuse old rollouts to compute gradients.

---

## 2️⃣ Token‑Level vs. Sequence‑Level Importance Sampling  

### The basic idea of importance sampling  

If we want an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ but can only draw samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, we re‑weight each sample:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
\right].
\]

Crucially, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ works **when we average over many *independent* samples** $z$. The law of large numbers smooths out the variance.

### What GRPO does  

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) carries over this idea **per token**:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

For every position $t$ in a response $y_i$, it multiplies the token’s log‑probability ratio by a *single* sampled token from the old policy. In other words, it treats each token as an independent sample from its own next‑token distribution.

#### Analogy  
Imagine you’re judging a whole novel by looking at just one word on each page and trying to infer how “good” the entire story is. One word tells you almost nothing about the overall plot; yet you still try to re‑weight your judgment based on that single word. The result? A wildly noisy estimate that can swing dramatically from page to page.

Because each token’s importance weight is based on **one draw**, there is little statistical smoothing. When responses get long (hundreds of tokens), these per‑token noises **accumulate**—the gradient becomes a sum of many shaky estimates. Adding PPO‑style clipping only magnifies the problem: if a weight lies outside $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, it gets truncated, which introduces bias while still carrying high variance.

The end result is exactly what practitioners observe: **high‑variance noise builds up**, eventually pushing the model into an unrecoverable low‑performance region—what we call *model collapse*.

---

## 3️⃣ Aligning Units: Reward ↔ Importance Sampling  

A vital observation is that **the reward lives at the sequence level**:

\[
r(x,y)\in[0,1],\quad y \text{ = entire response}.
\]

If the *unit of optimization* (the quantity we clip and multiply by advantages) does not match the *unit of reward*, the algorithm inherently misbehaves. Think of trying to improve a car’s fuel efficiency by adjusting each individual bolt’s torque while measuring fuel consumption after an entire drive—a mismatch that yields confusing gradients.

The fix is simple: *match* them. Use an importance ratio that evaluates **the whole sequence**, not individual tokens.

---

## 4️⃣ GSPO: Group Sequence Policy Optimization  

### The core objective  

GSPO replaces token weights with a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Bigg(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\Bigg).
\]

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ performs **length normalization**, ensuring that short and long responses live on comparable scales and that clipping thresholds stay meaningful across different lengths.

GSPO’s loss over a batch of $G$ responses per query $x$ looks like:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigg(
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_i
\bigg)
\right],
\]

where $\widehat A_i$ is the **group‑relative advantage**:

\[
\widehat A_i=
\frac{
r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j\bigr)
}{
\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j\bigr)
}.
\]

Key points:

* **Clipping now applies to entire responses**, so “off‑policy” samples are discarded at once.
* All tokens inside a response share the same scaling factor $s_i(\theta)$ → **equal weighting**.
* No value network is needed—advantages come directly from normalized rewards within each group.

### Gradient intuition  

When you unpack the gradient (ignoring clipping), GSPO’s update becomes:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\Bigg[
\dfrac1G \sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i \,
\dfrac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
\]

All logits inside a response are multiplied by **the same scalar** $s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i$, eliminating token-wise noise spikes. By contrast, GRPO’s gradient multiplies each token’s log‑likelihood by its own noisy weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can vary wildly across positions and cause gradient divergence.

---

## 5️⃣ Why This Matters for Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Models  

MoE models route each token through a selected subset of expert feedheads. After an update, **routing patterns may shift** dramatically—about 10 % of experts differ for the same token when training large MoEs with GRPO. Since GRPO’s importance ratios rely on *per‑token* probabilities from both old and new policies, any routing change blows up $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, making the gradient unstable.

Previous work introduced **Routing Replay**, i.e., caching old routing decisions and forcing new forward passes through identical experts just to keep $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ well behaved. This adds memory pressure and caps MoE capacity.

GSPO sidesteps routing entirely because it only needs the **overall sequence likelihood** $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ rather than per-token likelihoods after routing changes. The sequence likelihood stays relatively smooth even when internal expert assignments shuffle, so GSPO trains MoEs **without any routing tricks**—a cleaner, more memory‑efficient pipeline that fully exploits MoE capacity.

---

## 6️⃣ Practical Upsides Beyond Stability  

| Aspect | Traditional PPO / GRPO | GSPO |
|-------|------------------------|------|
| **Value model required?** | Yes (adds memory & compute) | No |
| **Routing replay needed for MoE?** | Yes (extra engineering) | No |
| **Precision sensitivity** | Token‐level likelihood recomputed on training engine | Can use inference engine scores directly (sequence level tolerant to float differences) |
| **Clipping fraction of tokens** | ~0–2 % clipped (still noisy) | ~0–2 % clipped at response level ➜ far fewer effective gradients needed but *more effective* learning |

Because GSPO only cares about whole‐response scores, you can pull likelihoods straight from an inference server (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). This cuts down on costly re-evaluation loops during partial rollouts or multi‑turn RL setups where train/infer engines differ in precision or architecture.

---

## 7️⃣ Empirical Evidence – Does It Work?  

The authors tested GSPO on a cold‑start Qwen3‑30B model fine‐tuned from its base version:

* **Training stability:** Reward curves rose smoothly throughout weeks of RL without any spikes or crashes.
* **Benchmark gains:**  
  * AIME’24 – higher Pass@1 rates after comparable compute  
  * LiveCodeBench – improved Pass@1 across eight samplings  
  * CodeForces – Elo rating climbs steadily.
* **Efficiency:** With identical compute budgets and query budgets as GRPO, GSPO achieved higher final rewards—showing better *sample utilization*.
* **Clipping behavior:** Even though GSPO clipped far more *responses* than GRPO clipped tokens (a two‐order‐of‐magnitude difference), it still learned faster—confirming that discarding noisy tokens altogether is better than keeping all but heavily weighted.
* **MoE handling:** Training dense models was stable; training MoEs succeeded without Routing Replay—a clear win for large sparse models.
  
Overall these results confirm that moving from *token-level* correction to *sequence-level* correction delivers tangible gains in both robustness and performance.

---

## 8️⃣ Takeaways – When Designing RL for Giant LLMs  

1️⃣ **Match units:** If rewards are given per response, compute importance weights per response—not per token.  
2️⃣ **Reduce variance early:** Aggregating likelihoods across an entire sequence smoothes out stochastic fluctuations inherent in next-token sampling.  
3️⃣ **Clip wisely:** Apply PPO-like clipping on whole responses; this removes outlier rollouts without contaminating gradients with noisy tokenwise ratios.  
4️⃣ **Simplify pipelines:** No value network + no routing replay = lower memory footprint + easier scaling to billions of parameters or thousands of experts.  
5️⃣ **Leverage inference engines:** Sequence-level scores tolerate floating point mismatches between training and inference runtimes—unlocking mixed training/inference architectures.

In short, “thinking at the sentence level” isn’t just linguistically nicer—it’s mathematically sound and practically essential when you push language models into the multi‑billion parameter regime. By aligning rewards with importance sampling at the right granularity, GSPO turns what used to be a precarious tightrope walk into a sturdy road toward ever larger—and far more capable—LLMs.

--- 

*If you’re gearing up for RL fine-tuning of your own massive model (dense or MoE), consider swapping out tokenwise importance ratios for sequencewise ones à la GSPO—you might just save your model from disappearing into oblivion.*



# Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO): The Algorithm Redefining RL for LLMs  

*In this post we walk through how GSVO works—from the sequence‑level importance ratio that replaces token‑wise weighting, to its novel whole‑response clipping mechanism. We’ll see why the math matters, how it fixes problems in earlier RL‑for‑LLM methods like PPO and GRPO, and what practical gains you can expect when fine‑tuning large language models.*  

---  

## 1️⃣ Why Reinforcement Learning Is Now Central to LLM Fine‑tuning  

Large language models (LLMs) have impressive language modeling abilities out of the box, but many downstream tasks—advanced math reasoning, code synthesis, multi‑turn dialogue—require **goal‑directed** behavior. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or other reward‑driven frameworks let us steer an LLM toward such objectives by treating the model as a policy \\(\pi_\theta\\) that generates a response \\(y\\) given a prompt \\(x\\).  

When scaling up to *hundreds of billions* of parameters—and especially when using sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures—the **stability** of the RL loop becomes the bottleneck. Small algorithmic quirks can cause catastrophic “model collapse,” after which training cannot recover even if you roll back checkpoints or retune hyperparameters.

---  

## 2️⃣ A Quick Recap: From PPO to GRPO  

### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)  
PPO is the classic workhorse for policy optimization under off‑policy data. For language generation it uses a **token‑level** importance weight  

\[
w_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

and clips it inside \\([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\\) before multiplying by an estimated advantage \\(\widehat A_t\\). The PPO objective is  

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,y}\!\left[\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
\min\bigl(w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\,
\mathrm{clip}(w_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_t\bigr)
\right].
\]

PPE works well when you have an accurate **value model** that predicts \\(\widehat A_t\\). However, large LLMs already push memory limits, and training a separate value network roughly doubles compute cost.



### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)  
GRPO removes the value model by estimating advantage **relative** to other responses sampled from the same prompt:

\[
\widehat A_i=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_j r(x,y_j)}{\operatorname{std}_j r(x,y_j)} .
\]

All tokens in response \\(y_i\\) share this scalar \\(\widehat A_i\\). The objective looks just like PPO’s but summed over a *group* \\(G\\) of responses:

\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^G 
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\min(w_{i,t}(\theta)\widehat A_i,\,
\mathrm{clip}(w_{i,t}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i)
\right],
\]

where \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) is the same token‑wise ratio defined above.



---  

## 3️⃣ What Goes Wrong With Token‑Level Importance Ratios?  

The **principle of importance sampling** tells us that correcting an off‑policy sample requires reweighting **the entire sample**:

\[
\mathbb E_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] 
=
\mathbb E_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}
   \!\Bigl[\, \frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\, f(z)\Bigr].
\]

In language generation each *sample* is an entire token sequence \\(y\\).  
GRPO applies the correction **per token**, i.e., it uses  

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
\]

which only corrects *one* draw from the next‑token distribution at time step \\(t\\). Because there is just one draw per distribution, the weight does not average out variance—it injects noisy scaling factors that grow linearly with sequence length. When responses become long (hundreds of tokens) or when expert routing changes in MoE models, those noisy factors explode and cause **catastrophic collapse** despite clipping.



> **Bottom line:** *Reward is assigned at the sequence level*, yet GRPO tries to fix off‑policy bias at the token level—an ill‑posed mismatch.



---  

## 4️⃣ Designing GSPO – Sequence ↔ Reward Alignment  

GSPO fixes the mismatch by moving every ingredient—importance weight, clipping, advantage—to the **sequence level**.



### 4️⃣️1 Sequence‑level Importance Ratio  

For a whole response \\(y\\),

\[
s(\theta)=
   \Bigl(
      \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}
   \Bigr)^{1/|y|}
=
   \exp\!\Bigl(
      \frac{1}{|y|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
      \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}
                {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})}
   \Bigr).
\]

The exponent \(1/|y|\) *length normalizes* the ratio so that responses of different lengths sit in roughly the same numerical range—otherwise long strings would produce astronomically large ratios.



### 4️⃣️2 Group Advantage (unchanged)  

We keep GRPO’s simple relative advantage estimator:

\[
\widehat A_i=
   \frac{
          r(x,y_i)- 
          \operatorname{mean}_{j} r(x,y_j)
        }
        {
          \operatorname{std}_{j} r(x,y_j)
        } .
\]

All responses in a group share this scalar advantage.



### 4️⃣️3 GSPO Objective With Whole–Response Clipping  

Putting them together gives

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
   \mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}
   \Bigg[
      \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
      \min
      \Bigl(
         s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\,
         \mathrm{clip}\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,
                             1+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\widehat A_i
      \Bigr)
   \Bigg]
}
$$

where \(s_i(\theta)\) is defined above for each response \(y_i.\)

> **Key difference:** clipping now decides whether an *entire* response is “too far” from the old policy before it contributes any gradient signal.



---  

## 5️⃣ Intuition From The Gradient Viewpoint  

If we drop clipping temporarily for clarity,

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
   \mathbb E[\;
      s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i\, 
      \nabla_\theta\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
          \!\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
   ] .
\]

Compared with GRGO’s gradient

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
=
   \mathbb E[\;
      \widehat A_i\, 
      \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t}
      w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
      \nabla_\theta\fbox{$\,\,$
          $\log$ $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|...)$}$ 
   ] ,
\]

we see two crucial changes:

* **Uniform weighting:** every token inside a response receives exactly the same scalar factor \(s_i(\theta)/|y|\).  
* **No per‑token importance noise:** the noisy term \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\)—the source of exploding variance—is gone.



Thus gradients become *much smoother*, especially when responses are long or experts switch frequently in MoE models.



---  

## 6️⃣ When You Need Token Granularity – GSPO‑token  

Sometimes you want advantages that vary across positions (e.g., giving extra credit for correct reasoning steps). GSPO supplies a simple extension:

```math
s_{i,t}(\theta)= 
    {\rm sg}[\,s_i(\theta)\,]\;
   	\times 
   	\tfrac{
           		\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
           }{
           		{\rm sg}[\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})]
           } ,
```

where `${\rm sg}` denotes “stop gradient”. Numerically `s_{i,t}` equals `s_i`, so when all tokens share the same advantage `GSPO-token` behaves identically to `GS PO`. When you supply per‑token advantages `$\widehat A_{i,t}$`, you keep the benefits of equal weighting while still being able to reward or penalize specific steps.



---  

## 7️⃣ What Does This Mean In Practice?  

### ✅ Stabilized Training Across Massive Models  
Empirical runs on a Qwen3‐30B base model show **smooth reward curves** throughout thousands of updates—no sudden drops or irrecoverable collapse as observed with GR PO.


### ✅ Faster Sample Efficiency  
With identical compute budgets and query budgets, GS  PO reaches higher validation scores on challenging benchmarks (AIME’24 math problems, LiveCodeBench code generation, Codeforces competitive programming). The authors report “continuous performance improvement” as more compute is poured in.


### ✅ No More Routing Replay For MoE  
MoE models activate different experts per token after each update. In GR PO this volatility makes `w_{i,t}` wildly unstable; developers previously introduced **Routing Replay**—caching old routing decisions and reusing them during gradient computation—to keep `w` well behaved.  
GS  PO’s reliance on *sequence likelihood alone* means it is indifferent to which expert handled each token; only the overall probability matters → **Routing Replay becomes unnecessary**, saving memory bandwidth and letting all experts contribute fully.


### ✅ Simpler RL Pipelines  
Because `$s_i$` needs only a single likelihood value per response, you can take this number straight from your inference server (`vLLM`, `SGLang`, …). No extra forward passes through a training‐engine version of the model are required—a big win when doing partial rollouts or multi-turn dialogues where latency matters.

In short: **GS  PO aligns every piece of mathematics—reward, importance weight, clipping—with the natural unit “the whole response”.** That alignment eliminates high‑variance noise sources inherent in tokenwise schemes and makes large‑scale reinforcement learning for LLMs both *practical* and *profitable*.

# Stabilizing Mixture‑of‑Experts Models Without Routing Replay: GSPO’s Secret Sauce  

*How a simple shift from token‑level to sequence‑level importance weighting eliminates the expert‑activation volatility that has plagued RL training of large sparse models.*

---

## 1. The stability problem when scaling RL for language models  

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become the go‑to method for teaching massive language models new skills—mathematical reasoning, code generation, dialog planning, … But scaling RL to billions‑parameter models brings a non‑negotiable requirement: **training dynamics must stay stable** even when we push compute limits or generate very long responses.

The classic Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm solves part of this problem by clipping *token‑level* importance ratios \(w_t(\theta)\). Unfortunately PPO relies on an auxiliary value model whose size matches the policy network, dramatically increasing memory consumption and being hard to scale reliably to longer sequences.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was introduced as a “value‑free” alternative. It estimates an advantage for each response in a *group* of rollouts per query and still uses token‑level importance ratios:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]
On dense models this works reasonably well, yet as model size grows—and especially when we introduce **Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE)** sparsity—GRPO begins to stumble dramatically.


---

## 2. Why MoE architectures are extra fragile  

MoE models contain many *experts*—independent feed‑forward sub‑networks that are activated sparsely per token based on a learned router. This sparsity yields impressive capacity–efficiency tradeoffs, but it also creates a new source of variance:

* **Expert‑activation volatility** – After each gradient update the set of experts that fire for a given token can shift noticeably. In experiments with a 48‑layer Qwen3‑30B‑A3B model we observed that roughly 10 % of activated experts changed between \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) and \(\pi_{\theta}\).

Because GRPO’s importance weight is computed **per token**, any sudden change in routed experts produces wild swings in \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Those swings amplify the clipping mechanism and inject high variance into the gradient estimate. The end result is frequent “model collapse” where performance degrades irreversibly—even after reverting to earlier checkpoints or tinkering with hyper‑parameters.


---

## 3. Routing Replay – a band‑aid fix  

To tame this volatility researchers invented **Routing Replay**:

1. When a batch of rollouts is generated under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), they store the exact routing decisions (which experts were chosen for each token).  
2. During the subsequent GRPO update they *force* the new policy \(\pi_\theta\) to use **the same expert masks** when evaluating \(\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})\).  

By sharing the routing mask between old and new policies, the token‑level ratios \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) become comparable again, restoring stability for MoE training.

While effective, Routing Replay carries notable downsides:

* **Memory overhead** – Caching per‑token routing information explodes with batch size and sequence length.  
* **Communication cost** – In distributed setups these cached masks must be shuttled across workers.  
* **Capacity bottleneck** – By forcing one fixed route per sample we prevent the model from exploiting its full dynamic routing ability during learning.


Thus we have a chicken–egg situation: without Routing Replay GRPO collapses on MoE; with it we pay substantial engineering cost.


---

## 4. The secret sauce of GSPO – sequence‑level importance weighting  

Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) proposes a radical simplification: **measure off‑policy discrepancy at the level of entire responses rather than individual tokens**. The key definition is the *sequence‐level* importance ratio
\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\Bigl(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\Bigr).
\]
A few design choices merit attention:

* **Geometric mean normalisation** (`^{1/|y_i|}`) keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) bounded across sequences of different lengths, dramatically reducing variance compared with raw likelihood ratios.
* The **advantage** remains group based:
  \[
  \widehat A_i=
  \frac{
    r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl\{r(x,y_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^{G}
  }{
    \operatorname{std}\!\bigl\{r(x,y_j)\bigr\}_{j=1}^{G}
  } .
  \]
* Clipping operates on \(s_i(\theta)\) *once per response*. In practice clipping bounds like \(3\times10^{-4}\)–\(4\times10^{-4}\) are enough—two orders of magnitude tighter than GRPO’s token‐level bounds (≈0.2–0.27).

The resulting objective mirrors PPO’s spirit but replaces every per‐token term by its response‐wise counterpart:
\[
\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\E_{
   x,\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G
 } 
   \Bigl[
      \tfrac1G
      \sum_{i=1}^G
      \min 
      \bigl(
        s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i,\;
        \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_i
      \bigr)
   \Bigr].
\]

### Gradient intuition  

When we expand the gradient (ignoring clipping for clarity), GSPO yields
\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\E[\cdots]
~ 
s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i~
\times~
\tfrac1{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta
\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]
All tokens inside a response are weighted **identically** by \(s_i(\theta)\). Contrast this with GRPO’s gradient,
which multiplies each token’s log‐gradient by its own raw ratio \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\). Those unequal weights are precisely what explode when expert routes change from step to step.



---

## 5. Why sequence likelihood neutralizes expert volatility  

Even though MoE routing can differ wildly between policies at the token level, **the product over all tokens—the full sequence likelihood—remains comparatively smooth**:

* A single token that switches experts may cause its local probability term to change substantially, but that term contributes only multiplicatively within an already exponentially small product.
* By taking the **geometric mean**, \(s_i(\theta)\) essentially averages out those fluctuations across the whole response.
* As long as the underlying language model retains its basic predictive ability after each update—a condition satisfied by standard fine‐tuning—the aggregate likelihood does not swing erratically even when individual experts do.

Consequently GSPO does **not** require us to freeze or replay any routing decisions. The algorithm automatically respects whatever experts fire during evaluation while still providing a reliable importance weight for off‐policy correction.


---

## 6. Empirical proof points  

* **Training curves:** On a cold‐start Qwen3‑30B model GSPO shows smooth monotonic improvement throughout thousands of updates; GRPO frequently spikes into collapse unless Routing Replay is applied.
* **Benchmark gains:** After comparable compute budgets GSPO attains higher Pass@1 scores on AIME’24 and LiveCodeBench, and achieves better Elo ratings on CodeForces than GRPO.
* **Clipping efficiency:** Despite clipping *much larger fractions of tokens* (up to two orders of magnitude more than GRPO), GS PO still extracts more learning signal per sample—evidence that noisy token‐wise gradients are less useful than clean sequence‐wise ones.
* **MoE without Routing Replay:** Using GS PO we successfully trained the latest Qwen3 Mixture‑of‑Experts models without any extra memory or communication tricks—simplifying both codebase and hardware utilization.


---

## 7. Bonus side effect – lighter RL infrastructure  

Because GS O relies only on whole‑sequence log probabilities, it tolerates modest precision mismatches between training kernels (e.g., Megatron FP16) and inference engines (e.g., vLLM). Practically this means we can reuse probabilities returned directly from an inference service instead of recomputing them with a heavyweight training engine—a noticeable win for multi‑turn RL pipelines or setups where rollout generation and policy updates live on separate clusters.


---

## 8. Takeaways  

| Aspect | GRPO | GS O |
|---|---|---|
| Importance weight | Token‐level \(w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}}\) | Sequence‐level \(s_i = (\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{old}}})^{1/|y|}\) |
| Stability on MoE | Requires Routing Replay → extra memory & limited capacity | No extra mechanism needed |
| Gradient variance | High (unequal token weights accumulate noise) | Low (uniform token weighting inside each response) |
| Clip fraction | Tiny proportion (~0.x %) | Larger proportion (~10–20 %), yet more efficient |
| Infrastructure burden | Must recompute old logits per token | Can consume inference logits directly |

In short, GS O’s “secret sauce” is *matching the granularity of off‑policy correction to that of the reward*. By aligning the objective with whole responses rather than individual next-token predictions, it sidesteps the volatile expert activation patterns inherent to MoE models while simultaneously simplifying RL pipelines and boosting sample efficiency.

If you’re planning large‑scale RL fine‐tuning of sparsely activated language models—whether you’re building next‐generation code assistants or math solvers—adopting GS O may let you skip the cumbersome Routing Replay trick altogether and focus on scaling compute rather than wrestling with engineering workarounds.

# Clipping More Tokens, Yet Learning Faster: Counter‑Intuitive Efficiency Gains with GSPO

## Why do we clip at all?

When we train language models with reinforcement learning (RL), the data we use for each update comes from an **old** policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ while we are improving a **new** policy $\pi_\theta$.  
If a sample is too far from the new policy, its contribution can destabilize the gradient estimate.  
Both Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \citep{ppo} and its value‑free cousin Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) tackle this by **clipping** the importance weights:

\[
\text{clip}\bigl(w,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\bigr),
\]

where $w$ measures how likely a token (or response) is under the new policy relative to the old one.  
The idea is simple – keep the optimization within a “proximal” region where our off‑policy correction remains trustworthy.

## Token‑level vs. sequence‑level clipping

* **GRPO** works at the **token level**.  
  For each generated token $y_{i,t}$ it computes a weight  

  \[
  w_{i,t}(\theta)
  =\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
         {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
  \]

  then clips $w_{i,t}$ individually before forming the loss.

* **GSPO** (Group Sequence Policy Optimization) flips this around and clips **entire responses** instead:

  \[
  s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}
                       {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
            =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
                \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
  \]

  The quantity $s_i(\theta)$ is just the *geometric mean* of all token‑level ratios in a response,
  and it is what gets clipped:

  \[
  \text{clip}\bigl(s_i,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\bigr).
  \]

Because rewards $r(x,y)$ are assigned **to whole sequences**, aligning the importance weight with that granularity makes theoretical sense – we are now correcting exactly what we evaluate.

## “More” clipped tokens, “less” noise

A striking empirical fact reported in the GSPO paper is that **the fraction of clipped tokens under GSPO is two orders of magnitude larger** than under GRPO — even though both algorithms use a clipping step!  
At first glance this seems paradoxical: if we discard more data, why does learning speed up?

### Imagine a fruit basket

Think of each token as a fruit in a basket:

* In GRPO we examine every fruit individually. Some bananas look oddly green (high importance weight) and get tossed out by clipping, but many still stay—yet every retained fruit carries its own idiosyncratic blemish that subtly skews the taste (gradient estimate).

* In GSPO we first blend *all* fruits from a single basket into a smooth juice (the geometric mean). If the overall taste deviates too much from what we expect, **the whole jug gets discarded**. We end up either keeping an entire well‑balanced batch or rejecting it completely.

The result? The **juice we keep** comes from baskets whose overall composition closely matches our target distribution, so it provides a *cleaner* learning signal despite fewer individual tokens being used.

### From equations to variance

The gradient of GRPO’s objective contains a term

\[
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
w_{i,t}(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\,
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}),
\]

where each token’s weight $w_{i,t}$ varies independently between roughly $(0,\!1+\varepsilon]$ or $[\,1-\varepsilon,\!\infty)$.  
Because these weights come from *single* samples of next‑token distributions,
they exhibit **high variance**, especially for long responses where many such noisy factors multiply together.^[The paper shows this accumulation leads to catastrophic model collapse.]

GSPO’s gradient replaces those per‑token weights with a single sequence‑level factor:

\[
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i\,\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}).
\]

Since $s_i$ is an *average* over all tokens (via length normalization), its fluctuations are dramatically smaller.  
Even if many tokens would have been clipped individually under GRPO,
the *average* stays close to 1 and only extreme outliers trigger clipping at the response level — precisely what you want when you care about whole‑sequence rewards.

## Empirical proof ‑‑ more clipping → higher efficiency

In the experiments:

* **Clipping ranges** differ markedly:
  * GRPO used $\varepsilon_{\text{left}}=0.2$, $\varepsilon_{\text{right}}=0.27$,
  * GSPO used $\varepsilon_{\text{left}}=3\times10^{-4}$, $\varepsilon_{\text{right}}=4\times10^{-4}$.
* With these settings GSPO **clips many more tokens**, yet:
  * Training reward curves remain smooth without any sign of collapse,
  * Benchmark scores on AIME’24, LiveCodeBench and CodeForces improve faster than GRPO given identical compute budgets,
  * The algorithm scales cleanly to Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models without needing auxiliary tricks such as *Routing Replay* (a memory‑heavy hack required by GRPO).

Figure 2 in the paper (described verbally here) shows GSVO’s reward rising steadily while GRPO’s curve flattens or spikes before collapsing—a clear illustration that discarding “noisy” responses early pays off later.

## The special case of Mixture‑of‑Experts models

MoE layers activate different expert sub‑networks per token. After just one gradient step,
about **10 %** of expert assignments change even for the same rollout sample—​a phenomenon dubbed ***expert‑activation volatility***.  

Because GRPO’s token weights hinge on those volatile per‑token probabilities,
its importance ratios become erratic and break stability unless you employ ***Routing Replay***,
which forces both old and new policies to use identical expert routes during calculation.  
Routing Replay adds memory overhead and caps effective model capacity.

GSPO sidesteps this entirely:
its importance ratio only needs the **overall sequence likelihood**
$\pi_\theta(y_i|x)$,
which remains reasonably stable despite individual expert switches.
Consequently MoE models trained with GSPO enjoy stable convergence *without* routing tricks—a major practical win.

## Infrastructure simplifications

In production RL pipelines there’s often a mismatch between:
* Training engines (e.g., Megatron) that compute logits at FP16/FP32 precision,
* Inference engines (e.g., SGLang or vLLM) serving sampled responses at lower precision or using kernel optimizations.

GRPO demands recomputing every token’s old‐policy likelihood on the training engine—a costly roundtrip.
GSPO’s reliance on **sequence‐level likelihoods** makes it tolerant of minor precision differences.
Practically this means you can feed the likelihoods returned directly by your inference backend into the optimizer, cutting down latency and simplifying “training‐inference disaggregated” setups such as partial rollouts or multi‑turn RL dialogs.

## TL;DR – Takeaways

* **Clipping more does not mean losing learning power** when you clip at the right granularity.
* Sequence‐level importance ratios ($s_i$) average out token‐wise noise, yielding low‑variance gradients even under aggressive clipping thresholds.
* Aligning the unit of importance sampling (*sequence*) with the unit of reward (*whole response*) eliminates a fundamental source of instability observed in token‐wise methods like GRPO.
* Empirically, GSPO achieves higher sample efficiency — faster reward growth and better benchmark scores — despite discarding a larger fraction of tokens.
* For MoE models and large‐scale RL pipelines:
  * No need for Routing Replay or other ad hoc stabilizers,
  * Simpler infrastructure thanks to tolerance for inference‐engine likelihoods,
  * Full model capacity can be utilized without artificial constraints.

In short, **GSPO shows that throwing away more “bad” data—by clipping whole responses—actually gives us cleaner gradients**, letting massive language models learn faster and more reliably. The counter‑intuitive lesson here is clear: sometimes *less* usable data leads to *more* effective learning when you match your correction mechanism to what you’re really optimizing.<br><br>---

# Simplifying RL Infrastructure: Using Inference Engine Likelihoods Directly with GSPO

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has become the workhorse behind today’s most capable language models. Yet, when we move from research prototypes to production‑scale pipelines, the *engineering* side often becomes the bottleneck. A recurring pain point is the need to **re‑compute old‑policy likelihoods** on the training hardware before each policy update—especially in token‑level algorithms such as PPO or GRPO.  

The recent **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** algorithm changes that picture dramatically. Because it operates on **sequence‑level importance ratios**, GSPO tolerates the inevitable precision mismatch between inference‑time engines (e.g., vLLM or SGLang) and training‑time engines (e.g., Megatron). This opens the door to a much leaner RL pipeline that can use the likelihoods produced by the inference engine *as‑is*.  

In this post we walk through why old‑policy recomputation is costly, how GSPO’s sequence‑level formulation sidesteps it, and what concrete engineering gains you can expect when you adopt GSPO in your own RLHF stack.

---

## 1️⃣ Why Do Traditional RL Algorithms Need Old‑Policy Likelihoods?

Most classic policy‑gradient methods—PPO, GRPO—work with **token‑wise importance weights**:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big)}.
\]

During a policy update we must evaluate both \(\pi_{\theta}\) *and* \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) at every token \(t\). The usual workflow looks like this:

1. **Rollout generation** – a cheap inference cluster samples several responses \(\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G\) using \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. **Log‑probability recomputation** – before we can compute \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\), we feed the same token sequences back through the *training* model (often in FP32 or higher precision) to obtain \(\log\!\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})\).
3. **Policy update** – compute the clipped objective and back‑propagate.

Step 2 is expensive for three reasons:

* **Extra forward passes** every iteration effectively double the compute budget.
* **Memory & bandwidth pressure** – we must store or stream full token logits for each rollout.
* **Precision mismatches** – training engines typically run at higher numerical precision than inference engines, so even tiny differences in logits can explode when multiplied across thousands of tokens, leading to noisy importance weights.

These challenges become especially acute when scaling up:
- With Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) models we have to also keep track of expert routing decisions.
- For long generations (code or math solutions) the token count grows into the tens of thousands.
- Distributed training systems often separate generation workers from gradient workers, adding network I/O overhead for re‑computations.

---

## 2️⃣ Sequence-Level Importance Ratios: The Core Idea Behind GSPO

GSPO replaces token‑wise ratios with a **single importance ratio per generated response**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=
\left(
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{\!\frac{1}{|y_i|}}
=
\exp\!\Bigg(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log
\frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big)}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\!\big(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\big)}
\Bigg).
\]

A few points are worth emphasizing:

* **Alignment with rewards** – Rewards \(r(x,y)\) are assigned **to whole sequences**, so correcting at the sequence level respects the unit of optimization.
* **Length normalization** (\(^{1/|y_i|}\)) keeps \(s_i(\theta)\) in a comparable range across responses of different lengths, making clipping thresholds much easier to set.
* The entire *group* objective becomes

  \[
  \mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
  \mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}
  \Bigg[
    \frac{1}{G}
    \sum_{i=1}^{G}
    \min\!\Big(
      s_i(\theta)\widehat{A}_i,\,
      \operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\!1+\varepsilon)\widehat{A}_i
    \Big)
  \Bigg],
  \]
  
  where \(\widehat{A}_i\) is the normalized advantage computed from group rewards—exactly as in GRPO but without a value model.

Because only **one scalar** per response is needed (`logprob_sum / length`), we no longer have to keep per-token logits around once generation finishes.

---

## 3️⃣ Tolerating Precision Differences Between Engines

Inference deployments often trade raw precision for throughput:
- FP16/BF16 kernels on GPUs,
- TensorRT or custom kernels on CPUs,
- Quantized kernels (INT8) for massive serving clusters.

When you recompute old‐policy logits on a separate training engine you introduce two sources of mismatch:
1. **Rounding error accumulation** – each token’s probability may differ by a few ULPs; multiplied across thousands of tokens this yields large discrepancies in the product.
2. **Different softmax implementations** – subtle differences in numerical stability tricks lead to distinct per-token scores even if underlying weights are identical.

In practice these mismatches make \(w_{i,t}(\theta)\) noisy enough to destabilize training—a phenomenon described in Section 3 of the GSPO paper (“high variance noise” that “precipitates model collapse”).

By contrast, GSPO’s \(s_i(\theta)\) aggregates *log‐probabilities* across tokens before any division occurs:

\[
\log s_i(\theta)=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\bigl(
  \log\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
 - 
  \log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}|x,y_{i,<t})
\bigr).
\]

A small per-token error translates into an additive constant inside a sum rather than a multiplicative factor. The averaging over thousands of tokens *smoothes out* those discrepancies. Empirically (see Figure 4 in the paper), clipping fractions are two orders of magnitude lower for GSPO than GRPO while still achieving higher sample efficiency—evidence that sequence‐level ratios are far less sensitive to precision drift.

---

## 4️⃣ How to Plug In Inference Engine Likelihoods Directly

### 4.1 What Needs to Be Logged?

When generating rollouts on your inference cluster you already obtain:
- The sampled text \(y_i\),
- The per‑token log probabilities from the model’s output distribution (`logits → log_softmax`).

All you need to persist is **the sum of those log probabilities**, i.e.:

```python
logp_sum = torch.sum(log_probs)   # log_probs[t] = log πθold(y_t | x, y_<t)
seq_len   = y_i.size(0)
```

Store `logp_sum` and `seq_len` alongside each `(x, y)` pair in your dataset (or write them into a cheap KV store). No additional forward pass is required after generation completes.

### 4.2 Computing \(s_i(\theta)\) During Training

During a gradient step you have access to:
- The current policy \(\pi_\theta\), which you evaluate *once* on each response `y` to obtain `new_logp_sum`.
- The stored `old_logp_sum`.

Then:

```python
# Length normalization
old_avg_logp = old_logp_sum / seq_len
new_avg_logp = new_logp_sum / seq_len

s_i = torch.exp(new_avg_logp - old_avg_logp)
```

Feed this scalar `s_i` into the clipped objective together with the group advantage `̂A_i`. That’s it—no per-token loops, no extra memory copies.

### 4.3 Optional Token-Level Variant

If you need finer granularity—for example multi‑turn dialogues where intermediate rewards matter—GSPO also offers a “GSPO‑token” variant that keeps per-token advantages while still using the same sequence‐level importance factor `s_i`. Implementation wise it means attaching `̂A_{i,t}` computed from turn‑specific rewards but still reusing `s_i` as shown in Equation (9) of the paper. This preserves all infrastructure benefits while enabling richer reward shaping.

---

## 5️⃣ Tangible Engineering Benefits

| Aspect | Traditional Token‑Level RL (PPO/GRPO) | GSVO Sequence‑Level RL |
|--------|---------------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Forward passes per iteration** | Two (old + new policy per token) | One (new policy only) |
| **Memory footprint** | Store logits for every token × rollout | Store one scalar (`logp_sum`) per rollout |
| **Network traffic** | Transfer full token logits if generation & training are decoupled | Transfer only aggregated scalar + length |
| **Precision sensitivity** | High – small FP16 errors amplify over long sequences | Low – errors averaged out |
| **MoE routing handling** | Requires *Routing Replay* or extra bookkeeping | No extra routing logic needed |
| **Latency in large clusters** | Dominated by recompute step | Generation → immediate ingestion → update |

### 5.1 Cost Savings

Running an extra forward pass on a GPU cluster can easily increase compute cost by ~30–40 % for typical batch sizes used in RLHF (~32 k tokens per GPU). Eliminating this step translates directly into lower GPU hours and lower electricity bills—critical when scaling up to hundreds of billions of parameters.

### 5.2 Simplified System Architecture

With GSPO we can truly decouple **generation workers** from **training workers**:
- Generation workers run optimized inference stacks (vLLM/SGLang), possibly on cheaper hardware or even CPU clusters.
- They export `(query, response, logp_sum, length)` records.
- Training workers consume those records without ever touching an inference engine again.
  
No cross‑process “replay” buffers are needed; no synchronized checkpoints of routing states for MoE layers are required; no special care about whether GPUs run FP32 or BF16 during recomputation—the data already exists where it was produced.

### 5.3 Multi-Turn & Partial Rollout Scenarios

Partial rollouts (e.g., generating only a prefix before receiving an external signal) become easier because you already have intermediate `logp_sum`s recorded at each turn. When you later decide how many additional tokens to generate under a new policy you simply re-use those values without re-running previous steps under high precision—something that would otherwise necessitate an additional “replay” phase.

## 6️⃣ Real‑World Validation: Qwen3 Experiments

The authors benchmarked GSPO on Qwen3-30B models—a Mixture-of‑Experts architecture—and observed:

- Stable training curves without any “Routing Replay” tricks that were mandatory for GRPO.
- Higher final reward scores despite clipping up to two orders of magnitude more tokens—a testament that sequence-level clipping does not sacrifice sample efficiency.
- Ability to continuously improve performance by extending generation lengths and feeding more queries—exactly what modern RLHF pipelines aim for.

These results confirm that the theoretical robustness of sequence-level importance weights materializes into tangible engineering robustness at scale.

## 7️⃣ Getting Started with GSPO

If you’re already running a PPO/GRPO pipeline, transitioning involves modest changes:

1. **Modify rollout logger:** Capture cumulative log probability (`logp_sum`) and length while generating responses.
2. **Replace old weight computation:** Drop per-token ratios `w_{i,t}` and implement Eq (5)/(6)’s `s_i(θ)` using stored sums.
3. **Tweak clipping ranges:** Because `s_i` lives near 1 after length normalization, typical clipping intervals are `[1−3e−4,\;1+4e−4]` rather than `[0.73,\;1.27]` used by GRPO.
4. **(Optional)** adopt `GSPO-token` if you need token-wise advantages but still want sequence-level importance scaling.
5. **Test on a small subset:** Verify that loss curves match expectations before scaling out to massive MoE clusters.

The code changes are minimal compared with rewriting major parts of your RL infrastructure—yet they unlock dramatic savings in compute and simplify your deployment topology substantially.

## 8️⃣ Takeaway

GSPO rewrites one core equation in RLHF—from a noisy product over thousands of tokens to an elegant average over sequence likelihoods—and that rewrite ripples through every layer of your system:

- Fewer forward passes → lower compute cost,
- Less data movement → faster pipelines,
- Tolerance to FP16/BF16 quirks → robust mixed‑precision deployments,
- No special MoE routing tricks → cleaner codebase,
- And ultimately more stable learning dynamics at scale.

If your team is grappling with “old policy recompute” bottlenecks or trying to squeeze extra performance out of massive MoE models, give sequence-level likelihoods a try. By letting your inference engine speak directly to your optimizer through simple scalars instead of massive tensors, you’ll discover a leaner path toward ever more capable language models—just as Qwen3 did with GSPO. 

Happy scaling!
