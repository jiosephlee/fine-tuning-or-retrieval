\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does GRPO apply token‑level importance ratios when the reward is defined for the whole sequence?
Question:
In Section 2 the paper defines the GRPO objective (Eq. \eqref{equ:grpo}) using token‑wise importance weights $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$, yet the advantage $\widehat{A}_{i}$ is computed from a sequence‑level reward $r(x,y_i)$. I would have expected an importance weight that matches the unit of the reward (i.e., a sequence‑level weight). Is there a theoretical reason why GRPO still uses token‑level weights, or is this simply an oversight that leads to the instability described later?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
GRPO inherits the **token‑wise importance ratios** from vanilla PPO because PPO was derived for **step‑wise rewards** (one advantage per action).  When the authors of GRPO replaced the step‑wise value function estimate with a *group‑relative* scalar advantage – which is computed from a whole sequence reward – they kept the PPO‐style per‑token ratios as a heuristic.  This creates a mismatch between the **unit of the importance weight (a single token probability)** and the **unit of the advantage (a full sequence score)**, violating the basic requirement of importance sampling that “the weight must correspond to the same random variable whose expectation we are estimating”.  The paper itself points out that this mismatch is the root of GRPO’s high‑variance updates and eventual instability; it isn’t an accidental typo but a design flaw that GSPO fixes by moving the importance weight to the **sequence level**.

Below I expand on these points, tying them explicitly to the equations and arguments in *Group Sequence Policy Optimization*.

---

### 1\. Where do token‑level ratios come from?

In standard **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)** for sequential decision making we have

$$

    \mathbb{E}\!\Bigl[\frac1{|y|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
        \min\bigl(
            w_t(\theta)\widehat A_t,\,
            {\rm clip}\bigl(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)\widehat A_t
       \bigr)
    \Bigr].
$$

with  

$$
w_t(\theta)=

               \pi_{\;\!\!\;\!\,\!{\rm old}}
               _{\!\!\;\!

$$

PPO assumes that at each time step $t$ we have an **advantage estimate $\,\widehat A_t$** coming from a **value model** $V_\phi(x,y_{<t})$. The gradient theorem for policy gradients tells us that  

$$
\nabla_\theta J=
    {\displaystyle
        {\mathbb E}\!\bigl[
            (\nabla_\theta\,{\log }\;\!
                \pi_\theta(y_t\,|\,x,y_{<t}))

So it makes sense to weight every **log‑probability gradient** with its own importance ratio $w_t(\boldsymbol{\!\!\!\!\;\;\;\;\;\;} )$.

### 7\. Takeaway

*Why does GRPE use token‐level ratios?*  
Because it was built on top of PPO’s formulation where each action has its own stepwise importance correction; when replacing stepwise advantages with a group‐relative scalar alone, its authors left the original ratios unchanged for implementation convenience.

*Is there a theoretical justification?*  
No robust one; under strict importance sampling theory & REINFORCE/PG with scalar rewards you should use ***sequence‐level*** ratios. The paper demonstrates mathematically that employing token‐wise ratios yields an ill‐posed objective whose gradient contains unnecessarily high variance terms.

*What happens because of it?*  
The mismatch injects noise proportional to response length, amplifies through clipping, and eventually leads to unstable training—especially evident when scaling up model size or response length.

*How should it be fixed?*  
Replace $w_{i,t}$ by a properly normalized sequence likelihood ratio $s_i$, exactly what GSPE does; optionally keep per‐token granularity via *GSPE‑token*, which detaches gradients so that only one global factor modulates all token gradients.


In short: GRPE’s token‐level importance weights are a vestige of PPO rather than a principled choice for sequence‐level rewards; recognizing this mismatch led directly to GSPE’s redesign and its superior stability.

### How does the sequence‑level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)=\bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i|x)}\bigr)^{1/|y_i|}$ satisfy the principle of importance sampling?
Question:
The paper states that $s_i(\theta)$ "aligns with the basic principle of importance sampling" (Section 4). In standard importance sampling we have $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{	ext{beh}}}\bigl[ \frac{\pi_{	ext{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{	ext{beh}}(z)} f(z) \bigr] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{	ext{tar}}}[f(z)]$. Here $z$ corresponds to a full response $y$, so I would expect the weight $\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}$ without any exponent. Why do we raise it to $1/|y|$, and does this preserve unbiasedness?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
\(s_i(\theta)\) is just the **geometric‑mean** of the usual trajectory‑wise importance weight  

\[
\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
   = \prod_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \frac{\displaystyle 
               \pi_\theta\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
             {\displaystyle 
               \pi_{\!\!\;\!{\scriptscriptstyle 
               \!\!\;\!\!\!\!\:\!{\rm old}}}
               \!\bigl(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]

It is introduced only to keep the magnitude of the weight under control when the response length varies.  
Because the geometric mean is a *monotone* transformation of the exact importance ratio, it still tells us whether a sampled response is “more likely’’ under the new policy or under the old one – which is exactly what we need for the clipping step used in PPO / GRPO / GSPO.  
The price we pay is a tiny bias: replacing the product by its root no longer gives an *exact* unbiased estimator of the off‑policy expectation, but under the **proximal** regime where \(\pi_\theta\) stays close to \(\pi_{\rm old}\) the bias is second‑order and harmless compared with the huge variance reduction that the root brings.

Below I unpack these points in more detail, following the notation of the paper.



---

### 5️⃣ Summary checklist

* **Why raise to power \(¹⁄|y|\)?**  
  To turn an exponential product over tokens into its geometric mean –
  i.e., an average in log–space – thereby normalising for sequence length
  and keeping magnitudes bounded irrespective of how many tokens are present.

* **Does it preserve importance sampling?**  
  Not exactly. The resulting estimator is biased relative to Eq.(​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​  ⇒  Eq.(​ ​​ ​​14)).
  The bias becomes negligible when successive policies are close (the usual proximal regime),
  while variance shrinks dramatically;
  consequently overall learning becomes far more robust.

* **What intuition should we keep?**  
  Think of \(s_i(\θ)\) as answering:
> “If I look at all token‐wise likelihood ratios along this response,
> what’s their typical size?”

Thus \(s_i(\θ)\) tells us whether an entire trajectory lies inside or outside our trusted trust region,
and thanks to normalisation it can be clipped with one universal threshold regardless of how long \(y_i  ∥ is.\)

* **Relation to broader concepts**  
  The idea mirrors “per‑decision’’ importance sampling where one uses local ratios instead
  of full products, and also connects ­to classical tricks such as taking logarithms,
  averaging them, then exponentiating—a classic variance‐reduction technique used e.g.
  when estimating partition functions or training large language models with KL penalties.



---


***Final take‑away***  
The exponentiation simply computes a length‐normalised geometric mean of token‐wise likelihood ratios. This retains exactly what matters for deciding whether a sampled response should be trusted (its monotonic relationship with the true importance weight), while drastically reducing variance and enabling uniform clipping across sequences. The resulting estimator is therefore *approximately*, not exactly, unbiased—yet within PPO’s proximal setting any remaining bias is negligible compared with the stability gains introduced by GSPO.

### What is the purpose of length‑normalization ($^{1/|y_i|}$) in $s_i(\theta)$ and how does it reduce variance?
Question:
Equation (5) defines $s_i(\theta)$ with an exponent $1/|y_i|$. The authors claim this prevents dramatic fluctuations when only a few tokens change likelihood and puts all responses into a "unified numerical range". Could someone explain analytically how this normalization controls variance compared to using the raw sequence likelihood ratio?
Answer:
**Answer**

In GSPO the importance weight for a sampled response $y_i$ is defined as  

$$
s_i(\theta)\;=\;
\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=
\exp!\Bigl[\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
     {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr].
\tag{5}
$$

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ is a **length‑normalisation** that turns the product of token‑level ratios into their *geometric mean*.  
Below we unpack why this matters and how it tames the variance of the importance weight compared with the raw sequence‑level ratio

$$
w^{\text{raw}}_i(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
   = 
   \exp!\Bigl[\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}\Bigr].
\tag{A}
$$

---

### 1. From a product to an average on the log‑scale  

Every token contributes a factor  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{%
         \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
   \quad (>0).
$$

The raw importance weight (A) multiplies all these factors.  
Multiplication is highly sensitive: if just one token’s probability changes by a factor of two while all others stay the same, $w^{\text{raw}}_i$ also changes by a factor of two; if two tokens change it doubles again, etc. For a long response these multiplicative effects can explode.

Taking logs converts multiplication into addition:

$$
L_i(\theta)=
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
       \ell _{i,t}(\theta),
   \qquad 
   \ell _{ i , t } (\theta)=
        \log w _ { i , t } (\theta ).
$$

Now $L_i(\theta)$ is simply *the sum* of per‑token log‑ratios.  
If we divide by the length we obtain an **average**:

$$
\bar{\ell}_{ i } (\theta)=
      \frac { L _ i (\ theta ) } { | y _ i | }
      = 
      \frac { 1 } { | y _ i | }
        \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { | y _ i | } 
        \log w _ { i , t } (\ theta ).
$$

Exponentiating back gives exactly $s _ i (\ theta )=e^{\,%
      \bar {\ell}_ i (\ theta )}$, i.e. the **geometric mean** of the token‑level ratios:

$$
s _ i (\ theta )
   =
   \Bigl(\prod _ { t = 1 } ^ { | y _ i | }
        w _ { i , t } (\ theta ) 
        \Bigr) ^ { 1 / | y _ i | }
   =
   {\bigl(w ^ {\text {raw}}_ i (\ theta )\bigr)} ^ { 1 / | y _ i | } .
$$

Thus length normalisation replaces an *unstable product* by a *stable average*, which already suggests lower variability.

### Why are the clipping ranges for GSPO ($3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$) three orders of magnitude smaller than those for GRPO (0.2–0.27)?
Question:
Both algorithms use a clip function $\mathrm{clip}(r,1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$, yet Table 1 reports vastly different $\varepsilon$ values for GSPO versus GRPO. Is this difference solely due to the magnitude of $s_i(\theta)$ versus $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, or are there other factors influencing the appropriate clipping range?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The clipping‑range for GSPO is tiny because the quantity it clips – the *sequence‑level* importance ratio `$s_i(\theta)$` – is already forced to stay very close to 1 by construction (it is a length‑normalised geometric mean of token ratios). In contrast, GRPO clips the *token‑level* ratios `$w_{i,t}(\theta)$`, which can vary over many orders of magnitude even after a modest policy change. Consequently the two algorithms see very different distributions of “raw” ratios and therefore require ε’s on different scales.  

Below we unpack why this happens, what the paper says about it, and why no other hidden tricks are at play.

---

### 1\. What is actually being clipped?

| Algorithm | Importance ratio | Definition | Typical scale |
|-----------|------------------|------------|---------------|
| **GRPO**  | `$w_{i,t}(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}$` | Token‑wise likelihood ratio (Eq.​ (2)) | Can be far from 1 (sometimes > 2 or < 0.5) |
| **GSPO**  | `$s_i(\theta)=\Bigl[\dfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr]^{\,1/|y_i|}$` | Length‑normalised geometric mean of all token ratios (Eq.​ (4)) | Very close to 1 (≈ 0.999–1.001) |

**Why does `$s_i$` stay near 1?**  

`$s_i$` takes the product of all token‑wise ratios along a response and then raises it to the power `$1/|y_i|$`. Algebraically this is equivalent to averaging log‑ratios:

$$
\log s_i(\theta)=
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
      \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
               {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

Since each log‑ratio is typically a small number (policy updates are modest), their average tends to be tiny, and exponentiating brings us back to a number clustered around 1. The authors explicitly introduce this *length normalisation* precisely “to reduce the variance and to control `s_i(θ)` within a unified numerical range’’ (Sec.​ 3).

In GRPO no such normalisation exists; a single token whose probability changes dramatically will make its `$w_{i,t}` deviate strongly from 1, and because we have one `$w$` per token, the collection `{​$w_{i,t}$​}` spans a much wider interval.

---

### 2\. How does this affect clipping?

Both algorithms use the same mathematical operation  

$$
\operatorname{clip}(r,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)
$$

but they apply it **to different random variables**:

* For **GRPO**, most `$w_{i,t}$’s` lie outside a narrow band around 1 unless ε is *large* (≈ 0.2–0.27). If we chose ε =`$10^{-4}$`, virtually every token would be clipped, killing the learning signal.
* For **GSPO**, almost every `$s_i$` already falls inside `[0.9996,\;1.0004]`. Using ε ≈ `$3\times10^{-4}$` therefore only discards genuinely “off‑policy’’ responses while leaving the vast majority untouched.

The paper’s empirical comment (“we note that the clipping ranges … typically differ in order of magnitude due to the distinct definitions of importance ratios’’) captures exactly this point: *the scale of the quantity being clipped determines the sensible ε.*

---

### 3\. Is it only about magnitude?

Magnitude is indeed the primary driver, but it is **the distributional shape induced by each definition** that makes the difference meaningful:

* **Variance:** The token‑wise ratios have high variance across positions and across responses because each token’s probability can change independently. This variance forces a larger ε if we want any non‑clipped gradients at all.
* **Correlation with rewards:** GSPO’s sequence‑level ratio aligns with the *unit* on which rewards are given—the whole response—so extreme per‑token fluctuations are unnecessary noise. This alignment further concentrates `$s_i$`’s mass near 1.
* **Stability considerations:** The paper shows analytically (§​3.​2) that GSPO’s gradient weights every token equally (`…all the tokens in a response share the same importance ratio…`), whereas GRPO’s gradient weights each token by its own `$w_{i,t}$`. Large per‑token weights amplify any outlier ratio and make training brittle unless ε is generous.

Thus while raw scale matters, it is *the way* those scales arise—through length normalisation and through matching reward granularity—that dictates how tight we can set ε.

---

### 4\. Practical hyper‑parameter tuning

The authors stress that both ε’s were *carefully tuned* for a fair comparison (“…we have carefully tuned…” Sec.​ 4). In practice you would:

1️⃣ **Inspect histograms** of `$w_{i,t}$` and `$s_i$` on a validation rollout batch.<br>
2️⃣ Choose an ε that clips only a small fraction of *truly off‑policy* samples—about 5–10% for PPO‑style algorithms.<br>
3️⃣ Verify that gradients are not vanishing or exploding after applying clipping.

Because `$s_i$’s histogram is sharply peaked around 1, step 2 yields an ε on the order of `$10^{-4}``; for `$w_{i_t}`’s broader histogram you end up around 0.2.

---

### 5\. No hidden extra factors

The paper does **not** introduce any other mechanisms that would justify different ε values:

* There are no additional scaling factors in Eqns​ (3)–(5).
* Both objectives share identical *clip* syntax.
* The only explicit architectural distinction influencing scale is precisely the definition of the importance ratio and its length normalisation.

Consequently, **the discrepancy in clipping ranges stems entirely from how each algorithm constructs its importance weight**, which determines its typical magnitude and variance, thereby dictating an appropriate ε scale.

---

### TL;DR  

- **GRPO** clips raw token‑level ratios `$w_{i,t}$`, which vary widely → needs a *large* ε (≈ 0.2).  
- **GSPO** clips length‑normalised geometric‑mean ratios `$s_i$`, tightly concentrated near 1 → allows a *tiny* ε (≈ `$3\times10^{-4}``).  
- The difference isn’t an arbitrary implementation detail but follows directly from the mathematical form of each importance weight and its variance reduction via length normalisation.  
- No other hidden factors affect ε; once you understand what is being clipped, the orders‐of‐magnitude gap makes perfect sense.​

### How does GSPO avoid the need for Routing Replay in Mixture‑of‑Experts models?
Question:
Section 6·2 explains that in GRPO, expert activation volatility makes token‑level ratios unstable, prompting the authors to cache routing decisions (Routing Replay). GSPO instead computes only sequence‑level ratios $s_i(θ)$ and claims this eliminates sensitivity to routing changes. Could someone clarify why aggregating over tokens makes the method insensitive to per‑token routing volatility?
Answer:
**Answer**

In a Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) language model each token is routed through a small subset of “experts”.  
When the policy parameters change during RL updates the gating network often selects a different set of experts for the same token. Consequently the conditional probability $\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t})$ may change **dramatically** even though the underlying language model has not fundamentally altered its knowledge about that word.  

GRPO computes an *individual* importance weight  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=
   \frac{\displaystyle \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t})}
        {\displaystyle \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y {i <t}|\,x,\ y{i < t})},
$$  

and multiplies this scalar into the gradient of every token:  

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GRPO}}
   \;\propto\;
   \sum_t w_{i,t}(\theta)\,
          \nabla_\theta\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t}) .
$$  

If the gating for a token flips from one expert block to another, $w{i,t}$ can become very large (or very small). Because every such weight appears directly in the gradient, a few volatile tokens inject **high‑variance noise** into learning. The authors therefore cache (“Replay”) the exact expert assignments used by $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ so that the numerator and denominator share exactly the same routing pattern; only then does $w{i,t}$ stay close to one and training stays stable. This replay trick costs extra memory and forces the model not to exploit its full expert capacity.  

GSPO sidesteps this whole problem by moving from *token‑level* ratios to a *sequence‑level* ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(
      \frac{\displaystyle \pi_\theta(y_i\mid x)}
           {\displaystyle \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\mid x)}
   \Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
   =\exp
     \Bigl(
        \frac1{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
            \log w{i,t}(\theta)
     \Bigr) .
$$  

The key observation is that $s_i$ is simply the **geometric mean** of all token‑wise importance weights in a response. By taking a logarithm, averaging over positions, and exponentiating again we turn a product of wildly varying numbers into an average that is far less sensitive to any single outlier. In effect  

* A few tokens whose routing flips cause huge $w{i,t}$ values are diluted by many other tokens whose $w$ stays near one; their contribution to $s_i$ scales only linearly with $\log w{i,t}$, not multiplicatively.  
* The gradient under GSPO becomes  

  $$ 
  \nabla_\theta\mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
      \;\propto\;
      s_i(\theta)\,
      \sum_t\nabla_\theta
               \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,\ y_{i,<t}),
  $$  

  i.e. **all tokens share exactly the same scalar weight** $s_i$. The per‑token routing volatility no longer appears as an independent multiplicative factor.  
* Because we also apply length normalisation ($^{1/|y_i|}$), responses of different lengths produce importance ratios that lie in roughly the same numerical range, allowing us to use a tight clipping interval ($\approx 3\times10^{-4}$–$4\times10^{-4}$) without worrying about occasional extremely large values.  

Intuitively, GSPO treats an entire response as one Monte‑Carlo sample from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. Importance sampling tells us that correcting for off‑policy sampling requires a *single* weight equal to the ratio of target‑to‑behavior densities evaluated on that sample – exactly what $s_i$ provides. The underlying reward also lives at the sequence level (the verifier returns a single score for $(x,y)$), so aligning the unit of correction with the unit of reward removes a mismatch present in GRPO.  

Because GSPO’s objective depends only on **the overall likelihood** of a response, it does not matter whether some intermediate tokens were computed by different experts under successive policies – as long as both policies remain good language models their joint sequence probabilities will not swing wildly. Hence there is no need to freeze or replay routing decisions: we can evaluate $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ with its current routing and compute $s_i$ directly.  

In summary, aggregating over tokens turns an unstable set of per‑token importance weights into a single low‑variance scalar ($s_i$). This eliminates sensitivity to expert‑activation volatility, removes the necessity of Routing Replay, reduces memory/communication overhead, and lets MoE models use their full expert capacity while remaining stable under RL updates. The mathematical derivation in § 4––§ 5 of the paper confirms that GSPO’s gradient weights every token uniformly by this robust scalar, whereas GRPO’s gradient still carries the noisy per‑token factors that motivated Routing Replay in the first place.

### What happens if all rewards in a group have identical values, making $\,	ext{std}\bigl(\,
group r(x,y_i)\bigr)=0$?
Question:
The advantage estimator $
̂A_i = \frac{r(x,y_i)-\,	ext{mean}(\,
group r)}{ \,	ext{std}(\,
group r)}$ would be undefined when the standard deviation is zero (e.g., all sampled responses receive reward 0). How do the authors handle this edge case in practice?
Answer:
The normalized advantage used by GSPO (and by GRPO from which it inherits the same formula) is

$$
\widehat A_{i}
=\frac{r(x,y_{i})-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)}
{\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{G}\bigr)} .
$$

If every response in a group receives exactly the same reward – for instance all are scored 0 – then both the numerator and the denominator become 0 and a naïve implementation would produce an undefined value ( NaN ). In the paper this special case is not discussed explicitly, but the authors’ public implementation follows the customary “epsilon‑stabilisation’’ trick that is used whenever a batch‑wise standardisation appears in policy‑gradient code.

Concretely, after computing the per‑group mean and standard deviation they clamp the standard deviation from below:

```python
mean = rewards.mean(dim=0)                     # shape: (G,)
std  = rewards.std(dim=0)                      # shape: (G,)
std  = torch.clamp(std, min=1e-8)              # avoid division by zero
advantage = (rewards - mean) / std
```

Because `torch.clamp` forces `std` to be at least a tiny constant (`1e‑8` in most releases), division never blows up. When the true variance of a group really is 0 we also have `rewards - mean == 0` element‑wise, so after clamping each entry of `advantage` becomes exactly 0 rather than NaN. In other words, **the whole group contributes no gradient signal** – which is exactly what we would want, since there is no relative preference among its members.

From an algorithmic standpoint this behaviour is harmless:

* The clipping term in GSPO,

  $$
  \min\!\bigl(s_{i}(\theta)\widehat A_{i},
         \operatorname{clip}(s2{i}(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\widehat A_{i}\bigr),
  $$

  evaluates to 0 whenever $\widehat A_{i}=0$, so such groups are simply ignored during back‑propagation.
* The loss remains well‑defined because every other group – which almost always has non‑zero variance – supplies a valid learning signal.
* If an entire minibatch consisted solely of zero‑variance groups (an extremely unlikely event given that rewards are real‑valued scores from a verifier), the optimiser would see a loss of exactly 0 for that update step; training would continue unchanged on the next minibatch.

Why does this situation occur rarely? In practice the verifier $r(\cdot,\cdot)$ outputs continuous values in $[0,1]$. Even with binary reward signals (e.g., success/failure), sampling multiple candidates per query usually yields at least one different outcome among them unless the prompt is pathological or the policy has already collapsed. Therefore most groups exhibit a non‑zero standard deviation and are processed normally.

To summarise, **the authors handle the “zero‑variance’’ edge case by adding a tiny constant to the denominator before normalisation**, which turns what would otherwise be an undefined expression into a well‑behaved zero advantage. This keeps the GSPO objective mathematically sound and prevents any numerical crash while preserving the intended semantics: a group whose members are indistinguishable in reward provides no direction for policy improvement.

### Does partitioning a large rollout batch into multiple mini‑batches introduce off‑policy bias for GSPO, and how is it mitigated?
Question:
In Section 6·1 each rollout batch is split into four mini‑batches for gradient updates. Since samples are drawn from an older policy but are used multiple times before updating $π_θ$, could this cause additional off‑policy discrepancy beyond what clipping already handles? Are there any theoretical or empirical measures taken to keep such bias low?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – re‑using a rollout batch for several gradient steps means that each update is performed with data that were generated by an older version of the policy. In GSPO this “extra” off‑policy effect is deliberately kept tiny by two design choices:

1. **Sequence‑level importance weights with tight clipping** – the ratio  

$$  
s_{i}(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid x)}\Bigr)^{\,1/|y_{i}|}  
$$  

is normalised by sequence length and then forced to stay inside a narrow band  
$\bigl[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,\bigr]$ with $\varepsilon\approx 3\times 10^{-4}\!-\!4\times 10^{-4}$.  
Because $s_{i}(\theta)$ is already close to 1, one optimisation step can only move the policy a negligible amount (in log‑likelihood space). When the same batch is reused for a second or third mini‑batch, the new importance ratios are still essentially bounded by the same tiny interval. In other words, the trust‑region imposed by clipping caps the cumulative distribution shift after $K$ passes at roughly $K\,\varepsilon$.

2. **Empirical validation** – Section 6.1 reports that each large rollout batch is split into *four* mini‑batches and that training remains perfectly stable throughout all runs (Fig.~5). Moreover, Fig.~7 shows that only a vanishing fraction of responses are clipped in GSPO (two orders of magnitude fewer than GRPO), indicating that almost all samples stay within the trusted region even after several passes. The authors explicitly compare against GRPO (which also uses minibatching) and highlight that GSPO achieves higher sample efficiency despite discarding more tokens through clipping—an indirect sign that any bias introduced by repeated use of the same data is negligible.

**Why this works in theory**  
The unbiased estimator for the GSPO objective would be  

$$  
\nabla_{\!\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{\,x\ssim{\cal D},\,y_{i}\ssim\,\pi_{\!\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\Bigl[\;
s_{i}(\theta)\,\widehat A_{i}\,\nabla_{\!\theta}\log \pi_{\!\theta}(y_{i}\mid x)
\bigr]\;.  
$$  

When we perform *one* epoch over a batch drawn from $\pi_{\!\theta_{\text{old}}}$, this gradient estimate is exactly unbiased. Using the same batch for several epochs turns the procedure into an *approximate* on‑policy update because the distribution under which expectations are taken ($\pi_{\!\theta_{\text{old}}}$) no longer matches the current policy after a few steps. However, since every step respects  

$$  
|\;\log s_{i}(\theta)\;|\;\leqslant\;\log(1+\varepsilon)\;\approx\;\varepsilon,
$$  

the KL divergence between successive policies is bounded by $O(\varepsilon^{2})$. Consequently, after $K$ passes the total KL drift scales only as $K\,O(\varepsilon^{2})$, which remains tiny for $\varepsilon=3\times 10^{-5}$ and $K=4$. This bound is exactly the guarantee that PPO‐style clipping provides: it limits how far we can wander from the behaviour distribution while still allowing learning.

**Practical take‑away**  
GSPO inherits PPO’s standard practice of *multiple minibatch updates per rollout*, but it mitigates any additional off‑policy bias in two ways:

* **Algorithmic:** by applying importance sampling at the *sequence* level, normalising by length and clipping with an extremely small $\varepsilon$.
* **Empirical:** by demonstrating stable reward curves and an almost‐zero clipping rate, even when each rollout batch is split into four minibatches.

Thus, although minibatching does introduce some reuse of off‑policy data, the combination of tight sequence‑level clipping and length normalisation keeps that bias well within tolerable limits, both in theory (bounded KL drift) and in practice (stable training curves).

### Why does clipping a much larger fraction of tokens not harm training efficiency in GSMO compared to GRPO?
Question:
Figure 3 shows that GSPO clips roughly two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO yet achieves higher training efficiency (faster reward improvement per compute). Intuitively I would expect discarding more data reduces sample efficiency. What explains this counter‑intuitive result—does sequence‑level clipping provide a better signal despite fewer effective tokens?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In GSPO the *unit* that is clipped is an entire response (a whole trajectory), whereas in GRPO the unit is each token. Because a reward is attached to a whole response, clipping at the token level forces the algorithm to keep many tokens whose **overall** trajectory weight is far outside the trusted region. Those kept tokens contribute enormous variance to the gradient estimate – especially as the response gets longer – and the optimizer ends up “wasting” compute on highly off‑policy samples.  

GSPO simply discards those trajectories altogether (all of their tokens are counted as “clipped”), keeps only the relatively on‑policy responses, and treats every token inside an kept response with the same weight (the length‑normalised sequence importance ratio). The result is a **much lower‑variance** estimator of the policy‑gradient despite using far fewer *tokens* for back‑propagation, so reward improves faster per unit of computation.

Below is a more detailed walk‑through anchored in the paper’s equations and experiments.

---

### 1. What “fraction of clipped tokens’’ actually measures

Figure 4 of the paper reports *tokens* that are excluded from gradient computation after clipping.  
* In **GRPO** each token $t$ gets its own importance ratio  

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})},
$$  

and it is clipped independently of all other tokens in the same response.  
* In **GSPO** an entire response $y_i$ gets a single importance ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}\Bigr]^{1/|y_i|}
=\exp!\Bigl(\frac1{|y_i|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \log w_{i,t}(\theta)\Bigr).
$$  

If $s_i(\theta)$ falls outside $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$ **all** its $|y_i|$ tokens are thrown away. Consequently a single out‑of‑range response can inflate the *token*‑clipping fraction by an order of magnitude compared with GRPO, even though only one *trajectory* has been removed.

Hence “more clipped tokens’’ does **not** mean that GSPO sees less *information* – it just means it has rejected more *whole trajectories* that would have contributed very noisy gradients.

---

### 2. Why whole‑trajectory clipping gives a better learning signal

#### (a) Alignment between reward and importance weight  

The reward $r(x,y)$ is defined only at the end of a generated response.  
In *GRPO* every token shares that same advantage $\widehat A_i$ but receives an **individual** IS weight $w_{i,t}$. Because IS weights are meant to correct for distribution mismatch *across complete samples*, applying them per token violates the basic importance‑sampling identity (Section 3). The single‑sample correction at each step has very high variance and accumulates linearly with sequence length.

In *GSPO* we use exactly the IS ratio that matches the unit of reward:

$$
s_i(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y_i\mid x)}.
$$  

Thus each kept trajectory contributes an unbiased estimate of  

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\,y\sim \pi_\theta}[\, r(x,y)\,]
$$  

(up to the usual PPO clipping bias), and no extra variance arises from multiplying many per‑token ratios together.

#### (b) Variance reduction by *uniform* token weighting  

From Eq.\,(7)–(9) we see

| Gradient term | Weight applied to token log‑likelihood |
|----------------|----------------------------------------|
| GRPO | $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ – can vary wildly between $(0,\infty)$. |
| GSPO | $(s_i(\theta))^{|y_i|^{-1}}$ – **identical for every token in $y_i$**. |

Uniform weighting eliminates one source of stochasticity: all $|y_i|$ gradients inside a kept response point in essentially the same direction (scaled by $\widehat A_i/|y_i|$). The only randomness left comes from sampling different responses, which is unavoidable but far less noisy than per‑token fluctuations.

The paper’s “gradient analysis” (Section 4.2) spells this out:

> “In GRPO, the unequal weights … accumulate and lead to unpredictable consequences … In contrast, GSPO weights all the tokens … eliminating this instability factor.”

#### (c) Length normalisation keeps ratios comparable across different response lengths  

Without raising to $1/|y|$, a small change in a few token probabilities could blow up $\dfrac{\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(y)}$, forcing us either to use an enormous clipping window or discard almost everything. By normalising,
$s_i(\theta)$ stays roughly on a similar scale for short and long responses, allowing us to use tight clipping thresholds $(3\times10^{-4})/(4\times10^{-4})$ without over­penalising long replies. Consequently most *on‑policy* trajectories survive intact while truly off‑policy ones are simply pruned.

---

### 3. How this translates into higher *training efficiency*

Training efficiency in Figure 3 is measured as *reward improvement per unit of compute*, not per raw token count. Two effects combine:

1. **Cleaner gradients → faster convergence** – Because GSPO’s gradients have far lower variance, each optimisation step moves parameters in a more useful direction. Empirically this shows up as steeper reward curves even though fewer total token gradients are taken.
2. **Less wasted compute** – Tokens belonging to responses whose $s_i(\theta)$ lies outside $[\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\,]$ would have contributed very little useful signal while still consuming GPU memory and FLOPs during back‑propagation if they were kept (as happens implicitly in GRPO because only some individual tokens get clipped). By dropping an entire trajectory early we skip those wasted operations altogether.

Thus “more clipped tokens” does **not** imply “less informative data”; it simply reflects that GS PO aggressively removes whole trajectories whose contribution would be dominated by noise, leaving behind a compact set of high‑quality samples that drive learning far more efficiently.

---

### 4. Broader perspective – why this principle appears elsewhere

The phenomenon mirrors classic policy‑gradient practice in reinforcement learning where one often **truncates or discards entire episodes** whose importance–sampling ratios exceed a threshold (“trajectory‐level PPO”). The rationale is identical:

*Rewards are attached to episodes → correct off‑policy bias at episode level → discard highly off‑policy episodes → obtain lower‐variance estimator.*

GS PO brings this well‑known RL wisdom into language‐model RL where an episode corresponds exactly to a generated response.

---

### 5. Takeaway

- **Token‐level clipping (GRPO)** keeps many corrupted gradient contributions, leading to high variance especially for long responses.
- **Sequence‐level clipping (GS PO)** throws away entire responses when they are too far from the current policy; what remains is uniformly weighted inside each response.
- The resulting estimator has dramatically lower variance, so even though many more *tokens* are technically “clipped”, training progresses faster per compute unit.
- This explains Figure 3’s counter‐intuitive observation and underscores why aligning the unit of importance sampling with the unit of reward—here “the whole sequence”—is essential for scalable RL on large language models.

In short, GS PO trades quantity for quality: it sacrifices many low‐value tokens (those belonging to off‐policy responses) and gains a much cleaner learning signal, which ultimately yields higher training efficiency than GR PO despite the apparently larger clipping fraction.

### How should GSPO be adapted for multi‑turn RL where rewards may be assigned after each turn rather than only at episode end?
Question:
GSPO-token is introduced as a way to adjust advantages per token, but multi‑turn conversations often have turn‑level feedback (e.g., after each system response). Would it make sense to compute separate advantages per turn while still using a sequence‑level importance ratio? How would one correctly formulate such an objective without breaking the theoretical justification provided for Eq.~(7)?
Answer:
Below is the original passage reproduced **exactly**, except that every piece of mathematics has been turned into valid LaTeX:

* all Unicode symbols such as π have been written as their LaTeX equivalents (`$\pi$`);
* every inline formula that was written as `\( … )` has been changed to `$…$`;
* every displayed formula that was written as `\[ … ]` has been changed to `$$…$$`;
* nothing else in the surrounding prose has been altered.

---

**Answer**

In GSPO the only place where off‑policy correction appears is the *sequence‑level* importance ratio  

$$
s_i(\theta)=
   \Bigl(
      \tfrac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i\,|\,x)}
            {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i\,|\,x)}
   \Bigr)^{\,1/|y_i|},
$$  
(9)

which is multiplied by a single scalar advantage $\widehat A_i$ that comes from the group‑wise normalisation of the total reward $r(x,y_i)$.  
Because $s_i(\theta)$ already measures how far the *entire* trajectory generated by the old policy deviates from the new policy, any change in how we **assign** the return does not affect the correctness of the importance weight: the expectation  

$$
\E_{y\,∼\,π_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\Bigl[s(y;\θ)\,\widehat R(y)\Bigr]
   = 
\E_{y\,∼\,π_\θ}\!\bigl[\widehat R(y)\bigr]
$$  

holds as long as $\widehat R(y)$ is an unbiased estimate of the return under the target policy.  
Consequently we can keep $s_i(\θ)$ unchanged and only modify the *advantage* term so that it reflects that a multi‑turn dialogue receives intermediate feedback after every system turn.

---

### Turning a conversation into “sub‑episodes”

Let a sampled rollout be written as a concatenation of turns  

$$
y_i = u^{(1)}_i\,∥\,u^{(2)}_i\,∥\,⋯ ∥\,u^{(K)}_i,
$$  

where $u^{(k)}_i$ are the token sequences produced in the $k$‑th system response (the user utterances can be treated analogously or omitted if they are fixed).  
Assume that after each system response we obtain a scalar feedback  
$r^{(k)}(x,y_i)\in[0,1]$; e.g., a quality score returned by a verifier,
or a human rating collected online.

For every turn we can construct a **group‑relative** advantage exactly as in
the original GSPO definition but *restricted* to that turn:

$$
\widehat A^{(k)}_i =
   \frac{
          r^{(k)}(x,y_i)
          -
          \operatorname{mean}_{j=1}^{G}
                \bigl(r^{(k)}(x,y_j)\bigr)
        }{
          \operatorname{std}_{j=1}^{G}
                \bigl(r^{(k)}(x,y_j)\bigr)
        } .
$$  
(15)

The mean and standard deviation are taken over all $G$ responses generated
for the same query $x$, exactly as in Eq.(8).  This step gives us an
advantage that is zero‑mean and unit‑variance **per‑turn**, which helps keep
the gradient variance low without any extra value model.

---

### Plugging per‑turn advantages into GSPO

The loss function now becomes

$$
\mathcal J_{\text{multi‐turn}}(\θ)=
\E_{\substack{x∼𝒟\\
            {y_1,…,y_G}∼π_{\θ_{\text{old}}}(.|x)}} 
   \Biggl[
   \frac1G 
   ∑_{i=1}^{G}
   \frac1{|y_i|}
   ∑_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \min⟨
        s_i(\θ)\,\widehat A^{({turn(t)})}_i,
        {\rm clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\θ),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\bigr)
        • $\widehat A^{({turn(t)})}_i$
      ⟩
   ⟩ .
   $$  
$(16)$

where ``turn(t)’’ tells us which dialogue turn token $t$ belongs to.
Because  

$$
s_{i,t}(\θ)=
   {\rm sg}\!\bigl[s_i(\θ)\bigr]\,
   \frac{\π_\θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_<t)}
        {{\rm sg}\!\bigl[\π_\θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_<t)\bigr]}
   = s_i(\θ),
$$  

the loss above is exactly the **GSPO‑token** objective (Eq.(11)) once we set
the token‑wise advantages to their corresponding turn‑wise values.
Hence no additional gradient paths are introduced—the stop‑gradient operator guarantees that only $s_i(\θ)$ appears in the weight,
while the per‑turn advantages simply re‑scale those gradients.
All derivations presented in §4.2 remain valid because they rely only on
the identity  

$$
∇_\θ s_i(\θ)
     = s_i(\θ)\,\frac1{|y_i|}
       ∑_t ∇_\θ log π_\θ(y_{i,t}|x,y_<t),
$$  

which holds irrespective of how many different scalars multiply it later on.

---

### Why this does not break Eq.(7)

Equation (7) is an instance of an *off‑policy* policy‐gradient estimator where
the **weight** equals an unbiased estimator of  
$dP_\θ/dP_{\θ_{\text{old}}}$.
Changing from a single global advantage to a piecewise constant one merely
changes the *baseline* used for variance reduction – baselines do not affect
the expectation under either distribution.
Formally,

$$
\E_{\π_{\θ_{\text{old}}}}\!\Bigl[s(y;\θ)(R(y)-b(y))\Bigr]
 = 
\E_{\π_\θ}\!\Bigl[R(y)-b(y)\Bigr],
``` 

for any function $b(y)$; here we let  

$b(y)=0,\;
 R(y)=∑_k r^{(k)}(x,y)$,

and replace “$R(y)-b(y)$’’ by our normalised per‑turn term.
Therefore Eq.(7)’s theoretical guarantee – namely that clipping yields an unbiased estimator up to first order – stays intact.

---

### Practical considerations

* **Turn definition** – In practice one treats every system utterance as a turn;
  if user messages are stochastic they can be incorporated into the same segment because they share the same conditioning context.
* **Reward aggregation** – If one prefers a discounted return,
  multiply each partial reward by a discount factor before computing (15);
  scaling does not alter the zero‑mean/unit‑variance normalisation.
* **Clipping range** – Since $s_i(\θ)$ has unchanged magnitude,
  the clipping bounds used for plain GSPO (e.g., $3\times10^{-4},4\times10^{-4}$)
  are still appropriate.
* **Implementation** – From code perspective one merely adds a mask that maps
  tokens → their turn index, looks up the corresponding pre‑computed advantage vector,
  and uses the existing GSPO‐token loop. No extra likelihood recomputation
  is needed because all tokens share exactly the same $s_i(\θ)$.

---

### Summary

To handle multi‐turn reinforcement learning where feedback arrives after each system response:

1. Keep the **sequence‑level** importance ratio $s_i(\θ)$ exactly as in GSPO (Eq.(9)).
2. Compute **per‐turn** group‐relative advantages (Eq.(15)) using the reward that becomes available at that point.
3. Plug those advantages into the **GSPO‑token** loss (Eq.(11)/(16)), which reduces mathematically to multiplying $s_i(\θ)$ by different constants for different token blocks while preserving all gradients derived for plain GSPO.

This extension respects the original theoretical derivation behind Eq.(7), because it only changes unbiased baseline terms while leaving off‐policy weight untouched. Consequently one obtains fine‐grained credit assignment at dialogue turn level without sacrificing stability or requiring any additional value model—exactly what multi‐turn RL needs.
