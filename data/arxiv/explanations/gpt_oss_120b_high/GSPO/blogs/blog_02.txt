\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

# From Tokens to Whole Sentences: Why Sequence‑Level Thinking Saves Giant LLMs from Collapse  

*An intuitive deep‑dive into why applying importance weights to whole responses—rather than individual tokens—keeps massive language models stable during reinforcement learning.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why RL for Large Language Models Is Hard  

Reinforcement learning (RL) lets us teach language models to solve math puzzles, write code, or reason over long passages by rewarding *entire* generated answers. The bigger the model (think billions of parameters) and the longer the responses, the more sensitive the training becomes. In practice we often see **catastrophic model collapse**—a sudden drop in quality that cannot be recovered even after rolling back checkpoints or tweaking hyper‑parameters.

The culprit is not just “more data = more instability.” It lies deeper in how we **correct for off‑policy samples** when we reuse old rollouts to compute gradients.

---

## 2️⃣ Token‑Level vs. Sequence‑Level Importance Sampling  

### The basic idea of importance sampling  

If we want an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_{\text{tar}}$ but can only draw samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\text{beh}}$, we re‑weight each sample:

\[
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}[f(z)] \;=\;
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[
\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)
\right].
\]

Crucially, the ratio $\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}$ works **when we average over many *independent* samples** $z$. The law of large numbers smooths out the variance.

### What GRPO does  

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) carries over this idea **per token**:

\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
\]

For every position $t$ in a response $y_i$, it multiplies the token’s log‑probability ratio by a *single* sampled token from the old policy. In other words, it treats each token as an independent sample from its own next‑token distribution.

#### Analogy  
Imagine you’re judging a whole novel by looking at just one word on each page and trying to infer how “good” the entire story is. One word tells you almost nothing about the overall plot; yet you still try to re‑weight your judgment based on that single word. The result? A wildly noisy estimate that can swing dramatically from page to page.

Because each token’s importance weight is based on **one draw**, there is little statistical smoothing. When responses get long (hundreds of tokens), these per‑token noises **accumulate**—the gradient becomes a sum of many shaky estimates. Adding PPO‑style clipping only magnifies the problem: if a weight lies outside $[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon]$, it gets truncated, which introduces bias while still carrying high variance.

The end result is exactly what practitioners observe: **high‑variance noise builds up**, eventually pushing the model into an unrecoverable low‑performance region—what we call *model collapse*.

---

## 3️⃣ Aligning Units: Reward ↔ Importance Sampling  

A vital observation is that **the reward lives at the sequence level**:

\[
r(x,y)\in[0,1],\quad y \text{ = entire response}.
\]

If the *unit of optimization* (the quantity we clip and multiply by advantages) does not match the *unit of reward*, the algorithm inherently misbehaves. Think of trying to improve a car’s fuel efficiency by adjusting each individual bolt’s torque while measuring fuel consumption after an entire drive—a mismatch that yields confusing gradients.

The fix is simple: *match* them. Use an importance ratio that evaluates **the whole sequence**, not individual tokens.

---

## 4️⃣ GSPO: Group Sequence Policy Optimization  

### The core objective  

GSPO replaces token weights with a **sequence‑level importance ratio**:

\[
s_i(\theta)=\left(
\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
\right)^{\!1/|y_i|}
=
\exp\!\Bigg(
\frac{1}{|y_i|}
\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
\log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
\Bigg).
\]

The exponent $1/|y_i|$ performs **length normalization**, ensuring that short and long responses live on comparable scales and that clipping thresholds stay meaningful across different lengths.

GSPO’s loss over a batch of $G$ responses per query $x$ looks like:

\[
\mathcal{J}_{\text{GSPO}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_{x,\{y_i\}}\!\left[
\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}
\min\!\bigg(
s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i,\;
\operatorname{clip}(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\widehat A_i
\bigg)
\right],
\]

where $\widehat A_i$ is the **group‑relative advantage**:

\[
\widehat A_i=
\frac{
r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j\bigr)
}{
\operatorname{std}\!\bigl(\{r(x,y_j)\}_j\bigr)
}.
\]

Key points:

* **Clipping now applies to entire responses**, so “off‑policy” samples are discarded at once.
* All tokens inside a response share the same scaling factor $s_i(\theta)$ → **equal weighting**.
* No value network is needed—advantages come directly from normalized rewards within each group.

### Gradient intuition  

When you unpack the gradient (ignoring clipping), GSPO’s update becomes:

\[
\nabla_\theta \mathcal J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb E_{x,\{y_i\}}\!
\Bigg[
\dfrac1G \sum_i s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i \,
\dfrac1{|y_i|}
   \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
   \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})
\Bigg].
\]

All logits inside a response are multiplied by **the same scalar** $s_i(\theta)\widehat A_i$, eliminating token-wise noise spikes. By contrast, GRPO’s gradient multiplies each token’s log‑likelihood by its own noisy weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, which can vary wildly across positions and cause gradient divergence.

---

## 5️⃣ Why This Matters for Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) Models  

MoE models route each token through a selected subset of expert feedheads. After an update, **routing patterns may shift** dramatically—about 10 % of experts differ for the same token when training large MoEs with GRPO. Since GRPO’s importance ratios rely on *per‑token* probabilities from both old and new policies, any routing change blows up $w_{i,t}(\theta)$, making the gradient unstable.

Previous work introduced **Routing Replay**, i.e., caching old routing decisions and forcing new forward passes through identical experts just to keep $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ well behaved. This adds memory pressure and caps MoE capacity.

GSPO sidesteps routing entirely because it only needs the **overall sequence likelihood** $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ rather than per-token likelihoods after routing changes. The sequence likelihood stays relatively smooth even when internal expert assignments shuffle, so GSPO trains MoEs **without any routing tricks**—a cleaner, more memory‑efficient pipeline that fully exploits MoE capacity.

---

## 6️⃣ Practical Upsides Beyond Stability  

| Aspect | Traditional PPO / GRPO | GSPO |
|-------|------------------------|------|
| **Value model required?** | Yes (adds memory & compute) | No |
| **Routing replay needed for MoE?** | Yes (extra engineering) | No |
| **Precision sensitivity** | Token‐level likelihood recomputed on training engine | Can use inference engine scores directly (sequence level tolerant to float differences) |
| **Clipping fraction of tokens** | ~0–2 % clipped (still noisy) | ~0–2 % clipped at response level ➜ far fewer effective gradients needed but *more effective* learning |

Because GSPO only cares about whole‐response scores, you can pull likelihoods straight from an inference server (e.g., vLLM or SGLang). This cuts down on costly re-evaluation loops during partial rollouts or multi‑turn RL setups where train/infer engines differ in precision or architecture.

---

## 7️⃣ Empirical Evidence – Does It Work?  

The authors tested GSPO on a cold‑start Qwen3‑30B model fine‐tuned from its base version:

* **Training stability:** Reward curves rose smoothly throughout weeks of RL without any spikes or crashes.
* **Benchmark gains:**  
  * AIME’24 – higher Pass@1 rates after comparable compute  
  * LiveCodeBench – improved Pass@1 across eight samplings  
  * CodeForces – Elo rating climbs steadily.
* **Efficiency:** With identical compute budgets and query budgets as GRPO, GSPO achieved higher final rewards—showing better *sample utilization*.
* **Clipping behavior:** Even though GSPO clipped far more *responses* than GRPO clipped tokens (a two‐order‐of‐magnitude difference), it still learned faster—confirming that discarding noisy tokens altogether is better than keeping all but heavily weighted.
* **MoE handling:** Training dense models was stable; training MoEs succeeded without Routing Replay—a clear win for large sparse models.
  
Overall these results confirm that moving from *token-level* correction to *sequence-level* correction delivers tangible gains in both robustness and performance.

---

## 8️⃣ Takeaways – When Designing RL for Giant LLMs  

1️⃣ **Match units:** If rewards are given per response, compute importance weights per response—not per token.  
2️⃣ **Reduce variance early:** Aggregating likelihoods across an entire sequence smoothes out stochastic fluctuations inherent in next-token sampling.  
3️⃣ **Clip wisely:** Apply PPO-like clipping on whole responses; this removes outlier rollouts without contaminating gradients with noisy tokenwise ratios.  
4️⃣ **Simplify pipelines:** No value network + no routing replay = lower memory footprint + easier scaling to billions of parameters or thousands of experts.  
5️⃣ **Leverage inference engines:** Sequence-level scores tolerate floating point mismatches between training and inference runtimes—unlocking mixed training/inference architectures.

In short, “thinking at the sentence level” isn’t just linguistically nicer—it’s mathematically sound and practically essential when you push language models into the multi‑billion parameter regime. By aligning rewards with importance sampling at the right granularity, GSPO turns what used to be a precarious tightrope walk into a sturdy road toward ever larger—and far more capable—LLMs.

--- 

*If you’re gearing up for RL fine-tuning of your own massive model (dense or MoE), consider swapping out tokenwise importance ratios for sequencewise ones à la GSPO—you might just save your model from disappearing into oblivion.*
