# One Loss to Rule Them All: A Practical Step‑by‑Step Guide to Fine‑Tuning LLMs with Direct Preference Optimization  

*How you can go from raw preference pairs to a fully aligned language model by training on a single cross‑entropy loss.*

---

## Why Fine‑Tune Large Language Models?  

Even the most powerful unsupervised LMs carry a mixture of knowledge, quirks, and unsafe habits inherited from their massive web crawls. To ship a useful assistant we need **steerability**—the ability to bias the model toward the *high‑quality* responses we care about (helpful answers, positive sentiment, safe code) while suppressing undesirable behavior that also lives in its weights.  

Historically this has been tackled with **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback** (RLHF): collect preference judgments, fit a reward model, then run an RL algorithm such as PPO. The pipeline works but is heavyweight:
- Two separate networks (the LM and a reward model).  
- An inner loop that repeatedly samples from the LM while computing rewards.  
- Careful tuning of KL constraints, learning rates, baselines…  

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** shows that you can skip **all** of that and optimize the same objective with **one simple binary cross‑entropy loss**. Below we unpack the theory just enough to understand why it works, then walk through every practical step needed to replicate it on your own data.

---

## The Classic RLHF Pipeline – In a Nutshell  

1️⃣ **Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT).**  
   Start from a pretrained LM and fine‑tune on high‑quality instruction–completion pairs → reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

2️⃣ **Reward Modelling.**  
   Sample two completions $(y_1,y_2)$ per prompt $x$ from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, ask humans which they prefer → dataset $\mathcal D=\{(x,y_w,y_l)\}$.  
   Train a reward network $r_\phi(x,y)$ by maximizing the Bradley–Terry likelihood  

   $$\mathcal L_R(r_\phi)= -\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\!\big[\log\sigma(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l))\big]$$  

   where $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$.

3️⃣ **RL Fine‑Tuning.**  
   Maximize expected reward while staying close (in KL) to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$  

   $$\max_{\pi_\theta}\;\mathbb E_{x,\;y\sim\pi_\theta}\! \big[ r_\phi(x,y)\big]\;-\;\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big).$$  

   Because language generation is discrete this step needs an RL algorithm (PPO) and extra tricks (value baselines, entropy bonuses).

All three stages are necessary **only because** we treat preferences as an *implicit* reward function first, then *optimize* that reward later. DPO collapses stages 2 and 3 into one.

---

## The Core Insight of Direct Preference Optimization  

Recall the optimal solution of the KL‑constrained problem above is known in closed form:

$$
\pi^{*}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big),
\tag{1}
$$

with $Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp({r(x,y)}/{\beta})$.
If we solve Eq.(1) for its underlying reward we obtain

$$
r(x,y)=\beta \log \frac{\pi^{*}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\tag{2}
$$

Crucially, **the Bradley–Terry preference model depends only on differences of rewards**, i.e.  
$p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2|x)=\sigma(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2))$.  
Plugging Eq.(2) into this difference eliminates the unknown partition term $Z(\cdot)$:

$$
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2|x)=
\sigma\!\Big(
\beta \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
-
\beta \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
\Big).
\tag{3}
$$

Equation (3) tells us that *the probability of a human preferring one completion over another can be expressed purely in terms of the two policies*—the current policy $\pi^{*}$ and its reference $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.  
Therefore we can treat $\pi_\theta$ itself as the “rewarded” policy and fit it directly via maximum likelihood:

$$
\boxed{
\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\theta)=
-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
\,\log\sigma
\Big(
\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
-
\beta \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
\Big)
}.
\tag{4}
$$

That’s it—one binary cross entropy loss that implicitly respects both the reward signal *and* the KL regularizer! The gradient has an intuitive form:

$$
\nabla_\theta \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}
=
-\beta\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)} 
\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))
[
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w)
-
\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l)
],
$$

where $\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\,\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ is the *implicit* reward induced by the current policy.  
The term $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(y_l)-\hat r_\theta(y_w))$ downweights examples that are already ordered correctly and **upweights misordered pairs**, preventing degeneration that appears in naïve “unlikelihood” tricks.

## From Data to Model – The Complete DPO Workflow  

Below is a checklist you can follow linearly; each block corresponds directly to an equation above or an implementation detail from the paper.

### 1️⃣ Gather Preference Pairs  

| Action | Details |
|--------|---------|
| **Prompt pool** | Any set of inputs $x$ you eventually want your model to serve (e.g., user queries, article starts). |
| **Generate candidates** | Sample *two* completions $(y_1,y_2)$ per $x$ from a *reference* LM ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$). In practice you can also reuse publicly available preference datasets if they were collected with a similar base model.| 
| **Human labeling** | Show annotators “Which response is better?” → produce tuples $(x,\; y_w,\; y_l)$. If human labeling is unavailable you may bootstrap using a trained sentiment classifier or other proxy—but keep in mind this introduces bias.|

Result → **preference dataset** $\mathcal D=\{(x_i , y_{w}^{i}, y_{l}^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$.

### 2️⃣ Pick / Build the Reference Model $\boldsymbol{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$  

- **If you already have an SFT checkpoint** (a model fine‑tuned on instruction data), simply set $\pi_{\text{ref}} = $ SFT model.
- **Otherwise**, create one by *supervised fine‑tuning on just the winners*:  

  $$ 
  \max_{\pi}\;\mathbb E_{(x,y_w)\sim \mathcal D}[\,\log \pi(y_w|x)\,].
  $$  

  This ensures $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ lives in roughly the same distribution as your preference data, limiting distribution shift when we later compute ratios $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}$.

### 3️⃣ Choose the KL Trade‑off Parameter $\boldsymbol{\beta}$  

$\beta$ controls how much you penalize drift away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$:  
- Small $\beta$ ⇒ stronger KL penalty → very conservative updates (policy stays close).  
- Large $\beta$ ⇒ weaker penalty → more aggressive alignment at risk of “reward hacking”.  

Empirically papers report good starting points across tasks:
- Sentiment control: $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$
- Summarization / dialogue: $\beta =0.5$–$2$

You can treat $\beta$ as a hyperparameter and sweep a few values early on; there’s no need for expert tuning of KL targets or clipping schedules as in PPO.

### 4️⃣ Assemble Mini‑Batches  

Each training example needs both **probabilities** under:
- The *current* policy $\pi_\theta$: computed by forward passing $(x,y)$ through your language model and extracting token‐wise logits → apply softmax → multiply across time steps for sequence probability.
- The *reference* policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$: precompute once per epoch (or cache) because it never changes during DPO training.

Both probabilities are typically very small; work in log space for numeric stability:

```
logp_theta_win = sum_t logsoftmax_theta_t[ token_t_of_yw ]
logp_ref_win   = sum_t logsoftmax_ref_t[ token_t_of_yw ]
```

Analogous quantities are obtained for $y_l$.

### 5️⃣ Compute the DPO Loss (Eq 4)  

```python
# pseudo-code
logit_ratio_win = beta * (logp_theta_win - logp_ref_win)
logit_ratio_lose = beta * (logp_theta_lose - logp_ref_lose)

# logistic “preference score”
pref_score = logit_ratio_win - logit_ratio_lose
loss = -torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
            input=pref_score,
            target=torch.ones_like(pref_score))   # label=1 means win > lose
```

That single `loss` value already embeds:
- The Bradley–Terry likelihood.
- The KL regularizer via `logp_ref`.
- The importance weighting (`σ`) through BCE’s gradient formulation.

### 6️⃣ Optimizer & Training Loop  

- Optimizer: AdamW or any Adam variant works fine (`lr`≈ 5e−5 for 6B models is a typical starting point).  
- No value network or baseline needed – gradients come directly from Eq 5 in the paper.
- Iterate over epochs until validation loss plateaus or until a desired KL budget is reached.
> **Tip:** Periodically compute average sequence‐level KL  
> $$ KL(\pi_\theta||\pi_{\text{ref}}) = 
>     \mathbb E_{x}\!\big[\, 
>     \sum_y \!\,\frac{\exp(\log p_\theta)}{{Z_x}}\,
>      (\log p_\theta - \log p_{\!ref}) 
>     ] $$   
> If KL explodes beyond what you deem safe you can lower β or stop early.

### 7️⃣ Validation – Win Rate & Human‐Level Checks  

Because ground truth rewards are rarely available outside toy settings:
- Use an offline held‑out preference set: compute win rate of your current policy against its counterpart (`π_ref`).  
- If possible run GPT‑4 or another strong evaluator as a proxy; see Section “Human Judgment Validation” in the paper for prompt engineering tips.
- For sentiment tasks you can directly evaluate against a calibrated classifier—this gives you an exact reward/KL frontier like Figure 1 in the paper.

## Practical Tips & Common Pitfalls  

- **Numerical stability:** always work in log probabilities before exponentiation; avoid explicit division by tiny numbers.
- **Caching reference scores:** Since $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ never changes during DPO training you can precompute `logp_ref_win`/`logp_ref_lose` once per epoch—dramatically speeds up GPU throughput.
- **Batch size vs diversity:** Larger batches provide smoother importance weighting because σ will see many varied mis‐ordered pairs.
- **Temperature at inference:** Because DPO already enforces a KL budget there’s less sensitivity to sampling temperature than PPO models—but you may still tune it for creativity vs safety.
- **Avoid naive unlikelihood:** Simply maximizing `log pθ(win)` while minimizing `log pθ(loss)` leads to degeneration because it ignores how badly misranked pairs are weighted—DPO’s σ term fixes this automatically.
- **When no SFT checkpoint exists:** Initialise `π_θ` randomly or from base LM weights and run a quick “Preferred‐FT” pass on winners only before starting DPO—to give γ something sensible for ratios.
- **Scaling beyond 6B:** The algorithm stays linear in model size — only forward passes are required per batch — making it friendly to multi‑GPU pipelines without any extra RL critics or rollout workers.

## How Does DPO Compare With Traditional PPO?  

Empirically (see Section 5 of the paper):
- On controlled sentiment generation DPO achieves higher reward *and* lower KL than PPO even when PPO has access to ground truth rewards (`PPO-GT`).  
- On summarization (Reddit TL;DR) DPO reaches ~61% win rate against reference summaries versus ~57% for PPO at their optimal temperature — all while requiring < 10% of PPO’s wall‑clock time because there’s no roll‑out loop.
- In single‐turn dialogue DPO matches or exceeds “Best‑of N” sampling baselines with only one forward pass at test time—something impossible with PPO unless you accept huge inference latency.
  
In short: **same objective → far simpler optimization → better trade­off between performance and stability**.

---

## When Should You Reach For DPO?  

✅ You have *preference data* (pairwise win/loss annotations).  
✅ You already possess—or can cheaply generate—a reference model ($π_{\text{ref}}$, typically SFT).  
✅ You want fast iteration cycles without fiddling with PPO hyperparameters such as clipping values, value networks, or advantage estimation tricks.  
✅ Your compute budget is modest: single GPU fine‐tuning of models up to several billion parameters fits comfortably within hours instead of days/weeks required by full RLHF pipelines.

If you need highly expressive policies that must explore far from any existing checkpoint—e.g., learning novel skills completely absent from your base LM—consider hybrid approaches where DPO serves as an initial alignment stage before more adventurous RL fine‐tuning.

## TL;DR – One Loss To Align Them All  

1️⃣ Build/obtain an SFT checkpoint → set it as `$π_{ref}$`.  
2️⃣ Collect binary preferences `(x, y_w , y_l)`.  
3️⃣ Pick β (start around `0.5`–`1`).  
4️⃣ For each batch compute log‐probabilities under both policies and evaluate Eq 4 (`L_DPO`).  
5️⃣ Optimize with AdamW—no critic, no rollouts, no baselines required.  
6️⃣ Monitor KL and win rate; stop when improvement stalls orKL exceeds your safety budget.  

You now have a language model that maximizes human preferences *and* stays anchored to its original knowledge—all thanks to one neat cross entropy loss called Direct Preference Optimization. Give it a spin—you’ll be surprised how quickly high quality alignment emerges without ever touching reinforcement learning code!
