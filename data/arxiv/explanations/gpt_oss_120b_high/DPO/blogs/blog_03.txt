# Secretly a Reward Model: How Direct Preference Optimization Turns Preferences Into a Simple Classification Task

## Introduction  
Large language models (LLMs) are trained on massive text corpora without any explicit notion of “good” or “bad” behavior. To make them helpful we usually collect **human preferences**—for each prompt users tell us which of two completions they like more—and then train the model with **reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF).  

RLHF, however, is a two‑stage dance:

1. **Fit a reward model** that predicts which completion humans will prefer.
2. **Run reinforcement learning** (often PPO) on the language model to maximize this reward while staying close to the original model (a KL‑constraint).

Both stages add engineering complexity, variance from sampling, and often require careful hyper‑parameter tuning.

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** shows that we don’t need this dance at all. By re‑expressing the unknown reward in terms of **policy‑to‑reference log‑ratios**, the whole problem collapses into a single binary cross‑entropy loss—a classic classification task. Let’s unpack the neat mathematical trick behind DPO and see why it works.

---

## 1. The RLHF Objective in One Equation  

In standard RLHF we start with a *reference* policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (the supervised fine‑tuned model). The goal is to find a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) that maximizes expected reward while keeping the KL distance from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) under control:

\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}
\Big[\, r(x,y) \Big]
-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big).
\tag{1}
\]

Here:
- \(r(x,y)\) is an (unknown) scalar reward that scores how much humans like completion \(y\) given prompt \(x\).
- \(\beta>0\) tunes how aggressively we let the new policy drift away from the reference.

Because \(r(x,y)\) is unknown we usually learn it from preference data using a Bradley–Terry (BT) or Plackett‑Luce model:

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)=
\sigma\big(r^{*}(x,y_1)-r^{*}(x,y_2)\big),
\qquad
\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\tag{2}
\]

With a dataset of triples \((x,y_w,y_l)\) (“\(y_w\) is preferred over \(y_l\)”),
the standard reward‑learning loss is

\[
\mathcal L_R(r_\phi)=
-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}
\Big[\log\sigma(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l))\Big].
\tag{3}
\]

After fitting \(r_\phi\), we plug it into Eq. (1) and run RL (e.g., PPO).

---

## 2. The Analytic Optimal Policy  

A remarkable fact—proved in many works on KL‑constrained RL—is that **the optimal policy has a closed form**:

\[
\boxed{
   \pi_{r}(y|x)=
   \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, 
   \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
   \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\Big)
}
\quad
Z(x)=\sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
      e^{\,r(x,y')/\beta}.
\tag{4}
\]

Intuitively, we start from the reference distribution and tilt it upward for high‑reward completions by an exponential factor; then we renormalize with \(Z(x)\).

If we take logarithms on both sides,

\[
\log \pi_{r}(y|x)=
    \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
    + \tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)
    - \log Z(x).
\tag{5}
\]

Rearranging gives an expression for *the reward* itself:

\[
r(x,y)=
   \beta\, 
   \underbrace{\big[\log\tfrac{\pi_{r}(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\big]}_{\displaystyle
        \text{policy–reference log‑ratio}}
   + 
   \underbrace{\beta\,\log Z(x)}_{\displaystyle f(x)}.
\tag{6}
\]

Crucially, **\(f(x)\) depends only on the prompt** \(x\), not on any particular completion \(y.\)

---

## 3. Why Log‑Ratios Matter  

Human preference models such as BT care only about *differences* of rewards (see Eq. (2)). When we plug Eq. (6) into Eq. (2), the additive term \(f(x)\) cancels out:

```
r*(x, y1) - r*(x, y2)
   = β [ log π*(y1)/π_ref(y1) - log π*(y2)/π_ref(y2) ].
```

Thus the *preference probability* can be written entirely in terms of **policy–reference log‑ratios**:

\[
p^{*}(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)=
\sigma
  \Big(
    β\, 
    [\, 
        \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
      - 
        \log\tfrac{\pi^{*}(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
    ] 
  \Big).
\tag{7}
\]

In other words, once you know how much more likely your *current* policy is compared to the reference for each candidate answer, you automatically know what humans would prefer—**the reward function has been absorbed into those probability ratios**.

---

## 4. From Preferences Directly to Binary Classification  

Now replace the *optimal* policy in Eq. (7) with our *learnable* policy \(\pi_\theta.\) Using our dataset of preference triples yields a classic binary cross‑entropy objective:

```math
\boxed{
   \mathcal L_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=
   -\, 
   \mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}
   [
      \log\sigma
      (
        β\, 
        [\, 
            \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
          -
            \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
        ]
      )
   ]
}.
```

All terms are fully differentiable:
- The numerator logits are obtained by feeding each completion through **the current LM** (\(\pi_\theta.\))
- The denominator logits come from **the fixed reference LM** (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)), typically an SFT version trained on high‑quality demonstrations.

No sampling loop, no separate value network, no extra “baseline” term—just a **binary classification loss** that tells us how often our model assigns higher relative probability to preferred completions than to dispreferred ones.

### Gradient Insight  
The gradient w.r.t. θ looks like:

```math
\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{DPO}}
=
-β\, 
\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}
[ σ(
      r̂(y_l)-r̂(y_w)
   )
  (
     ∇_\theta log πθ(y_w|x)
    -∇_\theta log πθ(y_l|x)
  )
],
```

where 

```math
r̂(x,y)=β\, log [πθ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)].
```

The scalar `σ(r̂(y_l)-r̂(y_w))` acts as an **importance weight**:
- When our current implicit reward rates the dispreferred option too highly (`r̂(y_l)>r̂(y_w)`), this factor approaches 1 and forces a strong update.
- When we already rank them correctly (`r̂(y_w)>r̂(y_l)`), it shrinks toward 0 so updates become gentle.

This weighting prevents degenerate “unlikelihood” behavior where naïvely maximizing `πθ(y_w)` and minimizing `πθ(y_l)` can drive the model into pathological states—a failure case documented in ablations of DPO.

---

## 5. What Does This Mean? “Your LM Is Secretly a Reward Model”

Because every term in Eq. (6) depends on *probabilities* output by our language model, **the LM itself encodes an implicit reward:**  

\( r̂_\theta(x,y)\equiv β\,ln[π_\theta(y|x)/π_\text{ref}(y|x)] .$

If you ask “what would this model consider good?” you can simply compute that log‑ratio—it’s mathematically equivalent to computing `r` in any explicit RLHF pipeline (up to an additive function of \( x·.\))

A formal result backs this intuition:

> **Theorem (rephrased):**  
> For any true reward function whose preference distribution follows Bradley–Terry (or more generally Plackett–Luce), there exists an equivalent representation of that reward as  
>   
> \( r(x,y)=β\,ln[π(y|x)/π_\text{ref}(y|x)] + f(x),  ∗∗∗∗∗∗∗∎  ∗∗∗∗∗∗∗"

Thus reparameterizing does *not* restrict what rewards can be expressed; it merely chooses one convenient member of each equivalence class (all rewards differing by an \(f(x)\) are indistinguishable under BT).

---

## 6. Putting It All Together – The DPO Pipeline  

Below is a practical checklist derived from the paper:

1️⃣ **Collect Preference Data**  
   - For each prompt \( x,\) have two completions \( y_w,\! y_l.\)

2️⃣ **Pick / Build a Reference Model**  
   - Usually the supervised fine‑tuned LM (\(\pi^\mathrm{SFT}\)).  
   - If unavailable, train a small “reference” LM by maximizing likelihood on all preferred completions only.

3️⃣ **Compute Log‑Probabilities**  
   - Run both `π_ref` and `πθ` on each candidate answer; retrieve `log π_ref(y_i | x)` and `log πθ(y_i | x)`.

4️⃣ **Form the Cross‑Entropy Loss** (`L_DPO` above).  
5️⃣ **Backpropagate & Update θ** – standard optimizer (AdamW etc.). No RL loops needed!  

Training proceeds exactly like any supervised classification task: batch over many `(x, y_w, y_l)` triples and minimize `L_DPO`. The only hyperparameter specific to DPO is **β**, which scales how strongly we penalize deviating from `π_ref`. In practice values such as `{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5}` work well; the paper reports good performance without heavy tuning.

---

## 7. Does Simpler Mean Worse? Empirical Evidence  

The authors evaluated DPO on three open‑ended tasks:

- **Sentiment Control:** Using an external sentiment classifier as ground truth reward,
  DPO achieved a *dominant* reward–KL frontier—higher sentiment score at lower KL than PPO (even PPO trained with true rewards).

- **Summarization (Reddit TL;DR):** Measured via GPT‑4 win rates against human references,
  DPO reached ~61 % win rate versus ~57 % for PPO at their best sampling temperatures.

- **Single–Turn Dialogue (Anthropic HH):**
  DPO matched or exceeded PPO while requiring dramatically less computation (no sampling during training). It also outperformed naive methods such as Preferred‑FT or Unlikelihood baselines.

Across all settings DPO showed:
- Faster convergence,
- Greater robustness to temperature changes,
- Simpler implementation (no value networks or KL penalty tricks).

These results underscore that turning preference learning into classification does not sacrifice performance—in fact it often improves stability and sample efficiency.

---

## 8. Why This Matters  

- **Lower Engineering Barriers:** You no longer need three separate training stages (reward fitting + PPO). A single `torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss`‐style step suffices.
- **Reduced Variance:** No Monte Carlo rollouts or baseline subtractions → smoother gradients.
- **Scalable:** Since you avoid repeated sampling during training, you can fine‑tune larger models with modest compute budgets.
- **Interpretability:** The learned log‑ratio directly tells you how much more likely each token sequence is compared to its reference counterpart—providing an immediate lens into what “reward” looks like inside your LM.

---

## Takeaways  

1️⃣ The heart of RLHF—the KL‐constrained maximization—has an analytically optimal policy that ties together rewards and probabilities via an exponential tilt.

2️⃣ By rearranging that relationship we can express every admissible reward as a *policy‐to‐reference log ratio*. Since BT preferences depend only on differences of rewards, those additive constants vanish completely.

3️⃣ Substituting this expression into the BT likelihood yields exactly a binary cross‐entropy loss over preference pairs—a textbook classification problem!

4️⃣ Training under this loss automatically implements both reward learning *and* policy optimization in one shot; hence your language model becomes its own hidden reward estimator.

5️⃣ Empirically DOP matches or surpasses classic PPO‐based RLHF across sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue benchmarks while being dramatically simpler and more stable.

---

## Looking Ahead  

DPO opens several research avenues:
- **Out‑of‑distribution Generalization:** Does learning an implicit reward change robustness compared with explicit rewards?
- **Self‑Labeling:** Could we generate new preference pairs by letting a trained DPO policy label fresh prompts?
- **Scale:** Extending beyond 6B parameters toward dozens or hundreds of billions will test whether hidden rewards remain well behaved.
- **Cross‑Modality:** The same reparameterization could apply to diffusion models or audio generation where preferences are cheaper than absolute scores.

In short, Direct Preference Optimization demonstrates that sometimes “reinforcement learning” isn’t needed at all—just reframe your objective cleverly enough, and let binary classification do the heavy lifting while your LM secretly learns its own reward function along the way.
