\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Direct Preference Optimization: Simplifying Alignment of Large Language Models  

# Capabilities and Limitations of Large Unsupervised Language Models  

Large language models (LMs) that are trained purely on massive text corpora—often referred to as *unsupervised* or *self‑supervised* models—exhibit surprisingly broad capabilities. When trained on data containing billions of tokens, they acquire substantial world knowledge, can perform basic reasoning, generate coherent prose, translate between languages, write code, and even answer factual questions without any task‑specific supervision. Empirical work such as PaLM \citep{chowdhery2022palm}, GPT‑3 \citep{brown2020language}, LLaMA \citep{touvron2023llama}, and recent theoretical analyses \citep{bubeck2023sparks} demonstrate that these models can solve many tasks *zero‑shot* or with a few examples provided in the prompt.  

Despite these successes, the very breadth of knowledge gathered during pre‑training also creates a fundamental tension. The training data are generated by humans pursuing diverse goals: some texts contain helpful explanations, others encode misinformation or harmful stereotypes, some exemplify expert coding practices while other portions reflect common bugs or low‑quality code. Because the learning signal during pre‑training treats all tokens equally—each token is simply predicted from its context—the model inherits both desirable expertise and undesirable artifacts. Consequently, when asked to produce a response under a new user query, the model may draw upon any of its learned patterns without any intrinsic mechanism for preferring the *desired* ones over the *undesired*. This lack of built‑in steering makes raw unsupervised LMs unsuitable for safely deployed applications where consistency, correctness, or alignment with user intent matter.

# The Alignment Problem: Safety, Performance, Controllability  

The misalignment between an LM’s raw generative distribution and what developers would like it to produce is often called the *alignment problem*. In practice alignment comprises three intertwined desiderata:

1. **Safety** – The model should avoid generating toxic language, disallowed advice, or misinformation that could cause real‑world harm.
2. **Performance** – When users request useful assistance—e.g., writing code, summarizing an article—the model should produce high‑quality outputs that respect factual accuracy and relevance.
3. **Controllability** – Developers need mechanisms to shape model behavior on demand (e.g., biasing toward a particular sentiment or style) without retraining from scratch.

These desiderata cannot be satisfied merely by enlarging the pre‑training corpus or by increasing model size; they require *selective* emphasis on certain behaviors while suppressing others. Importantly, many of these desired properties are easier to capture through *relative judgments* rather than absolute demonstrations. Human annotators can readily say “Response A is better than Response B” even when they cannot write a perfect exemplar themselves—a phenomenon leveraged throughout recent alignment research \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}. Therefore any practical alignment framework must be able to consume pairs of completions labeled with preferences and translate those preferences into a model that consistently produces preferred outputs.

# Existing Approaches Based on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)  

The dominant paradigm for incorporating human preferences into LMs is **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback** (RLHF). The pipeline typically proceeds through three stages:

### 1. Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)  
Starting from an unsupervised LM $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, one first performs supervised learning on a curated dataset of high‑quality examples (often instruction–response pairs). This step yields a baseline policy that already respects task conventions but has not yet been shaped according to explicit human preferences.

### 2. Reward Modeling  
Next, one generates candidate completions using $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$ for a collection of prompts $x$. Human annotators compare pairs $(y_{1}, y_{2})$ produced for each $x$, indicating which answer $y_{w}$ is preferred over $y_{l}$. These binary comparisons are assumed to be generated by an unknown latent reward function $r^{*}(x,y)$. A common statistical model linking rewards to observed preferences is the **Bradley–Terry** model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}:
\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
    = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{1}
\]
Training proceeds by fitting a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x,y)$ so that its induced preference probabilities match those observed in the data. This is done via maximum likelihood under Eq.~(1), which reduces to minimizing a binary cross‑entropy loss:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(r_{\phi}) = - \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})}\!\left[\, \log\sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})\bigr)\right],
\tag{2}
\]
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid.

### 3. Reinforcement Learning Fine‑Tuning  
Finally, one treats $r_{\phi}$ as a surrogate reward signal for policy optimization. To prevent catastrophic deviation from $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$, which serves as a reliable “reference” distribution $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, RLHF adds a KL‑divergence penalty:
\[
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\;
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x,\;y\sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[\,r_{\phi}(x,y)\,]}_{\text{reward maximization}}
    - \beta\, \underbrace{\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)\,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr)}_{\text{policy conservatism}}.
    \tag{3}
\]
Because language generation involves discrete token sequences and non‑differentiable sampling, Eq.~(3) is optimized with reinforcement‑learning algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman2017proximal}. The RL step iteratively samples responses from $\pi_{\theta}$, evaluates them using $r_{\phi}$ (often augmented by baselines), computes policy gradients via REINFORCE‐style estimators, and updates $\theta$ while clipping changes to enforce stability.

Although RLHF has demonstrated impressive results—producing models capable of nuanced dialogue or accurate summarization—it also suffers from several practical drawbacks:

- **Complexity:** Two distinct neural networks must be trained (the reward model and the policy), each requiring careful hyperparameter tuning.
- **Instability:** Policy gradients exhibit high variance; balancing reward improvement against KL shrinkage often demands delicate scheduling.
- **Computational cost:** Sampling from the policy during training introduces substantial overhead because each gradient step involves multiple forward passes through a large LM.
- **Sensitivity to reward mis‑specification:** Errors in $r_{\phi}$ propagate directly into degraded policy behavior unless careful regularization is applied.

These challenges motivate researchers to search for alternatives that retain the benefits of preference learning while removing reliance on explicit reinforcement learning loops.

# Motivation for a Simpler, More Stable Alternative  

The central insight behind seeking an alternative stems from recognizing that Equation (3) admits an analytical solution **if** we reparameterize the unknown reward function appropriately. Classic results in constrained reinforcement learning show that the optimal policy solving Eq.~(3) has closed form \citep{peters2007reinforcement,korbak2022reinforcement}:
\[
\pi_{r}(y|x)=
   \frac{
         \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\,
         \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\bigr)
       }{
         Z(x)
       }, 
    \qquad 
    Z(x)=
    \sum_{y'} 
        \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)
        \exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta} r(x,y')\bigr).
    \tag{4}
\]
Taking logarithms yields an expression for $r$ in terms of any candidate optimal policy $\pi_{r}$:
\[
r(x,y)=
   \beta\, \log
     \frac{\displaystyle \pi_{r}(y|x)}
          {\displaystyle \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
   + 
   \beta\, \log Z(x).
   \tag{5}
\]
Crucially, under the Bradley–Terry preference model only *differences* of rewards appear:
$p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)=\sigma(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2}))$. Substituting Eq.~(5) into this difference eliminates the unknown normalizer $Z(x)$ because it cancels out between numerator and denominator:
\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\bigr)
   = 
   \frac{
         1
       }{
         1 + 
         \exp
         \Bigl(
            \beta\, 
            \log
            \frac{\displaystyle 
                  \pi^{*}(y_{2}|x)}
                 {\displaystyle 
                  \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}
            -
            \beta\, 
            \log
            \frac{\displaystyle 
                  \pi^{*}(y_{1}|x)}
                 {\displaystyle 
                  \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}
         \Bigr)
        } .
   \tag{6}
\]
Equation (6) tells us that **the same likelihood over human preferences can be expressed directly as a function of policies**, without ever constructing an explicit reward network or performing stochastic policy evaluation. Consequently we can formulate an ordinary maximum‑likelihood objective over policies:
\[
%
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta)
=
-
\, 
\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim {\cal D}}
      [
        %
        \log\sigma
           (
               \beta\, 
               \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
               -
               \beta\, 
               \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
           )
      ] .
%
   \tag{7}
\]
The loss in Eq.~(7) is precisely a **binary cross‑entropy** loss where each example’s “logit” is a weighted difference between the log‑probabilities assigned by the current policy $\pi_\theta$ and those assigned by the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. No sampling loop or auxiliary value function appears; training reduces to standard gradient descent on a well‑behaved classification objective. 

From an algorithmic standpoint this reparameterization removes two major sources of instability:

- **Reward estimation noise** disappears because we never fit $r_\phi$ separately.
- **Policy‐gradient variance** shrinks since Eq.~(7) does not involve Monte Carlo estimates of future returns—every term can be computed exactly from token‐level log‐probabilities provided by the LM’s forward pass.

Thus we obtain an approach that retains exactly the same preference maximization criterion underpinning classic RLHF (the KL‐constrained expected reward), yet sidesteps reinforcement learning altogether.

# Contributions and Scope of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)  

The paper introduces **Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO), an algorithmic framework that implements precisely the transformation outlined above. Its contributions can be summarized as follows:

1. **Closed‑form Policy Parameterization:** By exploiting Eq.~(4)–(5), DPO shows that any reward function compatible with Bradley–Terry preferences can be represented as $\beta\,\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ up to an additive term independent of actions. This representation preserves full expressive power—no class of reward functions is lost—while providing direct access to its optimal policy without iterative RL steps.

2. **Single‑Stage Training Objective:** DPO replaces the two-stage process (“fit reward → run RL”) with a single binary cross‑entropy loss over policies (Eq.~ 7). The objective naturally incorporates an importance weight $\sigma(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w))$, where $\hat r_\theta$ denotes the implicit reward defined by Eq.~(5). This weighting automatically downweights examples already correctly ordered by the current model and upweights those where it errs—a mechanism akin to curriculum learning but derived analytically rather than heuristically.

3. **Stability and Computational Efficiency:** Empirically DPO attains comparable or superior performance relative to PPO‑based RLHF across diverse tasks such as sentiment control, summarization (Reddit TL;DR), and single‐turn dialogue (Anthropic Helpful/Harmful). Crucially it does so without sampling during training or extensive hyperparameter search; training costs are comparable to ordinary supervised fine‑tuning because each update only requires evaluating log probabilities for two completions per example.

4. **Broad Applicability:** The method works with any language model up to at least 6 B parameters evaluated in this work and can reuse existing preference datasets collected under standard RLHF pipelines—all that changes is setting $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ equal to whichever SFT model generated those preferences (or initializing it via maximum likelihood on preferred completions when no SFT checkpoint exists).

5. **Theoretical Foundations:** The authors formalize equivalence classes of rewards differing only by prompt‐dependent constants and prove (Theorem 1) that every such class admits a member expressible as $\beta\,log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$ for some policy $\pi$. They also analyze why actor–critic methods like PPO suffer from high variance due to missing normalizing terms—terms that DPO’s reparameterization automatically handles—thereby offering conceptual insight into observed instabilities in traditional RLHF pipelines.

Overall, Direct Preference Optimization offers a principled yet dramatically simpler route from human preference data to aligned language models: it eliminates dedicated reward modeling networks, dispenses with reinforcement‐learning loops altogether, and retains exact optimality guarantees under standard preference assumptions. By doing so it lowers both computational barriers and engineering complexity—a crucial step toward scaling alignment techniques alongside ever larger foundation models.

## Transformer Architecture and Next‑Token Prediction Objective

Modern autoregressive language models are built on the *Transformer* neural architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017).  A Transformer consists of alternating layers of **self‑attention** and **feed‑forward** sub‑networks applied to a sequence of token embeddings.  
For an input token sequence \(x = (x_1,\dots,x_T)\) each token is first mapped to a continuous vector via an embedding matrix \(E\).  Positional information is added (e.g., sinusoidal or learned positional encodings) so that the model can distinguish order despite being permutation‑invariant by design.

In a self‑attention layer every position \(t\) computes a weighted sum over all positions \(s \le t\) (for causal or “decoder‑only” language modeling).  The weight assigned to token \(s\) when attending from \(t\) is proportional to
\[
\operatorname{softmax}\!\Bigl(\frac{Q_t K_s^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Bigr),
\]
where \(Q_t= W_Q h_t\), \(K_s = W_K h_s\), and \(d_k\) is the dimensionality of the query/key vectors.  The resulting context vector is passed through a feed‑forward neural network with a non‑linear activation, followed by residual connections and layer normalisation. Stacking many such layers yields a deep network capable of modelling long‑range dependencies in text.

Once a forward pass has produced hidden representations for each position, the model generates **next‑token predictions** by applying a linear projection onto the vocabulary space followed by a softmax:
\[
\pi_\theta(y_t \mid x_{<t}) 
   = \operatorname{softmax}\bigl(W_\text{out} h_t\bigr)_ {y_t},
\]
where \(\theta\) denotes all learnable parameters (embeddings, attention weights, feed‑forward weights, output projection).  
The training objective for *autoregressive* language modelling is *maximum likelihood*, equivalently minimising the *cross‑entropy* between the model distribution \(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\) and the true next token observed in data:
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{LM}(\theta)
    = - \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_\theta(y_t^\ast \mid x_{<t}),
\tag{1}
\]
where \(y_t^\ast\) denotes the ground‑truth token at position \(t\).  Equation (1) encourages the model to assign high probability mass to data‐observed continuations while spreading remaining probability over plausible alternatives.  Training proceeds over massive corpora of natural language text, yielding “large unsupervised language models” that capture broad world knowledge and rudimentary reasoning abilities purely from this next‑token prediction task.

## Scaling Laws and Emergent Abilities

Empirically, researchers have observed simple *scaling laws* governing how performance improves as model size (\# parameters), dataset size (tokens), and compute budget increase (Brown et al., 2020; Chowdhery et al., 2022).  When plotted on log–log axes these quantities yield approximately linear relationships: doubling parameters or data typically reduces loss by a predictable factor.  

More intriguingly, certain capabilities appear **only after crossing specific scale thresholds**, a phenomenon often termed *emergent abilities*.  Examples include few‑shot prompting—where a model can solve new tasks after seeing only a handful of exemplars—as well as more sophisticated reasoning or coding skills (e.g., PaLM 540B achieving strong performance on coding benchmarks).  These abilities are not explicitly programmed; they arise because larger models can internalise richer distributions over language use present in massive corpora.

From an instructional perspective, scaling laws justify why contemporary research begins by pretraining *very large* Transformers on billions of tokens before any downstream adaptation.  The raw unsupervised phase supplies a broad knowledge base that later stages—such as *Supervised Fine‑Tuning*—can specialise without needing to relearn basic linguistic structure.

## Supervised Fine‑Tuning (SFT)

### Definition

After unsupervised pretraining yields a base policy \(\pi^{\text{pre}}(y \mid x)\), **Supervised Fine‑Tuning** adapts this policy toward a particular downstream behaviour using labelled examples.  
Formally we collect a dataset
\[
\mathcal{D}_\text{SFT}
   = \bigl\{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}_{\text{target}})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\]
where each input \(x^{(i)}\) is paired with a high–quality target completion \(y^{(i)}_{\text{target}}\).  
The fine‑tuning objective mirrors Eq. (1) but now conditioned on task‐specific prompts:
\[
\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT}(\theta)
   = - \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(i)}_{\text{target}}|}
      \log \pi_\theta \bigl(y^{(i)}_{\text{target},t} \mid
        x^{(i)},\, y^{(i)}_{\text{target},<t}\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]

Optimising Eq. (2) starts from the pretrained parameters (often denoted \(\theta^{\text{pre}}\)) so that learning focuses primarily on aligning outputs with the new supervision rather than relearning language fundamentals.

### Objectives

The chief objective is *distribution matching*: after fine‑tuning we desire that sampling from \(\pi^\text{SFT}(y|x)\)—the policy obtained after minimising \(\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT}\)—produces responses indistinguishable from those provided in \(\mathcal{D}_\text{SFT}\).  
Because Eq. (2) penalises deviations token‑wise, it implicitly encourages correct syntax, factual consistency, and style alignment wherever these aspects appear in the target data.

### Datasets

In practice SFT leverages **instruction‑following** corpora where humans write prompts together with desirable completions:
- Dialogues where an assistant response should be helpful,
- Summaries pairing longer texts with concise abstracts,
- Code generation pairs linking problem statements to correct programs,
- Sentiment‐controlled snippets where desired sentiment polarity is annotated.

Such datasets are considerably cheaper to obtain than exhaustive demonstrations for every possible task but still provide strong signals about “what good looks like”.  The article notes that RLHF pipelines typically source SFT data from “high–quality data for downstream task(s)” such as dialogue or summarisation before launching into preference learning.



---

## Effect of SFT on Downstream Tasks

The most immediate impact of SFT is **enhanced instruction following**: after fine‑tuning on human‐written responses, an LLM becomes markedly better at interpreting prompts that request assistance or summarisation rather than merely continuing text arbitrarily.  
Empirically this manifests as higher scores on benchmarks involving single‑turn dialogue (e.g., Anthropic Helpful & Harmless data), improved ROUGE scores for summarisation tasks sourced from Reddit TL;DR threads, or reduced incidence of undesirable behaviours such as generating disallowed content.

Additionally, SFT **conditions** the otherwise broad knowledge acquired during unsupervised pretraining toward specific behavioural norms:
- A coding assistant trained via SFT learns to *prefer* syntactically correct programs even though raw pretraining includes many buggy snippets.
- A sentiment control model tuned with SFT can bias generations toward positive polarity while preserving factuality thanks to its underlying world knowledge.
  
Crucially, SFT does **not** alter what knowledge resides inside the model—it merely reshapes how that knowledge is expressed under task constraints.  Consequently later stages like **Preference Learning** can operate on top of an already well‑aligned baseline rather than starting from scratch; this layered approach reduces drift away from the original distribution while still enabling controlled steering toward human preferences.

## Notation Used Throughout This Textbook

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| \(x\) | Input context or prompt (e.g., a user query or document prefix). |
| \(y = (y_1,\dots,y_T)\) | Completion generated by the model conditioned on \(x\). |
| \(\pi_\theta(y \mid x)\) | Autoregressive policy instantiated by a Transformer with parameters \(\theta\); probability of generating sequence \(y\) given prompt \(x\). |
| \(\pi^\text{ref}(y \mid x)\) | Reference policy used for regularisation; often set equal to the SFT model before preference optimisation (\(\pi^\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}\)). |
| \(r(x,y)\) | Latent reward function representing human preference for completion \(y\) under prompt \(x\). |
| \(\beta >0\) | Temperature–like coefficient controlling strength of KL regularisation in RLHF objectives (larger \(\beta\) permits greater deviation from reference). |
| \(\mathbb D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p\,||\,q]\) | Kullback–Leibler divergence measuring how much distribution \(p\) differs from distribution \(q.\) |
| \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\) | Logistic sigmoid function used in Bradley–Terry preference modeling. |
| \(Z(x)=\sum_y \pi^\text{ref}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\) | Partition function normalising exponentiated rewards when deriving optimal policies under KL constraints. |
| \(\mathcal D = \bigl[(x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)})\bigr]_{i=1}^N$ | Offline dataset of human preferences where \(y_w^{(i)}\succ y_l^{(i)}\,|\!|\,x^{(i)}.\)**|
| \(\mathcal L_R(r_\phi,\mathcal D)\),   \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi^\text{ref})$ | Losses defined respectively for reward modelling (Eq.~2 in paper) and Direct Preference Optimization (Eq.~4 in paper). |

When discussing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), we frequently encounter two nested optimisation problems:

1. **Reward Modelling:** Fit parameters $\phi$ so that $r_\phi$ reproduces observed preferences under a Bradley–Terry likelihood.
2. **Policy Optimisation:** Adjust $\theta$ so $\pi_\theta$ maximises expected reward while staying close to $\pi^\text{ref}$ according to KL divergence – traditionally via PPO but alternatively via Direct Preference Optimization using Eq.~4 above.

> **Key Takeaway:**  
> Modern large language models begin life as massive next‑token predictors trained on unlabelled text using Transformer architectures.  Their raw capabilities improve predictably with scale—a phenomenon captured by scaling laws—and occasionally give rise to new emergent behaviours once enough parameters and data are amassed.  By subsequently applying Supervised Fine‑Tuning on curated instruction sets we reshape these generic models into task‑aware assistants capable of following human commands safely and effectively.  This fine‐tuned policy serves as both **reference point** ($$\pi^\text{ref}$$) and foundation for later preference‐based alignment methods such as Direct Preference Optimization.

3. Human Preferences as Training Signals  

Large language models acquire broad world knowledge through unsupervised pre‑training, yet their raw generations reflect the uncontrolled mixture of goals present in the training corpus.  To endow such models with safe and helpful behavior practitioners attach **human preference signals** after the initial pre‑training phase.  In contemporary reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) pipelines this signal takes the form of *pairwise* judgments rather than absolute quality scores.  The following sections explain why comparative feedback is favored, how it is gathered at scale, what synthetic alternatives exist, how raw prompts are turned into labeled comparison triples, and which datasets currently serve as benchmarks for research on learning from preferences.

# Types of Feedback: Scalar Ratings vs Pairwise Comparisons  

Human evaluators can be asked either to assign a numeric **rating** \(s(x,y)\in \mathbb R\) to a single completion \(y\) given a prompt \(x\), or to indicate which of two presented completions is better according to some criterion (helpfulness, safety, relevance, …).  Scalar ratings appear attractive because they provide an immediate notion of “how good” an answer is, yet practical experience documented throughout the RLHF literature shows several obstacles.  First, judges must internally calibrate their rating scales across examples and across themselves—a process prone to drift and personal bias, especially when evaluations span multiple sessions or diverse annotators.  Second, numeric scores tend to have high variance; small differences in rating rarely convey reliable ordering information when many examples are considered jointly.

By contrast, **pairwise comparisons** ask respondents only a binary question—“which answer do you prefer?”—which drastically reduces cognitive load and eliminates the need for an external anchor point such as “zero represents neutral”.  Humans are reliably able to judge relative quality even when both answers are mediocre or both are excellent; this consistency is reflected mathematically by the Bradley–Terry (BT) formulation employed throughout modern preference‑learning work~\citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}.  Under BT the probability that a judge prefers answer \(y_{w}\) over \(y_{l}\) given context \(x\) follows  

\[
p^{*}\!\bigl(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\bigr)=
    \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)}
         {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\bigr)+
          \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\bigr)} ,
    \tag{\ref{eq:bradley-terry}}
\]  

where \(r^{*}\) denotes an unknown latent reward function mapping prompt–completion pairs onto real numbers.  
Because only *differences* \(r^{*}(x,y_{w})-r^{*}(x,y_{l})\) matter for predicting choices,
the absolute scale of \(r^{*}\) is irrelevant—a fact formalised later via Lemma 1 showing that any two rewards differing only by a prompt‑dependent constant induce identical preference distributions.
Consequently learning algorithms can treat each comparison as a binary classification problem with logistic link~$\sigma$, leading directly to the maximum‑likelihood loss  

\[
\mathcal L_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal D)=
   -\, \mathbb E_{(x,y_w ,y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
      \Bigl[\log\sigma
      \bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr],
    \tag{\ref{eq:reward_model}}
\]  

where $r_{\phi}$ denotes a parametric reward model fitted on a static set $\mathcal D$ of annotated triples.
The binary nature of this objective makes it straightforwardly optimizable with stochastic gradient descent and robust against calibration errors that would cripple scalar regression losses.



# Crowdsourcing Workflows for Preference Collection  

In practice the construction of $\mathcal D$ proceeds through an *offline crowdsourcing loop*.  
The starting point consists of a collection of textual prompts $x$, often drawn from domain‑specific corpora such as movie‑review prefixes~(\citep{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}), Reddit forum posts~(\citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}), or open‑ended user queries appearing in dialogue corpora (\citep{bai2022training}).  

A **reference language model**, typically denoted $\pi_{\text {ref}}$, supplies candidate completions for each prompt.  In most RLHF systems $\pi_{\text {ref}}$ is simply the supervised‑fine‑tuned (SFT) version $\pi^{\text {SFT}}$ obtained after initial instruction tuning~(\citep{ouyang2022training}).  For every chosen prompt $x$, two independent samples $(y_1 , y_2)$ are drawn independently from $\pi_{\text {ref}}(\cdot\,|\,x)$,
producing two plausible answers drawn from roughly the same distribution as any downstream generation task would encounter.

These candidate answers are presented side‑by‑side through a web interface designed for crowd workers
(e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk or bespoke annotation platforms).  
Annotators receive concise instructions stating which attribute(s) should guide their judgment—for example “Select the answer that most accurately addresses the user request while remaining polite”.  
Workers indicate whether $y_1$ or $y_2$ is preferred;
the system records both choices together with their originating prompt,
forming an ordered tuple $(x , y_{w} , y_{l})$
with $y_{w}$ denoting the chosen completion
and $y_{l}$ its counterpart.
To improve reliability Schmidt et al.\ have sometimes collected multiple independent judgments per tuple,
allowing noisy responses either to be resolved by majority vote or retained explicitly within probabilistic models;
however all published RLHF pipelines adopt at least one comparison per example because even single judgements capture strong ordinal information under Eq.~(\ref{eq:bradley-terry}).  

Once collected,
the raw annotations constitute precisely the data structure needed both for conventional reward modelling
(Eq.~(\ref{eq:reward_model})) 
and—via Direct Preference Optimization—
for direct policy inference without fitting an intermediate trainer branch.



# Synthetic Preference Generation Using Classifiers  

Human annotation remains costly when very large comparison corpora are required,
especially when fine‑tuning models spanning billions of parameters.
A pragmatic compromise consists in *synthetically* generating preferences through automatic classifiers whose predictions serve as proxy judges.
This approach appears explicitly in our controlled sentiment‐generation experiments,
where instead of asking humans whether response $y$ conveys positive affect,
we employ an off‑the‑shelf sentiment classifier trained on standard polarity datasets.
For every sampled prefix $x$, two continuations $y_{1}$ and $y_{2}$ are obtained from $\pi_{\text {ref}}$;
the classifier computes probabilities $p(\text {positive}\mid x , y_i)$,
and whichever continuation receives higher positivity probability becomes $y_{w}$ while its counterpart becomes $y_{l}$.
Formally

\[
p(\text {positive}\mid x , y_w ) > p(\text {positive}\mid x , y_l )
    \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
    y_w \succ y_l .
\]

Because modern classifiers attain high accuracy on domain‐relevant sentiment detection,
the resulting synthetic labels closely approximate true human rankings while being infinitely scalable—the supervision budget reduces essentially to compute time rather than paid labor.
Nevertheless researchers keep an eye on potential bias introduced by imperfect classifiers,
and frequently cross‑validate synthetic indices against small sets of genuine human judgments before deploying them at full scale.



# Prompt → Multiple Completions → Labeling Pipeline  

The full end‑to‑end recipe traditionally employed across RLHF studies can be described step by step:

1️⃣ **Prompt acquisition** – A repository $\mathcal P$ containing diverse input texts $x$ is assembled;
   sources range from benchmark corpora such as IMDB reviews,
   Reddit TL ;DR subreddit posts,
   up until richly annotated dialogue turns used in helpfulness studies (\citep{bai2022training}).  

2️⃣ **Reference generation** – For each prompt $x$, invoke the reference policy $\pi_{\text {ref}}$;
   draw at least two independent generations
   $y_{1},\,y_{2}\sim \pi_{\text {ref}}(\cdot|\,x)$.
   When computational budgets permit,
   more than two candidates may be sampled subsequently filtered down through random pairing,
   although most published pipelines retain exactly one pair per prompt because Eq.~(\ref{eq:DPO}) already leverages every observed ordering efficiently.  

3️⃣ **Preference elicitation** – As described above,
   either crowd workers observe $(y_{1},\,y_{2})$ together with their prompting context
   and indicate their preferred completion,
   or an automatic metric supplies a deterministic ranking.
   The outcome manifests formally as $(\, x,\; y_w,\; y_l\,)$ satisfying 
   $y_w \succ y_l|\, x$.  

4️⃣ **Dataset assembly** – All observed triples aggregate into 
   
   \[
   \mathcal D = 
     \bigl\{
        ( x^{(i)},\, y^{(i)}_w,\,
                 y^{(i)}_l ) 
     : i=1,\dots,N
     \bigr\},
   \tag{\hspace{-0pt}}
   \]
   
   wherein $N$ denotes total number of annotated comparisons.
   This static set serves dual purposes:
   *As input‐side information*, it defines an empirical estimation problem for any parametric model predicting preferences;
   *As downstream supervision*, it fuels either traditional reward modelling approaches—
     fitting $r_\phi$ through minimizing $\mathcal L_R$—
     or Direct Preference Optimization where one directly minimizes
  
   \[
    \mathcal L_{\mathrm DPO} (\pi_\theta ;\, 
        \pi_{\text {ref}})
        = -\, {\mathbb E}_{(x , y_w , y_l)\sim 
        {\mathcal D}}
        [\, 
        \log\sigma 
        (\beta 
               {\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_w|\, x)}
                            {\pi_{\mathrm ref}(y_w|\, x)}}
               -
               {\beta}
               {\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y_l|\, x)}
                            {\pi_{\mathrm ref}(y_l|\, x)}})
        ] .
      \tag{\ref{eq:DPO}}
    ]
   ] 

   where $\beta>0$ controls how aggressively new policies may deviate from $\pi_{\text {ref}}$
and where implicit rewards arise naturally via 
$\hat r_\theta(x , y)=
      β · {\log[\pi_\theta(y| {x}) / π_{\mathrm ref}(y| {x})]}.$  
   The loss therefore encourages higher log‑probability mass under $\pi_\theta$ 
   for preferably ranked completions while attenuating mass assigned their dispreferred counterparts—precisely what traditional RL objectives aim at—but without any inner reinforcement loop nor separate reward fitting stage.



# Dataset Statistics Commonly Used in RLHF Research  

Empirical studies have converged around three canonical collections whose statistics illuminate prevailing scales:

* **Anthropic Helpful–Harmless Dialogue Corpus** – As introduced by \citet{bai2022training},
  this resource comprises roughly $170\,000$ distinct dialogue turn triples—
  each consisting of a user query together with two assistant responses generated by a large language model,
  followed by a binary label indicating which response users deemed more helpful while remaining harmless.
  Because every entry already encodes $(x,\!y's…)$ format required by Equation (\ref{eq:DPO}),
  researchers may reuse it verbatim without performing additional sampling steps—providing immediately comparable benchmarks across papers employing Direct Preference Optimization or PPO‑based RLHF.\footnote{
    All methods discussed herein treat these entries uniformly as elements belonging	to	$\mathcal D$.}

* **Reddit TL ;DR Summarization Set** –
  The task dataset initially curated by Stiennon et al.~(\citeyear{stiennon2022learning}) couples forum posts ($x$) drawn from Reddit’s TL ;DR subreddit
  with crowdsourced rankings between competing abstractive summaries ($y_w$, $y_l$).
  While exact counts differ between publicly released splits,
  prior works repeatedly quote “tens of thousands” of labeled summary pairs—a magnitude sufficient for fitting high‑capacity transformer‐based reward models ($r_\phi$), yet modest enough that Direct Preference Optimization converges rapidly using standard minibatch stochastic optimization.\footnote{
    In Figure 4 our experiments employ this public set unchanged after tokenisation;} 

* **IMDb Sentiment Prefixes** –
  To evaluate fine tuning under controlled conditions researchers extract review beginnings from IMDB’s public movie‐review archive~(\citep{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}),
  couple these prefixes ($x$) with synthetic pairs generated via best‑of‐two sampling from an SFT decoder $\pi_{\mathrm ref}$,
  then let an off‑the‑shelf sentiment detector assign preferential labels rooted purely in positivity scores.
  Although this construction yields virtually limitless ($>10^5$ possible) comparison tuples,
  experimental protocols typically sample several thousand pairs so that statistical estimates reliably reflect underlying truth‐valued sentiment measure employed during evaluation.\footnote{
    Our implementations follow Appendix Section A regarding batch configuration;}

Collectively these three sources exemplify standard practice:
datasets usually contain anywhere between ten thousand up through several hundred thousand distinct triples;
coverage spans instructional prompting (\emph{summaries}), conversational assistance (\emph{dialogue}), and affective control (\emph{sentiment}).
Such scales suffice both for traditional REWARD learning---in which deep networks must infer latent score functions---and for Direct Preference Optimization---which learns policies directly whilst preserving identical statistical footing.



Together these components constitute the backbone upon which modern alignment methodologies rest.
By gathering *relative* judgements instead of absolute grades,
harnessing scalable crowdsourced pipelines or reliable automatic proxies,
and unifying those observations into neatly packaged $(prompt,\!preferred,\!dispreferred)$ triples,
researchers enjoy clear probabilistic semantics embodied by Bradley–Terry theory,
while simultaneously enabling efficient algorithms—including Direct Preference Optimization—that transform raw feedback directly into improved language‐model behaviour without costly reinforcement‐learning loops.

4. Modeling Preferences: Bradley‑Terry and Plackett‑Luce  

In contemporary alignment pipelines—most notably reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)—the only signal that tells a language model what it should prefer comes in the form of **pairwise** or **ranked** comparisons supplied by humans.  To turn these discrete judgments into a quantity that can be optimized by gradient‑based methods we need a probabilistic model that maps any two (or more) completions into a likelihood of being preferred.  The two most widely used models in the RLHF literature are the **Bradley‑Terry** (BT) model and its generalization, the **Plackett‑Luce** (PL) model.  Both belong to the exponential family of discrete choice models and share a simple intuition: each response is assigned a latent “utility’’ or “reward’’ value, and the probability of being selected depends monotonically on the difference (or ratio) of these utilities.  This chapter derives the BT formulation from first principles, sketches its PL extension, discusses why identifiability must be enforced, shows how maximum‑likelihood training reduces to a binary cross‑entropy loss, and finally interprets the learned reward differences as latent utilities underlying human preferences.

# Bradley‑Terry Model Derivation and Logistic Link to Reward Differences  

Suppose a prompt \(x\) is presented to a language model and it produces two candidate completions \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\).  Human annotators observe both candidates and indicate which one they consider better; we denote this event by \(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\), where \(y_{w}\) (“winner’’) is preferred over \(y_{l}\) (“loser’’).  The BT model postulates that there exists an **unknown** scalar reward function  
\[
r^{*}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R},
\]  
capturing how desirable response \(y\) is for context \(x\).  Given this hidden reward, the probability that a human prefers \(y_{1}\) over \(y_{2}\) follows a softmax over exponentiated rewards:
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
= 
\frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
      {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{1}
\]
Equation (1) is precisely the Bradley‑Terry formulation originally introduced for paired comparisons in psychometrics~\citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}.  

A convenient re‑expression uses the logistic function \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\).  By factoring \(\exp(r^{*}(x,y_{2}))\) from numerator and denominator,
\[
p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x)
= 
\sigma\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})-r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr).
\tag{2}
\]
Thus **the probability of preferring one completion over another is exactly the sigmoid of their reward difference**.  This observation yields an immediate intuition: if two responses have identical rewards their preference odds are 50/50; if one response’s reward exceeds its competitor’s by one “logit unit’’ (\(\Delta r = 1\)), it will be chosen roughly \(73\%\) of the time; larger gaps yield probabilities converging rapidly toward certainty.

The BT model’s simplicity makes it ideal for RLHF where data consists almost exclusively of binary judgments.  The underlying assumption—that humans behave as noisy maximizers of an additive latent utility—is an abstraction, yet empirical work in language model alignment has repeatedly validated its adequacy~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning,stiennon2022learning}.


# Plackett‑Luce Extension to Rankings Beyond Two Items  

While many RLHF datasets contain only pairwise comparisons, some collection processes elicit **rankings** of three or more candidates—e.g., “order these three generated answers from best to worst.”  The Bradley‑Terry model does not directly accommodate such ordered lists because it defines probabilities only for binary outcomes.  

The **Plackett‑Luce** (PL) model generalizes BT by assigning each candidate an exponentiated utility \(\exp(r^{*}(x,y))\) exactly as before but then sampling an entire ranking sequentially without replacement~\citep{plackett1975analysis}.  Concretely, given a set \(\mathcal{Y}=\{y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(K)}\}\), PL defines:
\[
p^{*}\bigl(y^{(1)} \succ y^{(2)} \succ \dots \succ y^{(K)} \mid x\bigr)
=
\prod_{k=1}^{K}
%
\frac{\exp(r^{*}(x,y^{(k)}))}
     {\sum_{\ell=k}^{K} \exp(r^{*}(x,y^{(\ell)}))}.
\tag{3}
\]
The factor for position \(k\) expresses exactly the same Bradley‑Terry logistic over the remaining items at that step; therefore BT appears as the special case \(K=2\).  

In practice RLHF pipelines rarely possess full rankings because gathering them is more expensive than pairwise labeling.  Nonetheless, when such data exist—as might happen when annotators are asked to rank multiple generations—or when synthetic augmentations produce multi‑way choices—the PL formulation offers a principled way to incorporate them without altering any downstream derivations shown later (e.g., the change‑of‑variables step used by Direct Preference Optimization).  


# Identifiability Issues and Common Normalization Tricks  

Both BT and PL enjoy an important **invariance property**: adding an arbitrary function \(f(x)\) that depends only on the prompt—not on the completion—to every reward leaves all preference probabilities unchanged.  Formally,
\[
r'(x,y)=r(x,y)+f(x)
%
\;\Longrightarrow\;
p^{*}_{r'}(y_{i}\succ y_{j}\mid x)=p^{*}_{r}(y_{i}\succ y_{j}\mid x).
\tag{4}
\]
This follows directly from Equation (2); every term contains \(r(x,\cdot)\) inside a sigmoid after subtracting two rewards, so any common shift cancels out.  

Consequences are twofold:

1. **Under‑specification**—the data cannot uniquely identify absolute reward levels.
2. **Optimization ambiguity**—gradient‑based methods may wander arbitrarily unless we anchor the scale or location of rewards.

A widely adopted remedy in RLHF literature is to impose an explicit **normalization constraint** on each prompt’s rewards:
\[
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \mathcal{D}_{x}}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x,y)\bigr]=0,
%
\qquad \text{for all } x,
\tag{5}
\]
where \(\mathcal{D}_{x}\) denotes all responses observed with prompt \(x.\) In practice this amounts simply to subtracting the empirical mean reward per prompt after each gradient step or initializing the final linear head with zero bias.\footnote{
The cited works often phrase this as “centering” or “zero‑mean” regularization.
}  

Other possibilities—such as fixing one reference completion’s reward to zero or penalizing large variance—are equally valid so long as they break the additive symmetry without altering pairwise differences.


# Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Reward Models (Binary Cross‑Entropy Loss)  

Given a static dataset of human preferences
\[
%
\mathcal{D}
=
%
%\left\\{
%
    \Bigl(x^{(i)},\,y_w^{(i)},\,y_l^{(i)}\Bigr)
%
%\right\\}_{i=1}^{N},
%
,
%
where each triple records which of two completions was judged better,
we can fit a parametric reward function \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) by maximizing its likelihood under the BT model~(2).  
The negative log‑likelihood---the loss minimized during training---is therefore:
%
\[
%
\mathcal L_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal D)
=
-
\,%
\E_{(x,y_w , y_l)\sim \mathcal D}
%
     \bigl[
        \log
        \sigma
        \bigl(
            r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)
        \bigr)
     \bigr].
%
   % 
   \tag{6}
%
   ]
%
Equation (6) has precisely the form of a **binary cross‑entropy loss**, where each preference pair yields a pseudo label “winner = 1”.  Under stochastic gradient descent one samples minibatches of pairs, computes logits 
\(d_{\phi}^{(i)}= r_{\phi}(x^{(i)},y_w^{(i)} ) - r_{\phi}(x^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)} ) , 
$
passes them through $\sigma$, evaluates $\log\sigma(d)$, and backpropagates w.r.t.\ $\phi$.  

The fact that this loss coincides with standard classification objectives makes implementation trivial: modern deep learning libraries already provide highly optimized binary cross‑entropy routines.  In RLHF pipelines this step constitutes what is often called **reward modelling**, i.e., learning $r_{\phi}$ so that it aligns with human judgments.


# Interpretation of Reward Differences as Latent Utility  

Even though $r_{\phi}$ is called a “reward”, mathematically it plays exactly the role of **latent utility**—an unobserved scalar score that drives human choice according to Equation (2).  Several insights follow:

* **Difference matters:** $r_{\phi}(x,y_w)-r_{\phi}(x,y_l)$ quantifies how much better $y_w$ is perceived compared with $y_l$ for prompt $x$.  
* **Scale matters but not offset:** Multiplying all rewards by a constant $\alpha>0$ sharpens or smooths preferences because $\sigma(\alpha\,d)$ becomes steeper or flatter; conversely adding an $f(x)$ does not affect preferences because it cancels out.
* **Link to policy updates:** Direct Preference Optimization later reinterprets this difference as an *implicit* policy advantage:
  $
    \hat r_\theta(x,y)=
    \beta
    \log
    {\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}} ,
  $
so that updating $\pi_\theta$ toward preferred completions amounts precisely to increasing estimated latent utility.
* **Probabilistic meaning:** Under BT,
$
    p(y_w\rvert y_l,x)=
    P(\text{choose } y_w)=
    P(\text{utility}(y_w)> \text{utility}(y_l))
$
when utilities are perturbed by i.i.d.\ Gumbel noise—a classic random­utility interpretation originally due to McFadden~[1974] but implicitly used throughout RLHF work.



Collectively these five subtopics provide a complete mathematical foundation for turning raw human preference data into trainable signals for large language models.  By fitting $r_{\phi}$ through binary cross‑entropy under either BT or PL assumptions—and by acknowledging identifiability constraints—we obtain a well-defined latent utility space which later algorithms such as Direct Preference Optimization can exploit directly without ever performing explicit reinforcement learning phases.
