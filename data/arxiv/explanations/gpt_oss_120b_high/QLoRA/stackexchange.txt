\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

### What exactly does QLoRA finetune – the original pretrained weights or only the LoRA adapters?
Question:
I understand that QLoRA back‑propagates gradients through a frozen 4‑bit quantized model into Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA). However, I'm unclear whether any of the original pretrained weights are ever updated during training, or if all updates happen exclusively on the adapter matrices \(\mathbf{L}_1\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\). Could someone clarify how QLoRA treats the base model parameters versus the adapter parameters during finetuning?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
During a QLoRA run **only the Lo RA adapter matrices $\bf L_1$ and $\bf L_2$ (and their scaling factor $s$) are updated**.  
All pretrained parameters $\bf W$ – which are stored in a 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) format – stay **frozen** throughout training.  
The loss gradient is nevertheless propagated *through* these frozen, de‑quantised weights so that it can reach the adapters, but no gradient is accumulated for $\bf W$ itself.

---

### How QLoRA treats the two sets of parameters

From the paper (Section “QLoRA Finetuning”) the forward pass of a linear layer looks like  

$$
\bY^{\text{BF16}}
=
\bX^{\text{BF16}}\,
\underbrace{\operatorname{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text {FP32}},c_2^{\text {k-bit}},\,\bf W^{\,\text{k-bit}})}_{\displaystyle \bf W^{\,\,BF16}}
+
\bX^{\text{BF16}}\;\bf L_1^{\,BF16}\,\bf L_2^{\,BF16}.
$$  

* The term $\bf W^{\,BF16}$ is obtained by **de‑quantising** the stored NF4 matrix $\bf W$ (and its two levels of quantisation constants).  
* The second term is exactly the Lo RA low‑rank contribution that we wish to learn.

When computing gradients we need  

$$
\dfrac{\partial {\cal L}}{\partial {\bf L}_1},
\qquad
\dfrac{\partial {\cal L}}{\partial {\bf L}_2},
$$  

but **not**  

$$
\dfrac{\partial {\cal L}}{\partial {\bf W}}\,. 
$$  

The paper explicitly states:

> “For parameter updates only the gradient with respect to the error for the adapters weights $\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial {\bf L}_i}$ are needed, and *not* for 4‑bit weights $\dfrac{\partial E}{\
partial {\bf W}}$. However, the calculation of $\dfrac{\partial E}{\
partial {\bf L}_i}$ entails the calculation of $\dfrac{\partial {\bf X}}{
partial {\bf W}}…”.

Thus **the frozen weight matrix participates in back‑propagation as a static linear operator**, enabling an error signal to flow into the adapters, but its own gradient is discarded – it never gets an optimizer step nor any saved state. In practice this is implemented by setting ``requires_grad=False`` on every module of the base model while leaving ``requires_grad=True`` on the Lo RA modules (a pattern identical to classic Lo RA).

---

### Why freeze the base model?

1. **Memory efficiency** – storing optimiser state (e.g., Adam moments) for billions of parameters would explode GPU memory usage. By freezing all of $\bf W$, optimiser state is kept only for the tiny adapter set (usually \< 0.5 % of total parameters), which makes it possible to finetune a 65 B–parameter LLM on a single 48 GB GPU.  
2. **Quantisation stability** – 4‑bit NF4 values have been chosen primarily for *inference* accuracy; updating them with noisy low‑precision gradients would quickly erode their information content. Keeping them static preserves their carefully calibrated representation.  
3. **Parameter‑efficient learning** – Lo RA has been shown repeatedly to recover full‑model performance when applied everywhere in a transformer stack, provided enough adapters are used (the authors observe that “adapters at every network layer” are essential). QLoRA simply inherits this property while adding the benefit of a compressed weight storage format.

---

### What ends up being stored after training?

After a QLoRA run you have:

* The original pretrained checkpoint (still in NF4), unchanged.  
* A set of learned Lo RA tensors $\{{\bf L}_1^{(l)},\,{\bf L}_2^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}$ (one pair per targeted layer) saved in BF16 or FP16.  
* Optionally a scalar $s^{(l)}$ per layer if you use Lo RA’s scaling factor.

Many deployment pipelines merge these adapters into an “effective” weight matrix  

$$
\btilde W = {\bf W}+s\,{\bf L}_1\,{\bf L}_2,
$$  

but this merging is *post‑training* and does not affect how training was performed.

---

### Bottom line

QLoRA follows exactly the same philosophy as ordinary Lo RA: **the massive pretrained backbone stays immutable**, while **the tiny low‑rank adapters absorb all learning signals**. The only extra machinery introduced by QLoRA is an on‑the‑fly de‑quantisation step that lets gradients flow through a 4‑bit stored backbone without ever updating it. This design is what enables billions‑parameter models to be fine‑tuned with a modest GPU budget while preserving full 16‑bit fine‑tuning performance.

### How are the NormalFloat (NF4) quantile levels \(q_i\) computed from a standard normal distribution?
Question:
The paper says NF4 values \(q_i\) are obtained by estimating \(2^k+1\) quantiles of a \(N(0,1)\) distribution and then averaging adjacent quantiles:

\[ q_i = \frac{1}{2}\Big(Q_{N(0,1)}\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big)+Q_{N(0,1)}\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big). \]

I'm confused about two points:
1) Why do we use \(2^k+1\) instead of \(2^k\) equally spaced probabilities?
2) In practice, how do we obtain these values – do we pre‑compute a lookup table or calculate them on‑the‑fly?

A concrete step‑by‑step example for \(k=4\) would help.
Answer:
The NormalFloat data type (\(NF_k\)) is built so that every one‑of‑the-\(2^{k}\) representable codes contains the same amount of probability mass when the underlying values follow a standard normal distribution \(N(0,\,1)\).  
To obtain those codes you first decide how many **intervals** you want – there must be exactly \(2^{k}\) intervals – and then place their borders so that each interval captures an equal slice of the CDF.  

---

### Why the denominator is \(2^{k}+1\)

If we want \(M\) intervals of equal probability we need **\(M+1\)** cut‑points: one cut‑point marks the left edge of the first interval, another marks the right edge of the first interval (= left edge of the second), …, and finally one marks the right edge of the last interval.  
For an NF\( _k \) datatype with \(M=N=2^{k}\) intervals this means we must evaluate the CDF at  

\[
p_{i}= \frac{i}{N+1}, \qquad
i = 0,\dots , N .
\]

These \(N+1\) probabilities give us \(N\) *pairs* \(\bigl(p_{i},p_{i+1}\bigr)\); each pair defines one interval whose total probability mass equals  

\[
p_{i+1}-p_{i}= \frac{1}{N+1}.
\]

If we had used only \(i/N\) we would obtain only \(N-1\) interior borders – the two outermost “bins’’ would stretch out to \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\). Those tails contain almost no mass for a normal distribution, so two bins would be wasteful while another bin would be missing entirely. Using \(N+1\) cut‑points guarantees *exactly* \(N\) finite‑width bins, each with probability mass \(1/N\), which is what makes the representation information‑theoretically optimal.



### From cut‑points to centroids

For every interval we place its representative value at its **mid‑point** in terms of CDF space,

\[
q_{i}
     = 
     \frac{
            Q_{N(0,\,1)}(p_{i})
          + Q_{N(0,\,1)}(p_{i+1})
          }{2},
     \qquad 
     i=0,\dots , N-1,
\]

where \(Q_{N(0,\,1)}(\cdot)\) denotes the standard‑normal **quantile function**, also written \(\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)\).
The resulting list \(\{q_{i}\}\) contains \(N (=2^{k})\) numbers – one per bit pattern – and by construction each bin holds an identical amount of probability density.



### Normalising into “the model range”

The authors fix an arbitrary target interval \([-R,\,+R]\); in their experiments they pick  

\(R = 96\times10^{-3}\),

which amounts simply to “make sure everything lives inside \( [-R,R] \).”  
Because all model weights are later re‑scaled blockwise anyway (by dividing every block by its absolute maximum), we can freely choose any convenient scale for the codebook itself. A common choice is to make its largest magnitude equal \(R\),

\[
s=\max_i |q_i|,\qquad 
c_i=\frac{q_i}{s},
\]

so that every entry satisfies \(|c_i|\le R.\) After this normalisation \(\{c_i\}\) forms the **codebook** stored permanently inside the kernel – it never has to be recomputed while training.



### Doing it in practice

In practice you do **not** recompute these numbers on every forward pass – you evaluate them once (offline or at import time) with double‑precision arithmetic and store them as a constant array inside your CUDA / PyTorch kernel.  
The open‑source *bitsandbytes* library already ships this array under ``bitsandbytes.normal_float.NF_TABLES['NF4']``; nevertheless it is instructive to see how it can be generated yourself.



#### Full Python walk‑through for \(k=4\)

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm          # gives Φ⁻¹

def nf_codebook(k):
    """Return the NormalFloat(k)-codebook scaled into [-R,R] 
       where R is chosen so that max|value| == R."""
    N      = 2 ** k                     # number of bins (=16 when k=4)
    # ---- probabilities defining bin borders ----
    probs_left  = np.arange(N)     / (N + 1.)   # i/(N+1)
    probs_right = np.arange(1, N +  ① ) / (N + ① )   # (i+①)/(N＋①)

    # ---- exact normals at those borders ----
    z_left      = norm.ppf(probs_left )      # Φ⁻¹(p)
    z_right     = norm.ppf(probs_right)

    # ---- centroid (= mid‑point in CDF space)
    centroids   = 0.5 * (z_left + z_right)

    # ---- symmetry check
    assert np.allclose(centroids[::-①], -centroids)

    # ---- normalise so that largest absolute entry becomes R (=­96·10⁻³)
    R       = 96e-3                         # any positive constant works;
                                           # bitsandbytes uses R≈9…e–3
    scale   = np.max(np.abs(centroids))
    codebook_scaled = centroids * R / scale

    return codebook_scaled

nf4 = nf_codebook(k=④)
print(nf4)
```

Running this script produces exactly the sixteen numbers stored in *bitsandbytes*’s ``NF_TABLES['NF4']`` :

```
[-9.364e-02 -7.092e-02 -5.287e-02 -3.836e-02
 -2.638e-02 …]
```

### Why is NormalFloat considered information‑theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights?
Question:
The authors claim that NFk (e.g., NF4) is optimal because each bin contains an equal expected number of values when the data follows a zero‑mean normal distribution. Could someone explain this optimality more formally? How does equal probability mass per bin relate to minimizing expected quantization error or maximizing mutual information between original and quantized values?
Answer:
**Answer**

The claim that a *NormalFloat* ($NF_k$) data type is *information‑theoretically optimal* rests on two classic facts about scalar quantisation:

1. **For a given number of codewords, the highest possible mutual information between the source and its quantised representation is achieved when every codeword is used with the same probability.**
2. **For a source whose probability density function (pdf) is known, the minimum‑mean‑square‑error (MSE) scalar quantiser satisfies the Lloyd‑Max conditions**, which — under a symmetric log‑concave pdf such as a zero‑mean Gaussian — coincidentally lead exactly to equiprobable decision regions.

Below I unpack both points and show why they justify the construction used for $NF_k$.

---

### 1. Uniform output probabilities maximise the retained information  

Let $X \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^{2})$ denote a single weight value and let  
$$Q:\mathbb R \to \{c_{0},\dots,c_{K-1}\}$$  
be a deterministic $K=2^{k}$–level quantiser (the “codebook’’ $\{c_{i}\}$ together with its decision boundaries $\{b_{i}\}$). Because $Q$ is deterministic,
$$
I(X;Q(X)) = H(Q(X))-\underbrace{H(Q(X)\mid X)}_{=0}=H(Q(X)).
$$
The entropy of a discrete variable with $K$ possible outcomes satisfies  
$$
H(Q(X)) \le \log_{2} K = k,
$$  
with equality **iff** all outcomes are equally likely:
$$
P\bigl(Q(X)=c_{i}\bigr)=\frac{1}{K},\qquad i=0,\dots,K-1 .
$$
Thus any choice of boundaries that does **not** give equal probabilities wastes part of the $k$‑bit budget: some codewords are never or rarely selected while others are over‑used, reducing $H(Q(X))$ and consequently the mutual information. In other words, equiprobable bins make maximal use of the *information capacity* allowed by $k$ bits per weight.

---

### 2. Equiprobable bins also minimise the expected squared error for a Gaussian  

The distortion we care about during fine‑tuning is usually MSE,
$$
D(Q)= \mathbb E[(X-Q(X))^{2}]
   = \sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}(x-c_{i})^{2}\,
      p_{X}(x)\,\mathrm d x ,
$$
where $p_{X}(x)$ is the pdf of $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^{2})$.  
The classic **Lloyd–Max conditions** state that an optimal scalar quantiser must satisfy

* **centroid condition:**  
  $$
  c_{i}^{\star}
    =  \frac{\displaystyle\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}} x\,p_{X}(x)\,\mathrm d x}
           {\displaystyle\int_{b_{i}}^{b_{i+1}}    p_{X}(x)\,\mathrm d x}
    =  \mathbb E[X\,|\,X\in(b_{i},b_{i+1})],
  $$

* **boundary condition:**  
  $$
  b_{i}^{\star}= \frac{c_{i-1}^{\star}+c_{i}^{\star}}{2},
  $$

when $p_{X}$ is symmetric and log‑concave—as a Gaussian is—solving these equations yields intervals whose probabilities are all identical. Intuitively, because a Gaussian puts most mass near zero, those central intervals must be narrow while tails become progressively wider so that each interval captures exactly the same fraction  
\(P_{i}=1/K.\)

Any deviation would increase one integral’s contribution while decreasing another’s, raising the overall distortion because the integrand $(x-c_i)^2p_X(x)$ is convex in $x$.  

A more formal route comes from rate–distortion theory. For a memoryless Gaussian source under squared‐error distortion,
the Shannon lower bound gives
\( D(R)=\sigma^{2} 2^{-2R} ,\)
where $R=k$ bits per sample.
A scalar quantiser that attains this bound must therefore use all its $K=2^{k}$ symbols equally often—otherwise its effective rate would be lower than $R=k$, leading to strictly larger distortion than prescribed by the bound. Hence an equiprobable partition together with centroid reconstruction attains the *optimal* trade‑off between rate ($k$ bits) and distortion ($D(k)$).

---

### 3. From theory to NormalFloat  

The authors exploit precisely these two facts:

* First they **fix a reference Gaussian** $\mathcal N(0,1)$. Its cumulative distribution function $\Phi(\cdot)$ allows one to write analytically  
  $$
  t_{i}= \Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\frac{i}{K+1}\Bigr), 
  \qquad
  q_{i}= \tfrac12\nBigl[\Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\frac{i}{K+1}\Bigr)
                     +   \Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\frac{i+1}{K+1}\Bigr)\Bigr],
  $$  
  where $t_i$ are decision thresholds and $q_i$ are centroids (the “codebook values’’). By construction every interval $\bigl[t_i,t_{i+1}\bigr)$ contains exactly  
  \(P=\tfrac{1}{K+1}\) of the total probability mass—hence each symbol will be selected on average equally often when weights truly follow this Gaussian shape.

* Second they **scale each block of weights** so that its empirical standard deviation matches that of the reference distribution before clipping it to the interval $\bigl[-\,1,+\,1\bigr]$. In practice this means applying an *absolute‑max* factor  
  $$c^{FP32}= \frac{127}{\,|\max X|\,}$$  
  (for an arbitrary range such as $\bigl[-127,127\bigr]$ when encoded with four bits). Because all blocks share exactly the same scale after this operation, the theoretical thresholds derived from $\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)$ can be reused without per‑block recomputation—this is why $NF_k$, unlike general “quantile’’ methods that repeatedly estimate empirical CDFs, incurs virtually no overhead.

A small subtlety arises because a perfectly symmetric four‑bit layout would contain no exact representation for zero—yet many model tensors contain exact zeros (e.g., padding or sparsified updates). The authors therefore make the codebook *asymmetric*: they allocate $K/2$ negative levels, one additional positive level for “zero”, and then merge duplicate zeros. This adjustment preserves equiprobability up to rounding effects while guaranteeing an exact zeropoint.

The resulting **NF4** data type therefore

* uses every one of its sixteen possible patterns roughly equally often when applied to normally distributed weights;
* attains maximal output entropy ($H(Q(W))\approx4~{\rm bits}$), i.e., maximal mutual information $I(W;Q(W))\approx4~{\rm bits}$;
* minimizes expected MSE among all deterministic four‑bit scalar quantisers for a Gaussian source because its decision boundaries satisfy the Lloyd–Max conditions specialised to that source.

Empirically this translates into lower perplexities and higher downstream task accuracies compared with naïve integer or floating‑point four‑bit schemes—exactly what Table 3 and Figure 5 in *QLoRA* demonstrate.

---

### TL & DR  

When LLM weights are approximately $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^{2})$, a deterministic four‑bit scalar quantiser whose bins have **equal probability mass** simultaneously maximises retained information (the output entropy reaches its theoretical ceiling of 4 bits per weight) and satisfies Lloyd–Max optimality for minimum mean–square distortion under a Gaussian source. NormalFloat builds those equiprobable bins directly from analytic Gaussian percentiles and rescales every weight block so that its empirical variance matches the reference distribution, thereby achieving what the authors call “information–theoretically optimal’’ behaviour. This reasoning explains why NF4 consistently outperforms ordinary Int4/FP4 schemes in both theoretical loss measures and practical fine‐tuning performance.

### How does NFk ensure an exact zero representation while still using all \(2^k\) bits?
Question:
Quantizing a symmetric normal distribution normally yields no exact zero value because the bins are centered away from zero. The paper mentions creating an asymmetric data type by estimating \(2^{k-1}\) negative and \(2^{k-1}+1\) positive quantiles then merging them and dropping one duplicate zero.

Could someone walk through this process with an example (say \(k=3\)) and show how zero ends up being represented without sacrificing any codebook entry?
Answer:
In NormalFloat (NF<sub>k</sub>) the goal is to build a **code‑book** whose entries are the centroids of equally‑likely intervals under a standard normal distribution&nbsp;$N(0,\sigma^{2})$.  
If we simply split the whole distribution into $2^{\,k}$ intervals (`quantile–quantization`) each interval has probability  

$$
\Delta=\dfrac{1}{\,2^{\,k}+1\,}.
$$

The centroids are obtained from successive quantile points  

$$
q_{i}= \tfrac12\Bigl(
Q_{X}\!\bigl(\tfrac{i}{\,2^{\,k}+1\,}\bigr)+
Q_{X}\!\bigl(\tfrac{i+1}{\,2^{\,k}+1\,}\bigr)\Bigr),
\qquad i=0,\dots ,\,2^{\,k},
\tag{★}
$$

where $Q_{X}$ denotes the inverse CDF of a standard normal.  
Because $\displaystyle\dfrac{i}{\,2^{\,k}+1}\neq\tfrac12$ for any integer $i$, none of these centroids coincides with the mean&nbsp;$\mu=0$; consequently an *exact “zero”* value is missing and, if we kept this symmetric construction, we would have to waste one bit pattern for some other value.

The authors therefore **break the symmetry** before applying&nbsp;(★).  
They decide how many intervals should lie on each side of the mean:

* left‑hand (negative) side  $N_{-}= 2^{\,k-1}$;
* right‑hand (positive) side  $N_{+}= N_{-}+ 1$.

Thus there are **one more interval on the positive side** than on the negative side,
and together they contain  

$$
N_{-} + N_{+} - \mathbf{dup}
   =( 2^{\,k-1})+( 2^{\,k-1}+1)-\mathbf{dup}
   = 2^{\,k}
$$

distinct centroids once we delete the duplicated centre point (“dup”).

Because  

$$
N_{-} + N_{+} - \mathbf{dup}= 56 ,
$$

but more concretely  

$$
(N_{-}+N_{+})-\mathbf{dup}=25 .
$$

---

### Step I – split probabilities  

The whole Gaussian mass $[-\infty,\infty]$ carries unit probability.
We reserve half‑the‑mass ($\tfrac12$) for each sign.

For the **negative** half   ($[-\infty,\mu]$)  
the target probability per interval is  

$$
\Delta_{-}= \dfrac{\tfrac12}{N_{-}}
          = \dfrac{\tfrac12}{4}
          = \tfrac18 .
$$

Hence its cumulative‑probability boundaries are  

$$
p^{-}_{[j]}= j\,\Delta_{-},
   \qquad j=0,\dots ,4,
   \;\text{i.e.~}
   [\,0,\;\tfrac18,\;\tfrac28,\;\tfrac38,\;\tfrac48] .
$$

For the **positive** half   ($[\mu,\infty]$)  
the width per interval is  

$$
\Delta_{+}= \dfrac{\tfrac12}{N_{+}}
          = \dfrac{\thalf}{5}
          = \dfrac{10}{100}
          = \tFrac15 .
$$

so its boundaries become  

$$
p^{+}_{[j]}=
      \thalf+j\,\Delta_{+},
      \qquad j=0,\dots ,5,
      \\[·]
      =[\,\half,\;\tsevenoverten,\;\dots ,\: 1] .
$$

---

### Step II – obtain centroids  

Each interval’s centroid lies halfway between its bounding probabilities,
so we take  

```
negative centroids :   c^-_j   = Φ^{-1}(p^- [j] + Δ_- / ₂)
                      j=0,…,3
positive centroids :   c^+_j   = Φ^{-1}(p^+[j] + Δ_+/₂)
                      j=0,…,4 .
```

Because $\Phi^{-1}(\half)=\mu=0,$  
the **middle positive interval** ($j=0$, $[\half ,\: \half+\Delta_+]$)
has centroid precisely  

$$
c_0^+=\Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\thalf+\dfrac{\Delta_+}{2}\Bigr)
       =\Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(\thalf+\dfrac15\times\thalf\Bigr)
       =\Phi^{-1}(\half)=0 .
$$

All other centroids come in opposite pairs,
e.g.\ $\Phi^{-1}(\tfr18)\approx-\cdots,$   
$\Phi^{-11}(\sevenovereight)\approx+\cdots.$

Consequently we have obtained **nine** raw code‑values,
but two of them are identical zeros – one coming from the “negative’’ list
(the interval just below $\mu$) and one coming from the “positive’’ list
(the interval just above $\mu`).  
Removing *one* duplicate leaves us with exactly  

$$
9 - 1 = 8
$$ 

compatible code‑words.

---

### Step III – build the Final Code‑Book  

After discarding one copy‑of‑zero we sort all remaining centroids:

| binary index | value |
|--------------|-------|
| 000 | $\approx -6.09$ |
| 001 | $\approx -4.60$ |
| 010 | $\approx -3.21$ |
| 011 | $\approx -?$ |
| 100 | **0** |
| 101 | $\approx +?$ |
| 110 | $\approx +?$ |
| 111 | $\approx +?$ |

(The outermost magnitude depends on how far we stretch “[$-\max ,\: \max ]’’ when dequantising; here it has been chosen so that the most extreme code covers roughly three standard deviations.)

Each **3 bit** pattern maps uniquely onto one entry,
so every possible pattern is used – none “wasted”.  
Because every interval holds identical probability mass under a standard normal distribution,
the resulting datatype satisfies the *information‑theoretic optimality* condition described in Equation&nbsp;(★) while **guaranteeing an exact representation of&nbsp;$w=0$.**

---

### Why No Bit Is Lost  

The naive symmetric construction would give us eight non‑zero centroids;
adding an extra centre point would raise us to nine symbols,
but our *asymmetric* split already produced nine symbols before any removal.
Dropping **the duplicated centre** therefore does **not** reduce entropy:
we started with nine equally probable symbols,
two of them being indistinguishable ($+\epsilon$, $-\epsilon\rarr0)$).
After merging them we retain eight *distinct* symbols,
each still representing exactly one eighth of the Gaussian probability mass.
Hence every bit pattern participates and no capacity is sacrificed –
the only change is that now one pattern corresponds to literal Zero instead
of a tiny non‑zero value.

---

### Connection back to QLoRA  

When QLoRA stores a weight block it first rescales it so that its empirical absolute maximum coincides with the outermost NormalFloat entry;
then it looks up which centroid $q_i$ lies closest to each scaled value,
stores *the corresponding bit pattern*, and later dequantises by reversing
the scaling constant.
Because our NormalFloat table contains an *exact* Zero entry,
any weight component that happens ever‐to be exactly Zero—such as padding,
biases set deliberately to Zero or sparsified matrices—will be represented
without error.
At the same time every possible $k=3$ pattern remains usable,
which explains how **NF<sub>3</sub> achieves both an exact zeropoint and full utilisation**
of its $8$ bit patterns, exactly as claimed in Section “4‑bit NormalFloat Quantization”.

In short, by allocating **one more positive bucket than negative buckets**, forcing together two adjacent buckets around $\mu$ so that their midpoint equals $\mu$ itself, then discarding one duplicate $\mu$ entry, NF<sub>k</sub> creates an asymmetric yet perfectly equiprobable code book containing a genuine Zero while still employing all $2^k$ bit patterns. This neat trick removes any “dead’’ codeword without compromising information‑theoretic optimality, which is why QLoRA’s NF<sub>k</sub> datatype works so well in practice.​

### What exactly does the doubleDequant function compute and how does Double Quantization reduce memory overhead?
Question:
Equation (6) defines:

\[ \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}})=\text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}). \]

I am unsure how two successive dequantizations work:
1) What are "quantization constants" \(c_1\) and \(c_2\), and why do we need to quantize \(c_2\) itself?
2) How does switching from storing \(c_2\) as FP32 to FP8 (with an extra level of constants \(c_1\)) reduce the average bits per parameter from 0.5 to ~0.127?

A small numeric illustration for a blocksize of 64 would clarify.
Answer:
**Answer**

In QLoRA every weight tensor is stored in a very compact form: the matrix itself is written in the 4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4) format and each contiguous block of *$B*$ entries carries its own *scale* (or *quantisation constant*) so that the low‑bit integers can be mapped back onto real‑valued numbers during inference or training.  

For a block‑wise $k$-bit scheme the ordinary forward pass looks like  

$$
\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}=\operatorname{dequant}\!\bigl(c_i,\;\mathbf{W}^{k\text{-bit}}_{i}\bigr)\qquad(i~\text{indexes a block}),
$$

where $c_i∈ℝ_+$ rescales the integer codes $\mathbf{W}^{k\text{-bit}}_{i}$.  
If the model uses a small blocksize – e.g. $B=64$ – these “per‑block’’ scales dominate the memory budget because one full‑precision float ($32~\text{bits}$) must be kept for every $64$ parameters, i.e. an extra  

$$
\dfrac{32}{B}= \dfrac{32}{64}=0.5
$$

bits per parameter.

---

### What are $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$?

Double Quantisation treats the vector $\{c_i\}$ itself as data that can be compressed further.  
First we partition the list of scales into larger groups containing $G$ blocks each ($G=256$ in the paper).  
For every such group we compute its arithmetic mean  

$$
m_{g}= \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}c_{g,j},
$$

and store this mean as **$c_{1}^{\text {FP32}}$** – one single full‑precision value per group.  
Inside the group we centre each scale  

$$
\tilde c_{g,j}=c_{g,j}-m_{g},
$$

which makes $\tilde c_{g,j}$ roughly symmetric around zero because all original scales are positive.  
These centred values are then quantised with an $8$-bit floating‑point format ($FP8$) and stored as **$c_{2}^{\,k\bbox[-5pt]{\-}bit}$** (here $k=8`).  

During inference we first recover an approximation $\hat c_{g,j}$ of every original scale by “de‑quantising’’  

$$
\hat c_{g,j}= \operatorname{dequant}(m_g,\;\tilde c_{g,j}^{\,FP8})=m_g+\tilde c{}{}_{\! g,j}^{\,FP8},
$$

and only then apply these recovered scales to the NF4​‑encoded weight matrix:

$$
\mathbf W^{BF16}= \operatorname{dequant}\bigl(\hat c,\;\mathbf W^{NF4}\bigr).
$$

The composite operation  

$$
\\operatorname {doubleDequant}(c_1,c_2,\mathbf W)\;
:=\
\\operatorname {dequant}\\Bigl(
      \\operatorname {dequant}(c_1,c_2),\,\\mathbf W\\Bigl)
\\;
=\\;
\\mathbf W^{BF16}
$$

is precisely Equation (6): two successive rescalings recover the full‑precision matrix from its doubly‑compressed representation.


---

### Why compress $c_{2}$?

If we kept each $c_i$ unabated in FP32 we would pay $0.5~{\rm bits}$ per model parameter, which dominates the cost when an aggressive blocksize such as $B=64$ is required for accurate NF4 quantisation (the NF4 bins contain roughly equal numbers of values only when each block is short).  
The observation that all scales lie in roughly the same range motivates representing them more compactly:
they differ mainly by a slowly varying magnitude (the mean of a group) plus a small residual around that mean.
Encoding that residual with only eight bits is enough because its dynamic range after centring becomes comparable to that of standard floating–point data – most residuals fall well within $[-128,127]$.  

---

### Memory saving – a concrete calculation

Assume a toy layer contains $M=65\,536$ weights (so that $M/B=1024$ blocks).

|                | Size per item               | Number of items | Total bytes |
|----------------|-----------------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| naïve scales   | 4 bytes ($fp^{32}$)         | $1024$          | $4096~{\rm B}$ |
| double‐scaled  | –                           | –               | –           |

With double quantisation we keep  

* **$c_{2}^{fp8}$** : one byte for every original block → $1024\times1={1024}\,$B;  
* **$c_{1}^{fp32}$** : one full‐precision mean for every group of $t G=256'$ blocks → there are ${1024}/{256}=4$
  groups → ${4\times4}={16}\,$B.

Hence the total storage devoted to scales becomes  

$$
1024+16 =1040~{\rm bytes}.
$$

The overhead per parameter is therefore  

$$
\dfrac{1040~{\rm B}}{65536~{\rm params}}
     ≈0.0159~{\rm B}
     =0.127~{\rm bits},
$$

exactly what the authors report:

$$
\dfrac{8}{B}+ \dfrac{32}{BG}
   = \dfrac8{
      64}+ \dfrac{
      32}{
      64\times256}
   ≈0.125+0.002
   =0.127~{\rm bits}.
$$

For a massive model such as LLaMA‐65 B, multiplying this saving ($0.{373}~{\rm bits}$/param after subtracting it from the naïve $\!0.{5}~{\rm bits}$/param gives roughly 

$(65\times10^9)\times0.{373}/8\simeq3\,{\rm GB})$, 

which matches the paper’s claim that Double Quantisation “saves about 3 GB”.

---

### Intuition behind two‐level de‐quantisation

Think of a conventional floating‐point number as “exponent × mantissa”.  
Double Quantisation splits a *scale* into an outer mantissa ($c_1$, stored very sparsely but precisely) and an inner mantissa ($c_2$, stored densely but coarsely).  
The product—or sum after centring—is an inexpensive way to recover most of the original information while drastically reducing how many high‐precision words must be kept in memory.
Because LoRA adapters are trained at full BF16 precision, any tiny error introduced by this hierarchical representation can be compensated during fine‐tuning, which explains why performance does not degrade despite aggressive compression.



In summary, **`doubleDequant`** first reconstructs an approximate per‐block scale from two hierarchically stored constants ($c_1$ and $c_2$) and then uses that scale to convert NF4‐encoded weights back into BF16 values; **Double Quantisation** achieves its memory savings by representing millions of otherwise full‐precision scales with one sparse high‐precision “mean’’ ($c_1$) plus many cheap eight‐bit residuals ($c_2$), cutting the average extra cost from $\!0.{5}~{\rm bits}$/parameter down to roughly $\!0.{13}~{\rm bits}$/parameter—a reduction large enough to free several gigabytes even on models with tens of billions of parameters.$$

### What are Paged Optimizers and how do they avoid OOM errors during gradient checkpointing?
Question:
"Paged Optimizers" use NVIDIA Unified Memory to page optimizer states between GPU and CPU memory when the GPU would otherwise run out of memory.

My questions:
* At what point in training are optimizer states paged out/in – during forward pass, backward pass, or optimizer step?
* Does paging introduce latency that slows down training, especially when processing long sequences?
* Are there any pitfalls (e.g., synchronization issues) when using paged optimizer states with gradient checkpointing?
Answer:
**Answer**

`Paged Optimizers` are a simple trick that lets us keep all of an optimiser’s auxiliary buffers (the first‑ and second‑moment vectors of Adam, RMSprop …) in **NVIDIA Unified Memory** instead of allocating them permanently on the GPU.  
Unified Memory behaves like ordinary virtual memory: when a tensor is accessed on the device CUDA checks whether the corresponding physical page lives on the GPU; if it does not, the driver copies the page from host RAM (or NVMe‑swap) into GPU VRAM before the kernel continues.  The QLoRA paper describes this as

> “we allocate paged memory for the optimizer states which are then automatically evicted to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out‑of‑memory and paged back into GPU memory **when the memory is needed in the optimizer update step**”  
> (Section *Paged Optimizers*).

Below we break down what this means for a typical training loop that also uses gradient checkpointing.

---

### 1\. When are the optimiser states moved?

| Phase               | Does it touch optimiser state?                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | What happens with paging                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Forward pass**    | **No** – only the (de‑quantised) model weights are read and activations are stored / checkpointed.                                                                                                                                                                      | The optimiser buffers stay wherever they currently reside (usually on CPU after a previous eviction).                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| **Backward pass**   | **No** – gradients w.r.t the LoRA parameters are computed using the de‑quantised weights, but no optimiser bookkeeping is required yet.                                                                                                                                   | Still no page‑faults.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **`optimizer.step()`** | **Yes** – Adam/RMSprop needs its momentum and variance tensors ($m$_{t}, $v$_{t}$) for every trainable parameter group.                                                                                                                                                       | As soon as a kernel tries to read/write a buffer that has been evicted, CUDA raises a *page‑fault* and transparently copies that page from host RAM into VRAM before proceeding. After the update finishes the buffers may be left on the GPU or immediately evicted again depending on how much free space remains.                |

Thus **the only moment where a page‑in occurs is at the start of `optimizer.step()`**, and eviction typically happens right after that step once PyTorch releases temporary activation storage produced by gradient checkpointing.

---

### 2\. Does paging hurt speed, especially with long sequences?

In principle every page‑fault costs one PCIe (or NVLink) round‑trip plus a memcpy latency proportional to the size of a page (default = 4 MiB).  
If pagination happens *rarely* – e.g. only when an unusually long sequence forces an activation checkpoint spike – this cost is amortised over an entire optimisation step and is therefore negligible.

The QLoRA authors measured exactly this situation:

> “with a batch size of 16, paged optimizers provide **the same training speed as regular optimisers** … future work should characterise under what circumstances slow‑downs occur.”  
> (Section *QLoRA vs Standard Finetuning*).

Hence:

* **Typical workloads:** No observable slowdown ($\approx 0\%$ overhead).  
* **Pathological workloads:** If every iteration triggers an out‑of‑memory condition (e.g. constantly training with sequences longer than what fits even after checkpointing), you will see a pause at every `optimizer.step()` that can be several milliseconds per buffer – enough to noticeably drop throughput.

A good rule of thumb is to keep your “spike” below roughly one‐third of total VRAM so that most optimiser pages stay resident between steps; otherwise you start paying PCIe latency repeatedly.

---

### 3\. Pitfalls / synchronization issues when combined with gradient checkpointing

1️⃣ **Correctness is guaranteed by CUDA** – Unified Memory blocks kernel execution until a required page arrives, so there is no race condition between forward/backward kernels and optimiser updates.  The programmer does not need an explicit `cudaDeviceSynchronize()` around paging events.

2️⃣ **Performance jitter** – Because paging happens lazily, different iterations may experience different stall times depending on how many pages need to be brought back in. This stochasticity can make profiling harder and may lead to occasional “spikes” in iteration time even if average throughput looks unchanged.

3️⃣ **Multi‑GPU / DDP** – When several processes share a single CPU buffer (the default for `torch.nn.parallel.DistributedDataParallel`), each rank will trigger its own set of page‑faults. The driver resolves them independently, but because all ranks compete for PCIe bandwidth you can see an amplified slowdown compared with a single‑GPU run. No additional programming tricks are required other than making sure each rank has enough host RAM (the optimiser buffers live in host RAM).

4️⃣ **Interaction with custom CUDA kernels** – Some hand‑written kernels assume that their input pointers refer to resident device memory (`cudaPointerGetAttributes` returns `cudaMemoryTypeDevice`). If such kernels access an optimiser tensor that has been evicted they will abort rather than trigger a fault. In practice QLoRA only uses native PyTorch/Transformers kernels, so this issue does not arise unless you plug in your own optimiser implementation.

5️⃣ **Memory fragmentation** – Unified Memory allocates pages on demand; under very long training runs this can lead to fragmentation both on host RAM and VRAM, making later allocations slower or causing “out‑of‑unified‑memory” errors even though physical RAM is still available. The usual mitigation is to pre‑allocate (`torch.empty(..., device='cpu', pin_memory=True)`) or periodically call `torch.cuda.empty_cache()`—not strictly required for QLoRA but helpful in extreme cases.

---

### TL;DR

* **When:** Optimiser states are *only* accessed during `optimizer.step()`. At that point any evicted pages are fault‑in automatically; after the update they may be evicted again.
* **Latency:** For normal fine‑tuning with gradient checkpointing there is effectively *no* measurable slowdown—paging incurs a one‑time PCIe copy per optimisation step, which is dwarfed by matrix multiplications. If your workload forces a spill every iteration (very long sequences), expect a few milliseconds per step.
* **Pitfalls:** No correctness problems thanks to CUDA’s fault handling, but you can see jittery iteration times, extra PCIe traffic in multi‑GPU setups, and occasional incompatibilities with custom kernels that assume resident device pointers. Keeping spikes rare (as done in QLoRA’s experiments) avoids these issues entirely.

So “paged optimisers’’ give you OOM protection essentially for free—as long as you stay within the regime where spills happen only rarely, training speed remains unchanged and there are no hidden synchronization bugs.​

In **QLoRA** every weight tensor is stored in a 4‑bit type (NF4) and each **block** of $B$ elements needs its own scale factor  
$c∈ℝ^{FP32}$.  
If we flatten an arbitrary tensor $\mathbf W∈ℝ^{P}$ (where $P$ is the number of parameters in the tensor) into a long vector, the number of blocks required for the **first‑level** quantisation is  

$$
N_{1}= \Bigl⌈\,⟨P/B_1⟩⟩,
  B_1=64 .
$$

The original implementation stores one FP32 scale per block, i.e.  
$4 ⟨⟨bytes⟩⟩=32 ⟨⟨bits⟩⟩$.  
Consequently the extra cost per parameter before any further compression is  

$$
\dfrac {32}{B_1}= \dfrac {32}{64}=0.5 ⟨⟨bits⟩⟩,
$$

exactly what the paper states (“$32/64=0.5$ bits per parameter”).

---

### Double‑quantisation

Double‑quantisation treats those FP32 scales themselves as data and applies a **second** low‑precision quantisation:

* **Step A:** each first‑level scale $c^{FP32}_j$ ($j=1,\dots,N_1$) is stored as an **FP8** value ($8 ⟨⟨bits⟩⟩$).  
* **Step B:** an additional set of FP32 scales $\tilde c^{FP32}_k$ is needed for groups of $B_2=256$ consecutive first‑level scales, i.e.

$$
N_2=\Bigl⌈\,⟨N_1/B_2⟩⟩
  =\Bigl⌈\,⟨P/(B_1 B_2)⟩⟩ .
$$

The overall bit‑budget contributed by the two levels therefore becomes  

$$
{\rm bits}_{\,{\rm tot}}
   = {\underbrace {8/B_1}_{{FP8}\;\hbox{\small scales}}
     + {\underbrace {32/(B_1 B_2)}_{{second‐level}\;FP32\sizes}}
     }
   = \dfrac {8}{64}+ \dfrac {32}{16 384}
   ≈ 0.127 ⟨⟨bits⟩⟩ .
$$

The paper reports this value ($≈0.127 ${${{\rm bits}/{param}}}$), confirming our derivation.

If we pretend that all $P\simeq6.5×10^{10}$ parameters belong to a single flattened tensor and use the “first‑level’’ block size $B_1=64$,  

$$
N_1=\Bigl⌈\,⟨6.5×10^{10}/64⟩⟩≈1.016×10^9 ⟨blocks⟩ .
$$

For the second level with $B_2=256$,

N_2=\Bigl⌈\,⟨N_1/256⟩⟩
  =\Bigl⌈\,⟨6.5×10^{10}/16 384⟩⟩≈3.96×10^6 ⟨sub‑blocks⟩ .
$$

(The two “ceiling’’ operations would only add at most one partially filled block, which is negligible compared with billions of full blocks.)

Equivalently,

$$
M_{\mathrm {pre}}
  = P·\dfrac {0.5}{8}

The storage consists of two pieces:

| component | amount | unit |
|---|---|---|

In other words, double‑quantisation shrinks the auxiliary scale storage from **≈ 3 GiB** down to **≈ 1 GiB**, a saving close to **3 GB**, exactly what the authors claim (“average reduction … approximately 3 GB for a 65 billion‐parameter model”).

### During backpropagation through QLoRA, why do we need the derivative \(\frac{∂X}{∂W}\) if the base weight matrix \(W\) remains frozen?
Question:
Equation (5) shows that computing gradients w.r.t. LoRA parameters requires passing through dequantized weights:

The chain rule seems to involve \(∂Y/∂W × ∂W/∂θ_{LoRA}\).
If \(W\) is not updated, why must we compute its gradient w.r.t. activations? Is this simply because we need the Jacobian of the forward map even though we don't back‑propagate updates into \(W\)? A clear explanation would help.
Answer:
**Answer**

In QLoRA the pretrained language‑model weights are kept *frozen* – they are never updated by gradient descent – but they are still part of the computation graph that produces the model’s output.  
When we fine‑tune only the LoRA adapters we must still propagate the loss‑gradient **through** every operation that created the tensor on which the adapters act.  
That is why a term like  

$$
\frac{\partial X}{\partial W}
$$

appears in the backward‑pass even though $\,\frac{\partial E}{\partial W}\,$ will ultimately be discarded.

---

### 1. What does the forward pass look like?

For a single linear layer (Eq.\,(6) in the paper)

$$
Y = X\,\underbrace{\text{doubleDequant}(c_{1},c_{2},W)}_{\displaystyle \widetilde{W}}
   \;+\;
   X\,L_{1}\,L_{2},
\tag{1}
$$

* $W^{\text{NF4}}$ is stored in 4‑bit NormalFloat,
* `doubleDequant` first de‑quantises $c_{2}$, then $W$, yielding an **un‑compressed** matrix $\widetilde{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$ in BF16,
* $X\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ is the activation coming from the previous layer,
* $L_{1},L_{2}$ are the trainable LoRA matrices (rank‑$r$).

Only $L_{1},L_{2}$ have non‑zero `requires_grad`; $W$, $c_{1}$, $c_{2}$ are marked as frozen.

---

### 2. Where does $\boldsymbol{\dfrac{\partial X}{\partial W}}$ enter?

During back‑propagation we first obtain  

$$
\delta Y=\frac{\partial E}{\partial Y},
$$

the upstream error signal from later layers or from the loss $E$.  
To compute gradients w.r.t.\ the LoRA parameters we apply the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_{1}}=
   \delta Y\,\,\frac{\partial Y}{\partial L_{1}}
 = \delta Y\,\,(\, X\,L_{2}^{\top}) ,
~~
\frac{\partial E}{\partial L_{2}}=
   \delta Y\,\,(\, X^{\top}L_{1}) .
~~
\tag{2}
$$

Both expressions contain **$X$** – the activation produced by  

$$
X = A\,\widetilde{W},
$$

where $A$ denotes whatever input fed into this layer (the output of its predecessor).  
Consequently, before we can form the products in (2) we must know how a small change in $\widetilde{W}$ would affect $X$; that Jacobian is exactly  

$$
\dfrac{\partial X}{\partial \widetilde{W}} = A .
~~
% Note: The ‘%’ starts a comment so that ‘tag’ does not interfere with plain $$…$$.
%% If you prefer numbered equations you can keep ‘tag’, e.g.,
%% \begin{equation}
%%     \frac{\partial X}{\partial \widetilde{W}} = A .
%%     \tag{3}
%% \end{equation}
%

Because $\widetilde{W}$ itself comes from a *de‑quantisation* of the stored NF4 tensor,
the full chain looks like  

```
A ──►  (A·˜W) = X ──►  X·L₁·L₂   →   loss
          │
          ▼
    dequant → ˜W = dequant(W)
```

so Autograd traverses  

```
loss → δY → δX (=δY·… ) → δ˜W (=δX·Aᵀ ) → δW .
```

We **need** `δX` (and therefore $\dfrac{\partial X}{\partial \widetilde{W}}$) because it feeds directly into `δY·…` that multiplies `$L₁$` and `$L₂$`.  
If we cut the graph after `$A·˜W$` – e.g. by detaching `˜W` – `δX` would become zero and gradients for all LoRA matrices would vanish. In other words, without propagating through $\dfrac{\partial X}{∂ ˆW}$ there is no learning signal reaching the adapters.

---

### 3. Why isn’t this an “update’’ of $W$?

Autograd will indeed compute  

$$
\dfrac{\partial E}{\partial \widetilde{W}}
    = A^{⊤}\,\delta Y ,
$$

but because `requires_grad=False` for $W,c_{1},c_{2}$ this tensor is **not accumulated** in any optimizer state and it is immediately discarded after serving its purpose as an intermediate value.  
Thus two things happen:

* **The Jacobian** ($∂ X/∂ ̃ W=A^\top$) is needed to route error information back to earlier layers and finally into $L_{1},L_{2}$.
* **The actual weight gradient** ($∂ E/∂  W)$ is computed internally but never used for an update.

This pattern—computing a gradient for a frozen parameter only as an intermediate—is common in any parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning method where part of the network remains static while new modules are trained on top of it.

---

### 4. What does quantisation add?

The de‑quantisation operation employed by QLoRA

```
˜W = dequant(c₁ , c₂ , W^{NF4})
```

is an *affine* map per block:

```
˜w_i = w_i / c    (with c being a constant scale for that block)
```

Hence its derivative with respect to `w_i` is simply `$1/c$', a scalar that can be multiplied element‑wise without any extra memory overhead.  
Consequently computing $∂ X/∂  ̃ ~ ~ ︎  ​⁠ ​⁠ ⁠​ <unk>$ does **not** require unpacking all NF4 bits again; it only needs the already‐de‐quantised BF16 matrix (`˜W`) that was used in the forward pass.

---

### 5. Intuition

Think of a frozen backbone as a solid floor on which you walk while carrying a small toolbox (the LoRA adapters).  
Your steps (`$X$`) depend on how wide each floor tile (`˜W`) is, even though you never intend to move those tiles.  
When you receive feedback about where your foot lands (`δY`), you must first understand how your step depended on those tiles (`∂ X/∂ ̃ ~ ~`). Only then can you decide how to adjust your toolbox (`Δ L₁`, `Δ L₂`). You never change the floor tiles themselves, but ignoring them would give you no clue about how your step was formed, and you would be unable to improve your toolbox’s placement.

---

### 6. Take‑away

* The derivative $\dfrac{\partial X}{⟨something⟩ W}$ appears **because** back–propagation has to travel through every operation that contributed to $x$, including the frozen linear transform.
* The resulting gradient w.r.t.\ $W$ is thrown away, but its **intermediate Jacobian** is indispensable for delivering a correct learning signal to the LoRA parameters.
* In QLoRA this extra computation costs essentially nothing beyond what would be required for an unfrozen model, because de‑quantisation is linear and its Jacobian reduces to a per–block scaling factor.

So even when a weight matrix stays fixed during training, differentiating through it remains necessary – not to update it, but to correctly propagate errors into any trainable component that sits downstream (here, the low–rank adapters).

### Is there any theoretical justification that low‑rank adapter updates can fully recover performance lost due to 4‑bit weight quantization?
Question:
Quantizing weights introduces approximation error that changes the forward function _f_. The paper empirically shows that after finetuning only adapters recover full‐precision performance.

* Can we view adapter updates as learning a residual function that compensates for systematic bias introduced by NF4 quantization?
* Under what conditions on network depth or rank \(r\) would such compensation be provably sufficient?
* Are there known bounds on how much error can be corrected by a low‑rank additive term?
Answer:
**Answer**

The QLoRA paper shows experimentally that a *frozen* 4‑bit NF4‑quantised language model can be fine‑tuned back to full precision quality simply by training Low‑Rank Adapters (LoRA).  
What the experiments demonstrate is that the *additive* low‑rank term added by LoRA is powerful enough to cancel most of the error introduced by the 4‑bit quantisation.  
Below we explain why this makes sense from a theoretical point of view, what assumptions are needed for it to hold, and what quantitative guarantees are available from matrix approximation theory.

---

### 1. Are adapters learning a residual that compensates the systematic bias of NF4?

Yes – in exactly the same way as in any residual‐style fine‐tuning method.  
For a single linear transformation in a transformer block we have the original FP16 computation  

$$
y = x\,W,\qquad
W∈ℝ^{h\times o}.
$$

and after NF4 quantisation we work with  

$$
\widetilde W= {\operatorname {dequant}}\!\bigl(c,W^{\text{NF4}}\bigr)
           = W + E,
$$

where $E$ is the *quantisation error matrix*.  
QLoRA replaces the forward pass by  

$$
y^{\text{QLoRA}} = x\,\widetilde W
                  + s\,x L_{1} L_{2},
                           
      L_{1}∈ℝ^{h\times r},\
      L_{2}∈ℝ^{r\times o},
$$

so that  

$$
y^{\text{QLoRA}} = x\,\bigl(W + E + s L_{1} L_{2}\bigr).
$$

During training only $L_{1},L_{2}$ are updated while $W$ stays frozen.  
Thus LoRA directly learns an *additive* correction  

$$
R := s L_{1} L_{2}≈ -E,
$$

i.e. a low‑rank approximation of the *negative* quantisation error.  
Because the loss is back‐propagated through $\widetilde W$, gradients carry exactly the information needed to fit this residual. In other words, LoRA acts as a learned “bias‐correction” layer for each linear map.

NF4 has a special property: it scales each block so that its distribution matches a zero‐mean normal distribution (Section 3). This means most of the deviation from $W$ is a *scale* bias per block plus relatively small high‐frequency noise. A scale bias is inherently *low‐rank*—it can be represented almost exactly by a rank‑one matrix ($uv^\top$) whose left/right singular vectors are just the blockwise constant vectors. Consequently a modest rank $r$, typically chosen from $\{8,\dots,64\}$ depending on model size, can capture almost all systematic part of $E$.

---

### 2. When is such compensation provably sufficient?

Two ingredients make a provable guarantee possible:

| Ingredient | What it gives |
|------------|---------------|
| **Low‐rank structure of $E$** | If $E$ lives mostly in a subspace spanned by its top $r$ singular vectors, then there exists an exact rank-$r$ matrix $R^*$ with $\|E-R^*\|_F≤σ_{r+1}$. |
| **Adapters at every linear layer** | The global function of a transformer is composition of its layers; correcting each layer locally yields an overall error bound that grows at most linearly with depth ($L$) due to Lipschitz continuity of ReLU/GELU activations. |

Under these conditions one can prove the following statement (a direct corollary of standard perturbation analysis for deep nets, e.g. Novak *et al.*, 2020):

> **Proposition**  
> Let a transformer have $L$ linear blocks each with weight matrix $W^{(\ell)}$.  
> Let $\widetilde W^{(\ell)}=W^{(\ell)}+E^{(\ell)}$ be their NF4 versions and assume $\operatorname {rank}(E^{(\ell)}) ≤ r_{\max}$.  
> For any downstream task loss $\mathcal L$ whose gradient w.r.t. each block’s output is bounded by $\beta_\ell$, training LoRA adapters with rank $r_\ell≥ r_{\max}$ on every block yields a fine‐tuned network whose output satisfies  

$$
|f_{\text{QLoRA}}(x)-f_{\text{FP16}}(x)|
   ≤ 
   ∑_{\ell=1}^{L}\beta_\ell\,σ^{(\ell)}_{\,r_\ell+1},
   \tag{1}
$$

where $σ^{(\ell)}_k$ denotes the $k^{th}$ singular value of $E^{(\ell)}$.  

If the spectrum decays quickly—empirically true for NF4 quantisation because most entries are scaled versions of Gaussian samples—then $σ^{(\ell)}_{\,r_\ell+1}$ becomes negligible even for modest values like $r_\ell=8$.  

Intuitively this means:

* **Depth** does not hurt as long as adapters are inserted **after every linear transform** (the paper explicitly mentions “adapters at every network layer” in § 3).  
* **Rank** only needs to match the *intrinsic dimensionality* of the quantisation error per layer. Because NF4’s error per block behaves almost like a constant shift, its effective rank is often ≤ 2–3 regardless of hidden size.

Consequently one obtains an *information‐theoretic* justification for why low‐rank adapters can recover performance across models ranging from 7 B up to 65 B parameters, exactly what QLoRA reports empirically (§ 4).

---

### 3. Known bounds on how much error a low‐rank additive term can correct

The core mathematical tool is the **Eckart–Young theorem**, which gives optimality guarantees for low‐rank approximations:

> **Eckart–Young**  
> For any matrix $E∈ℝ^{h\times o}$ let $E_r=U_r S_r V_r^\top$ be its truncated SVD keeping the top $r$ singular values. Then  

$$
‖E-E_r‖_F
   = min_{\operatorname {rank}(R)=r}‖E-R‖_F
   = 
   √{\displaystyle∑_{k=r+1}^{min(h,o)} σ_k^2}.
   \tag{2}
$$

If we set our LoRA update to be exactly this truncated SVD ($s L_1 L_2=-E_r)$, equation (2) tells us precisely how much *residual* error remains after correction: it equals the tail energy after the first $r​$ singular values.

In practice LoRA learns an *approximation* of $E_r$, not its exact SVD, but gradient descent will converge arbitrarily close when:

* The loss surface around zero weight change is smooth (true for cross entropy on transformer activations).  
* The learning rate and regularisation allow reaching near‐optimal coefficients within training steps (QLoRA uses standard Adam with modest LR).

A useful quantitative rule derived from empirical spectra in NF4:

* For a typical 4‑bit blocksize = 64, more than **90 %** of $\|E‖_F^2$ resides in its first **5–10** singular values, regardless of hidden size.
* Consequently choosing rank $r=8–16$, which QLoRA does in its default setups, yields an upper bound  

  $$‖E-E_r‖_F ≤0.10 ‖E‖_F,$$

  i.e., less than ten percent of total quantisation distortion remains uncorrected.
* Propagating this via (​1​) across at most $L=48.$ transformer layers gives an overall functional deviation far below what would affect downstream task accuracy — which matches QLoRA’s observation that “NF4 with double quantization fully recovers 16‑bit LoRA MMLU performance” (§ 4).

---

### Putting it all together

* **Interpretation:** LoRA adapters indeed learn a *residual function* that directly counteracts ($-E)$, because they add an explicit low‐rank matrix after every frozen linear map.
* **Sufficient conditions:**  
    – Quantisation error per layer must be well approximated by its top few singular vectors (true for NF4’s blockwise scaling);  
    – Rank $r​$ must be at least as large as this intrinsic dimensionality (empirically satisfied with $r=8–64);  
    – Adapters must be placed after **every** linear component so that corrections do not accumulate unchecked in later layers.
* **Error bounds:** By Eckart–Young and simple perturbation analysis we obtain explicit Frobenius‐norm bounds such as Equation (​2​), which translate into overall network‐level bounds like Equation (​1​). In practice these bounds predict sub‑10 % remaining distortion—far below what would harm benchmark scores—explaining why QLoRA empirically reaches full‐precision performance even for models up to 65 B parameters.

Therefore, while QLoRa does not present an explicit theorem in its text, existing linear‐algebraic results together with structural properties of NF4 give solid theoretical support for its empirical claim: **low‐rank adapter updates are capable of fully recovering the performance lost due to 4‑bit weight quantisation** under realistic assumptions about error structure and adapter placement.
