\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

Chapter 3: # Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning with Low‑Rank Adapters  

In recent years the size of pretrained language models has grown from millions to tens of billions of parameters, making full‑parameter finetuning prohibitively expensive for most research groups. A compelling way to alleviate this burden is **Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)**, in which only a tiny subset of new parameters is introduced while the vast majority of the pretrained weights remain frozen. The most widely adopted PEFT technique is **Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) finetuning** \citep{hu2021lora}. This chapter presents a thorough exposition of LoRA, covering its mathematical formulation, practical design choices such as rank and scaling factor, strategies for inserting adapters throughout a transformer model, and a detailed analysis of its memory footprint. We also explain why, despite its dramatic reduction in trainable parameter count, LoRA on its own does not resolve the dominant GPU memory bottleneck associated with large language model (LLM) finetuning—a key insight that motivates the development of **QLoRA** described later in this textbook.

---

## LoRA Decomposition  

At the heart of LoRA lies an elegant linear algebraic insight: a high‑dimensional weight matrix can be *augmented* with a low‑rank update without changing the underlying functional capacity of the model. Consider a single linear projection inside a transformer block that maps an input activation matrix \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) to an output \(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times o}\) via a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\). In standard (full) finetuning one would directly modify \(\mathbf{W}\) while backpropagating gradients through it. LoRA instead freezes \(\mathbf{W}\) and introduces two trainable “adapter” matrices,
\[
\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r},\qquad 
\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o},
\]
where \(r\ll \min(h,o)\) denotes the *rank* of the adapter. The forward computation becomes  

\[
\mathbf{Y}
= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}
+ s\,\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2 .
\tag{1}
\]

The first term is the original frozen transformation; the second term is a low‑rank correction that is learned during finetuning. The scalar \(s\) is a *scaling factor* that modulates the magnitude of the adapter contribution relative to the pretrained mapping. Because \(\mathbf{L}_1\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\) together contain only \(r(h+o)\) trainable parameters—far fewer than the \(h\times o\) entries in \(\mathbf{W}\)—the number of parameters that must be stored and updated during training can be reduced by orders of magnitude.

Intuitively, this decomposition treats \(\mathbf{W}\) as an *anchor* representing general knowledge learned during pretraining, while \(\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2\) provides a *task‑specific* adjustment that lives in a compressed subspace. By keeping updates confined to this subspace, gradient noise is reduced and optimization can proceed more stably with far smaller learning rates than would be required for full‑parameter updates.

---

## Choosing Rank \(r\) and Scaling Factor \(s\)  

The rank \(r\) directly controls the expressive power and memory cost of the adapter. Since each linear layer typically has dimensions on the order of thousands (e.g., hidden size \(h=4096\), output size \(o=4096\) for a 7 B‑parameter LLaMA model), even modest values such as \(r=4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 32\) already capture a nontrivial subspace while keeping parameter overhead tiny. In practice, researchers often set \(r=4\) or \(8\) for instruction‑following tasks; larger values are used when higher fidelity to the target task is required.

The scaling factor \(s\) compensates for the fact that low‑rank matrices have smaller spectral norm than their full‑rank counterpart. Empirically it is common to initialise \(s=1\), but many works adopt larger scalings—e.g., \(s=2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16\)—to amplify the adapter’s contribution without changing its internal weights. The product \(s\,\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2\) thus behaves like a “residual” that can be tuned independently from the frozen base.

Choosing an appropriate pair \((r,s)\) involves balancing three considerations:

1. **Parameter budget** – larger rank directly inflates storage needs for adapters.
2. **Training stability** – overly small rank may prevent convergence on complex instruction data.
3. **Inference latency** – because adapters are inserted into every forward pass they add an extra low‑rank matrix multiplication; however this cost is negligible compared with the main projection when \(r \ll h,o\).

In QLoRA experiments it was observed that using adapters at *every* linear layer together with modest ranks was essential to recover full‑precision performance (see § “Adapter placement strategies”). This finding reinforces that rank should be chosen not only per layer but also with respect to how many layers will host adapters.

---

## Adapter Placement Strategies Across Transformer Layers  

A transformer block comprises multiple linear transformations: query (\(Q\)), key (\(K\)), value (\(V\)) projections in multi‑head attention, as well as feed‑forward network (FFN) up/down projections and final layer normalisation steps. Early implementations of LoRA followed a minimalistic pattern—injecting adapters only into the query and value projection matrices—under the assumption that these components dominate task‑specific adaptation \citep{hu2021lora}. However, systematic ablations presented in the QLoRA study reveal that **this sparse placement fails to match full finetuning performance on large bases**, especially for instruction following.

The authors performed extensive hyperparameter sweeps on LLaMA 7 B finetuned on Alpaca data (Table fig:hyper). They discovered two key phenomena:

1. **Number of adapters matters more than their dimension** – Adding adapters at all linear layers—attention projections *and* FFN layers—substantially closes the performance gap.
2. **Rank becomes less critical once coverage is complete** – Varying \(r\) within reasonable bounds does not materially affect downstream scores when every layer receives an adapter.

Consequently, modern instruction‑following pipelines—including QLoRA—adopt an “adapter‐everywhere’’ policy: each linear map inside every transformer block receives its own pair \((\mathbf{L}_{1}^{(l)}, \mathbf{L}_{2}^{(l)})\). Although this multiplies the number of adapters by roughly ten (the approximate number of distinct linear transforms per block), each remains minuscule due to low rank; thus total trainable parameters still stay below 0.5 % of total model size even for 65 B‑parameter models.

From an intuition standpoint, distributing adapters uniformly allows each computational stage to express task‑specific deviations locally rather than relying exclusively on global adjustments in attention alone. This granular flexibility appears crucial when dealing with diverse instruction formats requiring nuanced transformations at multiple depths.

## Memory Cost Breakdown: Activations vs LoRA Parameters  

When training large language models under any PEFT regime, two major sources dominate GPU memory consumption:

1. **Activations (including their gradients)** – These are intermediate tensors produced during forward propagation that must be retained (or recomputed via checkpointing) for backpropagation.
2. **Trainable parameters & optimizer states** – While PEFT dramatically reduces raw parameter counts, modern optimizers such as Adam store moment estimates per parameter (“optimizer states”), which can become sizable if many parameters are unfrozen.

The QLoRA paper provides concrete numbers for a 7 B LLaMA model finetuned with LoRA covering roughly 0.2 % of total weights:

- The **LoRA weight tensors** occupy ~26 MB.
- Their **input gradients**—the activations needed for computing updates—require ~567 MB when no checkpointing is applied.
- With gradient checkpointing—a technique where selected activations are discarded during forward pass and recomputed when needed—the activation gradient footprint drops dramatically to ~18 MB per sequence, rendering it comparable or even larger than all adapter weights combined.
- In contrast, merely loading a **4‑bit quantized base model** demands ≈5 048 MB for storage alone.

These figures illustrate two important truths:

- *Adapters themselves consume negligible memory.* Even if one were to double or triple their count across all layers, total storage would increase by only tens of megabytes.
- *Activations dominate.* Optimising training speed therefore hinges on reducing activation memory rather than shrinking adapter size.

Because gradient checkpointing already reduces activation storage at modest computational cost, further memory savings arise from compressing *the base model* rather than increasing adapter capacity—precisely where QLoRA’s innovations (NF4 quantization and Double Quantization) become indispensable.

## Why LoRA Alone Does Not Solve the GPU Memory Bottleneck  

It might be tempting to conclude that because LoRA reduces trainable parameters by several orders of magnitude, it automatically resolves GPU memory constraints for finetuning massive LLMs. The evidence presented in QLoRA disproves this naïve assumption:

1. **Base model must still be materialised at high precision** – Even when all pretrained weights remain frozen, they must reside in GPU memory during forward passes because each matrix multiplication requires dequantised values (or at least an efficient kernel). A standard FP16 version of LLaMA 65 B consumes >780 GB—a quantity far beyond any single GPU’s capacity.
   
2. **Gradient flow traverses frozen weights** – Training proceeds by backpropagating through those frozen weight tensors to compute gradients w.r.t.\ adapters (see Equation 5). While gradients w.r.t.\ \(\mathbf{W}\) themselves are not stored or updated, intermediate derivatives such as \(\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\) still require temporary dequantisation into BF16 arithmetic (as shown in § “QLoRA finetuning”). Hence these operations cannot bypass loading weight data into memory entirely.
   
3. **Optimizer states for adapters remain non‑negligible at scale** – For very large models (~33–65 B), even an optimizer state proportional to <0.5 % of total parameters translates into several gigabytes due to sheer scale.
   
4. **Memory spikes from long sequences** – Processing long context windows creates temporary activation buffers whose size scales linearly with sequence length and batch size; these spikes often exceed available GPU RAM even before considering base weights.

Consequently, while LoRA solves one aspect—the explosion of trainable parameter count—it leaves untouched three parallel sources: storage of pretrained weights, activation intermediates during forward/backward passes, and optimizer states for even modest adapter sets. The QLoRA methodology addresses these residual constraints by quantising pretrained weights down to *4-bit NormalFloat* precision (NF4), applying *Double Quantization* to compress quantisation constants further, and employing *Paged Optimizers* leveraging NVIDIA unified memory so that optimizer states can overflow gracefully onto CPU RAM without halting training.

Only after these complementary techniques are combined does finetuning become feasible on modest hardware—for example reducing required GPU RAM from >780 GB to <48 GB for a LLaMA 65 B model—while preserving full FP16 performance levels.

---

### Concluding Remarks  

Low‑Rank Adapter finetuning constitutes a powerful paradigm shift: by treating task adaptation as learning tiny low‑dimensional corrections placed throughout a frozen backbone we can dramatically curtail parameter overhead and simplify optimisation dynamics. Nonetheless, because modern LLM training pipelines remain dominated by base model storage and activation memories rather than parameter counts, LoRA must be paired with aggressive weight compression strategies such as those introduced in QLoRa (NF4 quantisation, Double Quantisation, Paged Optimizers). Understanding both components—the algebraic elegance of low‑rank decompositions and their interaction with hardware constraints—is essential for any researcher seeking to push instruction following or chatbot performance at unprecedented scale without prohibitive computational expense.
