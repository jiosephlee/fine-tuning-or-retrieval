\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# QLoRA: Efficient Fine‑tuning of Quantized Large Language Models

## Introduction: Why Efficient Fine‑tuning Matters  

Fine‑tuning large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the most effective ways to unlock their full potential. By exposing a pretrained model to task‑specific data, researchers can dramatically improve performance on downstream applications such as instruction following \citep{min2021metaicl,wei2021finetuned,ouyang2022training}, few‑shot learning \citep{wang2022self,liu2022few}, or even steer models away from undesirable behaviors \citep{askell2021general,bai2022training}. Despite these benefits, fine‑tuning at the scale of modern LLMs remains prohibitively expensive for most academic labs and even many industry teams. The root cause is the massive memory requirement when training with conventional 16‑bit floating point (BF16) arithmetic. Understanding why this barrier exists and how it can be overcome is essential before diving into the technical contributions of **QLoRA**.

### The Role of Fine‑tuning in Improving LLM Capabilities  

Pretrained language models encode a broad distribution over text but are not specialized for any particular task beyond next‑token prediction. When we fine‑tune such models on a curated dataset—often consisting of instruction–response pairs—we adapt their internal representations toward the target behavior. Empirically this process has yielded state‑of‑the‑art results across a variety of benchmarks \citep{min2021metaicl,wei2021finetuned,ouyang2022training,wang2022super}. Fine‑tuning can also be used to add desirable traits (e.g., politeness) or remove harmful ones (e.g., toxic completions). In practice, most high‑performing chatbots deployed today have been obtained by fine‑tuning a large backbone model on billions of carefully curated instruction examples \citep{ouyang2022training,bai2022training}. Consequently, advances that make fine‑tuning more accessible directly expand the community’s ability to experiment with safer and more useful LLMs.

### Memory and Compute Costs of Traditional 16‑bit Fine‑tuning  

The naïve approach to fine‑tuning simply keeps all model parameters in BF16 (or FP16) format and updates them via backpropagation. For a model with \(P\) parameters, the storage cost alone is \(16\cdot P\) bits. More importantly, during training each forward pass must also allocate gradients and optimizer states for *every* parameter. In practice this results in an overwhelming GPU memory footprint:

- A LLaMA model with **65 billion** parameters requires **> 780 GB** of GPU memory when fine‑tuned in BF16 \citep{touvron2023llama}.  
- Even smaller models consume several tens of gigabytes simply for their weight tensors; activation gradients dominate the remainder \citep{chen2016training}.

When using parameter‑efficient methods such as Low‑rank Adapters (LoRA) \citep{hu2021lora}, only a tiny fraction (e.g., 0.2 % of total weights) become trainable. Nevertheless, most of the memory consumption still stems from *activations*—the intermediate representations that must be stored for gradient computation—rather than from the adapter parameters themselves \citep{hu2021lora}. The net effect is that even highly efficient PEFT techniques cannot circumvent the fundamental bottleneck imposed by storing full weight matrices in high precision during forward and backward passes.

### Limits of Existing Quantization Techniques for Training  

Quantization compresses tensors by mapping continuous values onto a low‑bit discrete set (e.g., converting FP32 to 8‑bit integers). Recent work has shown that aggressive blockwise quantization can reduce inference memory by an order of magnitude while preserving perplexity \citep{dettmers2022llm,dettmers2022case,frantar2022gptq,xiao2022smoothquant}. However these methods are tailored **solely** for inference:

1. **Static Scale Assumptions** – Blockwise schemes rely on per‑block scaling constants that are fixed at conversion time.
2. **Outlier Sensitivity** – Heavy tails in weight distributions lead to poor utilization of low‑bit bins unless each block is quantized independently.
3. **Backpropagation Breakdowns** – During training gradients must flow through the quantized representation. Prior attempts found that naively backpropagating through 4‑bit weights yields unstable updates or severe accuracy loss \citep{wortsman2023stable}.

Consequently, while a 4‑bit version of a LLM can be loaded into GPU memory for fast inference, it cannot be fine‑tuned without sacrificing predictive performance or incurring prohibitive memory overhead from reconstituting high precision copies during backpropagation.

### Goals and High-Level Contributions of QLoRA  

**QLoRA** was designed precisely to bridge this gap between *memory­-efficient inference* and *high-fidelity fine‑tuning*. Its overarching goal is simple yet powerful: **enable fine‑tuning of multi‑billion parameter models on a single consumer or professional GPU without any loss in task performance relative to full BF16 training**. To achieve this objective QLoRA introduces three complementary innovations:

1. **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** – A new data type whose quantization bins are derived from the theoretical quantiles of a zero‑mean normal distribution. Because pretrained LLM weights are empirically Gaussian \citep[Appendix A]{app:normality}, NF4 provides an information–theoretically optimal mapping that preserves more signal than conventional Int4 or Float4 encodings.
   
2. **Double Quantization** – After the primary NF4 compression, the per‑block scaling constants themselves are further compressed using an 8‑bit floating format with a larger blocksize (256). This “quantize the quantizer’’ step reduces the average overhead from roughly \(0.5\) bits per parameter down to \(0.127\) bits—a saving equivalent to several gigabytes for a 65 B model.
   
3. **Paged Optimizers** – Leveraging NVIDIA’s unified memory mechanism, optimizer states are allocated in pageable memory that automatically migrates between CPU RAM and GPU VRAM as needed. This design eliminates sudden out‐of‐memory spikes caused by gradient checkpointing over long sequences while keeping overall training speed comparable to nonpaged optimizers.

In addition to these engineering advances, QLoRA adopts an *adapter‐centric* training regime: all base model weights remain frozen in NF4 format while low‐rank LoRA adapters—trained entirely in BF16—receive gradients propagated through dequantized activations. This architecture accomplishes two things simultaneously:

- **Memory Savings:** The frozen 4‑bit backbone occupies only a fraction (< 5 GB for a 7 B LLaMA) of what full BF16 would require.
- **Performance Preservation:** By backpropagating through the exact quantized representation rather than reconstructing high precision copies at runtime, gradients faithfully reflect the true behavior of the underlying model.

Empirically these components allow QLoRA to fine–tune a **65 B** LLaMA model on a single **48 GB** GPU—over an order of magnitude reduction compared with traditional BF16 training—while matching or surpassing upstream baselines on downstream tasks such as Vicuna’s chatbot benchmark \citep{vicuna2023}. The best QLoRA‐derived family (named **Guanaco**) reaches **99.3 %** of ChatGPT’s Vicuna performance after only 24 h on one professional GPU.

In summary, QLoRA’s contribution lies not merely in squeezing models into smaller footprints but in exposing an entirely new regime where *quantized* large language models can be *trained* efficiently at scale. This paradigm shift democratizes access to cutting–edge LLM fine‐tuning, paving the way for more open research on instruction following, bias mitigation, and domain adaptation without requiring massive clusters or specialized hardware.

Fundamentals of Model Quantization  

# What is quantization? From FP32 to integer/fixed‑point formats  

Quantization is the process of mapping a numeric tensor that is represented with a high‑precision data type—typically 32‑bit floating point (FP32)—to a representation that uses fewer bits per element. The primary motivation behind this transformation is to reduce both the memory footprint and the computational cost of neural network inference or training while preserving as much information as possible about the original values. In practice, most quantization schemes are linear: every element $x$ of an input tensor $\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}$ is multiplied by a scalar “quantization constant’’ $c$, rounded to an integer code, and subsequently stored using a low‑bit integer datatype such as Int8 or a low‑precision floating format such as Float4. Formally, when we target an $k$‑bit signed integer range $[-2^{k-1}+1,\;2^{k-1}-1]$, the forward mapping can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int}k}= \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{2^{\,k-1}-1}{\operatorname{absmax}\!\bigl(\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\bigr)}\;\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\Bigr)
               = \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(c^{\text{FP32}}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}\bigr),
\]  

where $\operatorname{absmax}(\cdot)$ returns the maximum absolute value across all entries of its argument and $c^{\text{FP32}}$ denotes the scalar scale factor that stretches or shrinks the original dynamic range so that it fits precisely inside the target integer interval. Dequantization simply reverses this operation:

\[
\operatorname{dequant}\bigl(c^{\text{FP32}},\,\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int}k}\bigr) \equiv
    \frac{\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int}k}}{c^{\text{FP32}}}
    = \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}.
\]  

When we replace floating‑point arithmetic with integer arithmetic during inference—e.g., performing matrix multiplications on dequantized values—we can achieve substantial speedups on hardware that provides native low‑bit kernels. However, straightforward application of this scheme raises subtle difficulties when applied to very large weight tensors typical of large language models (LLMs).

# Block‑wise quantization: motivation and formal definition  

A naïve global scaling approach suffers whenever the tensor contains elements whose magnitudes are far larger than most other entries—a situation often called the “outlier problem’’ (see the next section). To mitigate this issue, practitioners partition a weight tensor into many small contiguous blocks and assign an independent scale $c_i$ to each block $i$. This technique is known as *block‑wise* or *per‑block* $k$‑bit quantization.  

Assume we have a two‑dimensional weight matrix $\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$. By flattening $\mathbf{W}$ into a one‑dimensional vector and slicing it into $n$ blocks each containing $B$ consecutive elements, we obtain  

\[
n = \frac{b\times h}{B},
\]  

provided $B$ divides $b\times h$ exactly (otherwise padding may be used). For each block $j \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ we compute its own scale factor  

\[
c_j^{\text{FP32}} = \frac{2^{\,k-1}-1}{\,\operatorname{absmax}\!\bigl(\mathbf{W}_j^{\text{FP32}}\bigr)},
\]  

where $\mathbf{W}_j^{\text{FP32}}$ denotes the $j$‑th block’s raw FP32 values. The actual stored representation for block $j$ consists of a $k$‑bit integer tensor $\mathbf{\widehat W}_j^{\text{k}}$ together with its scalar constant $c_j^{\text{FP32}}$. The full quantized matrix can therefore be expressed compactly as  

\[
\bigl(\;\{\mathbf{\widehat W}_j^{\,k}\}_{j=1}^n,\;\{c_j^{\,\text{FP32}}\}_{j=1}^n\bigr).
\]  

During forward execution we dequantize each block locally before multiplying it by activations:

\[
\mathbf{\widetilde W}_j^{\,\text{BF16}} = 
    \operatorname{dequant}\!\bigl(c_j^{\,\text{FP32}},\, \mathbf{\widehat W}_j^{\,k}\bigr),
\]  

and then perform standard BF16 matrix multiplication $\mathbf X \times \mathbf{\widetilde W}$ where $\mathbf X$ denotes the input activation matrix in brain‑float16 (BF16). This design isolates low‑precision storage from high‑precision computation while still allowing backpropagation through the dequantized values—a key requirement for fine‑tuning LLMs.

# Outlier problem and why per‑block scaling is needed  

When all parameters share a single global scale factor $c$, any unusually large entry—an outlier—forces $c$ to become very small so that even the extreme value fits inside the available integer range. Consequently most other entries become compressed into just a few adjacent bins near zero, dramatically reducing effective resolution where it matters most (the bulk of weight values). Conversely, if outliers are omitted during scaling (e.g., via robust statistics), part of their information gets clipped entirely, leading to systematic bias in those few critical dimensions.

Block‑wise scaling sidesteps this dilemma by localizing each scale constant to a small region of parameters where values tend to be more homogeneous. In practice one selects modest block sizes such as $B=64$ or $B=128$, which empirically strike an excellent balance between two opposing forces:  
* **Precision** – Smaller blocks mean fewer outliers within each group, thus allowing each scale $c_j$ to faithfully reflect the local dynamic range without sacrificing much resolution.
* **Overhead** – Each additional block incurs storage for its corresponding constant(s). Hence making blocks too tiny would inflate memory consumption through an excessive number of constants.

The QLoRA work demonstrates that with a moderate blocksize ($B=64$) one can preserve near‐full‐precision performance while keeping the extra overhead modest—approximately half a bit per parameter when naïvely storing full‐precision constants, and far less after applying double quantization (see below).

# Quantile‑based vs uniform quantization  

Uniform (“linear”) quantization—as described above—relies exclusively on the maximum absolute value in each block to define bin boundaries. While simple and computationally cheap, it does not guarantee that every bin will be used equally often; consequently many bins may remain underutilized if the underlying distribution deviates substantially from uniformity.

*Quantile‑based* (or *histogram equal­-frequency*) quantization addresses this inefficiency by explicitly constructing bin edges so that an equal fraction of values falls into each bin. Concretely, suppose we wish to encode values using $k$ bits yielding $2^{k}$ possible codes $\bigl(q_0,\dots,q_{2^k-1}\bigr)$. Let $Q_X(p)$ denote the theoretical quantile function (inverse cumulative distribution function) for some reference distribution $X$. For an ideal *information‑theoretic* data type we can define

\[
q_i = \tfrac12 \Bigl( Q_X(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}) + Q_X(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}) \Bigr), 
    \qquad i = 0,\dots ,2^k-1 .
    \tag{3}
\]

When applied directly to empirical data these thresholds would require estimating many percentiles—a costly operation for large tensors. Earlier work therefore employed fast approximations such as SRAM‐based histograms~\citep{dettmers2022optimizers}, at the price of occasional large errors on rare outliers.

QLoRA introduces *NormalFloat* (NF$_k$), a specialized version of quantile based encoding tailored for neural network weights that are known empirically to follow an approximately zero–mean Gaussian distribution after pretraining (\emph{i.e.,} $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$). By precomputing exact Gaussian percentiles once (using Eq.~3 with $X=N(0,1)$) one obtains a universal codebook whose shape matches what we expect from typical weight tensors irrespective of their individual scale $\sigma$. The remaining step consists merely of rescaling each weight tensor so its empirical standard deviation aligns with that assumed by NF$_k$, which can be performed efficiently by dividing by its absolute maximum—a computation already required by uniform linear scaling. Consequently NF$_k$ retains all desirable properties of pure quantile coding—equal expected occupancy across bins—while eliminating expensive runtime percentile estimation.

In practice NF$_4$, abbreviated as “NF4,” has been shown experimentally within QLoRA to outperform both naïve Float4 (FP4) and Int4 representations on downstream tasks such as language modeling perplexity and downstream benchmark accuracy (\S 5).

# Memory implications of storing quantified weights and scales  

The most immediate benefit from moving from FP32 ($32$ bits per weight) to an NF4 representation ($4$ bits per weight) is an eightfold reduction in raw storage required for model parameters themselves:

| Representation | Bits per parameter | Multiplicative compression |
|----------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| FP32           |           \(32\,{\rm bits}\) | × 1 |
| NF4            |  \(4\,{\rm bits}\)          | × \( \tfrac18 \) |

However this saving does not come for free because every block needs at least one scale constant \(c_j^{\,{\rm FP32}}\). If each constant occupies \(b_c = 32\,{\rm bits}\) (single‐precision float) and our chosen blocksize contains \(B=64\) weights, then on average we pay an extra  

\[
\dfrac {b_c}{B}= \dfrac {32}{64}=0.5~{\rm bits/parameter}.
\]  

Thus naïvely storing only NF4 weights together with naïve full‐precision scales yields an effective footprint of \(4 + 0.5 = 4.5~{\rm bits}\) per original parameter—still more than five times smaller than FP32 yet noticeably larger than pure NF4 alone.

QLoRA introduces *Double Quantization* (DQ) precisely to shrink this overhead further. The idea is simple: treat each original scale constant \(c_j^{\,{\rm FP32}}\) itself as data subject to an additional round of compression using an even coarser datatype—for example an \(8\,{\rm bit}\) floating format FM8—with its own secondary set of constants \(c_{j,k}^{\,{\rm FP32}}\). When applying DQ with a second–level blocksize \(B' =256,\) we incur only

\[
\dfrac {8}{64} + 
    \dfrac {32}{64\times256}
    = 0.125 + 0.00195 
    \approx 0.127~ {\rm bits/parameter},
\]

a reduction from \(0.\!5~{\rm bits/parameter}\) down by roughly three–quarters (\(≈0.\!373~{\rm bits/parameter}\)). For a gargantuan model such as LLaMA‑65B this translates into roughly three gigabytes saved compared with naïve per‑block scaling—a nontrivial gain when total GPU memory availability may be limited to only \(48~{\rm GB}\).

Beyond static storage costs there are dynamic memory requirements associated with training loops: gradients w.r.t.\ activations dominate during backpropagation regardless of how compactly weights are stored, while optimizer states traditionally mirror full‐precision parameter size unless special “paged’’ implementations are employed (\S 3). In QLoRA’s context, debits incurred by optimizer state allocation become negligible relative to overall savings because weights now occupy merely a few gigabytes—even after accounting for DQ’s extra constants—and because *paged optimizers* migrate rarely accessed state tensors between GPU VRAM and host RAM automatically via NVIDIA unified memory, thereby preventing sudden out‑of‐memory spikes during long sequence training passes.

Summarizing these numbers within QLoRA’s experimental regime:
* A standard BF16 fine­tuning run on LLaMA‑65B would demand > 780 GB GPU RAM.
* After applying NF4 plus DQ together with \(B=64,\ B'=256,\) total model memory collapses below **48 GB**.
* The computational path still runs entirely in BF16 at inference time—the dequantized representation allows existing highly optimized BF16 kernels (e.g., NVIDIA cuBLAS kernels) to execute without modification.
* When combined with LoRA adapters whose own parameters occupy merely tens–of–megabytes (\S 2), overall training regulation stays comfortably within the limits even for consumer GPUs equipped with modest VRAM capacities (~24 GB).

These observations collectively demonstrate how careful engineering choices—block segmentation, information–theoretically optimal NormalFloat coding, double compression of auxiliary scalars, and smart paging strategies—enable efficient fine-tuning at scales previously thought impractical without multi‐GPU clusters or specialized hardware accelerators.

# Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning with Low‑Rank Adapters  

In recent years the size of pretrained language models has grown from millions to tens of billions of parameters, making full‑parameter finetuning prohibitively expensive for most research groups. A compelling way to alleviate this burden is **Parameter‑Efficient Finetuning (PEFT)**, in which only a tiny subset of new parameters is introduced while the vast majority of the pretrained weights remain frozen. The most widely adopted PEFT technique is **Low‑Rank Adapter (LoRA) finetuning** \citep{hu2021lora}. This chapter presents a thorough exposition of LoRA, covering its mathematical formulation, practical design choices such as rank and scaling factor, strategies for inserting adapters throughout a transformer model, and a detailed analysis of its memory footprint. We also explain why, despite its dramatic reduction in trainable parameter count, LoRA on its own does not resolve the dominant GPU memory bottleneck associated with large language model (LLM) finetuning—a key insight that motivates the development of **QLoRA** described later in this textbook.

---

## LoRA Decomposition  

At the heart of LoRA lies an elegant linear algebraic insight: a high‑dimensional weight matrix can be *augmented* with a low‑rank update without changing the underlying functional capacity of the model. Consider a single linear projection inside a transformer block that maps an input activation matrix \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) to an output \(\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times o}\) via a weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times o}\). In standard (full) finetuning one would directly modify \(\mathbf{W}\) while backpropagating gradients through it. LoRA instead freezes \(\mathbf{W}\) and introduces two trainable “adapter” matrices,
\[
\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r},\qquad 
\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o},
\]
where \(r\ll \min(h,o)\) denotes the *rank* of the adapter. The forward computation becomes  

\[
\mathbf{Y}
= \mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{W}
+ s\,\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2 .
\tag{1}
\]

The first term is the original frozen transformation; the second term is a low‑rank correction that is learned during finetuning. The scalar \(s\) is a *scaling factor* that modulates the magnitude of the adapter contribution relative to the pretrained mapping. Because \(\mathbf{L}_1\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\) together contain only \(r(h+o)\) trainable parameters—far fewer than the \(h\times o\) entries in \(\mathbf{W}\)—the number of parameters that must be stored and updated during training can be reduced by orders of magnitude.

Intuitively, this decomposition treats \(\mathbf{W}\) as an *anchor* representing general knowledge learned during pretraining, while \(\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2\) provides a *task‑specific* adjustment that lives in a compressed subspace. By keeping updates confined to this subspace, gradient noise is reduced and optimization can proceed more stably with far smaller learning rates than would be required for full‑parameter updates.

---

## Choosing Rank \(r\) and Scaling Factor \(s\)  

The rank \(r\) directly controls the expressive power and memory cost of the adapter. Since each linear layer typically has dimensions on the order of thousands (e.g., hidden size \(h=4096\), output size \(o=4096\) for a 7 B‑parameter LLaMA model), even modest values such as \(r=4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 32\) already capture a nontrivial subspace while keeping parameter overhead tiny. In practice, researchers often set \(r=4\) or \(8\) for instruction‑following tasks; larger values are used when higher fidelity to the target task is required.

The scaling factor \(s\) compensates for the fact that low‑rank matrices have smaller spectral norm than their full‑rank counterpart. Empirically it is common to initialise \(s=1\), but many works adopt larger scalings—e.g., \(s=2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16\)—to amplify the adapter’s contribution without changing its internal weights. The product \(s\,\mathbf{L}_1\,\mathbf{L}_2\) thus behaves like a “residual” that can be tuned independently from the frozen base.

Choosing an appropriate pair \((r,s)\) involves balancing three considerations:

1. **Parameter budget** – larger rank directly inflates storage needs for adapters.
2. **Training stability** – overly small rank may prevent convergence on complex instruction data.
3. **Inference latency** – because adapters are inserted into every forward pass they add an extra low‑rank matrix multiplication; however this cost is negligible compared with the main projection when \(r \ll h,o\).

In QLoRA experiments it was observed that using adapters at *every* linear layer together with modest ranks was essential to recover full‑precision performance (see § “Adapter placement strategies”). This finding reinforces that rank should be chosen not only per layer but also with respect to how many layers will host adapters.

---

## Adapter Placement Strategies Across Transformer Layers  

A transformer block comprises multiple linear transformations: query (\(Q\)), key (\(K\)), value (\(V\)) projections in multi‑head attention, as well as feed‑forward network (FFN) up/down projections and final layer normalisation steps. Early implementations of LoRA followed a minimalistic pattern—injecting adapters only into the query and value projection matrices—under the assumption that these components dominate task‑specific adaptation \citep{hu2021lora}. However, systematic ablations presented in the QLoRA study reveal that **this sparse placement fails to match full finetuning performance on large bases**, especially for instruction following.

The authors performed extensive hyperparameter sweeps on LLaMA 7 B finetuned on Alpaca data (Table fig:hyper). They discovered two key phenomena:

1. **Number of adapters matters more than their dimension** – Adding adapters at all linear layers—attention projections *and* FFN layers—substantially closes the performance gap.
2. **Rank becomes less critical once coverage is complete** – Varying \(r\) within reasonable bounds does not materially affect downstream scores when every layer receives an adapter.

Consequently, modern instruction‑following pipelines—including QLoRA—adopt an “adapter‐everywhere’’ policy: each linear map inside every transformer block receives its own pair \((\mathbf{L}_{1}^{(l)}, \mathbf{L}_{2}^{(l)})\). Although this multiplies the number of adapters by roughly ten (the approximate number of distinct linear transforms per block), each remains minuscule due to low rank; thus total trainable parameters still stay below 0.5 % of total model size even for 65 B‑parameter models.

From an intuition standpoint, distributing adapters uniformly allows each computational stage to express task‑specific deviations locally rather than relying exclusively on global adjustments in attention alone. This granular flexibility appears crucial when dealing with diverse instruction formats requiring nuanced transformations at multiple depths.

---

## Memory Cost Breakdown: Activations vs LoRA Parameters  

When training large language models under any PEFT regime, two major sources dominate GPU memory consumption:

1. **Activations (including their gradients)** – These are intermediate tensors produced during forward propagation that must be retained (or recomputed via checkpointing) for backpropagation.
2. **Trainable parameters & optimizer states** – While PEFT dramatically reduces raw parameter counts, modern optimizers such as Adam store moment estimates per parameter (“optimizer states”), which can become sizable if many parameters are unfrozen.

The QLoRA paper provides concrete numbers for a 7 B LLaMA model finetuned with LoRA covering roughly 0.2 % of total weights:

- The **LoRA weight tensors** occupy ~26 MB.
- Their **input gradients**—the activations needed for computing updates—require ~567 MB when no checkpointing is applied.
- With gradient checkpointing—a technique where selected activations are discarded during forward pass and recomputed when needed—the activation gradient footprint drops dramatically to ~18 MB per sequence, rendering it comparable or even larger than all adapter weights combined.
- In contrast, merely loading a **4‑bit quantized base model** demands ≈5 048 MB for storage alone.

These figures illustrate two important truths:

- *Adapters themselves consume negligible memory.* Even if one were to double or triple their count across all layers, total storage would increase by only tens of megabytes.
- *Activations dominate.* Optimising training speed therefore hinges on reducing activation memory rather than shrinking adapter size.

Because gradient checkpointing already reduces activation storage at modest computational cost, further memory savings arise from compressing *the base model* rather than increasing adapter capacity—precisely where QLoRA’s innovations (NF4 quantization and Double Quantization) become indispensable.

---

## Why LoRA Alone Does Not Solve the GPU Memory Bottleneck  

It might be tempting to conclude that because LoRA reduces trainable parameters by several orders of magnitude, it automatically resolves GPU memory constraints for finetuning massive LLMs. The evidence presented in QLoRA disproves this naïve assumption:

1. **Base model must still be materialised at high precision** – Even when all pretrained weights remain frozen, they must reside in GPU memory during forward passes because each matrix multiplication requires dequantised values (or at least an efficient kernel). A standard FP16 version of LLaMA 65 B consumes >780 GB—a quantity far beyond any single GPU’s capacity.
   
2. **Gradient flow traverses frozen weights** – Training proceeds by backpropagating through those frozen weight tensors to compute gradients w.r.t.\ adapters (see Equation 5). While gradients w.r.t.\ \(\mathbf{W}\) themselves are not stored or updated, intermediate derivatives such as \(\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}\) still require temporary dequantisation into BF16 arithmetic (as shown in § “QLoRA finetuning”). Hence these operations cannot bypass loading weight data into memory entirely.
   
3. **Optimizer states for adapters remain non‑negligible at scale** – For very large models (~33–65 B), even an optimizer state proportional to <0.5 % of total parameters translates into several gigabytes due to sheer scale.
   
4. **Memory spikes from long sequences** – Processing long context windows creates temporary activation buffers whose size scales linearly with sequence length and batch size; these spikes often exceed available GPU RAM even before considering base weights.

Consequently, while LoRA solves one aspect—the explosion of trainable parameter count—it leaves untouched three parallel sources: storage of pretrained weights, activation intermediates during forward/backward passes, and optimizer states for even modest adapter sets. The QLoRA methodology addresses these residual constraints by quantising pretrained weights down to *4-bit NormalFloat* precision (NF4), applying *Double Quantization* to compress quantisation constants further, and employing *Paged Optimizers* leveraging NVIDIA unified memory so that optimizer states can overflow gracefully onto CPU RAM without halting training.

Only after these complementary techniques are combined does finetuning become feasible on modest hardware—for example reducing required GPU RAM from >780 GB to <48 GB for a LLaMA 65 B model—while preserving full FP16 performance levels.

---

### Concluding Remarks  

Low‑Rank Adapter finetuning constitutes a powerful paradigm shift: by treating task adaptation as learning tiny low‑dimensional corrections placed throughout a frozen backbone we can dramatically curtail parameter overhead and simplify optimisation dynamics. Nonetheless, because modern LLM training pipelines remain dominated by base model storage and activation memories rather than parameter counts, LoRA must be paired with aggressive weight compression strategies such as those introduced in QLoRa (NF4 quantisation, Double Quantisation, Paged Optimizers). Understanding both components—the algebraic elegance of low‑rank decompositions and their interaction with hardware constraints—is essential for any researcher seeking to push instruction following or chatbot performance at unprecedented scale without prohibitive computational expense.

Designing QLoRA: Core Innovations  

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, yet their sheer size makes full‑parameter finetuning prohibitively expensive in terms of GPU memory. The **QLoRA** framework overcomes this obstacle by applying three tightly integrated techniques that together reduce the memory required for finetuning a 65 billion‑parameter model to under 48 GB while preserving the performance of a conventional 16‑bit finetuning run. This chapter presents these innovations in depth:
the **NormalFloat (NFk) quantization** scheme that exploits the statistical properties of pretrained weights,
the **Double Quantization** strategy that compresses the auxiliary scale tensors accompanying blockwise quantization,
and **Paged Optimizers**, which use NVIDIA Unified Memory to avoid out‑of‑memory spikes during gradient checkpointing.  
Each component is described first from an intuitive standpoint and then formalized mathematically so that readers can both understand why it works and how it is implemented in practice.

# NormalFloat (NFk) Quantization  

## From blockwise k‑bit quantization to an information‑optimal representation  

A naïve reduction from high‑precision floating‑point numbers to low‑bit integers typically rescales an entire tensor by its absolute maximum value and then rounds each element into a discrete set of bins. While this “global” scaling guarantees that the full dynamic range of the target data type is exploited, it suffers when a single outlier dominates the scale factor: most bins become empty and effective precision collapses for the bulk of the values. The remedy adopted by modern LLM quantizers is **blockwise** k‑bit quantization, whereby a tensor \(\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times h}\) is partitioned into contiguous blocks \(\{\mathbf{W}_i\}\) of size \(B\). Each block receives its own scaling constant \(c_i\), allowing local rescaling that preserves finer granularity across the weight matrix.

Even with blockwise processing, however, most low‑bit formats still allocate their \(2^{k}\) discrete levels uniformly across an interval such as \([-127,127]\). This uniform allocation does not match the empirical distribution of LLM weights. Studies reported in the QLoRA work find that pretrained weight matrices are well approximated by a zero‑centered normal distribution \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^{2})\) after modest preprocessing (see Appendix A). If we could match the density of representation levels to this distribution, each bin would be equally likely to receive a weight value—an information‑theoretic optimum that minimizes expected quantization error under a mean‑squared loss objective.

## Constructing NFk from Gaussian quantiles  

The optimality condition suggests building a **NormalFloat** data type whose bin boundaries are dictated by the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). Concretely, let \(Q_X(p)\) denote the inverse CDF (quantile function) of a standard normal variable \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). To obtain \(2^{k}+1\) equally probable cut points, we evaluate

\[
q_{i}
   = \frac12\Bigl(
      Q_X\!\Bigl(\frac{i}{2^{k}+1}\Bigr)
      +
      Q_X\!\Bigl(\frac{i+1}{2^{k}+1}\Bigr)
   \Bigr),
   \qquad i=0,\dotsc,2^{k}-1 .
\tag{1}
\]

Equation (1) yields a set \(\{q_i\}\) that partitions the real line into \(2^{k}\) intervals each containing exactly \(1/2^{k}\) of the probability mass under \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). For example, with \(k=4\) we obtain eight negative thresholds and eight positive thresholds whose midpoints constitute the representable values of **NF4** (the version deployed throughout QLoRA).

A naïve symmetric design would place zero between two adjacent bins without ever representing it directly—a drawback because many tensors contain exact zeros (e.g., padding masks). To guarantee a discrete zeropoint while preserving full utilization of all \(2^{k}\) codewords, QLoRA builds an **asymmetric** set:
the negative side receives \(2^{k-1}\) thresholds sampled as above,
the positive side receives \(2^{k-1}+1\) thresholds,
the two resulting zero entries are merged into a single codeword.
The final codebook therefore contains exactly one value equal to zero and uses every remaining codeword for non‑zero magnitudes,
fulfilling both equal‑probability allocation *and* exact representation of zero.

## Mapping pretrained weights onto NFk  

Having defined an NFk codebook spanning roughly \([-1,1]\), any weight block \(\mathbf{W}_i\) can be rescaled so that its empirical standard deviation matches that implied by the codebook’s variance.
Practically this is achieved by a simple absolute‑maximum normalization:

\[
c_i = \frac{\operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{W}_i)}{\max_{q\in\mathcal C_{\text{NF}}}|q|},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_i = \operatorname{round}\!\bigl(c_i^{-1}\,\mathbf{W}_i\bigr),
\tag{2}
\]

where \(\mathcal C_{\text{NF}}\) denotes the set of NFk representable values.
Equation (2) mirrors standard uniform quantization but substitutes an *information‑optimal* codebook,
ensuring that after dequantizing via

\[
\operatorname{dequant}(c_i,\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_i)=c_i\,\widehat{\mathbf{W}}_i,
\tag{3}
\]

the reconstructed weights retain maximal fidelity given only \(k\) bits per element.
Empirically QLoRA observes that **NF4** consistently outperforms both **FP4** (a standard floating‑point format truncated to four bits) and **Int4** across multiple model families—including LLaMA, OPT and BLOOM—both in downstream task accuracy and language modeling perplexity (see Table 3 therein).

# Double Quantization  

## Motivation: scaling constants dominate blockwise memory  

Blockwise k‑bit quantization requires storing one scaling constant per block.
If blocks contain \(B=64\) parameters—a setting favored for its low reconstruction error—each constant traditionally occupies 32 bits (FP32), contributing an overhead of

\[
\frac{32~\text{bits}}{64~\text{params}} = 0.5~\text{bits/parameter}.
\tag{4}
\]

For a model containing billions of parameters this term translates into several gigabytes,
eclipsing even the compressed weight matrix itself when extreme compression is employed.

## Two–stage compression pipeline  

QLoRA’s solution—**Double Quantization**—applies a second round of lossy compression exclusively to these scaling tensors.
The procedure consists of three steps:

| Step | Input | Operation | Output |
|------|-------|-----------|--------|
| ① | \(c_i^{\,\text{FP32}}\) | center by subtracting its mean → \(c_i'\) | \(c_i'\in[-\,a,a]\) |
| ② | \(c_i'\) | blockwise FP8 quantize (\(8\,{\rm bits}\), blocksize \(=256\)) | \(c_{i}^{\,\text{FP8}}\) |
| ③ | \(c_{i}^{\,\text{FP8}}\) | store accompanying dequant constants \(c_{i}^{\,\text{FP32}}\!^{(1)}\)| compact representation |

Because centered scaling constants are symmetrically distributed around zero,
a uniform FP8 coder suffices—no specialized normal–distribution coder is needed.
The dequant step reconstructs an approximate FP32 constant:

\[
c_i^{\,*}= \operatorname{dequant}(c_{i}^{\,\text{FP8}}, c_{i}^{\,\text{FP32}}\!^{(1)}).
\tag{5}
\]

During inference or backward propagation this recovered constant replaces the original FP32 value inside Equation (3).

## Memory savings analysis  

The double‑quantized overhead now comprises two contributions:
the FP8 codes themselves require \(8/256 = 0.03125~{\rm bits/param}\),
while their accompanying fine‐scale constants occupy
\(32/(64\times256)=0.00195~{\rm bits/param}\).
Summing these gives

\[
0.03125 + 0.00195 \approx 0.0332~{\rm bits/parameter},
\tag{6}
\]

which is dramatically lower than Equation (4)’s naïve cost.
In absolute terms,
for a model with \(N=65\times10^{9}\) parameters,
the saved memory equals

\[
(N \times (0.5 - 0.0332))~{\rm bits}
   \approx N\times0.4668~{\rm bits}
   \approx 3~{\rm GB},
\tag{7}
\]

matching precisely the empirical reduction reported in QLoRA (“≈ 3 GB for a 65B model”).  
Crucially this gain comes without any measurable degradation in downstream task performance:
experiments across several LLM families show comparable accuracy between single‑level NF4 and double‑quantized NF4.

# Paged Optimizers  

## Why optimizer state matters after weight compression  

Even after compressing both base weights (\(4\,{\rm bit}\)) and their per‑block scale tensors (\(~0.13~{\rm bit/parameter}\)), training still requires storing optimizer momenta, variance estimates and other auxiliary buffers for every trainable parameter—in our case primarily for LoRA adapters plus any gradients retained during gradient checkpointing.
When processing long sequences or large batch sizes these buffers may exceed available device memory despite reductions elsewhere,
causing out–of–memory errors that interrupt training on a single GPU.

## Unified Memory based paging mechanism  

NVIDIA’s **Unified Memory** system provides an operating-system style virtual address space shared between host RAM and device VRAM.
When allocating large tensors such as Adam’s first–moment vector (\(m_t)\), second–moment vector (\(v_t)\), or gradient checkpoints,
the driver automatically migrates pages whose physical residency would otherwise exceed VRAM limits onto host RAM,
retaining only actively accessed pages on GPU at any moment.
QLoRA leverages this mechanism by allocating all optimizer states as *paged* tensors rather than regular device‐only allocations.
Consequently:

* When routine forward/backward passes need only local slices of these tensors,
those slices are automatically paged “in” from host RAM,
while unrelated pages remain evicted (“paged out”).
* Should an unusually long sequence cause many gradient checkpoints simultaneously,
Unified Memory seamlessly pulls additional pages into VRAM until either:
   – sufficient pages have been brought in to satisfy current demand,
   – or physical GPU memory is exhausted beyond what paging can remedy,
in which case training would still abort (a scenario observed only rarely).

## Trigger conditions and impact on throughput  

In practice QLoRA observed that paging activates primarily during minibatches with maximal sequence lengths—precisely those moments where gradient checkpointing generates temporary spikes in activation storage demand.
For typical instruction‐tuning workloads employing batch size \(=16,\ p_{\rm seq}=2048,\ {\rm GPU}=48~{\rm GB}\),
empirical profiling revealed *no measurable slowdown*: training throughput remained identical to runs using conventional unfactored optimizers because:
* The duration spent waiting for page‐ins was negligible relative to compute‐bound matrix multiplications;
* The amount of data transferred per page fault was small compared with overall bandwidth constraints;
* Most optimizer accesses are sequential or localized enough that resident pages stay hot across successive steps.

Nonetheless QLoRA acknowledges that edge cases—such as extreme sequence lengths or substantially larger batch sizes—might incur noticeable latency due to higher paging frequency.
The authors suggest future investigations systematically characterizing these regimes
(e.g., measuring page‐fault rates versus sequence length or batch size).

## Synthesis  

By aligning low‑bit storage formats with learned weight statistics (**NormalFloat**), compressing ancillary scale information via **Double Quantization**, and safeguarding training continuity through **Paged Optimizers**, QLoRA creates a self‑consistent pipeline where every component contributes toward reducing peak GPU memory while preserving—or even enhancing—the final task performance relative to full‐precision finetuning. These innovations together make it feasible to fine‑tune models up to 65 billion parameters on a single consumer or professional GPU without sacrificing accuracy, thereby democratizing access to state‑of‑the‑art large language models for research groups lacking multi‑GPU clusters.
