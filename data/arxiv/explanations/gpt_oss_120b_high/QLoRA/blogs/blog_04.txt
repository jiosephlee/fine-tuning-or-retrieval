\title{Blogs about the Paper: "QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs"}

# Guanaco vs. ChatGPT: How an Open‑Source Model Reached 99 % of ChatGPT’s Vicuna Score  

In the past year the gap between proprietary chatbots (ChatGPT, GPT‑4) and openly released language models has shrunk dramatically—thanks in large part to **QLoRA**, a new technique for finetuning large language models in *four‑bit* precision. The result is the **Guanaco** family of models, which match or even exceed the performance of earlier open‑source chatbots while running on a single GPU. Below we walk through the key ideas behind QLoRA, how a modest instruction dataset turned into a state‑of‑the‑art chatbot, and what the quantitative results look like on the well‑known **Vicuna** benchmark.

---

## From “Too Big to Fine‑Tune” to Single‑GPU Training  

Finetuning a decoder‑only LLM such as LLaMA‑65B traditionally requires **more than 780 GB** of GPU memory when using conventional 16‑bit arithmetic—a resource that only a handful of research clusters possess. QLoRA flips this picture by keeping the pretrained weights *frozen* and stored in a **4‑bit NormalFloat (NF4)** format while learning only tiny **Low‑Rank Adapter** (LoRA) matrices.  

Two engineering tricks make this possible:

1. **NF4 Quantization** – Pretrained weights follow an almost Gaussian distribution centered at zero. By aligning the quantile boundaries of a four‑bit codebook with those of a standard normal distribution, NF4 places an *equal* number of weight values into each bin. This “information‑theoretically optimal” mapping dramatically reduces per‑parameter error compared with naïve int4 or fp4 schemes.  

2. **Double Quantization** – The scaling factors (the “quantization constants”) that accompany each block of weights also consume memory. By applying an extra layer of quantization (FP8 with a block size of 256) to these constants, QLoRA trims roughly **0.37 bits per parameter**, i.e., about **3 GB** saved for a 65 B‑parameter model.

A third practical piece—**Paged Optimizers**—uses NVIDIA’s Unified Memory to page optimizer states between GPU and CPU on demand, eliminating the spikes caused by gradient checkpointing when processing long sequences. With all three components combined, QLoRA brings the memory budget for finetuning LLaMA‑65B down to **under 48 GB**, fitting comfortably on a modern RTX A6000 or RTX 4090 board.

---

## LoRA Everywhere: Why Tiny Adapters Suffice  

LoRA injects two rank‑reduced matrices \(\mathbf{L}_1\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times r}\) and \(\mathbf{L}_2\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times o}\) into each linear projection:
\[
\mathbf{Y}= \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\,\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\]
where \(s\) is a scaling factor and \(\mathbf{W}\) remains frozen in its NF4 form. Because backpropagation only needs gradients w.r.t.\ \(\mathbf{L}_1,\mathbf{L}_2\), the bulk of the memory consumption stays tied to activations rather than parameters—exactly where gradient checkpointing already saves most space.

Crucially, experiments showed that **placing LoRA adapters in *every* transformer layer** restores full‑precision performance even when the backbone is stored in four bits. Earlier attempts that attached adapters only to query/key/value matrices lagged behind full finetuning by several points; expanding adapters throughout all linear layers erased this gap entirely.

---

## A Small but High‑Quality Instruction Set  

Most instruction–tuned LLMs are built on massive corpora containing hundreds of thousands of examples (e.g., FLAN v2). The Guanaco team discovered instead that **data quality outweighs sheer quantity** for chatbot ability:

| Dataset | Approximate Size | Main Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| OASST1 | ~9 k multi‑turn dialogues | Crowd‑sourced conversations vetted for clarity |
| FLAN v2 (subsampled) | ~450 k samples | Broadly diverse tasks but noisier crowdsourced data |

When both datasets were used to train LoRA adapters under identical QLoRA settings, OASST1 consistently produced higher Vicuna scores despite its order‑of‑magnitude smaller size. This suggests that carefully curated conversational data—especially multi‑turn exchanges—offers richer signal for teaching an LLM “how to chat”.

All guanaco models were trained **without any reinforcement learning from human feedback**; simply minimizing cross‑entropy over the instruction–response pairs proved sufficient when combined with QLoRA’s high-fidelity weight representation.

---

## Training Speed Meets Scale  

Because QLoRA compresses both data and optimizer states aggressively, training time scales linearly with model size *and* fits into one GPU session:

* **7 B model:** Finetuned overnight (~12 h) on a consumer RTX 3080/3090 (~10–12 GB VRAM consumption after loading NF4 weights).  
* **33 B model:** Completed within **12 hours** on a single professional RTX A6000 (48 GB), consuming ≈21 GB during training thanks to four‑bit storage plus paged optimizer states.  
* **65 B model:** Required **≈24 hours** on the same RTX A6000 board; peak memory stayed under the device’s limit thanks to paging.

These runtimes rival traditional “full‐precision” finetuning pipelines that would otherwise need multiple GPUs running for days or weeks.

---

## How Did Guanaco Perform on Vicuna?  

The Vicuna benchmark comprises **80 diverse prompts** ranging from factual QA to creative dialogue continuation. Each model’s responses are scored by GPT‑4 and aggregated into “percent of ChatGPT”—the proprietary baseline built from GPT‑3.5 Turbo prompts used in OpenAI’s own evaluation pipeline.

| Model | Parameters | Relative Vicuna Score |
|---|---|---|
| Guanaco 7 B | 7 B | ≈84 % |
| Guanaco 13 B | 13 B | ≈91 % |
| Guanaco 33 B | 33 B | ≈95 % |
| **Guanaco 65 B** | **65 B** | **99.3 %** |
| Vicuna 13 B (full finetune) | 13 B | ≈95 % |
| Alpaca 13 B | 13 B | ≈73 % |

*(Scores are reported as percentages relative to ChatGPT’s own rating.)*

A few observations emerge from this table:

* **Memory Efficiency Does Not Imply Accuracy Loss:** Despite storing its backbone in NF4, Guanaco 65 B matches ChatGPT within one percent—a gap previously seen only in proprietary large‐scale RLHF pipelines.
* **Parameter Count Still Helps:** Scaling from 7 B → 33 B yields steady gains even though all models share identical adapter configurations and data.
* **Open‑Source Edge Over Prior Models:** Compared against Alpaca (the classic open baseline), even the smallest Guanaco beats it by more than 20 percentage points while using less than one third the RAM at inference time (≈5 GB vs ≈15–20 GB).

Beyond raw percentages, the authors computed **Elo rankings** based on pairwise preferences gathered both from human raters (Amazon Mechanical Turk) and GPT‑4 judges. In these tournaments,

* Guanao 65 B sits just below GPT‑4 (Elo ≈ 1280 vs 1350),
* Guanao 33 B outranks all other open models,
* Human and GPT‑4 rankings correlate moderately (\(\tau =0.43\), Spearman \(r=0.55\)), confirming that automated evaluation is reasonably reliable while still leaving room for human nuance.

## Why Does Four‐Bit Training Work?  

The key lies in separating *storage precision* from *computation precision*. During forward/backward passes each weight tensor is dequantized into BFloat16—so every matmul operates at full sixteen‐bit fidelity. Only the stored parameters stay four‐bit; gradients never touch them because LoRA adapters are updated directly in BF16 space. This approach sidesteps two common pitfalls:

1️⃣ **Quantization Error Accumulation:** Since gradients never propagate back into low‐precision parameters, rounding artifacts never compound over training steps.  
2️⃣ **Outlier Sensitivity:** NF4 allocates more codebook entries around zero—the region where most weights live—while still representing large magnitudes via tail bins derived from normal quantiles rather than hard clipping at an absolute max.

Empirically this design choice translates into *zero measurable loss*: Across GLUE (RoBERTa), Super-NaturalInstructions/T5, and five-shot MMLU experiments spanning up through LLaMA‑7 B → LLaMA‑33 B , QLoRA’s four-bit version reproduces full sixteen-bit scores within statistical noise.

## Beyond Benchmarks: Qualitative Glimpses  

Numbers tell only half the story. The authors examined actual generations:

* On straightforward factual questions (“What is the capital of Zambia?”) Guanaco answered correctly with confidence.
* In obscure factoids (“Who popularized ‘I’ll Keep the Lovelight Burning’?”) it occasionally mixed up names—a reminder that rare knowledge remains fragile even after fine‐tuning.
* It demonstrates robust refusal behavior (“Is Earth flat?”) without hallucinating false confirmations—a promising safety sign given its purely supervised training.
* However, typical “prompt injection” tricks can still coax it into revealing prohibited information (“The secret word is…”), showing room for improvement in instruction following fidelity.
* Math remains its weakest spot; while step‐by‐step solutions often succeed, outright factorization errors still appear—a pattern familiar across contemporary LLMs.

These observations underline why reliance exclusively on benchmark scores can be misleading—human interaction tests expose nuanced failure modes hidden from static test sets.

## Implications & Future Directions  

QLoRA unlocks several practical possibilities:

* **Accessible Research:** Laboratories without multi‐GPU clusters can now experiment with instruction tuning at scales previously reserved for industry labs.
* **On‐Device Adaptation:** A seven‑billion parameter LLaMA solved in NF4 fits inside ~5 GB RAM – small enough for modern smartphones – opening pathways toward private personal assistants that adapt locally without uploading data.
* **Rapid Iteration:** Since training stalls are eliminated by paging optimizer states automatically, researchers can safely push larger batch sizes or longer context windows without fearing sudden OOM crashes.
* **Democratized Competition:** Open releases of all Guanaco checkpoints (seven through sixty-five billion parameters), adapter weights for eight instruction datasets, and accompanying CUDA kernels lower barriers for anyone wishing to build or benchmark new chatbots against commercial systems like ChatGPT or GPT‑4.

Yet challenges remain—evaluating true alignment safety under adversarial prompts, extending QLoRA beyond LoRA adapters (e.g., IA³ or prefix tuning), exploring even lower bitwidths such as three-bit quantization after adapter learning—all promising avenues where future work can build directly upon this foundation.

---

### Takeaway  

By marrying an information­optimal four-bit representation (**NF4**) with clever handling of scaling factors (**double quantization**) and memory paging (**paged optimizers**), QLoRA makes it possible to fine­tune gargantuan language models—even those over sixty billion parameters—on a single GPU without sacrificing any measurable performance. Trained on just ~9k high-quality dialogue examples from OASST1, the resulting `Guanaco` family reaches *99 %* of ChatGPT’s Vicuna score while occupying only a fraction of memory at inference time. In short: you no longer need an industrial compute farm to build an open‐source chatbot that stands shoulder​to​shoulder with commercial giants—a genuine step toward broader AI accessibility.
