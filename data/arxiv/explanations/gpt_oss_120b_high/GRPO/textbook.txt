\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Introduction and Motivation

Mathematical reasoning has emerged as one of the most demanding tasks for modern large language models (LLMs).  While LLMs excel at generating fluent natural language, mathematics requires a uniquely structured form of thinking that intertwines symbolic manipulation, precise logical deductions, multi‑step inference, and often exact numerical computation.  This mismatch between the statistical nature of language modeling and the deterministic rigor of mathematics leads to systematic failures when LLMs are asked to solve even elementary problems such as those found in grade‑school textbooks.  The difficulty is compounded by the fact that mathematical problems are rarely expressed as plain prose; they embed equations, require intermediate derivations (often best expressed as a chain of thought), and may involve the use of external tools such as computer algebra systems or programming environments.  Consequently, training a model that can handle these challenges demands both carefully curated data that reflect the true character of mathematical discourse and algorithmic innovations that guide the model toward reliable reasoning pathways.

Over the past few years, closed‑source models such as GPT‑4 \citep{gpt4} and Gemini‑Ultra \citep{gemini} have dramatically narrowed this gap by achieving high accuracy on competitive benchmarks like MATH \citep{MATH} and GSM8K \citep{gsm8k}.  These systems benefit from massive scales—both in the number of parameters (hundreds of billions) and in the breadth of proprietary training data—combined with sophisticated instruction tuning pipelines that incorporate chain‑of‑thought prompting, tool integration, and reinforcement learning from human feedback.  In stark contrast, publicly available open‑source models lag considerably behind.  Even strong general‑purpose candidates such as Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} or specialized instruction‑tuned variants exhibit accuracies on MATH that are more than ten percentage points lower than their closed counterparts.  The performance gap is especially pronounced on competition‑level problems where success hinges on deep symbolic insight rather than superficial pattern matching.

The DeepSeekMath project sets out to answer a series of intertwined research questions that arise from this landscape:

1. **Is it possible for an open‑source model of modest size (approximately 7 billion parameters) to match or surpass the mathematical reasoning capability of far larger proprietary systems?**  
   This question probes whether model scale alone dictates performance or whether data quality and training methodology can compensate for a smaller parameter count.

2. **What role does domain‑specific data play in shaping mathematical competence?**  
   Prior work has largely relied on English‑centric corpora derived from textbooks or scientific articles such as arXiv \citep{minerva}.  DeepSeekMath investigates whether publicly accessible web data—when selected through a systematic pipeline—contains sufficient high‑quality mathematical content to drive substantial gains.

3. **Does initializing a math‑focused model from a code‐oriented checkpoint provide an advantage?**  
   Code training endows models with an implicit understanding of algorithmic thinking, variable binding, and symbolic manipulation—all valuable for solving equations or generating program‐aided solutions.  The project examines whether starting from DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1.5 7B yields measurable improvements over purely language‐pretrained seeds.

4. **Can reinforcement learning be made more efficient for large language models without sacrificing performance?**  
   Standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) methods require a separate critic network to estimate value functions, incurring considerable memory overhead during training.  DeepSeekMath therefore introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant that replaces the critic with a baseline derived from groupwise reward averages, dramatically reducing resource consumption while preserving—or even enhancing—learning signals.

Addressing these questions required assembling a collection of contributions that together define the DeepSeekMath approach:

### Large‑Scale Math Pre‑Training Corpus  
The cornerstone of DeepSeekMath is the DeepSeekMath Corpus—a multilingual collection of roughly 120 billion tokens harvested from Common Crawl web pages using an iterative fastText classifier trained on OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}.  The pipeline starts with a modest seed set of high‑quality mathematics webpages, trains a fastText model to recall similar pages across billions of HTML documents, de‑duplicates candidates at both URL and near‑duplicate levels, ranks them by classifier confidence, and repeats the process across several iterations to expand coverage while maintaining precision.  Human annotation refines positive examples after each round, ensuring that noisy or irrelevant content is excluded.  Notably, the resulting corpus is seven times larger than the math web pages curated for Minerva \citep{minerva} and nine times larger than OpenWebMath itself.

### Code‑First Initialization Strategy  
DeepSeekMath leverages an unconventional starting point: instead of initializing from a generic language model checkpoint, it begins from DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1.5 7B—a model already specialized in source code generation across many programming languages.  Empirical investigation shows that this code‐first seed accelerates acquisition of procedural reasoning patterns that are directly transferable to mathematical problem solving—particularly when models are later prompted to emit Python programs (the “program‐of‐thought” paradigm).  Through continual pre‑training on mixed data (56 % math corpus, 20 % GitHub code, plus natural language text), DeepSeekMath cultivates an integrated skill set spanning symbolic logic, numeric computation, and software engineering constructs.

### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)  
To refine reasoning capabilities after supervision via instruction tuning—a stage termed “mathematical instruction tuning”—the authors develop GRPO as an efficient alternative to PPO.  Traditional PPO optimizes a surrogate objective involving policy ratios multiplied by an advantage term computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) against a learned value function \(V_{\psi}\).  This critic incurs memory usage comparable to the policy network itself because it must evaluate each token in every sampled trajectory.  GRPO sidesteps this requirement by sampling multiple candidate outputs \(\{o_{1},\dots,o_{G}\}\) per question from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\), scoring them with a reward model \(r_{\varphi}\), and constructing a baseline simply as the mean reward within each group.  The resulting advantage \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) for token \(t\) in output \(o_i\) becomes
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t}= \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)} ,
\]
where \(\mathbf r\) denotes the vector of group rewards.  Because only relative rankings within each group matter, GRPO aligns naturally with pairwise preference data used to train reward models while eliminating any separate value network.  An additional KL regularization term directly penalizes divergence between the updated policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) and a reference checkpoint \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), preserving language fluency without complicating advantage computation.

### Comprehensive Experimental Validation  
DeepSeekMath’s efficacy is demonstrated through an extensive suite of benchmark evaluations spanning eight English and Chinese mathematical datasets—including GSM8K \citep{gsm8k}, competition‐level MATH \citep{MATH}, SAT‑style problems \citep{llemma}, Stanford OCW courses \citep{minerva}, MMLU-STEM \citep{mmlu}, MGSM–zh \citep{mgsm}, CMATH \citep{wei2023cmath}, and Gaokao–Math variants \citep{agieval}.  The base model DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B attains \(64.2\,\%\) on GSM8K and \(36.2\,\%\) on MATH without any tool assistance—surpassing Minerva 540B despite being more than two orders of magnitude smaller in parameters—and further improves on Chinese benchmarks thanks to its multilingual corpus component.

After applying chain-of-thought (“CoT”) and program-of-thought (“PoT”) instruction tuning (yielding DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B), performance climbs dramatically: on MATH it exceeds all open-source peers in the \(7\,\text{B}\)-parameter range and rivals proprietary models such as Inflection‑2 or Gemini Pro despite their substantially larger scales.  When GRPO‐based reinforcement learning refines this instruction‐tuned checkpoint (producing DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B), accuracies reach \(88.2\,\%\) on GSM8K and \(51.7\,\%\) on MATH under CoT prompting—a milestone crossing the half‑point barrier on competition‐level mathematics for any openly released model.

In addition to pure quantitative gains, auxiliary evaluations reveal broader benefits: improvement on Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) shows enhanced general knowledge reasoning; gains on BIG‑Bench Hard (BBH) indicate stronger multi-step logical processing; and sustained coding performance on HumanEval and MBPP confirms that math pre-training does not erode code generation abilities inherited from the initial coder checkpoint.

Together these findings substantiate three central claims: (1) high‑quality public web data can serve as an effective foundation for mathematical competence; (2) leveraging code pretraining provides transferable reasoning scaffolding; and (3) reinforcement learning can be streamlined via GRPO without sacrificing performance gains—making advanced mathematical LLMs accessible within modest computational budgets.

The remainder of this textbook will delve deeper into each component introduced above: first exploring why mathematics challenges neural language systems; then detailing closed versus open state-of-the-art capabilities; thereafter dissecting each research question tackled by DeepSeekMath; finally presenting full technical expositions of the large‐scale corpus construction pipeline, code‐first initialization methodology, GRPO algorithmic design, and experimental protocol—including ablation studies that illuminate why these design choices matter for robust mathematical reasoning in LLMs.

Large Language Models & Benchmarks for Mathematical Reasoning  

# Transformer Decoder Architecture Recap  

Decoder‑only transformers form the computational backbone of modern large language models (LLMs). Each model consists of a stack of identical layers whose core operation is **self‑attention**: every token embedded in the input sequence attends to all previous tokens while respecting causality through a triangular mask so that predictions never depend on future symbols. An embedding matrix transforms raw lexical tokens into dense vectors; positional encodings are added so that the model can distinguish order despite its permutation‐invariant attention mechanism.  

Within a layer, self‑attention produces a weighted sum of value vectors, where weights are derived from dot products between query and key vectors followed by softmax normalization. The resulting context vector passes through a feed‑forward network comprising two linear projections separated by a nonlinearity (typically GeLU). Residual connections wrap both attention and feed‑forward blocks, stabilizing training through identity shortcuts, while layer normalization keeps activation statistics well behaved at depth.  

The entire stack operates autoregressively: given a prefix \(x_{<t}\), the model computes a conditional distribution \(\pi_{\theta}(x_{t}\mid x_{<t})\). Generation proceeds token by token until an end‑of‐sequence marker appears or a maximum context length—often \(4\,000\) tokens in practice—is reached \citep{deepseek-coder}. This policy view underlies later reinforcement‑learning stages where one treats the decoder’s probability assignment as an action distribution over discrete symbols (Section “Reinforcement Learning”). Because every forward pass depends only on previously generated symbols, decoding can be parallelized across GPUs during pre‑training yet remains sequential during inference—a trade­off crucial for scaling models such as DeepSeekMath \(7\) B parameters.



# Pre‑training versus Instruction Fine‑tuning Paradigm  

Training an LLM typically proceeds in two successive stages whose goals differ markedly yet complement one another.  

**Pre‑training** exploits massive collections of raw text—from web pages crawled by Common Crawl to source code repositories—to teach the decoder general language patterns via next‑token prediction loss (also called causal language modeling). In DeepSeekMath \(7\) B the authors continue pre‑training a code–oriented base model (`DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7 B`) on an additional \(120\,\text{B}\) mathematical tokens drawn from carefully filtered web pages (`DeepSeekMath Corpus`). This stage supplies domain expertise: exposure to algebraic symbols, mathematical terminology, proof fragments, and informal derivations gives the network latent competence at manipulating quantities even before seeing any human instructions.\footnote{The fraction of mathematics‐related data amounts to roughly $56$\% of total tokens fed during continued training (Section “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath–Base 7 B”).}  

**Instruction fine‑tuning** reshapes this broadly knowledgeable network into an assistant that follows user prompts reliably —especially prompts asking for step‑by‑step explanations (“chain‑of‑thought”), program synthesis (“program‐of‑thought”), or tool integration (“tool‐integrated reasoning”). For DeepSeekMath it involves \(776\,000\) curated examples pairing problems with detailed solutions drawn from English datasets such as GSM8K and MathInstruct and from Chinese K–12 collections spanning \(76\) topics (Section “SFT Data Curation”). During this supervised stage SFT minimizes a standard cross entropy loss between model outputs and human–written targets while preserving fluency learned during pre‑training.\footnote{A batch size of \(256\) examples over four thousand token contexts was used together with a constant learning rate \(5\times10^{-5}\).}  

The paradigm gains further nuance when one adds **reinforcement learning** (§ “Reinforcement Learning”). Here the policy derived from SFT serves as an initialization for actor updates driven by reward signals produced either by handcrafted correctness checks (“outcome supervision”) or learned reward models (“process supervision”). By iteratively improving both policy and reward estimator—as done with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—the system learns subtle preferences such as preferring shorter derivations without sacrificing correctness.\footnote{GRPO eliminates an auxiliary critic network by using group‐wise average rewards as baselines, reducing memory demand relative to classic PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal}.}



# Quantitative Reasoning Benchmarks  

Assessing whether an LLM truly reasons mathematically requires carefully curated test suites spanning several difficulty regimes and interaction styles. The study employs six English‐language quantitative benchmarks whose designs expose different facets of numerical cognition.



### GSM8K  

GSM8K consists of grade‑school arithmetic word problems collected from online tutoring sites \citep{gsm8k}. Each question expects a brief natural language answer accompanied by an explicit chain‐of‐thought narrative explaining every intermediate computation step—a format well suited for probing autoregressive generation without external tools.\footnote{DeepSeekMath–Base \(7\,\text{B}\) reaches $64.\!2$\%\ accuracy on this benchmark after pre‑training alone; after reinforcement learning this climbs sharply to $88.\!2$\%.}  


### MATH  

The competition–level **MATH** dataset aggregates problems drawn from national mathematics contests spanning middle school through graduate Olympiad levels \citep{MATH}. Solutions demand rigorous symbolic manipulation together with logical justification; unlike GSM8K there is little tolerance for heuristic guesses—the answer must be exact.\footnote{DeepSeal… obtains $36.\!2$\%\ accuracy after pure math pre‑training; following instruction tuning it surpasses $50$\%\ without any tool assistance—a first among open‐source systems—as reported in the abstract ($51.\!7$\%). With self‐consistency over $64$ sampled completions it reaches $60.\!9$\%.}  


### SAT Arithmetic Subset  

A subset derived from publicly released SAT mathematics items focuses exclusively on elementary algebraic manipulation under time pressure.\footnote{Results displayed alongside other quantitative tasks indicate comparable progress patterns between GSM8K and SAT when applying chain‐of‐thought prompting.*}  


### OCW Courses  

OpenCourseWare (OCW) courses provide university­-level problem sets extracted from lecture notes across calculus, linear algebra, probability theory and more (see “OCW Courses” citation \citep{minerva}). These items probe higher‐order abstraction abilities typical of undergraduate curricula rather than rote computation.



All six benches share two common evaluation protocols applied throughout the work:


* **Zero/one-shot prompting:** The model receives only the problem statement (zero-shot) or a handful (\(\leq4\)) exemplars illustrating chain‐of‐thought style solutions (few shot).  
* **Tool–free generation:** Outputs must be self‐contained natural language derivations unless explicitly allowed to invoke Python libraries via program‑of‑thought formats.



Together these datasets constitute a gradient ranging from straightforward arithmetic toward sophisticated contest problems—making them ideal yardsticks when tracking progress across pre‑training → SFT → reinforcement phases.



# Chinese Benchmarks  

Open source progress in mathematical reasoning has historically concentrated on English corpora; nevertheless many educational systems rely heavily on Mandarin resources—a gap directly addressed by DeepSeal’s multilingual pipeline.


### CMATH  

CMATH comprises Chinese K–12 mathematics problems spanning categories such as linear equations, combinatorics, geometry basics and elementary probability (\citep{wei2023cmath}). Like GSM8K it asks for stepwise natural language explanations written entirely in Mandarin characters.


### MGSM‑zh  

MGSM−zh mirrors English GSM8K but translated into simplified Chinese scripts (\citep{mgsm}). This benchmark evaluates whether skills transferred during multilingual pre­training survive language conversion—a crucial capability since downstream applications often mix languages.



Both CMATH and MGSM−zh benefit noticeably from inclusion of non‑English webpages during data collection phase (Section “Data Collection”). Empirically “the deepgen… corpus enhances mathematical reasoning performance both in English *and* Chinese” scores indicate multilingually trained models close gaps left by monolingual baselines such as MathPile (English dominant).



# Multi-choice STEM Tasks (`MMLU–STEM`)  

The Massive Multitask Language Understanding (`MMLU`) suite evaluates broad knowledge across diverse subjects via multiple choice questions~\citep{mmlu}. The **STEM** slice isolates science / technology / engineering / mathematics items—all formatted as four-option MCQs demanding factual recall combined with elementary logical deduction.


Unlike free-form numeric answers used in GSM8K or MATH, MCQ format probes whether a model’s internal representations rank correct choices higher than distractors—a complementary perspective called *ranking accuracy*. After reward–guided reinforcement learning (`DeepSeekMath–RL`) there is measurable uplift even on this out-of-domain slice despite its different output modality (\textit{i.e.,} selecting letters instead of generating expressions). This suggests that strengthening chain-of-thought abilities also refines latent knowledge retrieval mechanisms relevant across task families.



# Formal Theorem Proving Benchmark (`miniF2F`)  

A distinct strand of mathematical AI research focuses on *formal* verification—translating informal statements into machine-checkable proofs within interactive theorem provers such as Isabelle~\citep{isabelle}. The **miniF2F** benchmark assembles Olympiad­-level propositions together with their informal proofs (\citet{dsp_proof}); successful models first generate a skeletal “proof sketch” which then passes through Isabelle’s automated prover (*Sledgehammer*) to fill low‑level proof obligations.


Success rates recorded for `DeepSeekMath–Base 7 B` demonstrate “strong few-shot autoformalization performance” relative to prior open-source systems (\citet{minif2f}). Though absolute scores remain modest compared with closed heavyweight counterparts (\citet{gpt4}), demonstrating any competence at this level solely through textual pre-training marks an important milestone—the model has internalized enough logical structure to propose viable intermediate lemmas without explicit symbolic guidance.



# General Language Understanding and Coding Suites  

Beyond mathematical aptitude it remains essential to gauge whether specialized data collection harms broader linguistic capabilities.


### Massive Multitask Language Understanding (`MMLU`)  

The full `MMLU` benchmark spans \(57\) subjects—including history, law, literature—and reports accuracy over expert-crafted multiple choice items~\citep{mmlu}. After math pre-training , `DeepSeekMath–Base 7 B` shows “significant enhancements” relative to its precursor (`DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.`), indicating transfer benefits accrue even when questions lie far outside mathematics.


### BIGBench Hard (`BBH`)  

`BBH` aggregates twenty-three challenging tasks deliberately selected for multi-step logical demands exceeding simple pattern matching~\citep{bbh}. Gains reported after mathematics-aware fine-tuning suggest that practicing long chains of deductive reasoning translates into better handling of arbitrary non-mathematical reasoning pipelines.


### Coding Benchmarks (`HumanEval` & `MBPP`)  

To certify retention of programming proficiency—as vital for program-aided mathematics—the authors evaluate against two standard code generation suites:\newline
* **HumanEval**, featuring Python functions paired with unit tests~\citep{codex},  
* **MBPP**, containing short programming problems requiring concise implementations~\citep{mbpp}.  


`DeepSeekMath–Base` maintains parity with its underlying code-trained ancestor while simultaneously excelling at numeric tasks—a compelling illustration that targeted domain data does **not** catastrophically erase previously acquired skills.


Collectively these three families furnish a holistic picture: improvements observed on purely mathematical probes coexist with stable—or even enhanced—performance on general linguistic understanding (`MMLU`), intricate commonsense/logic challenges (`BBH`), and software synthesis (`HumanEval`, `MBPP`). Such breadth validates the overarching claim articulated throughout the work that “math training benefits both language understanding and reasoning performance” (\citet{}).

## Synthesis  

The architectural simplicity of decoder-only transformers allows seamless integration of ever larger corpora—from generic web text up through specially curated mathematics pages (~\(120\,\textbf B}\)). When coupled with staged learning—in which unsupervised scale builds raw syntax awareness followed by instruction fine-tuning that injects disciplined problem solving habits—the resulting systems display dramatic leaps across every benchmark described above:  
* From modest baseline scores (\(36.\!2$\%\! overall competition maths),  
* To instruction-tuned proficiency exceeding $50$\%\ accuracy without external tools (*first open-source system* achieving this threshold),  
* And finally reaching $51.\!7$\%\ compound accuracy plus $60.\!9$\%\ under self-consistency techniques after reinforcement learning (`GRPO`).  

Moreover these gains extend beyond pure arithmetic—to multiple-choice STEM assessment (`MMLU–STEM`), formal theorem proving (`miniF2F`), cross-lingual math tests (`CMATH`, `MGSM−zh`)—and preserve general-purpose abilities measured through `MMLU`, `BBH`, `HumanEval`, and `MBPP`. Consequently these suites collectively function as reliable yardsticks guiding future research avenues such as larger multilingual corpora construction, more expressive reward modeling, or advanced sampling strategies—all themes highlighted later in the discussion sections.

Understanding both *how* modern LLMs operate internally (Section “Transformer Decoder Architecture Recap”) *and* *what* external pressures shape their abilities (Section “Pre-training versus Instruction Fine-tuning”) equips researchers with the conceptual tools needed to interpret performance tables like those reported throughout this work—and ultimately fuels progress toward ever more capable mathematical AI systems.)

Prompt Engineering for Structured Math Solving  

Modern large language models acquire remarkable ability to solve mathematical problems when their inputs are carefully crafted – instead of asking a model simply “What is \(12\times13\)?”, one supplies a structured prompt that guides the generation process step by step, encourages interaction with external computation tools, or asks the model itself to write a short program whose execution yields the answer.  
The DeepSeekMath series illustrates how three complementary prompting paradigms—Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT), Program‑of‑Thought / PAL, and tool–integrated interaction à la ReAct/ToRA—can be combined into an instructional fine‑tuning corpus that drives a \(7\,\text{billion}\)-parameter model close to the level attained by proprietary systems such as GPT‑4. The following sections describe each paradigm in detail, expose their empirical impact reported in the DeepSeekMath experiments, and explain how they guided the construction of millions of training examples.



# Chain‑of‑thought prompting principle and few‑shot usage  

Chain‑of‑thought prompting asks a language model to produce an explicit intermediate reasoning trace before stating its final answer.  Rather than emitting only the conclusion, the model generates sentences that correspond closely to how a human would write down deductions (“First compute … Then substitute … Finally …”).  
In practice one supplies a few exemplars – typically two or three solved problems written in this style – as part of the prompt (“few shot”). When presented with a new question the model continues the pattern established by the exemplars, thereby keeping track of intermediate quantities and logical dependencies across many tokens.



The DeepSeekMath experiments demonstrate that this simple change yields dramatic gains across eight benchmarks ranging from elementary quantitative tests (e.g., GSM8K) to competition‐level contests (MATH) and even Chinese datasets such as CMATH and Gaokao variants.  
When evaluated under few‐shot CoT covering both English and Chinese problems, DeepSeekMath‑Base \(7\,\text{B}\) surpassed every publicly available base model recorded in the literature—including much larger models trained without specialized mathematics data—and exceeded a closed \(540\,\text{B}\)-parameter baseline by more than ten percentage points on MATH alone.


Intuitively, forcing an explicit chain makes it harder for the decoder to “jump” directly to an incorrect result because any mistake must survive scrutiny along several successive steps encoded in natural language tokens. Moreover, during reinforcement learning stages later on (see Section ‘Self‑consistency’), having access to full traces lets reward models evaluate whether every intermediate statement aligns with known algebraic rules rather than merely checking whether the final numeral matches a ground truth answer.



# Program‑of‑thought / PAL: generating executable Python snippets  

Program‑of‑thought—or its variant called PAL—is a prompting strategy that asks the model not only *what* answer follows logically from a problem statement but *how* one would obtain it using an actual programming language such as Python.  
A typical PAL prompt displays several demonstrations wherein each problem is followed by a short script employing standard libraries (`math`, `sympy`, …) that performs exact arithmetic or symbolic manipulation; after execution, the script prints the desired numeral.


DeepSeekMath leverages this paradigm heavily because its underlying weights originated from DeepSeek Coder Base v1½ \(7\,\text{B}\), whose pretraining emphasized source code consumption and generation across billions of GitHub tokens. This prior exposure makes DeepSeekMath unusually proficient at producing syntactically correct Python fragments even when constrained to generate them under strict length limits (\(1024\,\text{tokens}\) maximum).  

Empirically, when evaluated on GSM8K and MATH under few‐shot program‐of‐thought prompting—the same set used for pure CoT—the \(7\,\text{B}\) checkpoint outperformed Llemma 34 B (the former state‐of‐the​art open source system specialized for coding) despite having far fewer parameters.<sup>\[citation to pot and pal]\</sup>  
The study further reports that adding a dedicated coding phase before any mathematics–focused training already improves programmatically assisted solving, while subsequent math pretraining refines numerical grounding and symbolic reasoning capabilities.



# Tool‐integrated reasoning such as ReAct / ToRA style interactions  

Beyond writing pure code blocks, recent work has explored architectures wherein a language model alternates “thinking’’ steps with explicit calls to auxiliary tools—a pattern popularized under names such as ReAct​<sup>\[citation]</sup> and ToRA​<sup>\[citation]</sup>.  
A typical interaction follows three phases repeated iteratively:

1️⃣ **Think** The model produces a natural–language rationale describing what information it lacks or what computation must be performed next.  
2️⃣ **Act** It emits a special token indicating which external service should run (e.g., “⟨run_python⟩” together with accompanying code).  
3️⃣ **Observe** The system executes the requested operation and feeds back its output so that subsequent thinking can incorporate this newly acquired result.


DeepSeekMath’s supervised instruction set contains “tool–integrated solutions’’ crafted precisely for this workflow: GSM8K and MATH questions were manually annotated so that their reference answers combine ordinary explanatory sentences together with one or more Python invocations using `math` or `sympy`. When inference allows these actions—as opposed to restricting generation strictly to text—the \(7\,\text{B}\) checkpoint reaches nearly sixty per cent accuracy on MATH, comfortably exceeding every other open–source system tested under identical conditions.



# Self‑consistency inference by aggregating multiple sampled answers  

Even with sophisticated prompts, stochastic decoding sometimes yields erroneous intermediate steps that nevertheless lead toward a correct conclusion in some runs while failing in others—a phenomenon especially pronounced when temperature sampling creates divergent logical pathways.^[citation]  
Self‑consistency mitigates this variability through ensembling at inference time:


* For each question one draws **many** completions independently (DeepSeekMath used sixty four samples).  
* Each sampled response follows one of the structured formats described above (CoT or PAL).  
* After decoding finishes one extracts the final numerical answer from every trace and applies majority voting.



Because correct solutions tend to appear repeatedly among diverse stochastic trajectories—even when individual runs contain minor slips—the consensus most often coincides with the true answer while isolated mistakes are drowned out.^[citation]  
Applying self‑consistency on top of raw chain­-of­-thought generation boosted DeepSeekMath’s competition–level score on MATH from roughly fifty­-one point seven per cent up past sixty point nine per cent—a gain comparable to adding another training stage yet requiring no additional gradient updates.



# How these formats shape the construction of the instruction‑tuning dataset  

The success reported above hinges critically on exposing the model during supervised fine–tuning (SFT) to all three structured response styles simultaneously.


* **English sources** were drawn primarily from GSM8K & MATH together with subsets of MathInstruct​<sup>\[citation]</sup> where authors had already furnished either pure CoT explanations or full program fragments (§SFT Data Curation).  
* **Chinese sources** consisted of K–12 examination items spanning dozens of subtopics; each was solved twice—once narratively following CoT conventions and once through an explicit program fragment parallel to PAL.<sup>\[citation]</sup>  

All together these examples totalled **seven hundred seventy six thousand** paired entries (“question → solution’’ instances). During training each example was concatenated randomly until reaching roughly four thousand tokens—the maximum context length supported by DeepSeek’s architecture—and fed through standard cross–entropy optimization for five hundred update steps at a constant learning rate.



By deliberately mixing languages, task difficulties, and output modalities within one unified corpus, DeepSeekMath learns how to recognize which format best matches a given prompt cue (“solve this problem stepwise’’ versus “write Python code’’), how to transition between thinking phases and tool actions when appropriate, and finally how to produce internally consistent traces amenable both to human inspection and automatic reward modeling later used during reinforcement learning.
