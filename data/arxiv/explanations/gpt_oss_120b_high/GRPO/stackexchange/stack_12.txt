### Why does reinforcement learning improve Maj@K accuracy but not Pass@K on MATH according to Figure 5?
Question:
"After applying GRPO Maj@K improves whereas Pass@K stays roughly unchanged" (Section 4). If Maj@K measures majority vote over $K$ samples while Pass@K measures whether at least one sample among $K$ is correct,
the observation suggests RL makes correct answers more probable but does not raise their absolute probability above some threshold.
Could someone explain why updating policy via outcome/process-supervised rewards leads specifically to higher consensus among samples rather than higher peak correctness?

**Short answer:**  
The GRPO reinforcement‑learning step reshapes the *distribution* of answers rather than raising the *absolute* chance of a correct answer. Because the objective uses a **group‑relative** advantage (the reward of each sample is normalized by the mean and variance of the other samples in the same batch), the training signal tells the model “*make this answer relatively better than its siblings*”. This pushes probability mass toward a single high‑scoring mode and away from all low‑scoring modes. As a result many sampled completions start to agree with each other (the majority vote over $K$ samples – **Maj@K** – goes up) while the overall probability that *any* sample is correct – which depends on how often a high‑probability mode crosses an absolute correctness threshold – hardly changes, so **Pass@K** stays flat.

Below is a more detailed walk‑through grounded in the paper’s own analysis.

---

### 1\. What Maj@K and Pass@K measure

* **Maj@K** counts a problem as solved when at least half of $K$ independently sampled completions give the *same* final answer (a majority vote).  
* **Pass@K** counts a problem as solved when *any* of those $k$ completions yields a correct answer.  

Thus Maj@k is sensitive to **consensus**, whereas Pass@\emph{k} is sensitive to **coverage** of at least one lucky draw.
