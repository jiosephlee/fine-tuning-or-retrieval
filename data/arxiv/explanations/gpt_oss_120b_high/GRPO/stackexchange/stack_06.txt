\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why do arXiv papers seem ineffective for improving mathematical reasoning according to this work?
Question:
The authors train models on MathPile (~85 % arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama corpora yet observe no notable gains—or even degradation—on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F benchmarks (Tables X-Y). I wonder whether methodological factors could explain this result:
• Were arXiv documents preprocessed into raw LaTeX without extracting equations or proofs?
• Might the token budget be dominated by non-mathematical prose thereby diluting useful signal?
• Could model scale (1.3 B / 7 B) be too small to benefit from highly technical scientific text?
I would like an explanation of why arXiv data did not help while web-scraped math pages did.
Answer:
The authors’ experiments show that pre‑training on corpora that are dominated by arXiv papers—MathPile ($\approx 85\%$ arXiv) and the full‑text ArXiv‑RedPajama dump—does **not** raise performance on any of the mathematical benchmarks they evaluate (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, miniF2F), and in some cases even hurts it (Tables X–Y).  Several concrete factors discussed in the paper explain why the arXiv material turns out to be ineffective while web‑scraped math pages do help.

**1 – Low signal‑to‑noise ratio of the tokens**  
Both MathPile and ArXiv‑RedPajama contain a very large amount of “non‑mathematical” prose.  Even after de‑duplication and basic cleaning the majority of tokens are narrative text, citation strings or bibliography entries rather than equations, definitions or step‑by‑step solutions.  Since the downstream tasks are solved by chain‑of‑thought or program‑of‑thought prompting—i.e., by generating explicit reasoning traces—the model benefits most from data where such traces are already present.  A token budget that is flooded with ordinary exposition therefore dilutes the useful mathematical signal and offers little guidance for learning the kind of structured reasoning required by GSM8K or MATH.

**2 – How the LaTeX was kept**  
The ArXiv‑RedPajama preprocessing removes only preambles, comments, macros and bibliographies (Section “Ar X iv-RedPajama”).  It does **not** extract raw equations or isolate proof fragments; instead the raw LaTeXS source—including many control sequences such as “\\begin{proof}”, “\\frac”, “\\mathbf”, etc.—remains as part of the token stream.  For a 7 B​−parameter model these rare symbols consume capacity without providing clear learning targets because they seldom appear in the evaluation prompts (which are given in plain English).  In contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus is built by a fastText classifier that deliberately ranks pages according to how much *visible* mathematical content they contain (problem statements together with worked solutions), thereby delivering a higher density of actionable patterns.

**3 – Domain mismatch**  
Research papers are written for specialists and typically present results in a terse style: a theorem statement followed quickly by a proof sketch that assumes many background facts.  Benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH consist of elementary​−to​−college problems phrased in everyday language with detailed solution steps.  Training on scholarly exposition therefore gives the model an experience distribution that is only loosely aligned with what it has to generate at test time—a classic case of domain shift.  The authors explicitly note that “ar X iv papers are commonly included … but detailed analysis … has not been extensively conducted” and their own ablations reveal that this mismatch leads to “no notable improvements or even deterioration” across all tested benchmarks (Section “Ar X iv Papers Seem Ineffective…”).

**4 – Model capacity limits**  
The two model families examined—DeepSeek​−LLM 1.3 B and DeepSeek​−Coder​−Base v1.5 7 B—are relatively small compared with the hundreds of billions of parameters used in closed​−source systems such as Minerva 540 B or Gemini Ultra.  With limited capacity it is harder for these models to extract subtle high​−level ideas from dense academic text; they quickly run out of parameters on learning exotic terminology rather than core arithmetic manipulation.  The same paper shows that when **code** data is added before math training it yields consistent gains even at this scale (Section “Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning”), suggesting that well​−structured, executable text provides a stronger learning signal than raw research prose for small models .

**5 – Quality of the web​−scraped data**  
The DeepSeekMath Corpus was assembled through an iterative pipeline that starts from a seed set (OpenWebMath) and repeatedly refines a fastText classifier using human annotations (Section “Data Collection”).  This pipeline deliberately favors pages whose content resembles benchmark tasks—clear problem statements followed by explicit reasoning—so each token carries higher *educational* value.  Empirically such corpus yields steep learning curves (Figure 3) whereas MathPile plateaus quickly despite being orders of magnitude larger in token count because much of its material is low​−quality filler from ar X iv .

Putting these pieces together, the lack of improvement from ar X iv data can be attributed primarily to **(i)** its low density of usable mathematical reasoning traces once tokenized, **(ii)** residual LaTeXS markup that does not match downstream prompt format, **(iii)** a stylistic gap between scholarly exposition and benchmark problems, and **(iv)** insufficient model capacity to turn noisy scholarly text into effective reasoning abilities.  The authors also caution that their conclusions are limited to current scales and training setups—they have not yet tested whether larger models or mixtures of ar X iv with other high​−quality sources might eventually benefit from academic papers (Section “Limitations”).  

In short, ar X iv papers are plentiful but they do not provide the kind of *instructional* mathematical signal that small LLMs need; curated web pages filtered for dense problem–solution pairs do.
