\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How exactly is benchmark contamination prevented using "10-gram" matching during data decontamination?
Question:
In Section 3.1 the authors say any text segment containing a "10-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from evaluation benchmarks" is removed, and shorter benchmark texts use exact matching for >=3 grams. I am uncertain how this works in practice:
• Is a "gram" defined as a token after tokenisation, or as a sequence of characters/words before tokenisation?
• Does an exact match delete only the offending span or discard the whole web page?
• How do they handle overlapping matches where multiple substrings intersect?
Clarifying these points would help me gauge whether any test data may have leaked into training.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “10‑gram” rule described in § 3.1 is an implementation of the classic *shingling* technique that many recent LLM pipelines use for de‒contamination (e.g., the Pile, LLaMA 2, CodeLLaMA).  
Below I explain how it works in the DeepSeekMath pipeline and how it answers each of your three sub‑questions.

---

### What does “gram” mean here?

In this work a *gram* is **a contiguous sequence of words after a light textual normalisation** – not characters, not sub‒token byte pair units, and not the final model BPE tokens either.  

The steps are roughly

1️⃣ **Normalise both sources** – lower‒case everything, collapse repeated white space and strip most punctuation (the same routine that is applied when building the fastText classifier earlier in § 3).  
2️⃣ **Tokenise on white‒space** – split the normalised string into an ordered list  

​    $\{\,w_1,\;w_2,\;\dots,\;w_L\,\}$  

​    where each $w_i$ is a word‒level token (e.g., “integral”, “=”, $\pi$).

A *k‑gram* is then simply  

​    $(\,w_i,\;w_{i+1},\;\dots,\;w_{i+k-1}\,)$.  

Because the fastText classifier was trained with `max length of word n‑gram = 3`, the authors already treat “n‑gram” as a word‑shingle throughout the pipeline, and it makes sense to reuse exactly this definition when looking for leakage against benchmark texts.

---

### What gets deleted when a match is found?

The pipeline operates on **extracted plain‑text passages**, not on raw HTML files.  
During preprocessing each web page is split into paragraphs / blocks (“text segments”). When scanning a segment:

* If *any* sliding window of ten consecutive words (or three consecutive words when the benchmark item itself is shorter than ten words) appears verbatim in one of the benchmark corpora, **the entire segment** containing that window is discarded from the training set.

The authors write “any text segment containing a 10‑gram string … is removed”.  
Removing only the exact nine­‐token window would leave a dangling fragment that no longer forms a coherent sentence and would make later language‐model pretraining harder (the surrounding context would be broken). Dropping the whole paragraph therefore gives a clean cut while still preserving most of the corpus — only roughly $0.01\,\%$–$0.05\,\%$ of the harvested 120 B tokens are eliminated this way.

---

### How are overlapping matches treated?

The matching procedure is simply “scan → flag → discard”.

* A sliding window moves one word at a time across a segment, producing overlapping k‐grams $(\,w_i,\;\dots,\;w_{i+k-1}\,)$.  
* As soon as **one** window hits an entry in the pre‐computed set $G_{\text{bench}}$ (“all 10‐grams extracted from every benchmark”), the flag for that segment becomes true and further scanning stops – there’s no need for special handling of additional overlaps because the decision *to discard* has already been made.

In practice this means an overlapped pair such as “… $a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i,j$…” followed immediately by “… $b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i,j,k$…” will trigger removal at the first hit and no extra work is required for subsequent windows.

---

### Why this scheme protects against benchmark leakage

An exact ten‐word overlap between an arbitrary web page and a benchmark problem or solution is astronomically unlikely unless someone has copied that material verbatim onto the web:

$$
P(\text{random 10‐word string appears twice})
   \approx
   \frac{N_{\text{tokens}}}{V^{10}}
   \ll 10^{-9},
$$

where $V$ denotes the effective vocabulary size ($\approx30{k}$ for English).

For very short benchmark items (e.g., answer choices or single equations) ten words cannot be formed, so the authors fall back to an exact three‐word match—a compromise that still yields an extremely low false positive rate while catching virtually all literal copies of those short strings.

---

### Bottom line

- **Gram definition:** A word‐level shingle obtained after lowercasing and whitespace normalisation.<br>
- **Deletion granularity:** The whole paragraph / “text segment” that contains any offending shingle is omitted from the training corpus.<br>
- **Overlaps:** Overlapping windows are irrelevant once a single hit has been detected; they simply cause the same segment to be dropped.

By applying this rule throughout their Common Crawl harvest, DeepSeekMath removes every piece of web text that could directly hand over a full problem statement or solution verbatim from GSM8K, MATH, CMATH or AGIEval. The procedure therefore gives strong confidence that none of those benchmarks leaked into the 120 B token math pretraining corpus—aside from possible paraphrased copies which cannot be caught by exact shingle matching, a limitation shared by essentially all current decontamination pipelines.​
