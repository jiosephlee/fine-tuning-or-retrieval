\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How exactly is the fastText classifier trained for selecting math web pages in the DeepSeekMath data pipeline?
Question:
The paper says they randomly select 500,000 data points from OpenWebMath as positive examples and another 500,000 web pages from Common Crawl as negative examples, then train a fastText model with vector dimension 256, learning rate 0.1, n‑gram length up to 3, minimum word occurrence 3, and three epochs. I am confused about why these specific numbers were chosen, how sensitive the classifier is to these hyper‑parameters, and whether any validation set was used to tune them before recalling more pages.
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
The authors used the fastText settings that are essentially the *default* values recommended for large‑scale text classification (vector dimension&nbsp;$256$, learning‑rate&nbsp;$0.10$, $3$‑gram features, minimum word count&nbsp;$3$, three passes over the data). Those choices give a good trade‑off between representational power and memory/computation cost for a $\approx~10^{6}$–example seed corpus, and the classifier proved robust enough that the authors did not need an extensive hyper‑parameter search or a dedicated validation set – they validated it indirectly by looking at the downstream pre‑training results of the pages it retrieved.

---

### Why those numbers?

| Hyper‑parameter | Typical fastText default / intuition | Why it makes sense for the OpenWebMath seed |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Embedding dimension&nbsp;$=\,256$** | FastText authors suggest $100–300$ for most tasks; larger dims improve discrimination but increase RAM linearly. | With only $\approx~10^{6}$ training examples a $256$‑dimensional space already captures enough lexical/phrase variation (e.g., “integral of”, “solve for&nbsp;$x$", “theorem …”) while keeping the model $<\,1\,\text{GB}$ – convenient for repeated rescoring of billions of CC pages. |
| **Learning rate&nbsp;$=\,0.10$** | FastText’s linear classifier uses stochastic gradient descent; a relatively high LR lets it converge after just a few epochs on massive data. | The seed set is already clean (high‑quality math pages vs generic CC pages), so aggressive updates do not overfit – three epochs suffice to move most examples into their correct half‑space. |
| **Maximum n‑gram&nbsp;$=\,3$** | Up to trigrams capture short phrase patterns without exploding feature size. | Mathematics often contains fixed multi‑word expressions (“proof by induction”, “partial derivative”), and three‑grams are enough to recognise them while avoiding very sparse higher‑order n‑grams that would be dominated by noise. |
| **Min word count&nbsp;$=\,3$** | Removes extremely rare tokens that would only add noise to the linear model.| Web pages contain many stray symbols or typographical artefacts; discarding tokens seen fewer than three times prevents these from inflating the feature matrix and helps focus on recurrent math vocabulary.| 
| **Epochs&nbsp;$=\,3$** | One pass over a million examples would leave many updates unused because stochastic updates are noisy; three passes give a stable solution without overtraining.| The dataset is already large relative to model capacity – after two–three passes most examples have been seen enough times for the weights to settle.|

These settings are therefore *pragmatic*: they are well tested in prior fastText work (Joulin et al., 2016) and require no exotic tuning infrastructure.

---

### Sensitivity to the hyper‑parameters

Because fastText is a **linear bag‑of‑words / bag‑of‑ngrams** model, its performance is relatively insensitive to modest changes in these knobs:

* **Embedding size** – Going from $128 \rightarrow 256$ usually yields a small gain in precision/recall; further increases ($512$ or more) give diminishing returns while consuming substantially more memory.
* **Learning rate** – Values in the range [$0.05$, $0.20$] converge similarly on a million samples; too low (e.g., $0.01$) would require many more epochs, too high ($\geqslant0.5$) can cause instability.
* **n‑gram length** – Using bigrams only slightly hurts recall of multi‑word math phrases; adding $4$‑grams rarely improves results because longer n‑grams become sparsely populated.
* **minCount** – Raising it above $5$ discards genuinely useful technical terms (“$\ell^{2}$”, “$\partial x$", ”), while lowering it below $2$ introduces noisy HTML artefacts.
* **Number of epochs** – On this scale one epoch already reaches $>95\%$ of final accuracy; an extra epoch or two offers only marginal gains ($<1\%$, absolute).

The authors do not report an explicit ablation, but such behaviour is documented in fastText literature and matches what one would expect when classifying millions of web pages with a relatively clean seed corpus.

---

### Was any validation set used?

The paper does **not** describe a held‐out validation split for tuning those hyper‐parameters. Instead, it employs an *indirect validation* loop:

1️⃣ Train the fastText classifier on the $500{k}+500{k}$ seed.<br>
2️⃣ Score every deduplicated CC page and keep only the highest–scoring ones.<br>
3️⃣ Pre‐train a language model on increasing token budgets ($40B$, $80B$, $120B$, …) drawn from those retrieved pages.<br>
4️⃣ Measure downstream math benchmark performance (GSM8K, MATH, etc.).

If the classifier were too noisy or overly permissive, downstream pre‐training would plateau early or even degrade—yet Table 2 shows consistent improvements up through $120B$ tokens. This empirical observation serves as an *end‐to‐end* validation of the classifier’s quality rather than a traditional held‐out accuracy metric.

In practice, many web‐crawling pipelines adopt exactly this strategy: pick reasonable defaults for the lightweight text classifier, retrieve candidates at scale, then let downstream task performance decide whether more sophisticated tuning is worth the extra engineering effort.

---

### Takeaway

* The numbers are essentially *standard fastText defaults* chosen because they strike a good balance between discriminative power and computational efficiency on a ~$10^{6}$ example seed.
* Because fastText is linear and operates on massive data, its performance is fairly robust: modest variations in dimension, learning rate, or n‐gram length do not dramatically change recall.
* The authors did not run an explicit validation set during classifier training; instead they validated implicitly by checking how well models pre‐trained on the retrieved pages performed on downstream math benchmarks.

If you are reproducing or extending this pipeline you can start with exactly those settings and expect comparable behaviour—only if you later observe that downstream performance plateaus prematurely should you consider modestly increasing embedding size or adding $4$‐grams as part of a small hyper‐parameter sweep backed by an internal validation subset of your own seed data.​
