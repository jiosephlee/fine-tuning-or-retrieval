\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does Group Relative Policy Optimization compute its advantage without a learned value function?
Question:
I am reading Section 4 where the authors introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). They state that instead of training a separate critic network as in Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), GRPO uses "the average reward of multiple sampled outputs … as the baseline." I am confused about the exact computation:
• Do they subtract the mean reward of the group from each individual reward before normalising?
• Is this same scalar baseline applied to every token $t$ of an output $o_i$, i.e., $\hat{A}_{i,t}= \tilde r_i$?
• How does this method achieve variance reduction compared with Generalized Advantage Estimation used in PPO?
Any concrete formula or small example would help me understand how $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is derived.
Answer:
**Answer**

In Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) the “advantage” is obtained without a separate critic network by exploiting the fact that we generate *many* responses for the same question at every training step.  
The key idea is that the average (or more precisely the *z‑score*) of the rewards of these responses provides a low‑variance estimator of the expected return for that question, and this estimator can be used as the baseline in the usual policy‑gradient term  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\!\log \pi_{\theta}(a|s)\;\bigl(R-b\bigr).
$$

Below I walk through the exact computation that appears in the paper, answer your three sub‑questions, and then explain why this yields variance reduction compared with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) used in PPO.

---

### 1 How GRPO builds the advantage

For each training question $q$ we sample a *group* of $G$ completions  

$$
\{\,o_{1}, o_{2},\,\dots , o_{G}\,\}\sim
   \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,|q)\,. 
$$

with the current “old” policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  
A reward model $r_{\varphi}$ scores every sampled output.  
Two variants are described:

| **Supervision** | **Reward per output** | **Normalization** | **Advantage per token** |
|-----------------|-----------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Outcome**     | a single scalar $r_{i}$ given at the end of $o_{i}$ | $\displaystyle \widetilde r_{i}= \frac{r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)}$, where $\mathbf r=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\}$ | $\displaystyle \hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_{i},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\: \forall t$ |
| **Process**     | a sequence of step‑wise scores $\bigl(r^{[\mathrm{id}](j)}_{i}\bigr)_{j=1}^{K_{i}}$   | first flatten all step scores over the whole group into $\mathbf R$; then $\displaystyle \widetilde r^{[\mathrm{id](j)]}_{i}= \frac{r^{[\mathrm{id](j)]}_{i}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)}$ | $\displaystyle \hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\mathrm{id}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r^{[\mathrm{id](j)]}_{i}$ |

Thus:

* **Do they subtract the mean before normalising?** – Yes. The raw reward of each output (or each reasoning step) is centred by subtracting the group mean and scaled by dividing by the group standard deviation.
* **Is one scalar applied to every token?** – In outcome supervision this scalar ($\widetilde r_{i}$) becomes the advantage for *all* tokens of output $o_{i}$; consequently every token receives exactly the same coefficient in Equation (5). In process supervision each token receives a sum of normalised step rewards that follow it, so different tokens within an output can have distinct advantages.
* **What does “baseline” mean here?** – The quantity subtracted from each raw reward, namely the group mean $\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)$, plays exactly the role of a baseline: it estimates what an *average* response would obtain for this question, so any deviation above or below it becomes a positive or negative advantage.

After computing $\hat A_{i,t}$, GRPO plugs it into the clipped surrogate objective (Equation (4)) exactly like PPO does with its $A_t$.

---

### 2 A concrete numeric illustration

Assume $G=4$ and we are using outcome supervision:

| output | raw reward $r_i$ |
|--------|-------------------|
| $o_1$ | 0.80 |
| $o_2$ | 0.30 |
| $o_3$ | 0.60 |
| $o_4$ | 0.90 |

The group statistics are  

$$
\mu = \frac{0.80+0.30+0.60+0.90}{4}=0.65,
$$

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i(r_i-\mu)^2}{4}}\;\approx 0.227 .
$$

The normalised scores become  

$$
\widetilde r_1=\frac{0.80-\mu}{0.227}\approx 0.66,\qquad
\widetilde r_2=\frac{0.30-\mu}{0.227}\approx -1.54,
$$

$$
\widetilde r_3=\frac{0.60-\mu}{0.227}\approx -0.22,\qquad
\widetilde r_4=\frac{0.90-\mu}{0.227}\approx 1.10 .
$$

If an output contains ten tokens, every token in $o_1$ receives advantage $+0.\!66$, every token in $o_2$ receives $-1.\!54$, and so on. In practice these numbers are multiplied by the probability‐ratio term inside Equation (4); tokens belonging to high‑reward outputs are reinforced, those belonging to low‑reward outputs are pushed down.

For process supervision one would first gather step‑level scores such as  
$r^{[\mathrm{id}](1)}_1=0.\!5$, $r^{[\mathrm{id}](2)}_1=1.\!2$, … , flatten them across all four outputs, compute a global mean and standard deviation,
normalise each step score, then accumulate suffix sums as described above.


---

### 3 Why this works as variance reduction

In vanilla REINFORCE we would use  

$$
\nabla_\theta J = {\mathbb E}_{q,o}\bigl[ R(o)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o|q)\bigr].
$$

where $R(o)$ is a single scalar return and its variance can be huge because it depends on both stochastic sampling of tokens *and* on which question we happen to draw at a particular iteration.

Two classic tricks lower this variance:

1️⃣ **A learned value function** – PPO computes an estimate $V_\psi(q,t)$ of the expected return given state “question + prefix”. Subtracting it yields an *advantage* with lower variance because it cancels part of the randomness due to future rewards.

2️⃣ **A constant baseline per state** – If we could know exactly ${\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$, using $b(q)= {\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$ would eliminate all variance coming from stochasticity *across* different trajectories for that same question.

GRPO implements version 2 but without learning a separate network: because we draw several trajectories *for exactly the same question*, their empirical mean  

$$
b_{\textit{group}}(q)= {\operatorname*{\mathsf {mean}}}_i(r_i)
$$

is an unbiased estimator of ${\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$. By centring every reward around this estimate we remove almost all inter‑sample variability within that group—exactly what a perfect baseline would do—while retaining only residual noise due to finite sampling ($G$ is typically modest such as 64).

In PPO’s GAE, variance reduction exploits temporal structure: rewards are bootstrapped using $\lambda$‑weighted multi‑step returns so that advantages vary smoothly along time steps inside one trajectory. GRPO instead exploits *sample diversity*: all tokens belonging to an output share exactly the same centred score (outcome case), or share a summed suffix of centred step scores (process case). Consequently:

* The **source** of variance being mitigated shifts from *temporal noise* (GAE) to *sampling noise across parallel completions*.
* No extra parameters are needed – no critic network → less memory consumption.
* The normalisation by standard deviation further stabilises learning because it puts advantages on roughly unit scale irrespective of how “hard” a particular batch of questions is.

Empirically this scheme yields comparable—or even better—training stability than PPO while saving roughly half the memory cost associated with storing a separate value head comparable in size to&nbsp;the policy model.


---

### 4 Summary

GRPO computes an advantage by first evaluating every sampled completion with a reward model, then subtracting – the group’s average – and dividing by – the group’s standard deviation; for outcome supervision this yields a single scalar that is attached to each token of its completion, while process supervision distributes step‑wise normalised scores backwards along each token sequence.
The subtraction of the group mean constitutes precisely the “baseline” used in policy‑gradient methods; because it approximates ${\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$, it cancels much of inter‑sample noise and thus provides strong variance reduction without any learned critic.
Compared with GAE’s temporal bootstrapping in PPO, GRPO’s variance reduction exploits parallel sampling over many completions rather than temporal structure—a natural fit for large language models where rewards are usually only available at sentence or solution level.
Consequently GRPO retains PPO’s theoretical benefits while dramatically cutting memory usage and simplifying implementation—a fact reflected in its superior performance on DeepSeekMath’s RL fine‐tuning stage reported in Section 4–5 of&nbsp;the paper.​
