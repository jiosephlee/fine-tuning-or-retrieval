\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does DeepSeekMath show larger improvement on Chinese mathematical benchmarks than on English ones?
Question:
"DeepSeekMath‐Base outperforms other models on Chinese tasks such as CMATH and Gaokao-MathCloze because their corpus includes multilingual data restricted mainly to English & Chinese" (Section 2). I’m trying to understand why multilingual pre-training helps Chinese performance more noticeably:
• Is there an imbalance where existing open-source models were trained almost exclusively on English data causing stronger relative gains when adding Chinese tokens?
• Does cross-lingual transfer between English mathematics and Chinese formulations play a role given shared symbols/equations?
Any insight into language-specific effects would be useful.
Answer:
**Answer**

The larger jump you see on the Chinese benchmarks comes mainly from two facts that the authors point out explicitly in the paper:

1. **The pre‑training data of most open‑source math models is almost completely English**, whereas DeepSeekMath’s corpus contains a substantial amount of high‑quality Chinese material.  
2. **Training on a truly multilingual mix lets the model learn both language‑specific tokens and language‑independent reasoning patterns**, so the benefit is felt most strongly where the baseline was weakest – i.e. on Chinese problems.

Below I unpack these points and explain why they translate into a visibly bigger gain for CMATH, Gaokao‑MathCloze and other Chinese tests than for the English suites such as GSM8K or MATH.

---

### 1. Imbalance in existing math corpora

The paper’s *Data Collection* (§2) makes it clear that prior publicly available math corpora—MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2—are **English‑centric**. MathPile, for instance, consists of $\approx 85\%$ arXiv papers which are written almost exclusively in English; OpenWebMath and Proof‑Pile‑2 are built from Common Crawl pages filtered with an English‑only seed. Because those corpora contain very few non‑English pages, models trained on them learn rich mathematical vocabulary for English but receive almost no exposure to Chinese characters, Chinese idioms for “solve”, “equation”, “integral”, etc., nor to the way Mandarin phrases structure a problem statement.  

Consequently, when those models are evaluated on CMATH or Gaokao‑MathCloze they have to rely on *transfer* from their knowledge of symbols and reasoning steps alone, which often leaves them stumbling over basic linguistic cues. Their scores on the Chinese benchmarks therefore start from a relatively low baseline.

DeepSeekMath’s *DeepSeekMath Corpus* (§2) deliberately adds **multilingual web pages** after an iterative fastText classification step. The authors state that “the two most represented languages are English and Chinese” and that “training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus enhances mathematical reasoning performance in both English and Chinese”. In Table 1 (referenced in §2) they show that when a 1.3 B model is trained on this corpus it outperforms the same model trained on Proof‑Pile‑2 **especially on Chinese tasks**, while its gains on English benchmarks are modest because it was already near saturation.

Thus the first part of the answer is simple: *the other open‑source models simply did not see much Chinese data*, so any injection of high‑quality Mandarin examples yields a noticeable lift.

---

### 2. Multilingual pre‑training brings direct language knowledge

DeepSeekMath’s training distribution (Table 3) allocates about **$56\%$** of its tokens to the DeepSeekMath Corpus itself, which—as noted—contains a sizable chunk of Mandarin text. In addition, **$10\%$** of the overall data comes from “natural language” Common Crawl pages that also include both languages. This means that during continual pre‑training the model learns:

* The Unicode token embeddings for thousands of frequently used Chinese characters,
* Word‑piece sub‑token patterns that capture common phrasing such as “求解方程” (solve an equation) or “面积公式” (area formula),
* The alignment between Mandarin problem descriptions and the same mathematical symbols (e.g., “$\sum$', ‘$\int$', matrices).

Because these embeddings are learned jointly with all other parameters (including those governing chain‑of‑thought reasoning), they become part of the same representational space used for English as well. When a Mandarin problem arrives at inference time the model already knows how to map its wording onto familiar symbolic reasoning steps; this reduces the *language barrier* that pure‑English models face.

The authors explicitly observe in §2 (“Multilingual”) that “existing mathematical corpora…show limited improvement and may even hinder performance in Chinese mathematical reasoning”. That phrasing captures precisely what happens when you train only on English tokens: you may improve symbol handling but you also bias the tokenizer/embedding space toward Latin scripts, which can *hurt* performance on non‑Latin scripts.

---

### 3. Cross‑lingual transfer plays a secondary role

Mathematical reasoning is largely language neutral – symbols, equations, proof structures are shared across cultures. A multilingual LLM can therefore *reuse* its reasoning circuitry learned from English examples when tackling Mandarin problems. This cross‑lingual transfer does happen; it explains why DeepSeekMath’s gains are not limited only to language modelling but also appear in pure reasoning metrics (e.g., higher self‑consistency scores). 

However, if cross‑lingual transfer were the dominant factor we would expect roughly proportional improvements on both sides: adding more Mandarin data would help Mandarin tasks but would also give some extra boost to English tasks through richer symbol exposure. The paper’s results show **the opposite pattern** – modest lifts on GSM8K/MATH but a pronounced jump on CMATH/ Gaokao–MathCloze. That asymmetry tells us that *direct exposure* to Mandarin text is responsible for most of the gain; cross‑lingual transfer merely smooths the transition by giving a shared reasoning backbone.

In other words:  
*The shared symbol vocabulary gives a universal boost*, but *the language‐specific token learning supplies most of the extra headroom for low‑resource languages such as Mandarin.*

---

### 4. Why the improvement looks larger relative to English

Two quantitative reasons amplify this effect:

1\. **Higher ceiling effect on English** – Since most open source models already sit near their performance ceiling after seeing billions of English math tokens, adding another 120 B mixed tokens yields diminishing returns (the curve flattens).  
2\. **Lower starting point on Chinese** – With almost no Mandarin exposure before DeepSeekMath’s corpus, even a modest absolute increase translates into a large relative percentage point jump.

The authors’ ablation experiments (§2 “Validating the Quality …”) illustrate exactly this behavior: when they train a 1.3 B model on increasing slices of their corpus $(40\,\text{B} \rightarrow 120\,\text{B})$ tokens, performance climbs steeply at first for both languages but plateaus earlier for English than for Chinese.

---

### 5\. Takeaway

To summarize:

* Existing open‑source math LLMs were trained almost exclusively on English resources; consequently they have strong baseline performance only where test data matches their training language.
* DeepSeekMath deliberately includes high‑quality Mandarin web pages in its 120 B token math corpus, which gives it direct coverage of Chinese vocabulary, syntax and domain terminology.
* Multilingual pre‑training lets language‑agnostic reasoning modules learned from English problems be reused for Mandarin problems—a useful secondary benefit—but most of the observed boost comes from learning *language‐specific* representations.
* Because English performance was already near saturation while Chinese performance started far below it, the *relative* improvement appears larger on CMATH and Gaokao benchmarks than on their English counterparts.

Thus, the paper’s claim that “their corpus includes multilingual data restricted mainly to English & Chinese” explains why DeepSeekMath shows a markedly bigger leap on Chinese mathematical benchmarks than on their English counterparts.​
