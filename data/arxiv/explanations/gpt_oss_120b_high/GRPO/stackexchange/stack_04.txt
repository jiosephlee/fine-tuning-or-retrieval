\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### What is meant by "self-consistency over 64 samples" achieving 60.9% on MATH in the abstract?
Question:
The abstract reports that "Self-consistency over 64 samples from DeepSeekMath 7B achieves 60.9% on MATH." I assume this refers to generating multiple candidate solutions per problem and aggregating them,
but I am not certain:
• Are all 64 samples generated independently with temperature sampling, nucleus sampling, or deterministic decoding?
• Is majority voting performed token-wise across full solutions, or do they compare final answers only?
• Does self-consistency also involve re-prompting with intermediate reasoning steps?
Understanding this procedure would let me compare their result with other self-consistency works.
Answer:
**Answer**

“Self–consistency over 64 samples” in the DeepSeekMath paper refers to the standard *self–consistency* inference procedure introduced by Wei et al., 2022, applied with a group size of $G=64$.  
In practice it works as follows:

1. **Generating many independent solutions** – For each MATH problem the model receives exactly the *same* prompting (the few–shot chain–of–thought prompt described in §3).  
   The authors draw $G=64$ completions from the model **independently** using stochastic decoding.  
   In §4.2 (“Insights of Reinforcement Learning”) they explicitly state that *“a naïve nucleus sampling”* is employed when producing multiple outputs, which is the usual top–$p$ sampler (commonly $p=0.95$) combined with a non–zero temperature (the exact temperature value isn’t reported, but all open–source works that apply self–consistency use $T\approximately0.7$). No beam search or deterministic decoding is used—each sample comes from an unbiased draw from $\pi_{\theta}$.

2. **Extracting the final answer** – Each sampled completion contains a full step–by–step reasoning trace together with its concluding numeric result (the MATH benchmark expects an expression such as “$\boxed{42}$” or “Answer = 42”). After generation the authors parse out this *final answer* using simple regular expressions—the same procedure that is used when evaluating a single solution.

3. **Majority voting on answers only** – The “self–consistency” label means that the authors do **not** perform token–wise voting across whole solutions. Instead they count how many times each distinct final answer occurs among the 64 traces and pick the *most frequent* one (ties are broken arbitrarily). This mirrors what was done in the original self–consistency work and reflects the intuition that correct mathematics often emerges even when some reasoning steps are noisy; taking a consensus on the endpoint filters out those occasional missteps.

4. **No re–prompting with intermediate steps** – The procedure consists of a single forward pass per sampled trace; there is no additional prompting step such as “given your previous reasoning, try again”. The model simply generates its own chain of thought from scratch each time it is invoked.

Putting these pieces together gives the reported number: when evaluated **without any voting** DeepSeekMath‑7B attains $51.5\,\%$ accuracy on MATH (the baseline quoted in Table 1). When its output distribution is made more robust by applying the above $G=64$ self–consistency aggregation, its accuracy rises to **$60.9\,\%$**—a gain of roughly nine percentage points that matches what other papers have observed when moving from single‐sample inference to majority‐vote inference.

A side note: the same group size $G=64$ appears elsewhere in the paper (§4 “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath‑RL”) because it is also used during Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for computing a group‐wise baseline when estimating advantages. That usage is unrelated to evaluation; it merely explains why “$G=64$” shows up both in training and test sections.

In summary, *self–consistency over 64 samples* means:

* generate sixty‑four independent chain–of–thought completions per problem using stochastic top–$p$/temperature decoding,
* extract each completion’s final numeric answer,
* select the most common answer among them,
* treat that consensus as the model’s prediction for computing accuracy on MATH,
* without any intermediate re–prompting or token‐level voting.

This simple aggregation dramatically improves performance while preserving a completely “open” inference pipeline—no external toolkits or specialized post‐processing beyond answer extraction are required.​
