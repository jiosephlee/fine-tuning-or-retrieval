\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 1: Introduction and Motivation

Mathematical reasoning has emerged as one of the most demanding tasks for modern large language models (LLMs).  While LLMs excel at generating fluent natural language, mathematics requires a uniquely structured form of thinking that intertwines symbolic manipulation, precise logical deductions, multi‑step inference, and often exact numerical computation.  This mismatch between the statistical nature of language modeling and the deterministic rigor of mathematics leads to systematic failures when LLMs are asked to solve even elementary problems such as those found in grade‑school textbooks.  The difficulty is compounded by the fact that mathematical problems are rarely expressed as plain prose; they embed equations, require intermediate derivations (often best expressed as a chain of thought), and may involve the use of external tools such as computer algebra systems or programming environments.  Consequently, training a model that can handle these challenges demands both carefully curated data that reflect the true character of mathematical discourse and algorithmic innovations that guide the model toward reliable reasoning pathways.

Over the past few years, closed‑source models such as GPT‑4 \citep{gpt4} and Gemini‑Ultra \citep{gemini} have dramatically narrowed this gap by achieving high accuracy on competitive benchmarks like MATH \citep{MATH} and GSM8K \citep{gsm8k}.  These systems benefit from massive scales—both in the number of parameters (hundreds of billions) and in the breadth of proprietary training data—combined with sophisticated instruction tuning pipelines that incorporate chain‑of‑thought prompting, tool integration, and reinforcement learning from human feedback.  In stark contrast, publicly available open‑source models lag considerably behind.  Even strong general‑purpose candidates such as Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} or specialized instruction‑tuned variants exhibit accuracies on MATH that are more than ten percentage points lower than their closed counterparts.  The performance gap is especially pronounced on competition‑level problems where success hinges on deep symbolic insight rather than superficial pattern matching.

The DeepSeekMath project sets out to answer a series of intertwined research questions that arise from this landscape:

1. **Is it possible for an open‑source model of modest size (approximately 7 billion parameters) to match or surpass the mathematical reasoning capability of far larger proprietary systems?**  
   This question probes whether model scale alone dictates performance or whether data quality and training methodology can compensate for a smaller parameter count.

2. **What role does domain‑specific data play in shaping mathematical competence?**  
   Prior work has largely relied on English‑centric corpora derived from textbooks or scientific articles such as arXiv \citep{minerva}.  DeepSeekMath investigates whether publicly accessible web data—when selected through a systematic pipeline—contains sufficient high‑quality mathematical content to drive substantial gains.

3. **Does initializing a math‑focused model from a code‐oriented checkpoint provide an advantage?**  
   Code training endows models with an implicit understanding of algorithmic thinking, variable binding, and symbolic manipulation—all valuable for solving equations or generating program‐aided solutions.  The project examines whether starting from DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1.5 7B yields measurable improvements over purely language‐pretrained seeds.

4. **Can reinforcement learning be made more efficient for large language models without sacrificing performance?**  
   Standard Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) methods require a separate critic network to estimate value functions, incurring considerable memory overhead during training.  DeepSeekMath therefore introduces Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a variant that replaces the critic with a baseline derived from groupwise reward averages, dramatically reducing resource consumption while preserving—or even enhancing—learning signals.

Addressing these questions required assembling a collection of contributions that together define the DeepSeekMath approach:

### Large‑Scale Math Pre‑Training Corpus  
The cornerstone of DeepSeekMath is the DeepSeekMath Corpus—a multilingual collection of roughly 120 billion tokens harvested from Common Crawl web pages using an iterative fastText classifier trained on OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}.  The pipeline starts with a modest seed set of high‑quality mathematics webpages, trains a fastText model to recall similar pages across billions of HTML documents, de‑duplicates candidates at both URL and near‑duplicate levels, ranks them by classifier confidence, and repeats the process across several iterations to expand coverage while maintaining precision.  Human annotation refines positive examples after each round, ensuring that noisy or irrelevant content is excluded.  Notably, the resulting corpus is seven times larger than the math web pages curated for Minerva \citep{minerva} and nine times larger than OpenWebMath itself.

### Code‑First Initialization Strategy  
DeepSeekMath leverages an unconventional starting point: instead of initializing from a generic language model checkpoint, it begins from DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base v1.5 7B—a model already specialized in source code generation across many programming languages.  Empirical investigation shows that this code‐first seed accelerates acquisition of procedural reasoning patterns that are directly transferable to mathematical problem solving—particularly when models are later prompted to emit Python programs (the “program‐of‐thought” paradigm).  Through continual pre‑training on mixed data (56 % math corpus, 20 % GitHub code, plus natural language text), DeepSeekMath cultivates an integrated skill set spanning symbolic logic, numeric computation, and software engineering constructs.

### Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)  
To refine reasoning capabilities after supervision via instruction tuning—a stage termed “mathematical instruction tuning”—the authors develop GRPO as an efficient alternative to PPO.  Traditional PPO optimizes a surrogate objective involving policy ratios multiplied by an advantage term computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) against a learned value function \(V_{\psi}\).  This critic incurs memory usage comparable to the policy network itself because it must evaluate each token in every sampled trajectory.  GRPO sidesteps this requirement by sampling multiple candidate outputs \(\{o_{1},\dots,o_{G}\}\) per question from the current policy \(\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}\), scoring them with a reward model \(r_{\varphi}\), and constructing a baseline simply as the mean reward within each group.  The resulting advantage \(\hat{A}_{i,t}\) for token \(t\) in output \(o_i\) becomes
\[
\hat{A}_{i,t}= \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)} ,
\]
where \(\mathbf r\) denotes the vector of group rewards.  Because only relative rankings within each group matter, GRPO aligns naturally with pairwise preference data used to train reward models while eliminating any separate value network.  An additional KL regularization term directly penalizes divergence between the updated policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) and a reference checkpoint \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), preserving language fluency without complicating advantage computation.

### Comprehensive Experimental Validation  
DeepSeekMath’s efficacy is demonstrated through an extensive suite of benchmark evaluations spanning eight English and Chinese mathematical datasets—including GSM8K \citep{gsm8k}, competition‐level MATH \citep{MATH}, SAT‑style problems \citep{llemma}, Stanford OCW courses \citep{minerva}, MMLU-STEM \citep{mmlu}, MGSM–zh \citep{mgsm}, CMATH \citep{wei2023cmath}, and Gaokao–Math variants \citep{agieval}.  The base model DeepSeekMath‑Base 7B attains \(64.2\,\%\) on GSM8K and \(36.2\,\%\) on MATH without any tool assistance—surpassing Minerva 540B despite being more than two orders of magnitude smaller in parameters—and further improves on Chinese benchmarks thanks to its multilingual corpus component.

After applying chain-of-thought (“CoT”) and program-of-thought (“PoT”) instruction tuning (yielding DeepSeekMath‑Instruct 7B), performance climbs dramatically: on MATH it exceeds all open-source peers in the \(7\,\text{B}\)-parameter range and rivals proprietary models such as Inflection‑2 or Gemini Pro despite their substantially larger scales.  When GRPO‐based reinforcement learning refines this instruction‐tuned checkpoint (producing DeepSeekMath‑RL 7B), accuracies reach \(88.2\,\%\) on GSM8K and \(51.7\,\%\) on MATH under CoT prompting—a milestone crossing the half‑point barrier on competition‐level mathematics for any openly released model.

In addition to pure quantitative gains, auxiliary evaluations reveal broader benefits: improvement on Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) shows enhanced general knowledge reasoning; gains on BIG‑Bench Hard (BBH) indicate stronger multi-step logical processing; and sustained coding performance on HumanEval and MBPP confirms that math pre-training does not erode code generation abilities inherited from the initial coder checkpoint.

Together these findings substantiate three central claims: (1) high‑quality public web data can serve as an effective foundation for mathematical competence; (2) leveraging code pretraining provides transferable reasoning scaffolding; and (3) reinforcement learning can be streamlined via GRPO without sacrificing performance gains—making advanced mathematical LLMs accessible within modest computational budgets.
