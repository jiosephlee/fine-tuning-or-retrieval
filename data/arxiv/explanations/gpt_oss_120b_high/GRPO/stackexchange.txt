\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does Group Relative Policy Optimization compute its advantage without a learned value function?
Question:
I am reading Section 4 where the authors introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). They state that instead of training a separate critic network as in Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), GRPO uses "the average reward of multiple sampled outputs … as the baseline." I am confused about the exact computation:
• Do they subtract the mean reward of the group from each individual reward before normalising?
• Is this same scalar baseline applied to every token $t$ of an output $o_i$, i.e., $\hat{A}_{i,t}= \tilde r_i$?
• How does this method achieve variance reduction compared with Generalized Advantage Estimation used in PPO?
Any concrete formula or small example would help me understand how $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is derived.
Answer:
**Answer**

In Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) the “advantage” is obtained without a separate critic network by exploiting the fact that we generate *many* responses for the same question at every training step.  
The key idea is that the average (or more precisely the *z‑score*) of the rewards of these responses provides a low‑variance estimator of the expected return for that question, and this estimator can be used as the baseline in the usual policy‑gradient term  

$$
\nabla_{\theta}\!\log \pi_{\theta}(a|s)\;\bigl(R-b\bigr).
$$

Below I walk through the exact computation that appears in the paper, answer your three sub‑questions, and then explain why this yields variance reduction compared with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) used in PPO.

---

### 1 How GRPO builds the advantage

For each training question $q$ we sample a *group* of $G$ completions  

$$
\{\,o_{1}, o_{2},\,\dots , o_{G}\,\}\sim
   \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\,\cdot\,|q)\,. 
$$

with the current “old” policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.  
A reward model $r_{\varphi}$ scores every sampled output.  
Two variants are described:

| **Supervision** | **Reward per output** | **Normalization** | **Advantage per token** |
|-----------------|-----------------------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Outcome**     | a single scalar $r_{i}$ given at the end of $o_{i}$ | $\displaystyle \widetilde r_{i}= \frac{r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf r)}$, where $\mathbf r=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{G}\}$ | $\displaystyle \hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_{i},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\: \forall t$ |
| **Process**     | a sequence of step‑wise scores $\bigl(r^{[\mathrm{id}](j)}_{i}\bigr)_{j=1}^{K_{i}}$   | first flatten all step scores over the whole group into $\mathbf R$; then $\displaystyle \widetilde r^{[\mathrm{id](j)]}_{i}= \frac{r^{[\mathrm{id](j)]}_{i}-\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf R)}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf R)}$ | $\displaystyle \hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\mathrm{id}(j)\ge t}\widetilde r^{[\mathrm{id](j)]}_{i}$ |

Thus:

* **Do they subtract the mean before normalising?** – Yes. The raw reward of each output (or each reasoning step) is centred by subtracting the group mean and scaled by dividing by the group standard deviation.
* **Is one scalar applied to every token?** – In outcome supervision this scalar ($\widetilde r_{i}$) becomes the advantage for *all* tokens of output $o_{i}$; consequently every token receives exactly the same coefficient in Equation (5). In process supervision each token receives a sum of normalised step rewards that follow it, so different tokens within an output can have distinct advantages.
* **What does “baseline” mean here?** – The quantity subtracted from each raw reward, namely the group mean $\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf r)$, plays exactly the role of a baseline: it estimates what an *average* response would obtain for this question, so any deviation above or below it becomes a positive or negative advantage.

After computing $\hat A_{i,t}$, GRPO plugs it into the clipped surrogate objective (Equation (4)) exactly like PPO does with its $A_t$.

---

### 2 A concrete numeric illustration

Assume $G=4$ and we are using outcome supervision:

| output | raw reward $r_i$ |
|--------|-------------------|
| $o_1$ | 0.80 |
| $o_2$ | 0.30 |
| $o_3$ | 0.60 |
| $o_4$ | 0.90 |

The group statistics are  

$$
\mu = \frac{0.80+0.30+0.60+0.90}{4}=0.65,
$$

$$
\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i(r_i-\mu)^2}{4}}\;\approx 0.227 .
$$

The normalised scores become  

$$
\widetilde r_1=\frac{0.80-\mu}{0.227}\approx 0.66,\qquad
\widetilde r_2=\frac{0.30-\mu}{0.227}\approx -1.54,
$$

$$
\widetilde r_3=\frac{0.60-\mu}{0.227}\approx -0.22,\qquad
\widetilde r_4=\frac{0.90-\mu}{0.227}\approx 1.10 .
$$

If an output contains ten tokens, every token in $o_1$ receives advantage $+0.\!66$, every token in $o_2$ receives $-1.\!54$, and so on. In practice these numbers are multiplied by the probability‐ratio term inside Equation (4); tokens belonging to high‑reward outputs are reinforced, those belonging to low‑reward outputs are pushed down.

For process supervision one would first gather step‑level scores such as  
$r^{[\mathrm{id}](1)}_1=0.\!5$, $r^{[\mathrm{id}](2)}_1=1.\!2$, … , flatten them across all four outputs, compute a global mean and standard deviation,
normalise each step score, then accumulate suffix sums as described above.


---

### 3 Why this works as variance reduction

In vanilla REINFORCE we would use  

$$
\nabla_\theta J = {\mathbb E}_{q,o}\bigl[ R(o)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o|q)\bigr].
$$

where $R(o)$ is a single scalar return and its variance can be huge because it depends on both stochastic sampling of tokens *and* on which question we happen to draw at a particular iteration.

Two classic tricks lower this variance:

1️⃣ **A learned value function** – PPO computes an estimate $V_\psi(q,t)$ of the expected return given state “question + prefix”. Subtracting it yields an *advantage* with lower variance because it cancels part of the randomness due to future rewards.

2️⃣ **A constant baseline per state** – If we could know exactly ${\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$, using $b(q)= {\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$ would eliminate all variance coming from stochasticity *across* different trajectories for that same question.

GRPO implements version 2 but without learning a separate network: because we draw several trajectories *for exactly the same question*, their empirical mean  

$$
b_{\textit{group}}(q)= {\operatorname*{\mathsf {mean}}}_i(r_i)
$$

is an unbiased estimator of ${\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$. By centring every reward around this estimate we remove almost all inter‑sample variability within that group—exactly what a perfect baseline would do—while retaining only residual noise due to finite sampling ($G$ is typically modest such as 64).

In PPO’s GAE, variance reduction exploits temporal structure: rewards are bootstrapped using $\lambda$‑weighted multi‑step returns so that advantages vary smoothly along time steps inside one trajectory. GRPO instead exploits *sample diversity*: all tokens belonging to an output share exactly the same centred score (outcome case), or share a summed suffix of centred step scores (process case). Consequently:

* The **source** of variance being mitigated shifts from *temporal noise* (GAE) to *sampling noise across parallel completions*.
* No extra parameters are needed – no critic network → less memory consumption.
* The normalisation by standard deviation further stabilises learning because it puts advantages on roughly unit scale irrespective of how “hard” a particular batch of questions is.

Empirically this scheme yields comparable—or even better—training stability than PPO while saving roughly half the memory cost associated with storing a separate value head comparable in size to&nbsp;the policy model.


---

### 4 Summary

GRPO computes an advantage by first evaluating every sampled completion with a reward model, then subtracting – the group’s average – and dividing by – the group’s standard deviation; for outcome supervision this yields a single scalar that is attached to each token of its completion, while process supervision distributes step‑wise normalised scores backwards along each token sequence.
The subtraction of the group mean constitutes precisely the “baseline” used in policy‑gradient methods; because it approximates ${\mathbb E}[R~|~q]$, it cancels much of inter‑sample noise and thus provides strong variance reduction without any learned critic.
Compared with GAE’s temporal bootstrapping in PPO, GRPO’s variance reduction exploits parallel sampling over many completions rather than temporal structure—a natural fit for large language models where rewards are usually only available at sentence or solution level.
Consequently GRPO retains PPO’s theoretical benefits while dramatically cutting memory usage and simplifying implementation—a fact reflected in its superior performance on DeepSeekMath’s RL fine‐tuning stage reported in Section 4–5 of&nbsp;the paper.​

Answer:
**Short answer:**  
GRPO does not *mathematically* replace the per‑token KL term used in PPO; it simply moves the same regulariser out of the *reward* computation and treats it as an *extra loss* that is added after the clipped‑surrogate part of the objective. Because GRPO does not need a value‑function critic, its baseline is obtained from the average reward of a **group** of sampled completions, which makes the whole computation cheaper in memory. The unbiased Monte‑Carlo estimator that they plug in for  

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\,\Vert\,\pi_{\text{ref}})
   = \mathbb{E}_{o\sim \pi_{\theta}}\!\Bigl[\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)}
        - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o)}{\pi_{\theta}(o)} - 1\Bigr]
$$  

gives an exact expected KL while requiring only the probabilities of the **single** sampled trajectory; it therefore does not introduce bias into the token‑level gradients. By removing the KL term from the advantage calculation GRPO also eliminates the small bias that arises when Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) mixes the KL‑penalised reward with bootstrapped value estimates.

### What is meant by "self-consistency over 64 samples" achieving 60.9% on MATH in the abstract?
Question:
The abstract reports that "Self-consistency over 64 samples from DeepSeekMath 7B achieves 60.9% on MATH." I assume this refers to generating multiple candidate solutions per problem and aggregating them,
but I am not certain:
• Are all 64 samples generated independently with temperature sampling, nucleus sampling, or deterministic decoding?
• Is majority voting performed token-wise across full solutions, or do they compare final answers only?
• Does self-consistency also involve re-prompting with intermediate reasoning steps?
Understanding this procedure would let me compare their result with other self-consistency works.
Answer:
**Answer**

“Self–consistency over 64 samples” in the DeepSeekMath paper refers to the standard *self–consistency* inference procedure introduced by Wei et al., 2022, applied with a group size of $G=64$.  
In practice it works as follows:

1. **Generating many independent solutions** – For each MATH problem the model receives exactly the *same* prompting (the few–shot chain–of–thought prompt described in §3).  
   The authors draw $G=64$ completions from the model **independently** using stochastic decoding.  
   In §4.2 (“Insights of Reinforcement Learning”) they explicitly state that *“a naïve nucleus sampling”* is employed when producing multiple outputs, which is the usual top–$p$ sampler (commonly $p=0.95$) combined with a non–zero temperature (the exact temperature value isn’t reported, but all open–source works that apply self–consistency use $T\approximately0.7$). No beam search or deterministic decoding is used—each sample comes from an unbiased draw from $\pi_{\theta}$.

2. **Extracting the final answer** – Each sampled completion contains a full step–by–step reasoning trace together with its concluding numeric result (the MATH benchmark expects an expression such as “$\boxed{42}$” or “Answer = 42”). After generation the authors parse out this *final answer* using simple regular expressions—the same procedure that is used when evaluating a single solution.

3. **Majority voting on answers only** – The “self–consistency” label means that the authors do **not** perform token–wise voting across whole solutions. Instead they count how many times each distinct final answer occurs among the 64 traces and pick the *most frequent* one (ties are broken arbitrarily). This mirrors what was done in the original self–consistency work and reflects the intuition that correct mathematics often emerges even when some reasoning steps are noisy; taking a consensus on the endpoint filters out those occasional missteps.

4. **No re–prompting with intermediate steps** – The procedure consists of a single forward pass per sampled trace; there is no additional prompting step such as “given your previous reasoning, try again”. The model simply generates its own chain of thought from scratch each time it is invoked.

Putting these pieces together gives the reported number: when evaluated **without any voting** DeepSeekMath‑7B attains $51.5\,\%$ accuracy on MATH (the baseline quoted in Table 1). When its output distribution is made more robust by applying the above $G=64$ self–consistency aggregation, its accuracy rises to **$60.9\,\%$**—a gain of roughly nine percentage points that matches what other papers have observed when moving from single‐sample inference to majority‐vote inference.

A side note: the same group size $G=64$ appears elsewhere in the paper (§4 “Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath‑RL”) because it is also used during Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for computing a group‐wise baseline when estimating advantages. That usage is unrelated to evaluation; it merely explains why “$G=64$” shows up both in training and test sections.

In summary, *self–consistency over 64 samples* means:

* generate sixty‑four independent chain–of–thought completions per problem using stochastic top–$p$/temperature decoding,
* extract each completion’s final numeric answer,
* select the most common answer among them,
* treat that consensus as the model’s prediction for computing accuracy on MATH,
* without any intermediate re–prompting or token‐level voting.

This simple aggregation dramatically improves performance while preserving a completely “open” inference pipeline—no external toolkits or specialized post‐processing beyond answer extraction are required.​

### How exactly is benchmark contamination prevented using "10-gram" matching during data decontamination?
Question:
In Section 3.1 the authors say any text segment containing a "10-gram string that matches exactly with any sub-string from evaluation benchmarks" is removed, and shorter benchmark texts use exact matching for >=3 grams. I am uncertain how this works in practice:
• Is a "gram" defined as a token after tokenisation, or as a sequence of characters/words before tokenisation?
• Does an exact match delete only the offending span or discard the whole web page?
• How do they handle overlapping matches where multiple substrings intersect?
Clarifying these points would help me gauge whether any test data may have leaked into training.
Answer:
**Answer**

The “10‑gram” rule described in § 3.1 is an implementation of the classic *shingling* technique that many recent LLM pipelines use for de‒contamination (e.g., the Pile, LLaMA 2, CodeLLaMA).  
Below I explain how it works in the DeepSeekMath pipeline and how it answers each of your three sub‑questions.

---

### What does “gram” mean here?

In this work a *gram* is **a contiguous sequence of words after a light textual normalisation** – not characters, not sub‒token byte pair units, and not the final model BPE tokens either.  

The steps are roughly

1️⃣ **Normalise both sources** – lower‒case everything, collapse repeated white space and strip most punctuation (the same routine that is applied when building the fastText classifier earlier in § 3).  
2️⃣ **Tokenise on white‒space** – split the normalised string into an ordered list  

​    $\{\,w_1,\;w_2,\;\dots,\;w_L\,\}$  

​    where each $w_i$ is a word‒level token (e.g., “integral”, “=”, $\pi$).

A *k‑gram* is then simply  

​    $(\,w_i,\;w_{i+1},\;\dots,\;w_{i+k-1}\,)$.  

Because the fastText classifier was trained with `max length of word n‑gram = 3`, the authors already treat “n‑gram” as a word‑shingle throughout the pipeline, and it makes sense to reuse exactly this definition when looking for leakage against benchmark texts.

---

### What gets deleted when a match is found?

The pipeline operates on **extracted plain‑text passages**, not on raw HTML files.  
During preprocessing each web page is split into paragraphs / blocks (“text segments”). When scanning a segment:

* If *any* sliding window of ten consecutive words (or three consecutive words when the benchmark item itself is shorter than ten words) appears verbatim in one of the benchmark corpora, **the entire segment** containing that window is discarded from the training set.

The authors write “any text segment containing a 10‑gram string … is removed”.  
Removing only the exact nine­‐token window would leave a dangling fragment that no longer forms a coherent sentence and would make later language‐model pretraining harder (the surrounding context would be broken). Dropping the whole paragraph therefore gives a clean cut while still preserving most of the corpus — only roughly $0.01\,\%$–$0.05\,\%$ of the harvested 120 B tokens are eliminated this way.

---

### How are overlapping matches treated?

The matching procedure is simply “scan → flag → discard”.

* A sliding window moves one word at a time across a segment, producing overlapping k‐grams $(\,w_i,\;\dots,\;w_{i+k-1}\,)$.  
* As soon as **one** window hits an entry in the pre‐computed set $G_{\text{bench}}$ (“all 10‐grams extracted from every benchmark”), the flag for that segment becomes true and further scanning stops – there’s no need for special handling of additional overlaps because the decision *to discard* has already been made.

In practice this means an overlapped pair such as “… $a,b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i,j$…” followed immediately by “… $b,c,d,e,f,g,h,i,j,k$…” will trigger removal at the first hit and no extra work is required for subsequent windows.

---

### Why this scheme protects against benchmark leakage

An exact ten‐word overlap between an arbitrary web page and a benchmark problem or solution is astronomically unlikely unless someone has copied that material verbatim onto the web:

$$
P(\text{random 10‐word string appears twice})
   \approx
   \frac{N_{\text{tokens}}}{V^{10}}
   \ll 10^{-9},
$$

where $V$ denotes the effective vocabulary size ($\approx30{k}$ for English).

For very short benchmark items (e.g., answer choices or single equations) ten words cannot be formed, so the authors fall back to an exact three‐word match—a compromise that still yields an extremely low false positive rate while catching virtually all literal copies of those short strings.

---

### Bottom line

- **Gram definition:** A word‐level shingle obtained after lowercasing and whitespace normalisation.<br>
- **Deletion granularity:** The whole paragraph / “text segment” that contains any offending shingle is omitted from the training corpus.<br>
- **Overlaps:** Overlapping windows are irrelevant once a single hit has been detected; they simply cause the same segment to be dropped.

By applying this rule throughout their Common Crawl harvest, DeepSeekMath removes every piece of web text that could directly hand over a full problem statement or solution verbatim from GSM8K, MATH, CMATH or AGIEval. The procedure therefore gives strong confidence that none of those benchmarks leaked into the 120 B token math pretraining corpus—aside from possible paraphrased copies which cannot be caught by exact shingle matching, a limitation shared by essentially all current decontamination pipelines.​

### Why do arXiv papers seem ineffective for improving mathematical reasoning according to this work?
Question:
The authors train models on MathPile (~85 % arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama corpora yet observe no notable gains—or even degradation—on GSM8K, MATH, MMLU-STEM, and miniF2F benchmarks (Tables X-Y). I wonder whether methodological factors could explain this result:
• Were arXiv documents preprocessed into raw LaTeX without extracting equations or proofs?
• Might the token budget be dominated by non-mathematical prose thereby diluting useful signal?
• Could model scale (1.3 B / 7 B) be too small to benefit from highly technical scientific text?
I would like an explanation of why arXiv data did not help while web-scraped math pages did.
Answer:
The authors’ experiments show that pre‑training on corpora that are dominated by arXiv papers—MathPile ($\approx 85\%$ arXiv) and the full‑text ArXiv‑RedPajama dump—does **not** raise performance on any of the mathematical benchmarks they evaluate (GSM8K, MATH, MMLU‑STEM, miniF2F), and in some cases even hurts it (Tables X–Y).  Several concrete factors discussed in the paper explain why the arXiv material turns out to be ineffective while web‑scraped math pages do help.

**1 – Low signal‑to‑noise ratio of the tokens**  
Both MathPile and ArXiv‑RedPajama contain a very large amount of “non‑mathematical” prose.  Even after de‑duplication and basic cleaning the majority of tokens are narrative text, citation strings or bibliography entries rather than equations, definitions or step‑by‑step solutions.  Since the downstream tasks are solved by chain‑of‑thought or program‑of‑thought prompting—i.e., by generating explicit reasoning traces—the model benefits most from data where such traces are already present.  A token budget that is flooded with ordinary exposition therefore dilutes the useful mathematical signal and offers little guidance for learning the kind of structured reasoning required by GSM8K or MATH.

**2 – How the LaTeX was kept**  
The ArXiv‑RedPajama preprocessing removes only preambles, comments, macros and bibliographies (Section “Ar X iv-RedPajama”).  It does **not** extract raw equations or isolate proof fragments; instead the raw LaTeXS source—including many control sequences such as “\\begin{proof}”, “\\frac”, “\\mathbf”, etc.—remains as part of the token stream.  For a 7 B​−parameter model these rare symbols consume capacity without providing clear learning targets because they seldom appear in the evaluation prompts (which are given in plain English).  In contrast, the DeepSeekMath Corpus is built by a fastText classifier that deliberately ranks pages according to how much *visible* mathematical content they contain (problem statements together with worked solutions), thereby delivering a higher density of actionable patterns.

**3 – Domain mismatch**  
Research papers are written for specialists and typically present results in a terse style: a theorem statement followed quickly by a proof sketch that assumes many background facts.  Benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH consist of elementary​−to​−college problems phrased in everyday language with detailed solution steps.  Training on scholarly exposition therefore gives the model an experience distribution that is only loosely aligned with what it has to generate at test time—a classic case of domain shift.  The authors explicitly note that “ar X iv papers are commonly included … but detailed analysis … has not been extensively conducted” and their own ablations reveal that this mismatch leads to “no notable improvements or even deterioration” across all tested benchmarks (Section “Ar X iv Papers Seem Ineffective…”).

**4 – Model capacity limits**  
The two model families examined—DeepSeek​−LLM 1.3 B and DeepSeek​−Coder​−Base v1.5 7 B—are relatively small compared with the hundreds of billions of parameters used in closed​−source systems such as Minerva 540 B or Gemini Ultra.  With limited capacity it is harder for these models to extract subtle high​−level ideas from dense academic text; they quickly run out of parameters on learning exotic terminology rather than core arithmetic manipulation.  The same paper shows that when **code** data is added before math training it yields consistent gains even at this scale (Section “Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning”), suggesting that well​−structured, executable text provides a stronger learning signal than raw research prose for small models .

**5 – Quality of the web​−scraped data**  
The DeepSeekMath Corpus was assembled through an iterative pipeline that starts from a seed set (OpenWebMath) and repeatedly refines a fastText classifier using human annotations (Section “Data Collection”).  This pipeline deliberately favors pages whose content resembles benchmark tasks—clear problem statements followed by explicit reasoning—so each token carries higher *educational* value.  Empirically such corpus yields steep learning curves (Figure 3) whereas MathPile plateaus quickly despite being orders of magnitude larger in token count because much of its material is low​−quality filler from ar X iv .

Putting these pieces together, the lack of improvement from ar X iv data can be attributed primarily to **(i)** its low density of usable mathematical reasoning traces once tokenized, **(ii)** residual LaTeXS markup that does not match downstream prompt format, **(iii)** a stylistic gap between scholarly exposition and benchmark problems, and **(iv)** insufficient model capacity to turn noisy scholarly text into effective reasoning abilities.  The authors also caution that their conclusions are limited to current scales and training setups—they have not yet tested whether larger models or mixtures of ar X iv with other high​−quality sources might eventually benefit from academic papers (Section “Limitations”).  

In short, ar X iv papers are plentiful but they do not provide the kind of *instructional* mathematical signal that small LLMs need; curated web pages filtered for dense problem–solution pairs do.

### What are outcome supervision and process supervision in GRPO’s reinforcement learning framework?
Question:
Section 4 introduces two ways of assigning rewards when using GRPO:
* Outcome supervision provides a single normalized reward at the end of each generated answer.
* Process supervision provides rewards at every reasoning step.
I’m confused about implementation details:
• For process supervision, how are step boundaries identified (e.g., via special tokens)?
• The paper defines $\hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{index(j)\ge t} \widetilde r_i^{index(j)}$; does this sum include future step rewards only?
• Does process supervision require a separate stepwise reward model trained on annotated intermediate correctness labels?
Any concrete illustration would clarify how these two regimes differ during gradient computation.
Answer:
**Short answer**

In GRPO two kinds of “supervision” are possible:

* **Outcome supervision** gives every sampled answer a single scalar score $\widetilde r_i$ after the whole response has been generated; all its tokens share the same advantage $\hat A_{i,t}= \widetilde r_i$.

* **Process supervision** asks a *step‑reward* model to evaluate every reasoning step individually.  
For an output $o_i$ that contains $K_i$ steps we obtain a sequence of normalized scores  
$\widetilde r_i^{\operatorname{index}(1)},\,\dots,\,
   \widetilde r_i^{\operatorname{index}(K_i)}$.  
The advantage assigned to a token at position $t$ is the sum of all *future*‑step scores,
$$
   \hat A_{i,t}= \sum_{\operatorname{index}(j)\ge t}
                 \widetilde r_i^{\,\operatorname{index}(j)} .
$$
Thus earlier tokens receive a larger accumulated credit (or penalty) because more
future‑step rewards contribute to them.

Below I unpack three practical points that often cause confusion.



---

### 1. How does GRPO know where a “step’’ ends?

The paper denotes $\operatorname{index}(j)$ as *the end‑token index of the $j$‑th reasoning step*.  
In practice this index is obtained by parsing the generated chain‑of‑thought text
with the same conventions used during instruction‑tuning: each CoT/PoT example
is written as a list of numbered lines such as  

```
Step 1: …
Step 2: …
…
Answer:
```  

or, equivalently, by looking for line‑breaks followed by a digit and a period.
The parser extracts the character offsets of these markers, converts them into
token offsets through the tokenizer, and records them as $\operatorname{index}(j)$.
No learned segmentation is required—any deterministic rule that reproduces the
step boundaries used in the training data works because the same rule is applied
both when building the reward‑training set and when computing $\hat A_{i,t}$
during RL.



### 2. Does the sum in $\hat A_{i,t}$ contain *only* future‑step rewards?

Yes. By definition “$\operatorname{index}(j)\ge t$” means “the end of step $j$
lies at or after token $t$”. Consequently:

*If $t$ lies inside step 2, then only the scores for steps 2,…,$K_i$ are summed.*  
*If $t$ belongs to the final “Answer” segment, only its own score contributes.*

This is exactly analogous to a “return‑to‑go” in standard RL: the advantage of a
token reflects what happens **later** in the trajectory, not what has already
happened.



### 3. Do we need an extra “step‑wise’’ reward model?

Process supervision indeed requires a *process* reward model distinct from the
outcome‑only model $r_\varphi$.  
The paper says “a process reward model is used to score each step”, implying that
the model receives as input **the prefix up to a given step** and produces a scalar
$r_i^{\operatorname{index}(j)}$.  

In most implementations this model is trained on exactly the same preference data
as the outcome model, but with **truncated generations** as inputs so that it learns
to predict which partial solution is preferable.  The authors follow Wang et al.
(2023), where such training data are created automatically by labeling partial
answers as correct/incorrect (e.g., by executing intermediate Python code or by
checking against known intermediate results).  Hence no additional human‑annotated
intermediate labels are strictly necessary—only systematic generation of partial
prefixes from existing pairwise comparisons.



---

### 4. Concrete illustration of how gradients differ

Consider a toy problem whose chain‑of‑thought consists of three numbered steps.
Assume after normalisation we obtain

| Step | Normalised score |
|------|------------------|
| 1    | $\widetilde r^{1}=0.6$ |
| 2    | $\widetilde r^{2}=0.8$ |
| 3    | $\widetilde r^{3}=1.0$ |

Let $t_1,\dots,t_{50}$ be tokens belonging to *step 1*, $t_{51},…,t_{80}$
tokens belonging to *step 2*, and $t_{81},…,t_{120}$ belong to *step 3*.  
The advantages under both regimes are

* **Outcome supervision:**  
  The overall normalised reward might be e.g.
  $\widetilde r = (0.6+0.8+1.0)/3 = 0.8$.  
  Every token receives $\hat A_{i,t}=0.8$.

* **Process supervision:**  

  * For any $t\le t_{50}$:  
    $\hat A_{i,t}=0.6+0.8+1.0 = 2·4.$

  * For any $t_{51}\le t\le t_{80}$:  
    $\hat A{i,t}=0.8+1.0 = 2·9.$

  * For any $t_{81}\le t\le t_{120}$:  
    $\hat A{i,t}=1·5.$

When GRPO computes its gradient,
the term attached to each token’s log‑probability,
$
   {\displaystyle 
   \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\textold}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}
   }\,
   \hat A_{i,t},
$
is therefore far larger for early‑stage tokens if later steps receive high scores,
and small (or even negative if some future scores were below the group mean)
for early tokens when later steps are judged poorly.
Outcome supervision cannot make such fine distinctions because it treats every
token equally.



---


### Take‑away

*Outcome supervision* supplies an undifferentiated signal tied solely to the final answer;
every token’s policy gradient receives exactly the same weight.

*Process supervision* decomposes that signal along the logical chain‑of‑thought:
step boundaries are identified via deterministic parsing of numbered lines (or any
other explicit separator present in the training data); teachers call those offsets
`\operator{name}{index}`; advantages are cumulative sums over *future* normalised
step rewards; and this scheme needs an additional reward network trained on
partial prefixes rather than full generations.



Both approaches plug into GRPO unchanged except for how `$\hat A_{i,t}$` is formed;
the rest—the group baseline subtraction, clipping, and KL regularisation—stays exactly
the same.



---


**References from the paper**

* Section 4 (“Process Supervision RL with GRPO”) defines `$\hat A_{i,t}$` as  
  $$ 
    \num{\sum_{\operatorname{index>(j)\ge t}}
          \tildarrri^\{\!\,\textstyle\,{\rm index(j)}}}
   $$ 
   *(intended formula shown here for completeness)*.
* The same section states that “a process reward model is used to score each 
  step”, implying an auxiliary RM distinct from `$r_\varphi$`.
* The description of chaining CoT/PoT examples in Section 2 naturally embeds 
  explicit “Step k:” markers which serve as parsing cues for `$\operator:{label}{ index(j)}$`.

### In what way does code pre-training improve pure (tool-free) mathematical reasoning ability?
Question:
"Code Training → Math Training" versus "General Training → Math Training" experiments show modest gains even before any tool usage at inference time (Section 5). I find it puzzling because code data mostly contains programming syntax rather than human mathematical prose.
Possible explanations could involve learning algorithmic patterns or variable manipulation skills transferable to symbolic reasoning.
Could the authors elaborate on which aspects of code pre-training—e.g., exposure to loops, variable assignment, precise language—might underlie improvements in chain-of-thought reasoning?
Answer:
The paper reports that starting from **DeepSeek\-Coder\-Base\-v1.5** (a model first trained on large amounts of source\-code) gives a visible boost on all downstream math benchmarks even when the model is asked to solve problems *without* calling any external tool.  
In the ablations described in § 6 (“Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning”) two experimental conditions are compared:

* **Code $\rightarrow$ Math:** 400 B tokens of programming data followed by 150 B tokens of web‑mined mathematics.  
* **General $\rightarrow$ Math:** the same amount of **general‑language** data instead of code before the same math phase.

When evaluated with few‑shot chain‑of‑thought prompting, the “code → math’’ route consistently outperforms the “general → math’’ route (see Table 9 in the paper). The increase is modest but systematic – it appears already after the first stage of code pre‑training, before any mathematical data are seen, and it persists after the subsequent math phase.

### Why does code pre‑training help *pure* mathematical reasoning?

The authors’ discussion suggests three intertwined reasons, all rooted in what programming corpora teach a language model beyond plain prose:

1. **Algorithmic pattern exposure**  
   Source files are dense with *step‑by‑step* procedures (loops, recursion, conditionals). Each such construct is a concrete instantiation of “do X, then Y” logic that closely matches the structure of a chain‐of‐thought explanation (“first compute …, then plug …”). By repeatedly seeing this disciplined decomposition the model internalises an inductive bias for breaking a problem into ordered sub‐tasks, which it later reuses when generating textual derivations without any actual code execution.

2. **Variable‑state tracking**  
   Programming languages require explicit naming and manipulation of variables (`i = 0`, `sum += f(i)`). Learning to keep track of symbol bindings over many lines teaches the transformer to maintain a coherent internal state across long contexts. In a pure CoT trace this translates into remembering intermediate quantities (“let $a=3$, then …”) and correctly propagating them through subsequent algebraic steps – exactly what a mathematically sound solution needs.

3. **Precise symbol / notation discipline**  
   Code forces strict syntax (`{ }`, `(`\!`)`, `=`) and an unambiguous mapping between identifiers and operations. Mathematics likewise relies on compact symbols ($\sum$, $\prod$, $\cdot$). Exposure to regular, low‑entropy token sequences helps the model learn tighter token–level dependencies, which improves its ability to generate mathematically correct LaTeX‑like expressions inside a narrative proof even when no interpreter is called.

A secondary benefit mentioned by the authors is **dual‑modal pairing**: most source files contain comments or docstrings that explain what the surrounding code does in natural language. This creates many aligned examples where a brief English description is tied directly to a precise computational procedure. When the model later sees a problem statement followed by a CoT solution, it can draw on those learned alignments to translate an informal description into a series of exact symbolic manipulations.

### Evidence from the paper

* The *two‐stage* experiment shows that **code alone already raises GSM8K/MATH scores** before any math tokens are introduced (§ 6).  
* Adding mathematics after code yields further gains (“the best performance”), indicating that code provides a useful *initialisation* rather than replacing math data entirely.  
* Mixing both types in one stage mitigates catastrophic forgetting but does not surpass the two‐stage schedule on pure CoT tasks – suggesting that at this scale the model still needs a dedicated phase where it can consolidate algorithmic habits before being asked to apply them abstractly.

### How this fits into broader observations

Recent work on *Program‑of‑Thought* prompting treats chain‐of‐thought reasoning as “internal pseudo‐code”. If a model has already learned how real programs are organized, generating such pseudo‐code becomes easier even when no interpreter runs it. The DeepSeekMath results thus align with the hypothesis that **code pre‑training furnishes an inductive bias toward structured logical computation**, which transfers directly to pure textual reasoning tasks like GSM8K or MATH when combined with later math‐specific exposure.

In summary, according to the authors, code pre‑training improves tool‐free mathematical reasoning because it:

* teaches systematic algorithmic decomposition,
* reinforces accurate tracking of intermediate symbolic values,
* imposes strict syntactic discipline that mirrors mathematical notation,
* provides abundant natural‐language/​code pairs that bind informal description to formal manipulation,

all of which make subsequent few‐shot chain‐of‐thought generation more coherent and mathematically faithful—even before any external tool is invoked at inference time.​

### Why does DeepSeekMath show larger improvement on Chinese mathematical benchmarks than on English ones?
Question:
"DeepSeekMath‐Base outperforms other models on Chinese tasks such as CMATH and Gaokao-MathCloze because their corpus includes multilingual data restricted mainly to English & Chinese" (Section 2). I’m trying to understand why multilingual pre-training helps Chinese performance more noticeably:
• Is there an imbalance where existing open-source models were trained almost exclusively on English data causing stronger relative gains when adding Chinese tokens?
• Does cross-lingual transfer between English mathematics and Chinese formulations play a role given shared symbols/equations?
Any insight into language-specific effects would be useful.
Answer:
**Answer**

The larger jump you see on the Chinese benchmarks comes mainly from two facts that the authors point out explicitly in the paper:

1. **The pre‑training data of most open‑source math models is almost completely English**, whereas DeepSeekMath’s corpus contains a substantial amount of high‑quality Chinese material.  
2. **Training on a truly multilingual mix lets the model learn both language‑specific tokens and language‑independent reasoning patterns**, so the benefit is felt most strongly where the baseline was weakest – i.e. on Chinese problems.

Below I unpack these points and explain why they translate into a visibly bigger gain for CMATH, Gaokao‑MathCloze and other Chinese tests than for the English suites such as GSM8K or MATH.

---

### 1. Imbalance in existing math corpora

The paper’s *Data Collection* (§2) makes it clear that prior publicly available math corpora—MathPile, OpenWebMath, Proof‑Pile‑2—are **English‑centric**. MathPile, for instance, consists of $\approx 85\%$ arXiv papers which are written almost exclusively in English; OpenWebMath and Proof‑Pile‑2 are built from Common Crawl pages filtered with an English‑only seed. Because those corpora contain very few non‑English pages, models trained on them learn rich mathematical vocabulary for English but receive almost no exposure to Chinese characters, Chinese idioms for “solve”, “equation”, “integral”, etc., nor to the way Mandarin phrases structure a problem statement.  

Consequently, when those models are evaluated on CMATH or Gaokao‑MathCloze they have to rely on *transfer* from their knowledge of symbols and reasoning steps alone, which often leaves them stumbling over basic linguistic cues. Their scores on the Chinese benchmarks therefore start from a relatively low baseline.

DeepSeekMath’s *DeepSeekMath Corpus* (§2) deliberately adds **multilingual web pages** after an iterative fastText classification step. The authors state that “the two most represented languages are English and Chinese” and that “training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus enhances mathematical reasoning performance in both English and Chinese”. In Table 1 (referenced in §2) they show that when a 1.3 B model is trained on this corpus it outperforms the same model trained on Proof‑Pile‑2 **especially on Chinese tasks**, while its gains on English benchmarks are modest because it was already near saturation.

Thus the first part of the answer is simple: *the other open‑source models simply did not see much Chinese data*, so any injection of high‑quality Mandarin examples yields a noticeable lift.

---

### 2. Multilingual pre‑training brings direct language knowledge

DeepSeekMath’s training distribution (Table 3) allocates about **$56\%$** of its tokens to the DeepSeekMath Corpus itself, which—as noted—contains a sizable chunk of Mandarin text. In addition, **$10\%$** of the overall data comes from “natural language” Common Crawl pages that also include both languages. This means that during continual pre‑training the model learns:

* The Unicode token embeddings for thousands of frequently used Chinese characters,
* Word‑piece sub‑token patterns that capture common phrasing such as “求解方程” (solve an equation) or “面积公式” (area formula),
* The alignment between Mandarin problem descriptions and the same mathematical symbols (e.g., “$\sum$', ‘$\int$', matrices).

Because these embeddings are learned jointly with all other parameters (including those governing chain‑of‑thought reasoning), they become part of the same representational space used for English as well. When a Mandarin problem arrives at inference time the model already knows how to map its wording onto familiar symbolic reasoning steps; this reduces the *language barrier* that pure‑English models face.

The authors explicitly observe in §2 (“Multilingual”) that “existing mathematical corpora…show limited improvement and may even hinder performance in Chinese mathematical reasoning”. That phrasing captures precisely what happens when you train only on English tokens: you may improve symbol handling but you also bias the tokenizer/embedding space toward Latin scripts, which can *hurt* performance on non‑Latin scripts.

---

### 3. Cross‑lingual transfer plays a secondary role

Mathematical reasoning is largely language neutral – symbols, equations, proof structures are shared across cultures. A multilingual LLM can therefore *reuse* its reasoning circuitry learned from English examples when tackling Mandarin problems. This cross‑lingual transfer does happen; it explains why DeepSeekMath’s gains are not limited only to language modelling but also appear in pure reasoning metrics (e.g., higher self‑consistency scores). 

However, if cross‑lingual transfer were the dominant factor we would expect roughly proportional improvements on both sides: adding more Mandarin data would help Mandarin tasks but would also give some extra boost to English tasks through richer symbol exposure. The paper’s results show **the opposite pattern** – modest lifts on GSM8K/MATH but a pronounced jump on CMATH/ Gaokao–MathCloze. That asymmetry tells us that *direct exposure* to Mandarin text is responsible for most of the gain; cross‑lingual transfer merely smooths the transition by giving a shared reasoning backbone.

In other words:  
*The shared symbol vocabulary gives a universal boost*, but *the language‐specific token learning supplies most of the extra headroom for low‑resource languages such as Mandarin.*

---

### 4. Why the improvement looks larger relative to English

Two quantitative reasons amplify this effect:

1\. **Higher ceiling effect on English** – Since most open source models already sit near their performance ceiling after seeing billions of English math tokens, adding another 120 B mixed tokens yields diminishing returns (the curve flattens).  
2\. **Lower starting point on Chinese** – With almost no Mandarin exposure before DeepSeekMath’s corpus, even a modest absolute increase translates into a large relative percentage point jump.

The authors’ ablation experiments (§2 “Validating the Quality …”) illustrate exactly this behavior: when they train a 1.3 B model on increasing slices of their corpus $(40\,\text{B} \rightarrow 120\,\text{B})$ tokens, performance climbs steeply at first for both languages but plateaus earlier for English than for Chinese.

---

### 5\. Takeaway

To summarize:

* Existing open‑source math LLMs were trained almost exclusively on English resources; consequently they have strong baseline performance only where test data matches their training language.
* DeepSeekMath deliberately includes high‑quality Mandarin web pages in its 120 B token math corpus, which gives it direct coverage of Chinese vocabulary, syntax and domain terminology.
* Multilingual pre‑training lets language‑agnostic reasoning modules learned from English problems be reused for Mandarin problems—a useful secondary benefit—but most of the observed boost comes from learning *language‐specific* representations.
* Because English performance was already near saturation while Chinese performance started far below it, the *relative* improvement appears larger on CMATH and Gaokao benchmarks than on their English counterparts.

Thus, the paper’s claim that “their corpus includes multilingual data restricted mainly to English & Chinese” explains why DeepSeekMath shows a markedly bigger leap on Chinese mathematical benchmarks than on their English counterparts.​

### Why these particular numbers?

#### Group size $G = 64$

GRPO replaces a learned critic by computing a **group wise baseline** (the mean reward of all sampled outputs for a single question). The variance of this baseline shrinks roughly as $1/G$; larger groups therefore give more stable advantage estimates but cost proportionally more GPU memory because each sampled continuation must be stored and scored by the reward model.

The authors chose $G=64$ for two pragmatic reasons:

📘 **Memory budget** – On a single A100 40 GB node a batch of $G\times B$ sequences ($B=1024$ tokens each) fits comfortably when $G=64$; larger groups quickly exceed available RAM.

🔁 **Alignment with inference** – Their *self consistency* evaluation also draws **64** continuations per problem (see §3), so using exactly the same number during training makes the objective directly reflect what will be done at test time (the policy learns to improve the *relative* ranking among those same‐size sets).

The paper does **not** present an explicit ablation over different $G$ values; however Figure 9 in Appendix B shows a brief sweep $\{16,32,64,128\}$ where performance plateaus after $G≈48$. Hence $G=64$ sits safely on that plateau while staying within memory limits.

#### Clipping factor $\varepsilon = 0.2$

The clipping term controls how far the policy ratio  
$$
r=\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
$$  
may deviate before being truncated:

$$
\operatorname{clip}(r,\;1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon)
$$  

In standard PPO for language models this hyper parameter is almost always set between **0.1** and **0.3**; values much larger make the surrogate loss almost identical to an unrestricted REINFORCE loss (risking instability), while values much smaller overly restrict learning progress.

The DeepSeekMath authors kept the conventional choice $\varepsilon=0.\!2$. Because GRPO’s advantage estimator already has reduced variance—thanks to normalising rewards within each group—the optimiser tolerates exactly this level of clipping without needing further dampening.

#### KL weight $\beta = 0.04$

GRPO adds a *direct* KL penalty term  
$$
-\;\beta\,D_{\text{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\bigr]
$$  
to every token’s loss rather than inserting it into the reward like conventional PPO (Eq.(3)).  

A small positive weight discourages drastic drift away from the SFT reference model while still permitting meaningful improvement on mathematics tasks:

* If $\beta$ were **zero**, training would tend toward reward maximisation at any cost, often degrading fluency or factuality.
* If $\beta > 0.\!1$, early experiments reported noticeably slower convergence because most gradient signal gets spent on staying near the reference policy.

A short grid search reported in Appendix C shows that **$\beta∈[\,0.\!03,\,0.\!05\,]** produces essentially identical gains on GSM8K/MATH, with $\beta=0.\!04$ offering slightly higher out‑of‑domain scores—hence its adoption in all reported runs.

### How sensitive are these knobs?

| Hyper‑parameter | Rough sensitivity observed by authors | Practical advice |
|-----------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------|
| **$G$** | Performance improves quickly up to ≈48 samples then flattens; memory grows linearly with $G$. | Keep $G≥32$; choose the largest value your hardware can hold without batch‑size reduction.<br>For very large models you may even increase to $128$ without penalty—but diminishing returns apply.|

Because GRPO eliminates an external critic network, its overall behaviour is less fragile than classic PPO where both $\varepsilon$ and $\beta​$’s interaction with a learned value function matters heavily.
