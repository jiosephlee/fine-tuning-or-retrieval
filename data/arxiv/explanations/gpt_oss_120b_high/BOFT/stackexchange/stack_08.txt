\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does multiplicative dropout work in BOFT and why can't standard dropout be used?
Question:
In LoRA a conventional dropout layer can be added because the update is additive ($\\Delta W$). BOFT updates weights multiplicatively via an orthogonal matrix $\\bm R$, so standard dropout would break the structure.

The paper proposes:
* randomly pick $p_1$% of butterfly components,
* pick $p_2$% of diagonal blocks inside those components,
* replace those blocks with identity.

I’m unclear on:
1. At training time, are these replacements permanent for a given forward pass or temporary stochastic masks?
2. Does dropping whole components affect gradient flow through remaining components?
3. How does this scheme preserve overall orthogonality during training?
A concrete example with $m=3$, $b=4$, $p_1=30%$, $p_2=50%$ would help.
Answer:
In BOFT the trainable weight matrix is written as  

$$
\bR(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\;\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
    \qquad 
    \widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\bR^{d\times d},
$$

where every factor $\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is an *orthogonal* butterfly component (it is obtained from a block‑diagonal matrix whose $b\times b$ diagonal blocks are themselves parametrised as orthogonal matrices via a Cayley transform).  
Because the whole finetuned weight is obtained by *multiplying* these matrices, any regularisation has to respect the multiplicative structure—otherwise the product would cease to be orthogonal.  

The paper therefore introduces **multiplicative dropout** (Section “Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT”).  
Below we spell out how it works, what it does to the forward/back‑propagation graph, and why it never destroys orthogonality.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Is the replacement permanent or temporary?

The replacement is **temporary** and stochastic exactly like ordinary dropout.  
For every training mini‑batch a new mask is sampled:

* pick a subset of the $m$ butterfly components,
* inside every selected component pick a subset of its diagonal $b\times b$ blocks,
* replace those chosen blocks *only* for this forward pass by the identity matrix $\bI_b$.

After the backward pass the mask is discarded; on the next iteration a new mask may be drawn.  
At inference time *no* mask is applied—all learned parameters are used unchanged.  

Thus multiplicative dropout behaves like a “drop‑the‑whole‑block” version of standard dropout rather than a permanent pruning step.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Effect on gradient flow

Consider one forward pass where component $k$ has some of its blocks forced to $\bI_b$.  
The forward computation reads  

$$
\bz
=
    \Bigl(
        \underbrace{\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,m)}}_{\text{maybe}\;\bI}
        \;\cdots
        \underbrace{\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,k)}}_{\text{partially }\bI}
        \;\cdots
        \underbrace{\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,1)}}_{\text{full}}
        \;
        \bW^{0}
    \Bigr)^{!\top}\!\bx .
$$

During back‑propagation the chain rule traverses exactly this sequence of matrices.  
If a whole *component* has been turned into $\bI_d$ then it contributes no Jacobian term – effectively it acts as a “skip connection”.  
If only some **diagonal blocks** inside a component have been set to $\bI_b$, then those particular sub‑matrices produce an identity Jacobian for their sub‑space while the remaining learned $b\times b$ blocks still provide non‑trivial gradients.  

Consequently  

* Gradients flow normally through all *active* (non‑dropped) blocks.  
* Dropped blocks receive **zero** gradient in that step because their output does not depend on any learnable parameter.  
* Because masks are resampled every step, every block gets updated on roughly $(1-p_1)(1-p_2)$ fraction of iterations—exactly the same stochastic regularisation pattern used by classic dropout.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Why does orthogonality stay intact?

Two facts guarantee that the product stays on the Stiefel manifold after each stochastic mask:

| Factor | Property |
|-------|-----------|
| Each **learned** butterfly component $\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ | By construction it satisfies $(\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{!\top}\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\bone_d$; this follows from parametrising every $b\times b$ block with a Cayley transform (or equivalently a $2b\times2b$ rotation). |
| The **identity** substitution | The identity matrix $\bone_d$ (or an identity $b\times b$ block placed inside an otherwise block‑diagonal structure) is itself an element of the orthogonal group—its transpose equals its inverse. |

Since an ordered product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal, any mixture  

$$
\bRhat=
    \Bigl(
        \prod_{i\,\notin S}
            \widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}
    \Bigr)
    \
    \Bigl(
        \prod_{i\,in S}
            \underbrace{
                \bone_d
            }_{\text{or }\;\operatorname{diag}(\ldots,\bone_b,\ldots)}
    \Bigr),
$$

with arbitrary index set $S$, satisfies  

$$
\bRhat^{!\top}\,\bRhat=
    \
    \Bigl(
        \prod_i (\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{!\top}
    \Bigr)
    \
    \Bigl(
        \prod_i   \widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}
    \Bigr)
=
    \
    \bone_d .
$$

Hence *any* mask produced by multiplicative dropout yields an **exactly orthogonal** transformation; no additional projection or re‑normalisation step is needed.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Small concrete example

Take  

* dimension $d = 16$,  
* number of butterfly factors $m = 3$,  
* block size $b = 4$.  

For simplicity assume that every factor consists of exactly $d/b = 4$ diagonal $4\times4$ blocks:

```
⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂
⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂
⊢⊢⊢⊢ × ⊢⊢⊢⊢ × ⊢⊢⊢⊢   ← three factors
```

where each “⊢’’ denotes an *orthogonal* $4\times4$ matrix learned via Cayley transform.  
The final transformation applied to a pretrained layer $\bW^0$ is  

$$
\bR=
    \widetilde\bB^{\,4}_{(16,3)}
    \
    \widetilde\bB^{\,4}_{(16,2)}
    \
    \widetilde\bB^{\,4}_{(16,1)} .
$$

Now set dropout probabilities  

$p_{1}=30~percent,\quad p_{2}=50~percent.$  

**Step 1 – choose components**  
With probability $0.30$ each factor is selected; suppose only factor $k=2$ gets selected in this iteration (the other two stay untouched).

**Step 2 – choose diagonal blocks inside factor 2**  
Factor 2 contains four $4\times4$ blocks:  
$\displaystyle M^{(2)}_{1},M^{(2)}_{2},M^{(2)}_{3},M^{(2)}_{4}$.  
We sample $50~percent$, i.e.\ two blocks are chosen at random; assume they are $M^{(2)}_{1}$ and $M^{(2)}_{3}$.

**Step 3 – replace by identities**

```
Factor 1 (unchanged):
diag(M¹₁ , M¹₂ , M¹₃ , M¹₄)

Factor 2 (after dropout):
diag(I₄ , M²₂ , I₄ , M²₄)

Factor 3 (unchanged):
diag(M³₁ , M³₂ , M³₃ , M³₄)
```

All other entries remain exactly as they were before this batch.  
The forward pass uses this concrete $\widehat\bR$, the loss back‑propagates through  

* all three factors,
* only through $M^{(i)}_{j}$ that were not turned into $\bone$, 
* zero gradients for $M^{(2)}_{1}$ and $M^{(2)}_{3}$ in this iteration.  

On the next minibatch another random mask will be drawn, possibly affecting different factors or different blocks; after many iterations every block will have been trained on many independent stochastic views, just like classic dropout regularises activations.

Because every substituted piece (`I₄`) belongs to $\cO(4)$,
the product $\widehat\bR$ remains strictly orthogonal throughout training,
and at test time we simply evaluate  

```
R_test = Π_{i=1}^{3} tilde B⁴_(16,i)
``` 

with all learned parameters restored—no extra inference cost.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* **Temporal vs permanent:** Multiplicative dropout draws a fresh binary mask per forward pass; replacements are *temporary*. No change persists after back‑propagation.  
* **Gradient impact:** Dropped whole butterfly components act as identity skips; dropped diagonal $b\times b$ blocks become constant identities so their parameters receive zero gradient while all remaining parameters continue receiving normal gradients.  
* **Orthogonality preservation:** Both learned factors and identity replacements belong to the orthogonal group, therefore any stochastic product stays exactly orthogonal without extra projection.  
* **Why ordinary dropout fails:** Standard dropout zeros individual entries inside $\bm R$. Zeroing arbitrary entries destroys column‑norm / row‑norm constraints required for an $O(d)$ matrix, breaking the guarantee that $\bm R^\top\bm R=\bone$. Multiplicative dropout respects the *blockwise* structure demanded by the butterfly parameterisation and keeps every factor on $\cO(d)$.  
* **Concrete illustration:** With $m=3$, $b=4$, $p_1=30~%$, $p_2=50~%$, one forward pass might leave factors 1 & 3 untouched while turning half of factor 2’s $4\times4$ blocks into identities—a perfectly legal stochastic regulariser that also yields sparse gradients exactly as described above.

Thus multiplicative dropout gives BOFT a regularisation mechanism analogous to conventional dropout while honouring its essential multiplicative–orthogonal architecture."
