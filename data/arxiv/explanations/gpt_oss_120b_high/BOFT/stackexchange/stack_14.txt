\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### When applying BOFT to transformer models, should every linear projection be replaced by an orthogonal transformation?
Question:
Transformers contain several linear layers per block (query/key/value projections, feed‑forward FC layers). The paper applies BOFT at "linear layers" – would you recommend applying it uniformly across all such layers or only at selected ones (e.g., attention projections)? Also, would placing OFT/BOFT before versus after layer normalisation change its effect?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In practice the authors treat “a linear layer’’ exactly as the place where BOFT lives, i.e. they replace the *weight matrix* $W^{0}$ by an orthogonal matrix $R$ times $W^{0}$. Consequently every dense projection inside a Transformer block—​the three query/key/value projections, the attention‑output projection, and both fully‑connected layers of the feed‑forward network—​can be substituted with a BOFT‑parameterised version without changing any other architectural element.  
If you want fewer trainable parameters you may restrict BOFT to a subset of these projections (e.g. only the attention‑output or only the MLP), but this is a budget decision rather than a requirement of the method.  

Placing the orthogonal transform **before** LayerNorm (i.e. inside the original Linear module) preserves the intended geometry: it rotates each neuron’s incoming weight vector while leaving its length unchanged, so pair‑wise angles between rows – the quantity that OFT/BOFT is designed to keep intact – are unchanged even after normalisation downstream. Moving the transform **after** LayerNorm would act on already normalised activations instead of on the pretrained weights, breaking the angle‑preserving property and usually harming stability; empirically this behaviour mirrors what was observed for plain OFT in earlier work.

Below I explain why this choice follows directly from the theory in the paper and how it translates into concrete implementation choices for Transformers.

### 1. What “applying BOFT’’ actually means  

OFS/BOFT re‑parameterises a pretrained linear mapping  

$$
z = (W^{0})^\top x \quad \longrightarrow \quad
z = (R\, W^{0})^\top x,
$$

where $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constrained to be orthogonal ($R^\top R = I$).  
All derivations – the information‑transmission view (Sec.\ 3), Cayley parameterisation, identity initialisation – are carried out **on this left‑multiplication**.  

Because every dense Linear module in a Transformer can be written as $Wx+b$, replacing $W$ by $RW$ implements exactly what the authors call *Orthogonal Finetuning*. The butterfly factorisation simply provides a compact way to represent $R$:  

$$
R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B_{(d,i)}^{\,b},
\qquad
\#\,\text{trainable}= \tfrac12(b-1)d\,m = \mathcal O(d\,{\log }d),
$$

so each Linear layer incurs only $\mathcal O(d\,{\log }d)$ extra parameters instead of $\mathcal O(d^{2})$.

### 2\. Which Transformers’ Linear maps are usually targeted  

A standard encoder/decoder block contains four distinct families of dense projections:

| Component | Shape (typical “BERT‑style’’) | Does it fit $RW^{0}$? |
|---|---|---|
| Query / Key / Value | $d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}$ | **Yes** – square, so $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$. |
| Attention output | $d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}$ | **Yes** |
| Feed‑forward up‑projection | $4d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}$ | **Yes** – $R$ multiplies from the left, matching the output dimension $4d_{\text{model}}$. |
| Feed‑forward down‑projection | $d_{\text{model}}\times 4d_{\text{model}}$ | **Yes** – now $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{model}}}$. |

Hence there is no structural obstacle: every projection can be rewritten as “$RW^{0}$”. In their experiments (“we apply BOFT … to large language models”, Sec.\ 5) the authors indeed replace *all* such matrices, which yields uniform regularisation across attention and MLP pathways and simplifies bookkeeping—each block shares exactly one hyper‑parameter pair $(m,b)$.

### 3\. When you might skip some projections  

Because each projection adds $\tfrac12(b-1)d\,m$ trainable scalars, a very deep model could still exceed a strict budget if all matrices are adapted simultaneously (e.g.\ tiny block size $b=2$ gives roughly $\sim 0.5\,d\,m \approx 0.5\,d\,{\log _2}d$).  
If your budget forces you narrower:
* **Attention‑only** finetuning – apply BOFT just to $Q,K,V,O$; this follows many LoRA studies that treat attention as most task‑relevant.
* **MLP‑only** finetuning – adapt just the two feed‑forward layers; useful for vision Transformers where spatial mixing tends to dominate.
* **Hybrid** – e.g., leave $Q,K,V$ unchanged while adapting only $O+$MLP, which reduces parameters roughly by half while keeping most expressive power (the split‐into‐heads structure still benefits from an orthogonal rotation at the final recombination).

These choices are *implementation knobs*, not algorithmic constraints—the method works identically whichever subset you select because each chosen matrix simply receives its own independent set of butterfly factors.

### 4\. Why placement relative to LayerNorm matters  

LayerNorm computes  

$$
\operatorname{LN}(u)=
\dfrac{u-\mu(u)}{\sigma(u)}\,.\;\!\odot\,\gamma+\beta,
$$

where $\mu,\sigma$ are statistics over the channel dimension for each token independently.  
Two observations follow from the analysis in Sec.\ 4 (“Spectral properties”)::

1\. **Angle preservation holds only for weight rotations**, not for rotations applied *after* scaling/shifting steps that break Euclidean geometry across channels.
2\. **Spectral norm preservation**, crucial for stability ($||RW^{0}||_{2}=||W^{0}||_{2}$), assumes that $R$ multiplies *directly* onto $W^{0}$. If we insert an extra BN/LN between them then  

$$
z = \operatorname{LN}\big((RW^{0})^\top x\bigr)
     \neq (R(W^{0})^\top)\,x,
$$

so singular values are altered by $\operatorname{LN}$’s learned scale $\gamma$—the theoretical guarantee disappears.



Consequently, placing BOFT **before** any LayerNorm––exactly where $W$ lives in the original model––keeps both angle‐preserving and spectral‐norm properties intact throughout training. Moving it *after* LayerNorm would therefore behave more like an additional learned affine transform rather than an orthogonal finetuning step; empirically such variants typically degrade convergence speed or final accuracy (this mirrors findings for vanilla OFT reported in § 3 of [qiu2023controlling]).

The discussion also clarifies why there is no “pre‑L​N vs post‑L​N” ablation in this paper: their design deliberately follows standard Transformer implementations where every learnable matrix appears *before* any normalisation step inside a sublayer, making that placement canonical.

### 5\. Practical recipe  

```python
# pseudo‑code illustrating a single transformer block
class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.q_proj   = OrthogonalLinear(d_model,d_model,
                                          boft_cfg=(m,b))   # ← replaces nn.Linear
        self.k_proj   = OrthogonalLinear(d_model,d_model,
                                          boft_cfg=(m,b))
        self.v_proj   = OrthogonalLinear(d_model,d_model,
                                          boft_cfg=(m,b))
        self.o_proj   = OrthogonalLinear(d_model,d_model,
                                          boft_cfg=(m,b))
        self.ff_up    = OrthogonalLinear(d_model,4*d_model,
                                          boft_cfg=(m,b))
        self.ff_down  = OrthogonalLinear(4*d_model,d_model,
                                          boft_cfg=(m,b))
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(d_model)

    def forward(self,x):
        # ---------- attention ----------
        q,k,v = self.q_proj(x), self.k_proj(x), self.v_proj(x)
        att   = self.o_proj(attention(q,k,v))          # <-- R·W applied here
        x     = self.ln1(x + att)

        # ---------- feed‑forward ----------
        y     = self.ff_down(F.gelu(self.ff_up(x)))    # <-- R·W applied here
        out   = self.ln2(x + y)
        return out
```

All ``OrthogonalLinear`` modules initialise their butterfly factors as identities (Section “Identity initialization”), guaranteeing that finetuning starts exactly at the pretrained checkpoint ($R=I$). During training each ``OrthogonalLinear`` multiplies ``x`` successively by its $m$ sparse butterfly components ($\tilde B_{(d,i)}^{b}$), incurring negligible extra runtime compared with ordinary Linear layers; after finetuning one can fold all $R$ into $W$ so inference sees no extra latency—a point emphasised in § “Concluding Remarks”.

If your GPU budget forces you prune further:
```python
# Example: adapt only attention output & MLP
self.o_proj   = OrthogonalLinear(...)
self.ff_up    = OrthogonalLinear(...)
self.ff_down  = OrthogonalLinear(...)
# keep q/k/v as ordinary nn.Linear(...)
```
The total number of trainable parameters scales linearly with how many projections you keep under BOFT.

### TL;DR  

* Replace every dense Linear projection whose output dimension equals some hidden size ($d$) by an ``OrthogonalLinear`` built from butterfly factors—this matches exactly what “apply BOFT to linear layers’’ means in the paper.
* You may skip some projections if your parameter budget demands it; doing so simply reduces expressivity but does not break anything conceptually.
* Keep these orthogonal matrices *inside* the original Linear modules (**before** LayerNorm). Moving them after normalisation discards angle preservation and spectral‐norm guarantees and usually hurts training stability.

Following this recipe reproduces how the authors obtained their state‑of‑the‑art results on large language models and vision Transformers while still enjoying zero inference overhead once finetuning finishes.
