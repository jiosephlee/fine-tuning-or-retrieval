\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How were random dense orthogonal matrices generated for the expressivity experiments (Anderson 1987)?
Question:
Figure 4 approximates random dense orthogonal matrices of size 512×512 using BOFT variants. The citation refers to Anderson’s method – could anyone summarise how those matrices were sampled (e.g., via QR decomposition of Gaussian matrices), and whether alternative sampling methods would change the observed approximation error curves?
Answer:
The experiments in Figure 4 are meant to test how well the different BOFT parametrisations can recover an arbitrary element of the orthogonal group $O(d)$.  
For this purpose the authors draw the “target’’ matrices from the distribution described in Anderson (1987), which is nowadays regarded as **Haar‑uniform** random orthogonal matrices.  

In practice the Anderson procedure works as follows. First one generates a square matrix $\,\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ whose entries are independent standard normal variables  
$$
Z_{ij}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\qquad(i,j=1,\dots ,d).
$$
Because a multivariate Gaussian is rotationally invariant, the column vectors of $\mathbf{Z}$ are themselves i.i.d.\ isotropic directions in $\mathbb R^{d}$. One then computes a QR‑decomposition  
$$
\mathbf{Z}= \mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{R},
$$
where $\mathbf{Q}\in O(d)$ has orthonormal columns and $\mathbf{R}$ is upper‑triangular. The factor $\mathbf{Q}$ alone would already be orthogonal, but its distribution would be biased toward having positive diagonal entries in $\mathbf{R}$. To obtain an exactly uniform (Haar) draw one finally multiplies each column of $\mathbf{Q}$ by the sign of the corresponding diagonal element of $\mathbf{R}$:
$$
\mathbf{U}= \mathbf{Q}\,\operatorname{diag}\!\bigl(\operatorname{sign}(R_{11}),\dots ,\operatorname{sign}(R_{dd})\bigr).
$$
If one wishes to restrict to rotations ($\det\!\mathbf U=+1$) one can flip an additional column when $\det\!\mathbf U=-1$; otherwise both signs appear with equal probability and $\mathbf U$ is drawn uniformly from $O(d)$. The matrix $\mathbf U$ is what the authors refer to as “a random dense orthogonal matrix’’ and it serves as the ground‑truth target for each run in Figure 4.  

An equivalent formulation goes back to Anderson as well: instead of QR one may successively apply **Householder reflections** built from independent Gaussian vectors.  If $\mathbf v_{k}\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathbf I_d)$ then  
$$
\mathbf H_k = \mathbf I_d-2\,\frac{\mathbf v_k\,\mathbf v_k^\top}{\|\mathbf v_k\|^2}
$$
is an elementary reflector, and the product  
$$
\displaystyle 
   \bigl(\prod_{k=d}^{1} \!\,\!\,\! H_k\bigr)
   = H_d\,H_{d-1}\cdots H_1
$$  
has exactly the same Haar distribution as obtained by the QR‑method above.  Both procedures are merely different ways of performing an isotropic Gram–Schmidt process on a set of Gaussian vectors; they therefore generate indistinguishable ensembles of orthogonal matrices apart from numerical round‑off differences.  

Because all reasonable implementations (Gaussian‑QR, Gaussian‑Householder, or a cascade of Givens rotations with angles drawn uniformly from $[0,2\pi)$) converge to the same Haar measure, swapping one for another does **not** change the qualitative shape of the approximation‑error curves shown in Section 5. The curves plot error versus number of effective trainable parameters after averaging ten independent draws.  As long as each draw comes from a distribution that is invariant under left‑multiplication by any fixed orthogonal matrix—i.e.\ any proper Haar sampler—the expected error for a given parametrisation depends only on how rich that parametrisation’s subspace is within $O(d)$.  Consequently each BOFT variant experiences essentially the same statistical difficulty across all samplers and their relative ordering stays unchanged.  

Only a non‑Haar sampling scheme could appreciably shift the absolute error levels. For example, drawing target matrices that happen to be close to block‑diagonal or have many zero entries would make any dense parametrisation look easier than it truly is, whereas sampling from a biased family concentrated near low‑entropy rotations could make all methods appear harder. The authors deliberately avoided such shortcuts by using Anderson’s construction precisely because it produces **unbiased**, fully dense objects spread uniformly over $O(d)$; this guarantees that Figure 4 truly reflects generic expressivity rather than artefacts of a particular generator.

In short, the “random dense orthogonal matrices’’ used in the expressivity experiment were produced by drawing a $512\times512$ Gaussian matrix, performing a QR factorisation, correcting column signs according to the diagonal of $R$, and optionally flipping a column if one desires orientation‑preserving samples only. Any alternative algorithm that yields samples from the same Haar distribution—such as successive Householder reflections—will give virtually identical results, whereas deliberately biased generators would alter both absolute errors and possibly distort comparisons among BOFT variants.
