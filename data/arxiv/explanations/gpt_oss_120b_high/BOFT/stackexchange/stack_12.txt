\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does identity initialization ensure zero change in BOFT compared to zero init in LoRA?
Question:
Both LoRA and OFT/BOFT aim for small deviation from pretrained weights at start.

LoRA sets added low‑rank matrices to zero so ${\\bm W}'={\\bm W}^{0}$ initially.
BOFT sets all butterfly components to identity via Cayley’s skew‑symmetric matrix initialized as zero.

My confusion:
1. Since BOFT composes several identity matrices, isn’t ${\\bm R}=I$, thus ${\\bm W}'={\\bm W}^{0}$ trivially—so far so good.
2. But during training gradients flow through products of factors; could early updates break this property more aggressively than LoRA’s additive updates?
3. Is there any theoretical justification that starting from identity yields similar stability as zero init in additive schemes?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In BOFT every butterfly component is initialized as an exact identity matrix (the Cayley‑parameterised skew‑symmetric matrix **Q** is set = 0), so the product  

$$
\mathbf{R}= \tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,m)}\,\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,m-1)}\,\dots\, \tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,1)}
$$  

starts as $\displaystyle \mathbf{R}= \mathbf I$. Consequently  

$$
\mathbf W'^{\,\top}= (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\,\top}= (\mathbf I\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\,\top}= \mathbf{W}^{0\,\top},
$$  

exactly the same “no‑change” condition that LoRA obtains by setting its low‑rank matrices **A**, **B** = 0. The difference lies only in how a deviation from this point is produced during training.



### 1. Why does “$\displaystyle \mathbf R= \mathbf I$” give a trivial start?

Both LoRA and BOFT deliberately begin at the pretrained checkpoint so that any improvement must be earned by learning a *delta* rather than by discarding the pre‑training knowledge.  

* **LoRA** adds a low‑rank perturbation  
  $\displaystyle \Delta\bW=\bA\,\bB^{\,!\!\top}$; with $\bA=\bB=\bfzero$ we have $\Delta\bW=\bfzero$.
* **BOFT** multiplies the frozen weights by an orthogonal matrix  
  $\displaystyle\bR=(\bfI+\bQ)(\bfI-\bQ)^{-1}$; when **Q** = 0 the Cayley map reduces exactly to $\bR=\bfI$.  

Because a product of identities is still the identity, the composed orthogonal matrix does not alter any column of $\bW^{0}$, i.e. $\displaystyle\bW' = \bW^{0}$. This “zero‑change” property follows directly from the algebraic definition—not from an approximation.



### 2. Do gradients through many factors make BOFT more aggressive than LoRA?

At first glance one might worry that back‑propagating through a chain  

$$
L\xrightarrow{\;\partial L/\partial\bz\,}
   \xrightarrow{\;\partial((\,\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)})\,\,\,!\,\!\,\!\,\!\!\!\,\!\,\betriack\rho)\;}
   \xrightarrow{\;\partial L/\partial\tilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)}}
$$  

could amplify changes because each factor multiplies the next.  
However two facts keep the update magnitude under control:

1️⃣ **All factors are identities at iteration 0**.  
   When every $\displaystyle\tilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)}=\bfI$, the Jacobian of the product collapses  

   $$
   \frac{\partial}{\partial\twidetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)}}%
        (\prod_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,j)})
   =%
        (\prod_{j<i}\widetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,j)})
        (\prod_{j>i}\widetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,j)})
        =\bfI .
   $$  

   Hence each component receives exactly the same gradient that would be obtained if we updated a single orthogonal matrix **R** directly. There is no multiplicative amplification at the very beginning.

2️⃣ **Linearisation of the Cayley map near the identity**.  
   For a tiny skew‑symmetric perturbation $\displaystyle\bDelta Q$ we have  

   $$
   (\bfI+\Delta Q)(\bfI-\Delta Q)^{-1}
     =(\bfI+\Delta Q)\bigl(\bfI+\Delta Q+O(\|\Delta Q\r|^{2})\bigr)
     =\bfI+2\,\Delta Q+O(\|\Delta Q\bigr|^{2}) .
   $$  

   To first order,  

   $$
   \Delta(\!R)\approx2\,\Delta Q .
   $$  

   Therefore a gradient step on **Q** translates into an *additive* rotation  
   $$
   R\simeq\bI+2\,Q_{\text{new}} .
   $$  
   This mirrors LoRA’s additive update where a step on **A**, **B** produces  
   $\displaystyle\bW'\approx\bW^{0}+A_{\text{new}}B_{\text{new}}^{\,!\!\top}$.  
   The essential point is that *the size of the change is proportional to the Euclidean norm of the underlying parameters*, just as in LoRA, and not multiplied repeatedly by downstream factors.



Consequently, early updates in BOFT are not inherently more aggressive than those in LoRA; both evolve from a zero perturbation with comparable first‑order dynamics provided learning rates are chosen sensibly.



### 3️⃣ Why does starting from the identity give comparable stability?

There are two complementary theoretical lenses that explain why identity initialisation yields stability akin to LoRA’s zero‑initialisation:

#### (a) Manifold–retraction view  

The set of orthogonal matrices $O(d)=\{R\:|\:R^{\,T}R=\bfI{}\}$ is a smooth Riemannian manifold. Optimising on this manifold is usually done by moving along its *tangent space* (the space of skew‑symmetric matrices) and then retracting back onto $O(d)$.  

The Cayley transform used in BOFT,

$$
R(Q)= (\bfI+Q)(\bfI-Q)^{-1},    Q=-Q^{T},
$$  

is precisely such a retraction: it maps a tangent vector $Q\simeq0$ onto a nearby point on $O(d)$. Near $Q=\!0,$  

$$
R(Q)=\bfI+2Q+O(\|Q\bigr|^{2}),    ~~ dR/dQ|_{0}=2\,I .
$$  

Thus taking an Euclidean gradient step on $Q$ corresponds—up to a constant factor—to taking a *geodesic* step on $O(d)$. Standard results from optimisation on manifolds guarantee that—as long as step sizes are moderate—the iterates stay within a neighbourhood where this linear approximation holds, preserving smoothness and avoiding sudden jumps. This property directly mirrors LoRA’s Euclidean updates where staying close to zero ensures small changes in weights.



#### (b) Spectral‑norm preservation  

Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix leaves all singular values unchanged:

$$
\sigma(R\,W^{0})=\sigma(W^{0}) .
$$  

Therefore every intermediate model during BOFT training inherits exactly the same spectral norm as the pretrained model. By contrast, LoRA’s additive perturbation can arbitrarily increase or decrease singular values unless explicit regularisation is applied.

Preserving the spectral norm has two immediate benefits for stability:

* **Gradient magnitudes stay bounded** – large singular values tend to amplify back‑propagated signals; keeping them fixed avoids exploding gradients.
* **Training dynamics remain close to those seen during pre‑training** – this alignment explains why OFT/BOFT often exhibit smoother loss curves compared with unconstrained finetuning.



Together these two perspectives justify why beginning at $R=\mathbb{I}$ provides a mathematically sound “no‑drift” start whose subsequent optimisation behaves like ordinary Euclidean optimisation on unconstrained parameters (**Q**, or **A**, **B** for LoRA). Empirically, Table X in the paper shows that BOFT attains comparable—or better—training stability than LoRA across vision‑transformer, language‑model and diffusion‑model tasks despite using far fewer trainable parameters.



### TL;DR

*Both methods initialise their *perturbation* at zero*: LoRA sets its low‑rank matrices to zero → additively nothing changes; BOFT sets every butterfly component’s skew‑symmetric generator $Q=0$ → via Cayley retraction each component equals $\displaystyle\bI$ → multiplicatively nothing changes.*

*At iteration 0 all factors equal $\displaystyle\bI$, so back‑propagation treats each factor independently and there is no hidden amplification.*

*Close to $\displaystyle\bI$, $R(Q)$ expands linearly ($R\simeq\bI+2Q$), i.e.\ a tiny Euclidean step on $Q$ creates an additively small rotation—exactly parallel to LoRA’s additively small $\Delta W$. The retraction guarantees that these steps stay inside the orthogonal manifold without inflating singular values.*

*Hence identity initialisation gives BOFT precisely the “zero‑change” guarantee required for safe finetuning, and—thanks to manifold geometry and spectral–norm preservation—offers theoretical stability comparable to LoRA’s zero initialisation.*
