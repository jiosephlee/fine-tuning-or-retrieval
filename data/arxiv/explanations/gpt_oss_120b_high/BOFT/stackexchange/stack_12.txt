### How can BOFT be interpreted as learning a bilinear similarity function?
Question:
The paper rewrites BOFT as learning $
 w^{0}_{i}\!^{\top}\! \n R \, x$, i.e., a bilinear form between pretrained neuron vector $
 w^{0}_{i}$ and input $
 x$. How does enforcing $
 R$ to be orthogonal relate to metric learning objectives such as learning Mahalanobis distances? Are there known benefits compared to unconstrained bilinear forms?
Answer:
**Answer**

In BOFT each pretrained linear layer $\displaystyle \mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ is left‑multiplied by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle \mathbf R\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$ :

$$
\displaystyle \mathbf z=(\mathbf RU)\! ^{\!\top }\;\!\\boldsymbol{x}.
$$

If we write the $i$‑th column of $\displaystyle \boldsymbol W^{0}$ as the “neuron vector’’ $\displaystyle \boldsymbol w_{i}^{\,0}\in\mathbb R^{d}$, then the $i$‑th output component becomes

$$
z_{i}= (\boldsymbol w_{i}^{\,0})^{!\:\!\!\top }\;\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{x}.
$$  
$(1)$

Equation $(1)$ shows that finetuning amounts to **learning a bilinear similarity** between the frozen neuron $\displaystyle \boldsymbol w_{i}^{\,0}$ and the input $\displaystyle \boldsymbol{x}$: the similarity is given by the bilinear form $\langle \boldsymbol w_{i}^{\,0},\,\boldsymbol{R}\,\boldsymbol{x}\rangle$. Because $\displaystyle \boldsymbol{R}$ is multiplied only on the side of the input, we can equally view it as rotating each neuron:

$$
z_{i}= (\boldsymbol{R}^{!\:\!\!\top }\;\boldsymbol w_{i}^{\,0})^{!\:\!\!\top }\;\boldsymbol{x}.
$$  
$(2)$

Thus BOFT searches for an **isometric change of basis** for the pretrained neurons that best aligns them with downstream data while keeping their *pairwise geometry* unchanged.
