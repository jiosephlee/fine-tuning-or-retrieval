\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does a block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix reduce trainable parameters, and what expressivity do we lose?
Question:
OFT introduces $
\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_1, \dots , \mathbf{R}_r)$ where each $
\mathbf{R}_i$ is $b \times b$ with $br=d$. I understand this reduces parameters from $O(d^2)$ to $O(bd)$, but I am confused about what kinds of transformations cannot be represented anymore when dimensions are split into independent blocks. Does this restriction affect the ability to capture cross‑block correlations?
Answer:
**Answer**

In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the weight matrix of a frozen pretrained layer $\,{\bf W}^{0} \,\in\, {\mathbb R}^{\,d\times n}$ is updated by left‑multiplying an orthogonal matrix $\,{\bf R} \,\in\, {\mathbb R}^{\,d\times d}$,

$$
{\bf z}=({\bf R}{ {\bf W}^{0}})^{{!\!\top}}{\bf x}.
$$

If ${\bf R}$ were a generic orthogonal matrix it would contain  
$\displaystyle{\frac{d(d-1)}{2}=O(d^2)}$ free parameters (the dimension of the Lie group ${\cal O}(d)$).  
The paper therefore proposes the **block‑diagonal** parametrisation

$$
{\bf R}= {\rm diag}({{\bf R}}_{1},\,dots ,{{{\bf R}}}_{r}), 
        \qquad {{ {\bf R}}}_{i} \,\in\, {\mathbb R}^{\,b\times b},
        \;\;\; br=d .
$$

Each block ${ {\bf  }R}_{i}$ is itself orthogonal, so it needs only  
$\displaystyle{\frac{b(b-1)}{2}}$ independent numbers (for instance via its skew‑symmetric Cayley parameterisation).  
The whole matrix therefore has

$$
r\,{\frac{b(b-1)}{2}}
     = { {d}/{b}}\,{\frac{b(b-1)}{2}}
     = {\frac {(b-1)}{2}}\, d
     = O(bd)
$$

trainable parameters – a linear reduction when $b\dlll d$.

### What transformations are lost?

A block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix can *only* rotate the sub‑space spanned by each coordinate block independently:

* If the input vector is split as  
  $$[\,{\bf x}^{(1)};\dots ;{\bf x}^{(r)}\,],$$  
  with ${ {\bf x}}^{(i)}\,{\in}{\mathbb {R}}^{\,b}$, then  
  $$ {\bf Rx}= [\,{{ {\bf     }R}}_{1}{ {\bf x}}_{ } ^ {(1)};
     	 	 	\dots ;
    	 	 {{ {\bf      }R}}_{r}{ {\bf     }x}^{(r)}]\,. $$  
* All off‑block entries are forced to zero, so there is **no path** for information originating in one block’s coordinates to reach any coordinate belonging to another block.

Consequently every orthogonal matrix that contains a non‑zero element outside these diagonal blocks—for example a rotation mixing component 1 with component 3, or any transform whose rows/columns are not confined to a single $b\times b$ sub‑matrix—is *unrepresentable*.  In group–theoretic terms the admissible set is  

$$
{\cal O}(b)\times\dots\times{\cal O}(b)\;(r~{\rm times}),
$$  

a strict subset of ${cal O}(d)$. Its dimension,
$r\,b(b-1)/2=O(bd)$,
is smaller than that of the full group,
$d(d-1)/2=O(d^2)$.

### Does this hurt cross‑block correlations?

Yes—by construction any linear coupling between different blocks must be realised **later** in the network (by subsequent layers or by nonlinearities) rather than by this single orthogonal transformation.  
If a downstream task critically depends on global interactions among features that originally live in separate blocks, a pure block‑diagonal OFT may be unable to adapt those interactions efficiently.  
The paper points out exactly this limitation:

> “the block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix reduces expressiveness and cannot approximate classic linear transforms’’  
> *(see § “Block‑diagonal structure”)*

Classic fast transforms such as the discrete Fourier transform or Hadamard transform have dense mixing patterns; none can be expressed by a block‑diagonal rotation unless one chooses $b=d$, which defeats the purpose of parameter saving.

### Why not simply make each block larger?

Increasing $b$ indeed enlarges expressive power because each block can mix more coordinates, but it also raises the number of trainable parameters linearly with $b$.  
At one extreme ($b=d$) we recover ordinary OFT with full expressivity but $O(d^2)$ parameters; at the opposite extreme ($b=2$) we obtain maximal compactness but very limited cross‑block interactions.  
The trade–off between *parameter efficiency* and *expressivity* is therefore controlled by $b$.

### How does BOFT address this loss?

The authors propose **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, which builds a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of several sparse orthogonal factors arranged in a butterfly topology.  
The butterfly graph guarantees dense connectivity (every input coordinate can influence every output coordinate) while keeping the total number of trainable entries at $O(d\,log d)$—substantially more expressive than the block‑diagonal case yet far fewer than $O(d^2)$.



In short, the block‑diagonal parametrisation reduces trainable parameters because it restricts each orthogonal factor to an independent low–dimensional rotation.  
The price paid is precisely the inability to mix information across different blocks—that is, loss of cross–block correlations and inability to represent many useful global linear maps.  
BOFT restores those correlations through a structured sequence of sparse rotations without reverting back to a fully dense parametrisation.
