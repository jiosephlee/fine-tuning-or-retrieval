\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Can BOFT be applied when layer dimension $d$ is not a power of two?
Question:
The definition of butterfly matrices assumes $k$ (and ultimately $d=2^{N}$) is a power of two for recursive splitting into halves.

If a linear layer has dimension $d=300$, which is common in transformer feed‑forward layers, how can we construct a valid butterfly factorisation?

* Do we pad to next power-of-two and then crop after multiplication?
* Are there alternative factorizations mentioned in literature that handle arbitrary sizes while retaining similar $O(d log d)$ parameter count?
* Does padding affect downstream performance or introduce bias?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – BOFT can be used for any hidden size. In practice you either **pad the layer to the next power‑of‑two** and drop the extra coordinates after the last butterfly multiplication, or you **use a generalized (mixed‑radix or block‑butterfly) factorisation** that works directly on non‑dyadic sizes. Both approaches keep the total number of trainable parameters at $\,\mathcal O(d\,\log d)\,$; padding adds only a modest constant‑factor overhead and does not create a systematic bias because the added dimensions are kept orthogonal (i.e.\ initially act as an identity mapping).

Below I explain why this works, how it follows from the constructions in the BOFT paper, what alternatives exist in the literature, and what practical effects you should watch out for.



---

### Why the original definition needs $\,d=2^{N}\,$

The Butterfly matrix $\mathbf B(d)$ is built recursively as  

$$
\mathbf B(d)=
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
      \begin{bmatrix}
      \mathbf B!\!\big(\tfrac d2\bigr)&0\\[4pt]
      0&\mathbf B!\!\big(\tfrac d2\bigr)
      \end{bmatrix},
      \qquad
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
      \operatorname{diag}\Bigl(
         \mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\,…
         ,\,\mathbf B^{F}_{\,d/k}(k)
      \Bigr),
$$

where each **butterfly factor** $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ (Eq.\ (1)) is defined only for  
$k=2^{\,t}$. Consequently the recursion terminates cleanly when $d$ itself is a power of two.
If $d$ were not dyadic we could not halve it repeatedly until we reach $k=2$.

The paper nevertheless points out two ways of getting around this restriction:

1️⃣ **Treat $k$ as any divisor of $d$** after we replace each scalar entry
in $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ by a small dense block $\mathbf C\rule[-3pt]{0pt}{13pt}\in{\mathbb R}^{b\times b}$
(*block–butterfly*). This yields
$\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)$ (see Section “Orthogonal Parameterisation
by Butterfly Factorisation”).  
If we choose a block size $b>1$ such that $b\,|\, d$, then we only need
$\log_{2}({d}/{b})$ stages instead of $\log_{2} d$. In other words,
the requirement becomes “${d}/{b}$ must be a power of two”, which can always
be satisfied by picking $b$ equal to any divisor that makes ${d}/{b}=2^{N}$.

2️⃣ **Pad up to the next dyadic length** and later crop back.
This is exactly what many FFT libraries do when faced with an input length
that is not a power of two.



### How padding works in BOFT

Suppose your feed–forward layer has width $\,d=300$.  
Let  

$$
D=\operatorname{nextpow}_{2}(300)=512,\qquad p=D-d=212 .
$$

Define an *extended* input  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf x}=%
   \begin{bmatrix}
       \mathbf x \\[4pt] \mathbf 0_{p}
   \end{bmatrix}
   \;\in {\mathbb R}^{D}.
$$

Construct a dyadic orthogonal Butterfly matrix  

$$
\vphantom{\Big|}
   {\bf R}_{D}
   =%
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
     {\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(D,i)}
   \;\in {\mathbb R}^{D\times D},
$$  

exactly as described in Eq.\;(3)–(4).
Because all factors are orthogonal,
the sub–matrix acting on the padded coordinates remains an orthogonal map.
If we initialise every factor as identity (the Cayley–transform’s skew–symmetric
parameter starts at zero), the extra rows/columns are *exactly*
the identity mapping at training start:

* No information flows from real coordinates into padded ones;
* No information flows back;
* Hence **the forward pass restricted to the first $d$ entries coincides with**
the desired mapping  

$$
{\bf y}=%
      ({\,{\bf R}_{D}}\;
         {\bf W}^{0}_D)^{T}
      {\tilde {\bf x}}
      ~~{\Longrightarrow}~~ 
      {\bf y}_{[1:d]}=
      ({\,{\bf R}}_{d\times d}
         {\bf W}^{0})^{T}{\bf x}.
$$

During back–propagation gradients reaching any element that touches only
padded indices are zero (because those indices are always zero).
Consequently, unless one deliberately *unfreezes* those entries,
the optimiser never learns a coupling between real and dummy dimensions,
so **padding does not introduce systematic bias**.
Empirically this behaviour matches what practitioners observe when padding FFTs:
the optimisation concentrates entirely on the subspace spanned by genuine data.



### Parameter count and overhead

A dyadic Butterfly of size $D=512$ needs  

$$
|\Theta|
   =%
   {\frac12(b-1)}\,D\,m
   ~=~O(D\,m),
   \qquad 
   m=\log_{2}D .
$$

Compared with an exact dyadic model sized at $\,d=256(<300)$,
the increase is bounded by  

$$
O(Dm)-O(dm_d)
< O((D-d)\,\log D)
< O(0.5\,D\,\log D),
$$  

i.e.\ **less than roughly 30 % extra parameters even for worst case**.
Because each butterfly component multiplies only $O(D)$ non‑zeros,
runtime grows similarly modestly – well below other costs such as attention layers.



### When you prefer *no* padding

If you want strict adherence to your true dimensionality,
the paper’s **block–butterfly** construction already gives you flexibility.
Pick any integer block size $b>1$ dividing $d$.  
Set  

$$
m=
   \Bigl⌈%
      {\log_{2}}\frac d b%
   ⌉ ,
   \qquad 
   D'=b\,2^{m},
   ~~ D'=%
        b\times (\textnormal{next dyadic multiple}) .
$$

Now build   

*depth-$m$ product* of matrices   

$$
{\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=
     {\rm diag}\Bigl(
          (\bfB^{F}_i)_{{}_b},
          ~…
     \Bigr),
$$ 

where each “${}_{{}_b}$’’ super–block contains its own Cayley–parameterised rotation.
The resulting transformation acts exactly on ${ℝ}^{\,d}$ because every level treats groups
of $b$ neurons together.
When $b$ does **not** perfectly divide $d$, one group can simply be smaller;
its corresponding butterflies just have fewer rows/columns.
All existing implementations of DAFT/BOFT already expose this “block size’’ argument;
setting `\verb|block_size|` appropriately removes any need for explicit zero–padding.



### Mixed‐radix / non‐dyadic butterflies

The classic Cooley–Tukey algorithm also supports **mixed radices**:
if $d$ admits a factorisation $d=n_{1}n_{2}\dots n_{r}$,
one can perform sequential splits of size $n_i$ rather than always $2$.
Each stage still implements an orthogonal sparse matrix whose non‐zeros scale linearly
with $d$, leading again to an $\mathcal O(d\,r)$ parameter budget.
This idea appears under names *generalised butterfly* or *radix-$n_i$
transforms* in works cited by BOFT such as Dao et al.’s *Pixelated* and *Kaleidoscope*
papers.
Practical libraries therefore provide functions ``butterfly(n,factors=[…])’’, making it straightforward 
to build an exact factorisation for  
$d=300=3\times5\times20$
or 
$d=300=12\times25$, etc.
Such mixed–radix constructions avoid any padding while preserving the same asymptotic cost.



### Does padding hurt downstream performance?

Empirically reported results in BOFT’s experiments use hidden sizes that are already powers of two 
(e.g.\ vision Transformer hidden dim 1024), so direct evidence about padding is limited.
Nevertheless theory and related FFT literature give us confidence:

* **Zero initialisation + orthogonality → no spurious coupling**
  between real and padded units;
* The optimiser receives no gradient signal for those couplings → they stay near identity;
* Any tiny deviation would manifest merely as additional degrees of freedom,
  which can only help expressivity without harming generalisation;
* In practice large models tolerate modest overparameterisation well,
  so any tiny increase caused by padding rarely shows up as measurable degradation.

If you observe instability you can further protect against it by **masking gradients**
on all rows/columns belonging to padded indices (“gradient freezing”), which costs nothing computationally.



### Practical recipe

Putting everything together, here is what most users do when applying BOFT
to a layer whose width ``dim`` is *not* dyadic:


this only needs three additional rows per stage compared with pure dyadic case –

## Take‑away points

| Situation                     | How to obtain an orthogonal Butterfly ✅                                          | Parameter growth                               |
|------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Exact dyadic width ($d=256$) | Standard recursion ($k=2$)                                                       | $\mathcal O(d\,\log_{} d)$                    |
| Non‑dyadic width ($d=300$)  | **A)** Pad → $D=512$, run standard BF, slice output<br>​**B)** Use *block–butterfly*: pick $b|300$, depth $\approx⟨⋯⟩$\;<br>​**C)** Mixed radix ($300=3·5·20$) – builds directly without padding | Same asymptotic order; constant factors differ<br>(A) ≤ ≈30 % extra vs.\ (B),(C) essentially none |
| Concern about bias           | Padding leaves extra dimensions decoupled at init → no bias.<br>Optionally freeze gradients on padded indices for safety.| negligible |

In short, **BOFT does not require your hidden dimension to be exactly a power of two**.  
Pad–and–crop is a simple robust choice used widely in FFT implementations;
if you wish to avoid even that modest overhead you can exploit *block–butterfly*
or mixed‑radix extensions already discussed in the BOFT paper and its cited works—all retain the attractive $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ parameter budget while delivering dense orthogonal transformations suitable for Transformer feed‑forward layers such as those with width 300.
