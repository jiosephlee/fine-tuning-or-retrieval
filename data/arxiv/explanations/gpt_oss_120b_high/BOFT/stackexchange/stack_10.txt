\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Could you elaborate on the weight interpolation property illustrated in Figure 2?
Question:
Figure 2 shows that BOFT provides an intrinsic interpolation between the identity matrix and the learned orthogonal matrix. Since $
 R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{
 B}_{i}$, how can we define a continuous path from $\,
 I\,	o \,
 R\, $? Is it simply scaling each skew‑symmetric generator or something else? Why is this property useful during finetuning?
Answer:
**Answer**  

In BOFT every learnable orthogonal matrix is obtained by a product of sparse *butterfly components*  

$$
R=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{B}_{i},
\qquad 
\tilde{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},
$$

and each component is itself built from a **Cayley‑parameterised** $k\times k$ block  

$$
\tilde{B}_{i}=({\bf I}+Q_{i})({{\bf I}}-Q_{i})^{-1},
\qquad 
Q_{i}^{\,!\top}=-Q_{i}.
$$

During training the skew‑symmetric matrices $Q_{i}$ start from zero  
($Q_{i}=0\,\Rightarrow\,\tilde{B}_{i}={\bf I}$).  
Because the Cayley map takes any $Q_{i}$ into an orthogonal matrix,
the whole product $R$ is guaranteed to stay on the *orthogonal manifold*
$O(d)$.  

---

### A continuous path $I\rightarrow R$

Let us denote by $Q_{i}^{*}$ the value that the optimiser finally learns
for component $i$.  
For any scalar $t\in[0,1]$ we can scale these generators and obtain  

$$
\tilde{B}_{i}(t)
   =({{\bf I}}+t\,Q_{i}^{*})({{\bf I}}-t\,Q_{i}^{*})^{-1}.
$$

When $t=0$ every factor equals the identity;
when $t=1$ we recover the trained factor.
Because the Cayley transform preserves orthogonality for *all*
$t∈[0,1]$, each $\tilde{B}_{i}(t)\in O(d)$.
Putting them together gives the **interpolation curve**

$$
R(t)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{B}_{i}(t)\in O(d), 
   \qquad   R(0)={\bf I},~R(1)=R .
$$

Equivalently one may view the same curve through the exponential map.
Each block of a butterfly component consists of many $2\times2$
rotations  

$$  
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos \theta & -\,\sin \theta\\
    \sin \theta &   \cos \theta
  \end{pmatrix},
$$  

so interpolating amounts simply to shrinking every angle,
$\theta(t)=t\,\theta^{*}$.
Thus “scaling each skew‑symmetric generator’’ – i.e. multiplying
$Q_{i}^{*}$ by a scalar – **is exactly** how the continuous
path is defined; naively scaling individual entries would destroy
orthogonality because an arbitrary linear combination of rotations
is no longer a rotation.

The figure in the paper therefore shows this *intrinsic* trajectory:
as training proceeds the learnt angles grow from zero toward their final
values, so $R(t)$ smoothly moves from the identity matrix toward
the fully trained dense orthogonal matrix.

### Why does this matter for finetuning?

1. **Preserving pretrained knowledge**  
   Starting at $t=0$ means that before any update all hidden activations are
   processed exactly as in the frozen model.  The optimisation therefore
   stays close to a region where pair‑wise neuron angles are unchanged,
   which is precisely what OFT was designed to protect.
   The gradual increase of $t$ lets the model explore new directions only
   after it has already learned a stable representation, thereby reducing
   catastrophic forgetting.

2. **Stable optimisation**  
   Along every point of the curve we have an exact orthogonal matrix,
   so spectral norms are preserved throughout training.
   Consequently gradient magnitudes do not explode or vanish,
   which explains why BOFT enjoys smoother loss curves than methods that
   change weights additively (e.g., LoRA).

3. **Implicit regularisation**  
   Because only a *single scalar* per generator controls how far we have moved
   away from identity, BOFT behaves similarly to a learning‑rate schedule
   but operates directly on the geometry of $O(d)$.  
   Early iterations therefore see a very low‑capacity model (essentially
   unmodified), which discourages over‑fitting when data are scarce.
   Later iterations gradually increase capacity as more rotation‑angle mass
   is injected.

4. **Compatibility with multiplicative dropout**  
   The same parametrisation makes it trivial to “turn off’’ some factors:
   setting their corresponding scalar $t=0$ (or replacing them by an explicit identity block)
   implements dropout without breaking orthogonality.
   This further strengthens regularisation while keeping inference latency
   unchanged after finetuning.


In short, the weight‑interpolation property follows directly from how BOFT
parameterises each butterfly component via a Cayley (or exponential)
map applied to a skew‑symmetric generator.  Scaling those generators furnishes
a mathematically clean geodesic $I\xrightarrow{}R$ inside the orthogonal group.
Because this geodesic is built into the optimisation process,
the finetuning starts from a perfectly safe point (the pretrained model)
and moves smoothly toward richer transformations,
yielding better stability, reduced forgetting and stronger generalisation—exactly what Figure 2 intends to illustrate.
