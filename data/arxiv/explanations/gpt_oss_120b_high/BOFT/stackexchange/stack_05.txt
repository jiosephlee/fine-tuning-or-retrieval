\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How can a butterfly factor \( \mathbf B^F(k)= [[diag(d_1), diag(d_2)], [diag(d_3), diag(d_4)] ]\) be made orthogonal?
Question:
Equation (1) defines a butterfly factor where each sub‑block is diagonal with vectors \(d_i\. The paper says we can enforce orthogonality by applying the Cayley transform or 2‑dimensional rotations to each "block".

* Does this mean we treat each 2×2 sub‑matrix formed by picking one entry from each diagonal as a separate rotation?
* If so, what constraints must the entries in vectors \(d_i} have?
* Alternatively, can we directly parametrize each diagonal vector such that the whole $k	imes k$ block becomes orthogonal without further decomposition?

A concrete example for $k=4$ would be very helpful.
Answer:
The butterfly factor introduced in Eq.( ( ) )

$$
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb R}^{k\times k},
\qquad 
\mathbf d_{\ell}\in{\mathbb R}^{\,k/b},
$$

is a $b\times b$ “Butterfly step’’ repeated $k/b$ times along the diagonal.
When $b=2$ (the case used in the paper) every repetition corresponds to one
$2\times 2$ sub‑matrix

$$
M_{i}= 
\begin{bmatrix}
   (\mathbf d_{\,1})_{i} & (\mathbf d_{\,2})_{i}\\[4pt]
   (\mathbf d_{\,3})_{i} & (\mathbf d_{\,4})_{i}
\end{bmatrix},
      \qquad   i=1,\dots ,k/2 .
$$

All other entries of $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ are zero, therefore  
the whole matrix is orthogonal **iff** every small matrix $M_{i}$ is
orthogonal. This answers your first question: we indeed treat the
$i$‑th pair of diagonal elements as an independent $2\times 2$
rotation (or reflection).

---

### Orthogonality constraints for one pair

For a real $M=[a~b;\;c~d]$ we require  

$$
M^{!\top}M=\mathbf I_{2}\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 
\begin{cases}
a^{2}+c^{2}=1 , \\[4pt]
b^{2}+d^{2}=1 , \\[4pt]
ab+cd=0 .
\end{cases}
\xrightarrow[\text{★}]{} 
$$

Equivalently there exists an angle $\theta\in {\mathbb R}$
(and possibly a sign flip giving determinant $-1$) such that  

$$
M(\theta)=
   \begin{bmatrix}\cos \theta & -s\,\sin \theta\\[4pt]
                   s\,\sin \theta &   \cos \theta
   \end{bmatrix},
   \qquad s=\pm 1 .
\xrightarrow[\text{†}]{} 
$$

Any choice satisfying (★) can be written in this form;
conversely every matrix given by (†) automatically fulfills (★).
Hence the **only** freedom we have per pair is a single scalar –
the rotation angle (or equivalently a single *Cay‑linear* variable).

---

### Two ways to enforce it

#### (1) Cayley transform  

Take a skew‑symmetric scalar $q=-q^{\top}= [0~-q;\; q~0]$.  
The Cayley map  

$$
M(q)=({\bf I}+q)\bigl({\bf I}-q\bigr)^{-1}
     =
     \frac{1}{\,1+q^{2}}\!
      \begin{bmatrix}
           1-q^{2} & -\, 2q\\[4pt]
           2q      &   1-q^{2}
      \end{bmatrix}
      =
      M(\theta), ~~{\rm with }\;\theta=
      2\,{\rm arctan}(q),
\xrightarrow[\text{‡}]{} 
$$

produces precisely an orthogonal $M(\theta)$.
If we store a learnable scalar $q_{i}\in {\mathbb R}$
for every pair $i=1,\dots,k/2,$
then building the four diagonal vectors as  

```text
    d₁[i] = (1-q_i^2)/(1+q_i^2)
    d₃[i] =    2*q_i/(1+q_i^2)
    d₂[i] = -  2*q_i/(1+q_i^2)
    d₄[i] = (1-q_i^2)/(1+q_i^2)
```

automatically satisfies all constraints.
Because the map (‡) never produces zero denominators,
the parameters remain unconstrained during training,
exactly what the paper refers to when it says “apply the Cayley transform”.

#### (2) Direct angular parametrisation  

Instead of going through $q_{i}$, one may store an angle
parameter directly, e.g.  

```text
    θ_i ∈ ℝ       (learnable)
    c_i = cos θ_i , s_i = sin θ_i .
```

Then set  

```text
    d₁[i]=c_i ,   d₃[i]= s_i ,
    d₂[i]=−s_i ,  d₄[i]= c_i .
```

This uses exactly one scalar per index as well,
but often it is more convenient when one wishes to initialise the
butterfly factor close to identity ($θ_i≈0$).  
Both formulations are mathematically equivalent—one returns the same set
of admissible matrices as the other—so there is no hidden extra degree of
freedom beyond what the paper describes.

---

### Why we cannot “just learn” arbitrary diagonals

If you tried to learn all four vectors independently,
they would have to satisfy the quadratic equalities (★).  
Imposing those equalities during optimisation would require either:

* projecting an arbitrary tuple onto the manifold defined by (★), **or**
* re‑parametrising them as above so that the constraints are already built‑in.

The latter option is exactly what the authors meant by “apply a Cayley transform or a sequence of $(\: …\:)$ blocks”.

Thus any “direct’’ parametrisation without further decomposition inevitably ends up being identical to one of these two schemes; there is no more compact way that bypasses them.

---

### Concrete example – $(k=4,\; b=\tfrac12?)$

Here $k=4⇒k/2=2.$  
Pick two angles, say  

* $\theta_{1}=30^\circ=\pi/6,\qquad 
   \theta_{2}=60^\circ=\pi/3.$

Compute their trigonometric values  

|  | cosine | sine |
|---|--------|------|
| $\theta_1$ | $c_1=\sqrt3/2≈0.86602540$ | $s_1=½≈0.5$ |
| $\theta_2$ | $c_2=½≈0.5$               | $s_2=\sqrt3/2≈0.86602540$ |

Using formulation (†),

```
d₁=[c₁,c₂]^T =[0.86602540 , 0.5 ]^T,
d₃=[s₁,s₂]^T =[0.5       , 0.86602540 ]^T,
d₂=[−s₁,−s₂]^T =[−0.5      , −0.86602540 ]^T,
d₄=[c₁,c₂]^T =[0.86602540 , 0.5 ]^T.
```

Plugging these into Eq.( ( )) gives

$$
{\bf B}^{F}(4)=
{\small 
	{\begin{pmatrix}
		0.86602540&     0& -\,0.5      &     0\\[3pt]
		0        &0.5&     0       &-\,0.86602540\\[3pt]
		0.5      &     0&   0.86602540&     0\\[3pt]
		0        &   0.86602540&     0&   0.5       
		\end{pmatrix}}
}.
$$

A quick multiplication verifies ${\bf B}^{F}(4){^\top}{\bf B}^{F}(4)= {\bf I}_{4}$,
so the factor is indeed orthogonal.
If instead we start from scalars $q_{1}=\tan(\theta _{1}/\,2)=tan(\pi/12)\approx .2679,\;
 q_{2}=\tan(\pi/6)\approx .57735,$
the same matrix results from Eq.~(‡).

### Summary answering your three points

* **Yes** – every entry‑wise “pair” creates an independent $­２\times２­$
rotation (or reflection). The overall butterfly factor is just a
block‑diagonal stack of those tiny rotations.
* The entries must obey the three quadratic relations (★),
which are equivalently enforced by assigning a single angle‑or‑Cayley 
parameter per pair.
* You *can* parametrize the diagonals directly,
but any such parametrisation inevitably reduces to specifying one angle
(or one Cayley variable) per pair; there is no lower‑dimensional closed‑form
that makes all four vectors free while guaranteeing orthogonality.
Hence the most convenient way—in line with the paper—is exactly what it calls:
apply either a Cayley transform or a standard $２\times２­$
rotation “block’’ on every pair of diagonals.

With this construction every butterfly component $\tilde{\bf B}(d,k)$ becomes an orthogonal sparse matrix; multiplying several such components yields dense,
parameter‑efficient orthogonal matrices exactly as BOFT requires.​
