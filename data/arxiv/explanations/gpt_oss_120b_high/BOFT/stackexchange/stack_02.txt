\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does Cayley parameterisation enforce orthogonality during training?
Question:
The paper uses $
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$ with $
\mathbf{Q}$ skew‑symmetric ($\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\top}$). I am unsure how this guarantees that $
\mathbf{R}$ stays orthogonal for any $
\mathbf{Q}$. Additionally, how is numerical stability handled when $(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})$ becomes close to singular during optimisation?
Answer:
**Answer**

The Cayley transform  

$$
\bR(\bQ)= (\bI+\bQ)\bigl(\bI-\bQ\bigr)^{-1},
\qquad 
\text{with }\bQ^{\top}= -\,\bQ ,
$$

is used in the paper as a *re‑parameterisation* of an orthogonal matrix.
Below we explain why this mapping always produces an orthogonal matrix,
what part of the orthogonal group it covers, and how numerical stability
is handled in practice.

---

### 1. Why does the Cayley transform give an orthogonal matrix?

$\bQ^{T}= -\,\bQ$.  
Define  

$$
\bR \;\triangleq\: (\bI+\bQ)(\,\bI-\bQ )^{-1}.
$$

Consider the transpose  

$$
\bR^{T}
   = [(\bI-\bQ)^{-1}]^{T}\!\bigl(\bI+\bQ\bigr)^{T}
   = (\bI-\bQ^{T})^{-1}\bigl(\bI+\bQ^{T}\bigr).
$$

Because $\bQ^{T}= -\,\bQ$,  

$$
\bI-\bQ^{T}= \bI+\bQ , 
    \qquad 
\bI+\bQ^{T}= \bI-\bQ .
$$

Hence  

$$
\bR^{T}= (\bI+\bQ)^{-1}(\bI-\bQ).

Thus $\boxed{\;\;\bm R^{T}\bm R=\bm I_{d}\;}$.   
The matrix produced by the Cayley map is therefore orthogonal **by construction**, no matter what values appear in~$\bm Q$.


*Intuition.*  Skew‑symmetric matrices form precisely the tangent space at the identity element of the Lie group $SO(d)$.  Exponentiating a tangent vector $e^{\,t\Sf S}$ ($S=-S^\top$) yields a rotation, but evaluating a matrix exponential is costly.  The rational map above is a first‑order retraction from the tangent space onto the manifold—it “projects’’ any infinitesimal rotation onto a genuine rotation while staying cheap to evaluate.



---

### 2. What part of the orthogonal group can be reached?

For **real** matrices the eigenvalues of a skew‑symmetric $\bm Q$
are purely imaginary ($i\,\lambda_{k},~\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}$).   
Consequently

* The singular values of $\bm I-\bm Q$ equal  
  $$ 
  s_{k}= \sqrt{\,1+\lambda_{k}^{2}}\;\ge 1 .
  $$

Hence $\bm I-\bm Q$ is never singular and its inverse exists for all finite~$\bm Q$.  

The map reaches exactly the **connected component** of the identity,
i.e.\ all matrices in $SO(d)$ with determinant $+\,1$.  
Matrices with determinant $-\,1$ cannot be obtained by a single Cayley step,
but this does not matter for finetuning because we initialise at  
$\bm R=\bm I$ (determinant +$1$) and stay inside that component throughout training.



---

### 3. Numerical stability in optimisation

In theory there is no danger of division by zero—$(\bm I-\bm Q)^{-1}$ always exists because its smallest singular value is at least $1$.  
Nevertheless two practical issues can arise:

| Issue | Why it matters | Typical mitigation |
|-------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Large entries in** $\bm Q$ | If elements grow very large, solving $(\bm I-\bm Q)x=b$ with finite‑precision arithmetic can become ill‑conditioned even though the exact condition number stays ≥ 1. | Initialise $\bm Q=0$ and use a modest learning rate so that updates keep entries small; many implementations clip ‑the Frobenius norm of~$\bm Q$ or bound individual entries (e.g. `torch.clamp`). |
| **Explicit matrix inversion** | Forming $(\<…>)^{-1}$ explicitly incurs round‑off errors especially for high dimension $d\simeq10^{3}\!-\!10^{4}$. | Libraries such as PyTorch/NumPy compute the product via solving a linear system `<code>torch.solve(I+Q, I‑Q)</code>` instead of forming an inverse. This uses an LU factorisation which is numerically stable and costs essentially the same as dense multiplication. |
| **Accumulated drift away from exact orthogonality** | After many steps tiny rounding errors can make $\bm R^\top\rule[-0pt]{0pt}{10pt}\bm R ≠ \bI$. | After each optimisation step one may optionally reapply a *retraction*: recompute $\bm R$ via the Cayley formula from the updated~$\bm Q$, or perform an inexpensive QR decomposition on each butterfly block and replace it by its unitary factor. In practice drift stays below $10^{-6}$, so additional reprojection is rarely needed. |

Because every butterfly factor itself consists of many independent $2\times2$
(or $b\times b$) blocks parameterised by their own skew‑symmetric matrices,
the safeguards operate at block level; solving each block’s linear system costs only $O(b^{3})$, where typically $b=2$.



---

### 4. Putting it together – what happens during training?

* Initialise all skew‑symmetric blocks as zeros → every block equals its own identity → whole product $\bm R=\bm I$.  
* Optimiser updates the independent parameters that populate each block’s skew‐symmetric part ($q_{ij}$ with $i<j$). Since these parameters appear linearly inside~$\bm Q$, gradients are straightforward.  
* At forward pass we compute  

```python
# Pseudocode (PyTorch)
R_block = torch.linalg.solve(I - Q_block, I + Q_block)   # stable solve, O(b³)
```

and then multiply all blocks according to the butterfly ordering.  
* Because each block stays orthogonal by construction, their product remains orthogonal ($R^\top R= I$) without any extra projection step.  
* Numerical safety relies on using ``solve`` rather than ``inverse``, modest learning rates (the paper starts from $\bm R=\bm I$), optional norm clipping, and occasional QR‐reorthogonalisation if drift becomes noticeable.



---

### 5. Summary

* The Cayley transform maps any real skew‑symmetric matrix onto an element of $SO(d)$; algebraically one verifies  
  $$ 
  {\boxed{\;\;\bm R^{T}\bm R=\bm I_{d}\;}} ,
  $$ 
  so orthogonality is guaranteed **by definition**, not by a penalty term.
* The denominator $\bm I-\bm Q$ is always invertible for real skew‑symmetric~$\bm Q$, its smallest singular value being ≥ 1, which makes the map well‑conditioned provided that the entries of~$\bm Q$ stay moderate.
* In practice we avoid forming an explicit inverse, solve linear systems instead,
keep learning rates low (the paper starts from $\bm R=\bm I$), optionally clip $\|\bm	Q||_{F}$,
and re‑orthogonalise via QR only if drift becomes noticeable.
* Consequently during finetuning every butterfly component—and therefore the whole matrix $\bm R$—remains exactly orthogonal while still being differentiable,
allowing efficient gradient‐based optimisation without sacrificing numerical stability.



This explains both **why** Cayley parameterisation enforces orthogonality and **how** stable training is achieved in the *Parameter‑Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization* framework.
