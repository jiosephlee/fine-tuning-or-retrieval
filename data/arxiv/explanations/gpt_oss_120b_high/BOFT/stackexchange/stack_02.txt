\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the Cayley transform guarantee that \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) is orthogonal?
Question:
OFT uses the Cayley parameterisation where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric (i.e., \(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top\)). The paper states that this ensures \(\mathbf{R}\) stays orthogonal during optimisation. I would like an explicit proof or intuition: starting from \(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^\top\), why does \((\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\) satisfy \(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}=\mathbf I\)? Moreover, are there any numerical stability concerns when \(\mathbf I-\mathbf Q\) becomes ill‑conditioned?
Answer:
In Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) the trainable orthogonal matrix $\displaystyle {\bf R}$ is written as  

$$
{\bf R}=({\bf I}+{\bf Q})({{\bf I}-{\bf Q}})^{-1},
\rule[0pt]{0pt}{12pt}\quad {\bf Q}^{\,T}= -{\bf Q},
$$

i.e.~${\bf Q}$ lives in the Lie algebra ${\<mathfrak>{so}}(d)$.
The claim that “the Cayley parametrisation guarantees ${\bf R}$ stays orthogonal’’ follows from a short algebraic argument that uses only the skew‑symmetry of ${\bf Q}$.

---

**Practical mitigations** used in OFT and BOFT are straightforward:

* **Initialize at identity** ($𝑄=0$). The first few optimisation steps keep
‖𝑄‖ small, so $M$ stays well‑conditioned.
* **Cap the norm** – many implementations add a penalty or simply clip
the Frobenius norm or spectral norm of $𝑄$ after each update.
* **Solve instead of invert** – modern libraries provide a stable
`\verb|solve(M,b)|'
routine (LU with partial pivoting); using it avoids forming an explicit inverse ${(𝕀−𝑄)}^{−¹}`
and reduces rounding error.
* **Alternative retractions** – if ‖𝑄‖ does become large,
one may switch temporarily to another manifold retraction such as QR‑based reorthogonalisation or even $\exp(Q)$,
both guaranteed to stay on $O(d)$ albeit at higher cost.
* **Block structure** – in OFT/BOFT only blocks of size $b\times b$
are parametrised jointly; restricting $b$
limits how large an individual skew block can become,
improving conditioning per block even when the whole
matrix has sizable dimension.


Because OFT’s block‑diagonal or butterfly components keep each block modest ($b=2$, $4$, …),
ill‑conditioning rarely appears in practice;
when it does,
the remedies above keep training stable without breaking orthogonality.

### Summary   

Starting from any real skew‑symmetric matrix $\displaystyle {{𝐑}}\,$,
the Cayley transform preserves orthogonality.
Numerical warnings arise only when $\Vert𝑄∥$
grows very large,
and standard mitigations mentioned above keep training stable.
