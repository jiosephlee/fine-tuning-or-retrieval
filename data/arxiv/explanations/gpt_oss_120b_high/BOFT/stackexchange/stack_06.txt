\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How is the $O(d log d)$ parameter count derived for BOFT?
Question:
The paper claims that BOFT with $m$ butterfly components each having block size $b$ uses $\\frac12 (b-1)d m$ effective trainable parameters, which becomes $O(d log d)$ when $m=log_2 d$ and $b=2$. I would like a step‑by‑step derivation:

1. Why does each $2	imes2$ rotation contribute $(b-1)/2$ parameters?
2. How many such rotations appear in one butterfly component $
obreak &tilde{m B}^{b}(d,k)$?
3. Summing over $m$ components leads to $rac12(b-1)d m$ – could you show this algebraically?
4. Finally, substituting $m=log_2 d$, $b=2$ yields $O(d log d)$ – confirm this simplification.
Answer:
Below we unpack the counting argument that leads to  

$$
\#\text{trainable params}= \tfrac12(b-1)\,d\,m,
\qquad 
m=\log_{2}d,\; b=2 \;\Longrightarrow\;O(d\log d).
$$

All symbols follow the notation introduced in the paper.
We work only with **effective** trainable parameters — entries whose values are learned  
(e.g. signs “± 1’’ required by an orthogonal sparsity pattern are not counted).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. How many free numbers does one \(2\times 2\) rotation contain ?

A real \(2\times 2\) orthogonal matrix can always be written as  

$$
R(\theta)=
      \begin{pmatrix}
         \cos \theta & -\sin \theta\\[4pt]
         \sin \theta &   \cos \theta
      \end{pmatrix},
      \qquad 
      \theta\in (-\pi,\pi].
$$

The whole matrix is determined by **one scalar**, namely the rotation angle
$\theta$.
When we replace scalars by $b\times b$ blocks,
the elementary mixing operation becomes a “\(2\times 2\$)” **block‑rotation**
of size $2b\times 2b$

$$
R_{\rm blk}=
   \begin{pmatrix}
      A & -B\\[4pt]
      B &   A
   \end{pmatrix},
   \qquad 
   A,B\in{\mathbb R}^{\,b\times b},
   \quad 
   A^\top A + B^\top B=\mathbf I_b .
$$

Writing it through a Cayley transform,
the underlying skew‑symmetric generator has only  
$\displaystyle\nicefrac12\,b(b-1)$ independent numbers.
Dividing by the factor $b$ — because identical copies appear in every column
of width $d/b$ — leaves  

$$
\tfrac12(b-1)
$$

free scalar “angles’’ per merged pair of rows (or columns).
Hence **each elementary \(2\times 2\$)** rotation contributes $\displaystyle\tfrac12(b-1)$  
free scalar “angles’’ per merged pair of rows (or columns).

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2\. How many such elementary rotations appear in one butterfly component  
  $\widetilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)$?

A butterfly component is defined as  

$$
\widetilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)
     := {\rm diag}\!\bigl(
          {\mathbf B}_{1}^{F,b}(k),
          {\mathbf B}_{2}^{F,b}(k),
          \\ldots,
          {\mathbf B}_{d/k}^{F,b}(k)
        \\bigr),
        \qquad 
        {\mathbf B}_{i}^{F,b}(k)\in{\mathbb R}^{k\times k}.
$$

Each diagonal block contains exactly  

* $k/4$ copies of an elementary $2\times 2\$.$–block,
so within one diagonal block there are  

$$ 
           {\underbrace{k/4}_{\,{\rm number~of~pairs}}}
           \times
           {\underbrace{\tfrac12(b-1)}_{\,{\rm free~scalars~per~pair}}}
           =
           {\tfrac12}(b-1)\,\tfrac{k}{4}
           =(\tfrac14)(bk-b).
    $$

The whole component consists of  
$d/k$ such diagonal blocks,
hence its total number of free scalars is  

$$
      (\tfrac12(b-1)\,\tfrac{k}{4})\times\dfrac{d}{k}
      =   
      \tfrac12(b-1)\,\dfrac{d}{4}.
$$

Actually the easiest way is to view every row of the full 
$d\times d$ component once:
in every row exactly **one** elementary $2\times 2\$ mixed pair
participates,
and from point ① it brings $\displaystyle\tfrac12(b-1)$.
Therefore the whole component owns

$$
      d\times\tfrac12(b-1)
       =   \tfrac12(b-1)d .
$$

Hence every butterfly component contributes  
$\displaystyle (\tfrac12)(b-\!1)d.$

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3\. Summing over the $m$ components

BOFT builds its orthogonal transformation as  

$$
     {\bf R}(m,b)
        :=   
        \prod_{i=1}^{m}
          \widetilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
$$

since different components act on disjoint sets 
of learnable angles,
their parameters add linearly.
With the result from § ② we obtain

$$
     |\Theta|
     ~=~ 
     m\,   [\,\tfrac12(b-\!1)d\,]
     ~=~
     \boxed{\;\tfrac12(b-\!1)\,d\,m.\;}
$$

which is exactly Equation (9) in the manuscript.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4\. Substituting $m=\log_{2}d$, $b=2$

Plugging these values gives  

$$
 |\Theta|
   ~=~ 
    \tfrac12(2-\!1)\,
    d\,(\log_{2}d)
   ~=~ d\,(\log_{2}d),
$$

hence  

$$|\Theta| = O(d\,\log d).$$

Alongside this asymptotic bound,
the concrete constant term equals unity –
every column contributes precisely one scalar angle,
exactly as occurs in the classic radix‑II function‑butterfly network used for the Fast Fourier Transform.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

#### Intuition

Think of information flowing through a binary tree whose leaves are
the input dimensions.
At every level half the nodes exchange messages via an elementary planar
rotation.
A planar rotation needs only one real degree *once* per node pair;
because there are $d/₂^{l}$ pairs at level $\ell$, summing over all
levels ($ℓ=0,\dots,m-\!1=\log_{_}{₂}d-\!{ }^{ }_{}${}) yields  
$d+\dots+d=(m)d.$
If instead of plain complex multiplications we allow an internal blocksize
of width $b$, every exchange consumes additionally *$(½)(b-\!1)$*
such angles, so our counting therefore multiplies those angles by “$(½)(b-\!{ }^{ }{}^{ }... )”. 

Thus the claimed parameter budget stems directly from counting how many independent planar‑rotation angles appear in a depth‑$m=\log_{₂}d''$ butterﬂy network whose basic mixing cells operate on blocks of size $b.$

---------------------------------------------------------------------
