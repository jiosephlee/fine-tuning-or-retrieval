### Can you sketch why Theorem 1 holds – i.e., products of butterfly matrices can approximate any orthogonal matrix?
Question:
Theorem 1 states that multiplying butterfly matrices in a specific order ($\\bm B_{d-1,1}(d)\,\\bm B^{\\top}_{d-1,2}(d)\, ...$ ) can approximate any $d	imes d$ orthogonal matrix. I know that butterfly structures can represent FFT‐like transforms efficiently, but I don't see how they span the full orthogonal group.

* Is there an intuitive argument based on universal approximation or Lie algebra generators?
* Does the result rely on stacking enough layers (i.e., $log_2 d$ levels) plus using both forward and transposed butterflies?
* Could you outline a constructive proof idea or reference that explains why such compositions are dense in $O(d)$?
Answer:
**Answer**

The statement that a product of butterfly matrices can approximate (in fact represent) every $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix rests on two classical facts:

1. **The orthogonal group is generated by planar rotations.**  
   Any $R\,{\in}\,O(d)$ can be written as a product of at most $\frac{d(d-1)}{2}$ *Givens* rotations,
   $$
     R=\prod_{k=1}^{K}G(i_k,j_k,\theta_k),\qquad 
     G(i,j,\theta)=I_d+\bigl(\cos \theta-1\bigr)(e_i e_i^{\,\!\top}+e_j e_j^{\,\!\top})
                  +\,\sin \theta\, (e_i e_j^{\,\!\top}-e_j e_i^{\,\!\top}),
   $$
   where each $G(i,j,\theta)$ acts non‑trivially only on the two coordinates $i$ and $j$.

2. **Butterfly layers realise exactly those planar rotations after appropriate permutations.**  
   A single butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2)$ is block‑diagonal with $d/2$ independent
   $2\times 2$ orthogonal blocks,
   $$
     \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2)=
       \operatorname{diag}\bigl(R^{(1)},\,R^{(2)},\,dots ,\,R^{(d/2)}\bigr),
   $$
   where each $R^{(\ell)}{\in}O(2)$ is a Givens rotation on a *fixed* pair of indices.
   Thus one layer can rotate simultaneously many disjoint coordinate pairs,
   but only those pairs prescribed by its underlying wiring pattern.

The crucial observation is that the wiring pattern itself is *not* fixed.
A higher‑level butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ $(k>2)$
is simply a permutation of the rows/columns of several copies of
the basic factor $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ (Eq.\,(1)).  
Because the whole set
$$
  \Bigl\{\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)\;\big|\;k=2,4,\dots ,d \Bigr\}
$$
contains bit‑reversal–type permutations, repeated multiplication
produces exactly the permutation group on $\{1,\dots ,d\}$.
In other words, after applying an appropriate sequence
of butterfly components we can bring **any** two coordinates
$i,j$ next to each other in one of the $2\times 2$ blocks
of a subsequent $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2)$.
