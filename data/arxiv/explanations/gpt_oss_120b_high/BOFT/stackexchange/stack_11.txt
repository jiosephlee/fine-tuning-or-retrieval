\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### After finetuning with BOFT, do we need extra computation at inference time?
Question:
The paper says "there is no additional inference latency" because "the BOFT‑learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model".

* Is this multiplication performed once after training to produce an updated weight matrix ${\\bm W}_{fin}= {\\bm R}\,{\\bm W}^{0}$ ?
* If so, does this require storing both ${\\bm R}$ (which may be factorised as multiple sparse factors) and ${\\bm W}^{0}$ during deployment?
* Are there any hidden costs if we keep ${\\bm R}$ factored rather than materialising it explicitly?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – after BOFT finetuning you can collapse the learned orthogonal transform into the frozen weight matrix once, obtain a single “new’’ weight matrix  

$$
\mathbf W_{\mathrm {fin}} = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},
\vspace{-2pt}
\\
\qquad 
\mathbf R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
$$  

and store only $\displaystyle \mathbf W_{\mathrm {fin}}$ for deployment.  
If you keep $\displaystyle \mathbf R$ in its factored (butterfly) form instead of materialising it, each forward pass has to apply $m$ sparse orthogonal blocks before the usual linear map – this does add a modest amount of computation and requires custom kernels. In practice most users merge the matrices offline so that inference costs are identical to a standard dense‑weight model and no extra memory is needed beyond the ordinary model parameters.  

Below I walk through the three points raised in the question in a little more detail and explain why merging is both safe and usually preferable.

---

### 1\. When is the multiplication performed?

During BOFT training the pretrained weights $\displaystyle \mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ remain frozen while a **layer‑wise** orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf R = 
      \tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m)}
      \,\tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m-1)}
      \cdots
      \tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}
$$  

is learned. In the forward pass of training the model evaluates  

$$
\mathbf z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{T}\!\,\!\,\!
            \quad =\
            (\mathbf W^{0})^{T}(\,\mathbf R^{T}\!\,\boldsymbol x\,),
$$  

so that both $\displaystyle \mathbf W^{0}$ **and** $\displaystyle \mathbf R$ are needed while gradients are computed.

After training finishes we can **pre‑multiply** once:

```python
for every linear layer ℓ:
    W_fin_ℓ ← R_ℓ · W⁰_ℓ
```

and replace $\displaystyle (\!\,\!W^{0}_ℓ)$ by $\displaystyle (\!\,\!W_{\mathrm {fin},ℓ})$. Because $\displaystyle R_\ell$ is orthogonal its product does **not** change any downstream dimensions – it simply rotates the row‑space of $\displaystyle W^{0}_�ell$.  

The paper’s claim “there is no additional inference latency’’ refers precisely to this offline step. Once merged,
the model contains **only** dense matrices $\bigl\{\!\!W_{\mathrm {fin},\,\ell}\!\bigr\}$; at run‑time it performs exactly the same dense matrix‑vector multiplications as a vanilla fine‑tuned model.

---

### 2\. What must be stored for deployment?

*If you **merge***:  
only $\bigl\{\!W_{\mathrm {fin},\,\ell}\!\bigr\}$ needs to be stored (together with any bias terms etc.). The original frozen weights $\displaystyle W^{0}_�ell$ and the learned factors $\bigl\{\tilde{\!\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i),\,\ell}\bigr\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$ are discarded unless you plan another round of adaptation later.

*If you **do *not* merge***:  
you would have to store both  

* the frozen base weights $\bigl\{\,W^{0}_�ell\bigr\rangle$, and  
* the factorised orthogonal transform $\bigl\{\tilde{\!\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i),\,\ell}\bigr\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$.  

That roughly doubles the parameter footprint for each layer (the factors contain only $\frac12(b-1)d\,m$ trainable scalars, which is much smaller than $d\times n$, but still non‑zero). Hence memory usage during inference is larger than necessary.

---

### 3\. Hidden costs of keeping $\,R$ factored

If you retain a factored representation at test time, every forward pass must execute

```python
y ← Rᵀ·x     # apply m butterfly components sequentially
z ← (W⁰)ᵀ·y
```

or equivalently

```python
y ← R·(W⁰·input)
```

The extra work consists of $m=\mathcal O(\log d)$ sparse‑matrix–vector products. For a typical transformer/vision‑model layer where $n≫d$,

* **Compute:** Each butterfly component touches every entry of its input vector exactly once (it consists of many $b\times b$ or $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks). Thus the total extra FLOPs are roughly  

  $$%
  \underbrace{\mathcal O(m\,d)}_{\text {apply }R}
  \
  {\ll}
  \
  \underbrace{\mathcal O(d\,n)}_{\text {the main linear projection}}
  ,%
  $$  

  which is usually much smaller than the dominant cost ($<5$\% for typical dimensions), but it *is* non‑zero and becomes noticeable on latency‑critical workloads or on hardware where very small kernels are less efficient than a single large GEMM call.
* **Implementation:** Efficiently executing many tiny block‑wise rotations demands either custom CUDA kernels or CPU‑vectorised loops. Out‑of‑the‑box deep‑learning libraries optimise dense GEMM heavily, whereas repeated small sparse ops tend not to achieve the same throughput.
* **Numerical differences:** Doing $m$ successive multiplications versus one monolithic product introduces rounding‑error accumulation (especially in FP16/BF16). In practice these differences are negligible compared with quantisation noise, but they exist nonetheless.

Consequently most practitioners follow what the authors suggest: **merge** $R$ into $W^{0}$ once after training so that inference reduces to a single dense matmul per layer – exactly what existing serving stacks expect – while still enjoying BOFT’s parameter‑efficiency during training.

---

### When might you keep $R$ factored?

* **Multi‑task or continual‑learning scenarios:** If several downstream adapters share the same frozen backbone, storing each task’s compact set of butterflies ($½(b-1)d m$, typically far cheaper than storing several full “merged’’ copies) may be far cheaper overall.
* **Extreme memory constraints:** For very large models where even an additional copy of *all* weights does not fit on device memory, keeping just the base model plus tiny factored adapters can enable on‑device switching between tasks at the price of a few extra FLOPs.
* **Research/diagnostic use:** When probing how different factorisations affect performance it can be convenient to leave them unfused.

In those cases you should expect a modest increase in latency proportional to $m=\log _2 d$, because each input activation passes through $m$ butterfly stages before hitting the usual linear map.



---

### Bottom line

After BOFT finetuning there is **nothing** inherent that forces an extra forward‑pass computation. You can (and usually should) multiply the learned orthogonal matrix into the frozen weights *once* after training, store only the resulting dense matrices, and run exactly as fast as any standard fine‑tuned model.  

Keeping $R$ in its factored form is possible but incurs an additional sequence of sparse operations at every inference step and modestly raises memory consumption – benefits arise only when you deliberately want a very lightweight adapter representation for multi‑task or continual learning settings.
