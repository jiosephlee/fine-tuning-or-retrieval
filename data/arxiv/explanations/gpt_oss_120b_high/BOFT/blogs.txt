# Orthogonal Finetuning 101: A Geometry‑Driven Way to Adapt Giant AI Models  

*An introductory guide that explains why preserving pairwise neuron angles matters, how Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) works, and why it offers stability advantages over popular methods like LoRA.*

---

## 1. The geometry behind finetuning

When we train a massive foundation model (think GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion) we end up with weight matrices that have already learned a useful internal geometry. In a linear layer each column of the weight matrix corresponds to a neuron’s “direction” in the high‑dimensional activation space.  

Two simple geometric quantities capture a lot of this knowledge:

1. **Pairwise angles between neurons** – the angle between any two columns tells us how similar or orthogonal their feature representations are.  
2. **Spectral norm** – the largest singular value controls how much a layer can amplify its inputs.

If a finetuning method dramatically distorts these angles or blows up the spectral norm, the model may quickly forget what it learned during pretraining, leading to instability or poor generalization. This observation motivates **geometry‑preserving finetuning**: keep the pretrained relational structure intact while still allowing enough flexibility to adapt to a downstream task.

---

## 2. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) in a nutshell

### 2.1 What OFT does

Instead of adding new parameters (as adapters do) or tweaking each weight entry independently (as full finetuning does), OFT inserts a *single* orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) **to the left** of a frozen pretrained weight matrix \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\):

\[
\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top} \mathbf{x},
\qquad
\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{d},\;\;
\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}.
\]

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\)), it performs a **rotation** (or reflection) of the neuron space:

* **Angles are preserved:** for any two columns \(\mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{w}_j\) of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\),

\[
\cos\!\bigl(\angle(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_i),\,(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{w}_j)\bigr)
=
\cos\!\bigl(\angle\mathbf{w}_i,\;\mathbf{w}_j\bigr).
\]

* **Spectral norm stays unchanged:** multiplying by an orthogonal matrix does not change singular values, so the largest singular value of \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) remains exactly the same after finetuning.

In practice we initialize \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\) (the identity), so the model starts from exactly the pretrained checkpoint and only learns a small rotation during training.

### 2.2 Enforcing orthogonality

Directly optimizing an orthogonal matrix is tricky because standard gradient descent does not respect the constraint \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}\). OFT adopts the **Cayley transform**, which rewrites any orthogonal matrix as

\[
\mathbf{R}= (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1},
\qquad
\mathbf Q = -\mathbf Q^{\top},
\]

where \(\mathbf Q\) is skew‑symmetric and can be freely updated with ordinary gradients. Starting from \(\mathbf Q = \mathbf 0\) yields \(\mathbf R = \mathbf I\).

---

## 3. Why OFT beats LoRA on stability

LoRA (Low‑Rank Adaptation) inserts an additive low‑rank update:

\[
\Delta\!\mathbf W = \underbrace{\mathbf A}_{d\times r}\underbrace{\mathbf B}_{r\times n}, 
\qquad
r \ll \min(d,n).
\]

Both methods are *parameter‑efficient*: LoRA needs only \(r(d+n)\) trainable entries; OFT needs roughly \(d^{2}\) for a full orthogonal matrix or fewer when we impose structure (see below). The key differences are:

| Aspect | LoRA | OFT |
|--------|------|-----|
| **Transformation type** | Additive low‑rank change | Multiplicative orthogonal rotation |
| **Angle preservation** | No guarantee – angles can shift arbitrarily | Exact preservation by construction |
| **Spectral norm** | Can increase if low‑rank update aligns with leading singular vectors | Remains exactly that of original weights |
| **Training stability** | Often needs extra tricks (e.g., dropout) | Naturally stable because optimization stays on a compact manifold (the orthogonal group) |

Empirically (see Qiu et al., 2023), OFT converges more reliably on text‑to‑image diffusion models and exhibits fewer spikes in loss curves compared to LoRA, especially when learning rates are modest.

---

## 4. Making OFT practical: sparse structures and butterflies

A naïve dense orthogonal matrix needs \(\mathcal O(d^{2})\) parameters—prohibitively large for layers where \(d=1024\) or more. The original OFT mitigates this by **block‑diagonal** factorization:

\[
\mathbf R = 
\operatorname{diag}(\,\underbrace{\mathbf R_{1}}_{b\times b},
                     \underbrace{\mathbf R_{2}}_{b\times b},
                     \dots,
                     \underbrace{\mathbf R_{r}}_{b\times b}),
                     \qquad d = br.
\]

Each block is itself orthogonal, so we only train \(r\,b(b-1)/2 = d(b-1)/2\) parameters. This works well but imposes an artificial sparsity pattern—different groups of dimensions never interact—potentially limiting expressivity.

### 4.1 Butterfly factorization (the next step)

Recent work introduces **Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)**, which replaces block diagonals with a *product* of several sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly pattern (the same communication pattern used in Fast Fourier Transform).  

Key properties:

* With only \(m = \log_{2} d\) butterfly components each having \(O(d)\) non‑zero entries, BOFT needs only \(O(d\,\log d)\) trainable parameters—far fewer than \(O(d^{2})\) yet still able to generate dense orthogonal matrices.
* The butterfly graph guarantees *dense connectivity*: every input dimension can influence every output dimension after passing through all components.
* When \(m=1\) and block size \(b=d\), BOFT collapses back to original OFT; when \(m=1, b<d\), BOFT recovers the block‑diagonal variant—so it *subsumes* earlier designs.

From a practical standpoint, BOFT preserves all geometric benefits of OFT while dramatically cutting parameter count and offering an inductive bias reminiscent of classical transforms (FFT, Hadamard). The extra computation overhead is modest because after finetuning the learned rotation can be folded into the pretrained weight matrix for inference at zero cost.

---

## 5. Putting it all together – when should you use OFT?

1. **You care about preserving pretraining knowledge** – if your downstream task is data‑scarce or you want to avoid catastrophic forgetting, an angle‑preserving rotation is ideal.
2. **Stability matters** – projects that struggle with exploding gradients or noisy loss curves can benefit from OFT’s inherent spectral norm preservation.
3. **Parameter budget is tight but you need expressivity** – start with block‑diagonal OFT for simplicity; switch to BOFT if you need richer transformations without blowing up parameters.
4. **Inference latency must stay unchanged** – unlike adapter or prompt tuning methods that add extra computations at inference time, both OFT and BOFT produce an equivalent set of weights that can be baked into the model before deployment.

---

## 6. TL;DR Takeaways

* **Geometry is crucial:** preserving pairwise neuron angles keeps the pretrained relational structure alive during finetuning.
* **OFT implements this geometry via an orthogonal left‑multiplication**, guaranteeing angle and spectral norm preservation.
* **Compared to LoRA**, OFT offers deterministic stability because it optimizes on a compact manifold rather than adding arbitrary low‑rank perturbations.
* **Parameter efficiency tricks:** block diagonal structures reduce parameters but restrict interaction; butterfly factorization (BOFT) achieves dense connectivity with only \(O(d\,log d)\) parameters while retaining all geometric benefits.
* **Practical tip:** initialize the orthogonal matrix as identity via Cayley transform; optionally apply multiplicative dropout on butterfly components for extra regularization.

Orthogonal Finetuning thus provides a clean, geometry‑driven alternative for adapting today’s gigantic AI models—offering both theoretical elegance and empirical robustness. Give it a try on your next vision or language adaptation task and see how well maintaining “angles” can keep your model’s knowledge intact!



# When Flexibility Meets Cost: The Parameter Bloat Problem in OFT  

*Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) promises geometry‑preserving adaptation of huge foundation models, but its original formulation carries a hidden price tag – millions of extra trainable weights. In this post we unpack why the full‑dimensional orthogonal matrix blows up the parameter budget and why the naïve block‑diagonal fix trades off expressiveness for savings.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why Orthogonal Finetuning Looks So Attractive  

Modern vision transformers, language models, or diffusion backbones share a common pattern: each linear layer can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is **frozen** after pre‑training and \(\mathbf{x}\) is the layer input.  
OFT proposes to modify the forward pass by inserting a learnable **orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) on the left:

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_{d}\), *pairwise angles between neuron weight vectors are preserved*. In other words, the geometric knowledge baked in \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) stays intact while the model still enjoys a flexible linear transformation.

Mathematically this is neat, but it comes with a concrete engineering cost: **how many parameters does \(\mathbf{R}\) contain?**  

---

## 2️⃣ The Full‑Dimensional Orthogonal Matrix = \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) Parameters  

An arbitrary orthogonal matrix has \(d^{2}\) entries, but its degrees of freedom are only \(d(d-1)/2\). In practice we store it implicitly (e.g., via a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) in the Cayley transform \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\)), still requiring **\(d^{2}\)** scalars to be learned and updated during finetuning.

Consider a typical transformer feed‑forward layer with hidden size \(d=4096\). A full orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) would need roughly

\[
4096^{2} \approx 1.68\times10^{7}
\]

trainable numbers – *17 million* extra parameters for just one layer!  
When you stack dozens of such layers (as in GPT‑style language models or large vision transformers), the total **parameter bloat** easily reaches hundreds of millions, dwarfing classic PEFT methods like LoRA that add only a few thousand per layer.

Such a blow‑up hurts:

* **Memory** – each gradient step must store the full dense \(\mathbf{R}\), increasing GPU RAM usage.
* **Compute** – multiplying a dense \(d\times d\) matrix per forward pass adds an \(O(d^{2})\) overhead.
* **Optimization stability** – more degrees of freedom can make training noisier unless carefully regularized.

---

## 3️⃣ Block‑Diagonal — A Shortcut That Cuts Parameters

To tame the memory explosion, the original OFT paper introduced a **block‑diagonal** structure:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname*{diag}(\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{1}}_{b\times b},
                                 \underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{2}}_{b\times b},
                                 \dots,
                                 \underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{r}}_{b\times b}), 
\qquad br = d .
\]

Each block \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) remains orthogonal, but now only \(b\,d\) trainable scalars are needed (actually \((b-1)\frac{b}{2}r = \frac12(b-1)d\)). For the same \(d=4096\), picking \(b=64\) reduces parameters from ~17 M down to roughly

\[
64\times 4096 = 262\,144,
\]

a **~65× reduction**!

The savings are compelling—this “sparsity pattern” lets OFT compete with LoRA on memory while still employing an orthogonal transform.

---

## 4️⃣ What We Lose When We Go Block‑Diagonal  

The elegance of the full orthogonal group lies in its *unconstrained mixing* of all input dimensions. The block‑diagonal approximation imposes two major restrictions:

### 4.1 No Cross‑Block Interaction  

Each block only sees its own slice of the activation vector \(\mathbf{x}\). If we split a 4096‑dimensional vector into 64 blocks of size 64, dimension 5 in block 1 never talks to dimension 200 in block 3 during the orthogonal transformation. Consequently:

* Information that could have been re‑distributed across the whole feature space stays trapped within its block.
* Certain useful linear transforms—e.g., discrete Fourier transform (DFT), Hadamard transform, or any operation requiring global mixing—cannot be realized with a purely block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix.

### 4.2 Artificial Grouping By Index  

The division into blocks is driven purely by index order (`[1:b], [b+1:2b], …`). There’s no guarantee that neurons sharing an index segment have any semantic relationship after pretraining. By enforcing independent rotations per block we impose an *inductive bias* that may clash with the learned representation geometry.

### 4.3 Reduced Expressivity → Potentially Weaker Adaptation  

From a hypothesis‑space viewpoint, full OFT can explore any point on the orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\). A block‑diagonal restriction restricts us to the subgroup

\[
\mathcal{O}(b)\times \mathcal{O}(b)\times\dots\times \mathcal{-O}(b),
\]

a dramatically smaller set when \(b \ll d\). Empirically this translates into:

* Fewer degrees of freedom to align pretrained features with downstream task specifics.
* Higher risk that some tasks simply cannot be represented well within this limited subspace.

The original paper notes precisely this trade‑off: *“the block-diagonal sparsity pattern may introduce undesirable inductive biases … and cannot approximate classic linear transforms.”*

---

## 5️⃣ A Quick Numerical Illustration  

| Layer size \(d\) | Full OFT params (\(=\mathcal O(d^{2})\)) | Block size \(b\) | Block‑diag params (\(\approx b d/2\)) | Parameter reduction |
|------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------|--------------------------------------|---------------------|
| 1024             | ~1 M                                   | 32                | ~16 K                                | ~62×                |
| 2048             | ~4 M                                   | 64                | ~65 K                                | ~62×                |
| 4096             | ~17 M                                  | 128               | ~262 K                               | ~65×                |

Even when we choose relatively large blocks (e.g., \(b=128\)), we still shave off more than **two orders of magnitude**. The price is exactly what we described above: each row/column can only interact inside its own block.

---

## 6️⃣ Why This Matters – Setting Up for Better Solutions  

Understanding where OFT’s parameter bloat originates helps us see why later works propose *structured factorizations* (butterfly matrices, low‑rank updates, etc.) instead of simple sparsity patterns:

* **Goal:** retain dense connectivity (every source can reach every destination) while keeping trainable parameters near \(O(d\,\log d)\).
* **Approach:** factorize a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal factors—each factor cheap to store but together capable of global mixing.
* **Result:** recover much of full OFT’s flexibility without paying the quadratic cost; at the same time avoid block‑diagonal’s expressivity ceiling.

These ideas culminate in BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly), which builds exactly such factorizations using butterfly graphs from fast Fourier transforms. But before diving into BOFT we needed to clearly diagnose *the problem*: full‑dimensional orthogonal matrices are too heavy and naïve sparsity kills too much expressivity. That diagnosis is what we’ve walked through here.

---

## 7️⃣ Takeaways  

1. **Full OFT’s strength—geometric preservation—comes from using an unrestricted \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix**, whose storage scales quadratically with hidden dimension.
2. **A dense orthogonal matrix quickly becomes impractical** for modern backbones where \(d=1024–4096\): millions of extra learnable parameters strain memory and compute budgets.
3. **Block‑diagonal OFT chops parameters down** dramatically by restricting transformations to independent subspaces defined by index-based blocks.
4. **Such restriction sacrifices global mixing**, hindering approximation of classic linear transforms and potentially limiting downstream performance because neurons can no longer communicate across blocks.
5. The tension between *flexibility* (full connectivity) and *cost* (parameter budget) fuels research into smarter factorizations—an avenue explored by BOFT and related methods.

Understanding this trade‑off equips practitioners with a clear mental model when they decide whether plain OFT fits their hardware constraints or whether they should reach for more sophisticated, butterfly‐based schemes that promise “the best of both worlds.”



# Butterfly Factorization Unleashed – From FFT to Parameter‑Efficient AI  

*Learn how the butterfly graph used in fast Fourier transforms inspires a sparse‑yet‑dense connectivity pattern that reduces the number of learnable parameters from \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) to \(\mathcal{O}(d\log d)\).*

---

## 1️⃣ Why Finetune at All?

Foundation models such as GPT‑3, Stable Diffusion, or the latest vision transformers have billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is often out of reach for most research labs, so *parameter‑efficient finetuning* (PEFT) has become the de‑facto strategy to adapt these models to downstream tasks without blowing up compute budgets.

There are three main PEFT families:

| Approach | How it works | Typical trade‑off |
|---|---|---|
| **Model finetuning** | Update a subset of the original weights | No extra inference latency |
| **Adapters** | Insert small trainable modules between layers | Adds a tiny overhead at inference |
| **Prompt tuning** | Prefix extra tokens to the input | Very cheap but sometimes less expressive |

Among them, plain model finetuning remains attractive because it leaves the original architecture untouched and incurs zero extra cost during inference.

---

## 2️⃣ Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): A Principled Baseline  

The classic way to keep a pretrained model “close” to its original representation is to limit how much each weight matrix can change. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) does exactly that: it learns an **orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that left‑multiplies every frozen layer weight \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\),

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

so that pairwise angles between neurons stay unchanged.  

- **Why orthogonal?** An orthogonal transformation preserves inner products and singular values, thus retaining most of the knowledge encoded during pretraining.  
- **Implementation trick:** OFT uses the Cayley transform  
  \[
  \mathbf{R}= (\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1},
  \]  
  where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is skew‑symmetric (\(\mathbf{Q}=-\mathbf{Q}^{\!\top}\)). This guarantees exact orthogonality throughout training.  

Unfortunately, a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix carries \(\Theta(d^{2})\) free parameters – far too many for PEFT. The original OFT mitigates this by **forcing a block‑diagonal structure**, i.e.,  
\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{R}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{r}), 
\quad \mathbf{R}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}, 
\quad br=d.
\]  
While this cuts down parameters dramatically (\(\Theta(bd)\)), it also imposes an artificial sparsity pattern: each neuron is split into independent groups that never exchange information. The resulting hypothesis space can be overly restrictive for many downstream tasks.

The open question was: *Can we build a *dense* orthogonal matrix—so every neuron can talk to every other—while still staying parameter‑efficient?*  

---

## 3️⃣ An Information‑Transmission Lens  

Think of a dense matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) as a fully connected bipartite graph between an input set of \(d\) nodes and an output set of \(d\) nodes. If we factorize \(\mathbf{R}\) into a product of sparse matrices,

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m}}_{\text{level }m}
            \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m-1}}_{\text{level }m-1}
            \cdots
            \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{1}}_{\text{level }1},
\]

the multiplication can be visualized as **information flowing across a grid with \(m+1\) layers** (see Figure 1 in the paper). Each sparse factor supplies edges only between two adjacent layers. The whole network becomes dense only if *every* source node can reach *every* sink node through some path across those layers.

Two desiderata emerge:

1. **Dense connectivity** – each source must have at least one path to each sink.
2. **Minimal free edges** – keep total trainable edges as low as possible while respecting orthogonality (each factor must be full rank, thus at least \(d\) edges per layer).

A naïve design would use one dense factor (\(\mathcal O(d^{2})\) edges). The block‑diagonal design satisfies (2) but fails (1). What topology satisfies both? Enter the **butterfly graph** from the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

---

## 4️⃣ Butterfly Graphs: From FFT to AI  

The FFT repeatedly splits a signal into even/odd parts, mixes them, and recombines—exactly what a *butterfly* does:

```
   x0 ----\
           >---- y0
   x1 ----/
```

When applied recursively across log₂ \(d\) stages, every input influences every output using only \(\mathcal O(d\,\log d)\) connections. In matrix terms, each stage corresponds to a **butterfly factor**:

\[
\mathbf{B}^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
    \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix}
    \in \mathbb R^{k\times k},
    \qquad k=2^{s}.
\]

Stacking these factors yields a **butterfly matrix** 
\[
\mathbf B(d)=
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
   \tilde{\mathbf B}\!\bigl(d,\tfrac d2\bigr)\,
   \dots\, 
   \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,2),
\]
where each component $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ is block‑diagonal with blocks $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ placed along the diagonal.

Key observations:

- Each component has only **\(k/2\times k/2\times 4 = 2k$ non‑zeros**, i.e., linear in \(k\).
- The total number of trainable entries after \(m = \log_{2} d\) stages is roughly \(d\,m = d\,\log_{2} d.\)
- By enforcing *orthogonality* on every factor (e.g., using Cayley transforms on its $2\times2$ sub‑blocks), their product stays orthogonal automatically.

Thus we get an **orthogonal butterfly matrix** that is dense overall but built from very few free parameters—a perfect match for our PEFT goals.

---

## 5️⃣ Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)

Putting everything together yields **BOFT**, a new PEFT scheme that replaces OFT’s block‑diagonal $\mathbf R$ with an orthogonal butterfly product:

### Forward pass
\[
    \boxed{\;
    \mathbf z = 
        (\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}}_{\displaystyle
        \textstyle \textbf R(m,b)}
        ~\,\,\,\,\,\,~ 
        {\bf W}^{0})^{\!\top}
        {\bf x}
    }.
\]

- $\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ are *block butterfly components*: each is an orthogonal version of $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ where the scalar diagonals become $b\times b$ blocks.
- The hyperparameters:
  - \(m = \log_{2}(d/b)\): number of butterfly stages.
  - \(b ≥ 2\): block size controlling how many dimensions are bundled together.
- Parameter budget:
  - Each stage contributes roughly $(b-1)d/2$ trainable scalars.
  - Overall cost:  
    \[
      |\theta_{\text{BOFT}}| = 
      \frac12(b-1)\, d\, m
      = 
      \mathcal O(d\,b\,\log d).
    \]
    Setting $b=2$ gives the pure $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ scaling—*orders of magnitude smaller* than OFT’s $\mathcal O(d^{2})$.

### Edge cases  
| Setting | Reduces to |
|---|---|
| $m=1$, arbitrary $b$ | Block‑diagonal OFT |
| $b=d$, $m=1$ | Full‐matrix OFT |
| $b=2$, $m=\log_2 d$ | Dense orthogonal BOFT |

### Training tricks

- **Identity initialization:** All $\tilde{\mathbf B}$ start as identities (Cayley’s skew part set to zero), guaranteeing zero deviation from pretrained weights at epoch 0.
- **Multiplicative Dropout:** Randomly replace selected butterfly components—or their internal diagonal blocks—with identity matrices during training to curb overfitting while preserving the multiplicative nature of OFT.

---

## 6️⃣ Expressivity vs. Regularity  

A natural worry is whether restricting ourselves to butterfly‐structured orthogonal matrices hurts expressiveness. Two pieces of evidence settle this:

1. **Simulation study:** Approximating a random dense orthogonal matrix ($512\times512$) shows that BOFT with modest block size ($b=2$, $m=9$) reaches far lower approximation error per trainable parameter than block‑diagonal OFT even with much larger blocks ($b=16$). Smaller blocks + more stages consistently beat larger blocks alone.
   
2. **Theorem (Expressivity of BOFT)** *(informally)* – By composing alternating products of forward and transposed butterfly matrices,
   \[
     \prod_{i=1}^{l}
       {\bf R}_{i,1}(m_1,b_1)
       {\bf R}_{i,2}^{^\top}(m_2,b_2),
   \]
   one can represent *any* orthogonal matrix when \(l = d-1,\; m_1=m_2=\log_2 d,\; b_1=b_2=2.\)

Thus BOFT sits somewhere between two extremes:
- Full finetuning → universal function class but prone to overfitting.
- Block‑diagonal OFT → heavily regularized but possibly underexpressive.
  
BOFT’s structured inductive bias—rooted in classic linear transforms like FFT and Hadamard—offers just enough flexibility while keeping training stable and generalizable.

---

## 7️⃣ Spectral Guarantees  

Because BOFT multiplies an *orthogonal* matrix on the left, it leaves every singular value of $\mathbf W^{0}$ untouched:

```math
W_{\text {finetuned}} = R W^0 = R U Σ V^\top,
⇒ σ_{\max}(W_{\text {finetuned}})=σ_{\max}(W^0).
```

Preserving spectral norm has been linked to smoother gradients and better generalization in deep networks, providing another theoretical edge over low‑rank methods like LoRA.

---

## 8️⃣ Empirical Highlights  

The authors tested BOFT across three domains:

| Domain | Model family | Tasks | BOFT vs. baselines |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vision | Large Vision Transformers | Image classification / detection | Consistently higher accuracy at < 5 % extra parameters |
| Language | LLaMA / GPT‑style LLMs | Zero‑shot QA, sentiment analysis | Outperformed LoRA (+~0.5 % avg.) with far fewer trainable weights |
| Diffusion | Stable Diffusion (text→image) | Prompt steering & style control | More stable training curves and better alignment than OFT |

Across all settings BOFT matched or surpassed state-of-the-art PEFT methods while keeping the parameter budget within $\mathcal O(d\,log d)$ — confirming that butterfly factorization scales gracefully even for multi‑billion‑parameter backbones.

---

## 9️⃣ Takeaways & Future Directions  

- **Butterfly factorization turns a dense orthogonal operator into a trainable recipe composed of cheap sparse pieces**, mirroring exactly how FFT achieves its speedup.
- By viewing matrix factorization as an *information transmission problem* on a grid graph, we gain a clean design principle: dense connectivity can be achieved with logarithmic overhead.
- BOFT demonstrates that *structured sparsity* can be more effective than generic low-rank reparameterizations for finetuning large models.
- Open questions remain:
  - Are there even more efficient topologies (e.g., expander graphs or hypercube routing schemes) that improve upon butterflies?
  - Can runtime overhead be reduced further by fusing all butterfly factors into a single linear operator after training?
  - How does BOFT interact with other PEFT tricks such as quantization or mixed precision?

If you’re curious about plugging butterfly factorization into your own model zoo—or just want to see how classic signal processing ideas keep resurfacing in AI—take away this recipe:

> **For any layer size \(d:\)**  
> • Build \(m=\log_{2} d/b\) block–butterfly components with block size \(b.\)  
> • Enforce orthogonality per component via Cayley transforms (or simple Givens rotations).  
> • Initialize all components as identity and optionally apply multiplicative dropout during early epochs.  

You’ll end up with a dense orthogonal transformation that costs only $\mathcal O(d\,log d)$ trainable numbers—a sweet spot where *fast algorithms meet efficient finetuning*. Happy coding!



# BOFT in Action: How Orthogonal Butterfly Slashes Parameters While Keeping Performance  

*An accessible walkthrough of the Orthogonal Butterfly finetuning paradigm – its sparse‑matrix product formulation, identity initialization, multiplicative dropout – and why it preserves all the virtues of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with far fewer trainable weights.*

---

## Why Efficient Finetuning Matters  

Foundation models such as GPT‑3 or Stable Diffusion contain billions of parameters. Training them from scratch is becoming prohibitively expensive, so most practitioners adapt a **pretrained** checkpoint to a downstream task instead of relearning everything.  

Parameter‑efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods try to keep the number of *learned* parameters tiny while leaving inference speed untouched. Popular families include adapters, prompt tuning, LoRA‑style low‑rank updates, and **model finetuning** where the original architecture stays intact and only a subset of its weights changes.  

Model finetuning has a clean advantage: no extra modules are added at inference time. The challenge is how to **constrain** those changes so that the expensive knowledge baked into the pretrained weights isn’t erased.


## A Quick Recap of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

OFT attacks this challenge by restricting finetuning updates to an **orthogonal transformation** applied *on the left* of each weight matrix:

\[
\boldsymbol z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^\top \boldsymbol x ,
\qquad 
\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R = \mathbf I .
\]

Here $\mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ is frozen, $\boldsymbol x\in\mathbb R^{d}$ is the input activation and $\boldsymbol z\in\mathbb R^{n}$ is the output activation. Because $\mathbf R$ is orthogonal:

* **Pairwise angles between neurons are preserved** – relational knowledge stays intact.
* **Spectral norm stays unchanged** – singular values of $\mathbf W^{0}$ are untouched → stable training.
* When $\mathbf R=\mathbf I$, finetuning starts exactly at the pretrained model (“zero‑init”).

The naïve implementation would store a full dense orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$, costing $\mathcal O(d^{2})$ trainable scalars—clearly impractical for large layers ($d=1024$ already implies > 500 K parameters). The original OFT alleviates this by imposing a **block‑diagonal** sparsity pattern on $\mathbf R$. While this reduces storage dramatically ($\mathcal O(b d)$ when each block has size $b$), it also forces independent transformations inside each block and sacrifices expressive power.


## From Information Transmission to Butterfly Factorization  

The authors reconceptualize constructing a dense orthogonal matrix as an **information‑transmission problem** on a grid graph:
* Each column represents one “source” neuron.
* Each row represents one “receiver” neuron.
* Multiplying by a sparse matrix corresponds to letting information flow only along existing edges between adjacent levels of the grid.
* A sequence of sparse factors yields dense connectivity if every source can eventually reach every receiver through some path.

In networking theory this exact problem appears in the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Its **butterfly network** connects all inputs after only $\log_{2}\!d$ stages while using merely $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ edges. Borrowing this topology gives us an elegant way to build a dense orthogonal matrix out of *many* cheap sparse ones.


### The Butterfly Building Block  

A single **butterfly factor** of size $k$ ($k$ is a power of 2) looks like

\[
\boxed{\;
 \boldsymbol B^{F}(k)=
   \begin{pmatrix}
      \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{2})\\[4pt]
      \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol d_{4})
   </pmatrix}
 }\!,
\qquad 
\boldsymbol d_i \in \mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

Each block is diagonal, so only $k$ entries are non‑zero—much cheaper than a full $k\times k$ matrix.  

A **butterfly component** $\tilde{\boldsymbol B}(d,k)$ repeats this factor along the diagonal :

\[
\tilde{\boldsymbol B}(d,k)=
   \operatorname{diag}\bigl(
        \underbrace{\boldsymbol B^{F}_{1}(k),\, 
        \dots,\,
        \boldsymbol B^{F}_{d/k}(k)}_{\text{$d/k$ copies}}
   \bigr)\!,
   \qquad k\,|\,d .
\]

By recursively chaining components with decreasing block sizes ($k=d,d/2,\dots ,2$), we obtain a **butterfly matrix** 
$\displaystyle 
   \boldsymbol B(d)=
      \tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!(d,d)\,
      \tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!(d,\tfrac d 2)\cdots 
      \tilde{\boldsymbol B}\!(d,2).
$

If every factor is made **orthogonal**, their product remains orthogonal—a direct consequence of $(AB)^\top(AB)=B^\top A^\top A B = I$. Orthogonality per factor is enforced by applying a Cayley transform on each tiny $2\times 2$ diagonal block:


\[
   \boldsymbol Q = -\,\boldsymbol Q^\top ,       ~~   
   \textstyle 
   \boldsymbol M = (\boldsymbol I + \boldsymbol Q)\bigl(\boldsymbol I - \boldsymbol Q\bigr)^{-1},
   ~~ 
   {\rm where}\;\;\boldsymbol M^\top M=\boldsymbol I .
\]

Thus every learnable scalar sits inside one tiny skew‑symmetric submatrix.


### Parameter Count  

For a layer of dimension $d$, choosing *block size* $b$ and *number of stages* $m$, BOFT needs only  

\[
N_{\text{params}}
   = \frac12\, (b-1)\, d\, m
   = 
     {\cal O}\bigl(d\, b\, m\bigr).
\]

Typical choices:
| Setting | Meaning | Trainable scalars |
|---|---|---|
| $b=d$, $m=1$ | Full dense OFT | ${\cal O}(d^{2})$ |
| Block‑diagonal OFT | $b<d$, $m=1$ | ${\cal O}(b\,d)$ |
| **BOFT** ($b=2$, $m=\log_{2}d$) | Butterfly structure | ${\cal O}(d\,\log d)$ |

For instance with $d=1024$:  
* Full OFT → ≈ 524 K params  
* Block‑diagonal ($b=16$) → ≈ 16 K params  
* BOFT ($b=2$, $m=10$) → ≈ 5 K params – an order‑of‑magnitude reduction over even block‑diagonal OFT while still yielding a *dense* orthogonal transformation.


## Building Boosted Orthogonal Finetuning – BOFT  

Putting everything together, BOFT replaces the single hidden ℝ⁽ᵈˣᵈ⁾ orthogonal matrix in OFT with a **product** of butterfly components:

```math
    \boxed{
    \begin{aligned}
        &\displaystyle 
         {\bf R}(m,b)
            = 
            \prod_{i=1}^{m}
               {\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} ,
        \\[4pt]
        & {\rm s.t.\ }
          ({\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^\top 
          {\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} 
           = {\bf I}_d ,
          ~~~ i=1,\dots,m .
    \end{aligned}}
```

During forward propagation we simply multiply this compactly stored ${\bf R}$ onto the frozen weight matrix:
$\displaystyle {\bf z}=({{\bf R}}(m,b)\,\!{\bf W}^{0})^\top {\bf x}$.
At inference time ${\bf R}$ can be merged into ${\bf W}^{0}$ once training finishes—no extra latency.



### Identity Initialization  

Just like classic OFT starts from the identity transformation so that finetuning begins exactly at the pretrained point, **BOFT initializes every butterfly component as an identity matrix**:
* Set every underlying skew‑symmetric matrix $\bf Q = {\bf 0}$ → Cayley transform yields ${\bf M}= {\bf I}$.
* Consequently ${\bf R}= {\bf I}_d$.  

This “zero‐change” start encourages small updates (the usual low learning rate trick) while guaranteeing that early gradients respect the geometry learned during pretraining.


### Multiplicative Dropout – Regularizing A Product  

Standard Dropout works well when you add *extra* parameters (e.g., LoRA’s low‑rank matrices), but BOFT’s update is *multiplicative*. Dropping out activations would break the strict orthogonality constraint. The authors therefore propose **multiplicative dropout**, which stochastically replaces whole components—or even individual blocks inside them—with identities during training:

* Choose a fraction \(p_{1}\) of the \(m\) butterfly factors uniformly at random.
* Within each selected factor pick another fraction \(p_{2}\) of its diagonal blocks.
* Those selected blocks are forced to stay equal to ${\bf I}$ for that forward/backward pass.

Because every remaining component stays perfectly orthogonal, their product remains an orthogonal matrix even under dropout. This simple trick injects randomness akin to classic Dropout while respecting BOFT’s structural constraints.


## What BOFT Keeps From OFT – And What It Gains  

| Property | Why it holds in BOFT |
|----------|----------------------|
| **Angle preservation** | All learned transformations are orthogonal ⇒ inner products between any pair of neurons stay unchanged (`⟨Ru,Rv⟩ = ⟨u,v⟩`). |
| **Spectral‐norm invariance** | For any SVD `W⁰ = U Σ Vᵀ`, `RW⁰ = (RU) Σ Vᵀ` has identical singular values because `RU` remains orthonormal (`(RU)ᵀ(RU)=I`). |
| **Training stability** | Keeping singular values fixed avoids exploding/vanishing gradients—a benefit documented for both OFT and BOFT (see Appendix calculations). |
| **Expressivity vs regularity trade‑off** | Theorem 1 (“Expressivity of BOFT”) proves that with identical block size $b$, any transformation realizable by block‑diagonal OFT can be realized—and often surpassed—by an appropriate choice of $(m,b)$ butterflies. In other words: *BOFT ⊇ OFT*. |
| **Implicit inductive bias** | Butterfly factorizations efficiently encode classic linear transforms (FFT, Hadamard...), so learning proceeds within a hypothesis class already known to generalize well across signal domains.|

Thus BOFT enjoys *all* theoretical guarantees that made OFT attractive while dramatically shrinking its parameter budget.


### Empirical Evidence  

The authors evaluated BOFT on three families of large foundation models:

* **Vision Transformers** – image classification / segmentation tasks showed higher accuracy than LoRA and vanilla OFT despite using ∼10× fewer trainable weights.
* **Large Language Models** – downstream reasoning benchmarks benefited from lower overfitting thanks to multiplicative dropout + structured bias.
* **Text‑to‑Image Diffusion models** – controllable generation quality matched or exceeded prior best results while keeping memory footprint modest.

Across all experiments “BOFT outperforms current state‑of‑the‑art PEFT methods by a considerable margin,” confirming that sparsity induced by butterflies does *not* sacrifice performance.


## Practical Takeaways  

If you already have an OFT implementation you can switch to BOFT almost effortlessly:

1️⃣ Replace each layer’s learnable orthogonal matrix `R` with its butterfly decomposition `R(m,b)` – most libraries already expose easy-to‑use Cayley transforms for tiny skew‑symmetric blocks.  

2️⃣ Set `(m,b)` according to your memory budget (`b=2`, `m≈log₂(d)` gives maximal density at minimal cost).  

3️⃣ Initialize every component via Cayley(Q=0).  

4️⃣ Hook up multiplicative dropout (`p₁≈0.15`, `p₂≈0.25` work well empirically).  

5️⃣ Train exactly as you would an ordinary fine‑tune – after training merge `R` into `W⁰` once and discard all auxiliary variables.


## Limitations & Future Directions  

The main drawback lies in *training time*: multiplying several sparse factors incurs an extra ‑\(m − 1 ≈ log₂(d)-1​​​ ≃​ 9​⁠​⁠ ∎​ `\` overhead per forward/backward pass compared with vanilla OFT’s single multiplication.\  
Fortunately this overhead disappears after merging at inference time, leaving zero latency impact.



The authors also note that butterfly networks are just one point on an expansive design space motivated by their “information transmission” view. Other network topologies studied in computer networking could potentially achieve denser connectivity with even fewer edges—a promising avenue for next‑generation PEFT designs.



---

### Bottom Line  

BOFT shows how *structured sparsity*—borrowed directly from fast Fourier algorithms—can turn an otherwise costly dense orthogonal update into something that scales like \(𝒪(d·log d)\). By preserving angles, keeping spectral norms intact, starting from exact identity initializations and adding an ingenious multiplicative dropout scheme, it delivers all the generalisation strength people fell in love with Orthogonal Finetuning—but with dramatically smaller parameter footprints across vision models, language models and diffusion generators alike.

Give BOFFOT’s butterﬂy anatomy a try next time you need cheap yet high‑fidelity model adaptation—the sky isn’t falling; it’s just being rotated faster!



# Beyond LoRA – Benchmarking BOFT on Vision Transformers, Large Language Models, and Diffusion Generators

*How a butterfly‑structured orthogonal fine‑tuning scheme pushes the frontier of parameter‑efficient adaptation across vision, language, and text‑to‑image generation.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why look past LoRA?

Large foundation models—think GPT‑3‑scale language models or Stable Diffusion—are too expensive to train from scratch for every downstream task.  Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT) techniques therefore dominate research and practice.  

LoRA (“Low‑Rank Adaptation”) has become the de‑facto baseline because it injects only a handful of low‑rank parameters while leaving the pretrained weights untouched.  Yet low‑rank updates are **additive** and they do **not** preserve the geometric relationships encoded in the original weight matrices (e.g., pairwise angles between neurons).  Recent work on **Orthogonal Fine‑tuning** (OFT) showed that a *multiplicative* orthogonal transformation can preserve those relationships, yielding better stability and generalization—at the cost of a large number of trainable parameters (a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ scalars).

Enter **BOFT** (Orthogonal **B**utterfly Fine‑tuning).  By factorising a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse butterfly components, BOFT brings down the trainable size to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ while still delivering a full‑rank orthogonal transform.  In short:

- **Expressivity** ≈ full OFT (dense orthogonal matrix).  
- **Parameter budget** ≈ LOw‑Rank PEFT ($\ll \mathcal{O}(d^{2})$).  
- **Training dynamics** ≈ stable (spectral norm preserved).

The following sections walk through how this design translates into real gains when we benchmark it on three very different families of foundation models.

---

## 2️⃣ BOFT in a nutshell

Before diving into numbers, let’s recap the core recipe:

1. **Orthogonal factorisation** – Represent the learnable matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as   
   \[
   \mathbf{R}\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)},
   \]  
   where each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is a *butterfly component*: a block‑diagonal matrix composed of $2\times2$ (or $b\times b$) orthogonal blocks arranged in the classic Cooley–Tukey pattern.

2. **Parameter count** – Every $2\times2$ block contributes one free scalar via Cayley parameterisation; with $m=\log_{2}d$ layers and block size $b$, BOFT needs roughly  
   \[
   \frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m\;=\;\mathcal{O}(d\log d)
   \]  
   trainable scalars—far less than OFT’s $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ and competitive with LoRA’s $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ for modest rank $r$.

3. **Zero/Identity start** – All butterfly components are initialised as identity matrices so that initially $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ and the model’s behaviour is unchanged.

4. **Multiplicative dropout** – Randomly replace selected butterfly components (or their internal blocks) with identity matrices during training; this regularises the multiplicative update without breaking orthogonality.

5. **Universal applicability** – The same $\mathbf{R}$ is applied *left‑multiplicatively* to any linear layer regardless of domain (vision transformer feed‑forward layers, language model attention projections, diffusion UNet kernels).

With this scaffold in place, let’s see how BOFT fares when put to work.

---

## 3️⃣ Benchmark suite

The authors evaluated BOFT across three representative downstream families:

| Domain | Foundation model | Typical layer size $d$ | Tasks |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------|-------|
| Vision | Vision Transformer (ViT‑B/16) | ~768 | Image classification (ImageNet‑1k), few‑shot transfer |
| Language | Large Language Model (e.g., LLaMA 7B) | ~4096 | Zero‑shot reasoning benchmarks (MMLU, BoolQ) |
| Diffusion | Stable Diffusion UNet | ~1024 | Text‑to‑image controllability & style adaptation |

Each task was also tackled with several strong baselines:

* Full fine‑tuning (the “gold standard,” but prohibitively expensive).  
* LoRA ($r=8$, $r=16$ variants).  
* Adapter tuning (standard bottleneck adapters).  
* Original OFT with block‐diagonal orthogonal matrices.

All methods shared identical training hyperparameters apart from their intrinsic parameter budgets—ensuring a fair comparison of *accuracy vs.\ efficiency* tradeoffs.

---

## 4️⃣ Results highlight

Below we summarise the three most important takeaways observed throughout the experiments.  Numbers are expressed relative to the strongest baseline in each column; exact absolute scores follow naturally from those baselines.

### 4.1 Accuracy / Performance

- **Vision Transformers** – On ImageNet‐1k fine‐tuning, BOFT reached *+0.8 %* top‑1 accuracy over LoRA with roughly *30 %* fewer trainable parameters.  When compared against full fine‐tuning, BOFT lost less than *0.3 %*, confirming that preserving orthogonality does not sacrifice predictive power.
  
- **Large Language Models** – On MMLU (a multi‐task language understanding benchmark), BOFT achieved an average *+1.2 %* absolute improvement over LoRA while using only *≈20 %* of LoRA’s trainable weight budget per layer.  Interestingly, BOFT’s performance was within *0.5 %* of full fine‐tuning despite updating far fewer parameters.
  
- **Diffusion Generators** – For controllable text‐to‐image generation tasks (e.g., style transfer or pose conditioning), BOFT not only preserved image fidelity scores (FID ↓) relative to OFT but also reduced catastrophic forgetting of pretrained generative capabilities—a direct consequence of its spectral norm preservation.

### 4️️⃣ Training stability

Across all three domains BOFT exhibited markedly smoother loss curves:

- **Spectral norm** remained exactly equal to that of the frozen pretrained weights because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal by design (`$\|\mathbf R\mathbf W^0\|_2 = \|\mathbf W^0\|_2$`). This eliminates exploding gradients that sometimes haunt low‐rank additive methods.
- Early in training dynamics were indistinguishable from an identity transform thanks to zero initialization; consequently no sudden performance dip was observed when switching from pretraining to fine‐tuning.
- Multiplicative dropout contributed an extra regularisation knob without breaking orthogonality—empirically lowering validation loss variance by ~15 % compared with vanilla OFT.

### 4️⃣ Parameter efficiency

The theoretical advantage bears out empirically:

| Method | Trainable params per $d\times n$ layer | Relative reduction vs.\ OFT |
|--------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Full OFT | $\mathcal O(d^2)$ | — |
| Block–diag OFT ($b=64$) | $\mathcal O(bd)$ ≈ $64d$ | ~60 % ↓ |
| LOw-RAnk Adaptation (LoRA $r=8$) | $8(d+n)$ | Comparable but *additive* |
| **BOFT ($m=\log_2 d$, $b=2$)** | $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ ≈ $9d$ | **~85 % ↓** vs.\ full OFT |

In practice this translates into adapting a ViT layer ($d=768$) with just ~7k scalar variables instead of >590k needed for a dense orthogonal matrix—a saving that scales dramatically for larger LLMs ($d=4096$ → ~50k vs.~16M).

---

## 5️⃣ Why does BOFT win?

1. **Dense connectivity + structured sparsity** – The butterfly graph guarantees that every input dimension can influence every output dimension after $m=\log_2 d$ stages while only using $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ edges. Contrast this with block‑diagonal OFT where different groups never interact—a restriction that hurts expressivity especially on tasks requiring global feature mixing.
   
2. **Preserved geometry** – By left‑multiplying with an orthogonal matrix we keep pairwise neuron angles intact (`$\langle \mathbf w_i,\mathbf w_j\rangle = \langle \mathbf R\mathbf w_i,\mathbf R\mathbf w_j\rangle$`). This respects what pretraining has already learned (semantic directionality), so fine‐tuning only reshapes representations where necessary.
   
3. **Implicit inductive bias toward classic transforms** – Butterfly factorizations naturally encode Fourier/Hadamard-like operations which are known to be good at mixing information across dimensions efficiently. Empirically this bias appears to improve generalisation on both vision and language tasks.
   
4. **Spectral norm stability** – Since singular values are unchanged (`$\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf R\mathbf W^0)=\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W^0)$`), optimiser step sizes can stay large without risking divergence—a property often cited in spectral normalisation literature.

---

## 6️⃣ Practical takeaways

- **Drop-in replacement:** Switching from LoRA or adapters to BOFT is as simple as swapping in a butterfly module before each linear projection; after fine‐tuning you can fold $\mathbf R$ into the original weight matrix so inference incurs zero overhead.
- **Memory savings:** When fine‐tuning massive LLMs (>30 B params) on single GPUs, BOFT’s $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ footprint enables fitting multiple task heads simultaneously—a scenario infeasible with dense OFT or even LoRA at higher ranks.
- **Robustness:** The method handles both classification/regression heads and generative backbones alike; developers needing stable training pipelines for diffusion models should especially consider BOFT because it avoids “mode collapse” often seen with additive PEFT tricks.
- **Extensibility:** The authors prove that stacking multiple butterfly components (`$\mathbf R^G = \prod_k \mathbf R_k^{\pm}$`) can cover the entire orthogonal group given enough depth—so if an application truly demands full expressivity you can increase `m` without blowing up parameters dramatically.

---

## 7️⃣ Limitations & future directions

While BOFF shines in most respects, two caveats remain:

1. **Runtime overhead during training:** Multiplying several sparse butterfly matrices per forward pass adds modest compute compared with a single dense update in OFT or LoRA. In practice this overhead is offset by faster convergence thanks to stability—but further optimisation (e.g., fused kernels) would make BOFT more attractive for ultra‑large models.
   
2. **Topology alternatives:** The butterfly structure is not provably optimal for information transmission; other network topologies from graph theory or computer networking might yield even lower edge counts while preserving density. Exploring such alternatives could push parameter budgets down further.

---

## 8️⃣ Bottom line

BOFT demonstrates that you don’t need to choose between *full expressive power* and *tiny adapters*. By marrying classic fast Fourier transform ideas with modern orthogonal fine‑tuning theory, it delivers:

- **Higher or comparable accuracy** across vision transformer classification, large language model reasoning, and diffusion generator control,
- **Significant parameter reduction** relative to original Orthogonal Fine‑tuning,
- **Training stability** thanks to exact spectral norm preservation,
- **Zero inference cost**, because learned orthogonal matrices are folded back into pretrained weights after adaptation.

If you’ve been relying on LoRA or adapters as your go‑to PEFT toolbox, give Orthogonal Butterfly a spin—it may just become your new default for efficient yet expressive model adaptation across modalities.



# The Information Transmission View – Visualizing Matrix Factorizations as Network Graphs  

*How treating an orthogonal matrix factorization as a flow of information on a grid‑structured graph makes sparsity patterns intuitive—and why this perspective opens the door to network‑topology inspired designs.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why we need a new way to look at orthogonal finetuning  

Large foundation models (LLMs, vision transformers, diffusion models) are powerful but costly to train from scratch. Finetuning them efficiently is therefore essential.  
**Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** proposes a principled way to adapt a pretrained linear layer  

\[
\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^0,
\]

where $\mathbf{W}^0$ is frozen and $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an *orthogonal* matrix ($\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$).  
Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise angles between neurons, OFT tends to keep the pretrained knowledge intact while still allowing task‑specific changes.

The catch? A full $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ parameters—far too many for parameter‑efficient finetuning.  The original OFT mitigates this by forcing $\mathbf{R}$ into a **block‑diagonal** form, drastically cutting parameters but also imposing a rigid sparsity pattern that limits expressivity.

Can we generate **dense** orthogonal matrices **without** paying the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ price? The authors answer “yes” by reframing the problem as **information transmission** on a multipartite graph.

---

## 2️⃣ From a Dense Matrix to a Flow of Information  

Consider any square matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as describing how $d$ *source* dimensions can influence $d$ *target* dimensions.  
If entry $R_{ij}=0$, then signal from source $j$ cannot reach target $i$; if $R_{ij}\neq0$, there is a direct connection.  

Now imagine building $\mathbf{R}$ not as a single monolithic matrix but as the product of $m$ smaller factors:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_m}_{\text{level }m}
           \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_{m-1}}_{\text{level }m-1}
           \cdots
           \underbrace{\mathbf{B}_1}_{\text{level }1}.
\]

Each factor $\mathbf{B}_k$ maps one “layer” of nodes to the next. If we place these layers side‑by‑side we obtain a **grid‑structured graph** with $d$ nodes in each of $(m+1)$ levels (Figure 1 in the paper). An edge from node $j$ in level $k$ to node $i$ in level $k+1$ exists exactly when $(\mathbf{B}_k)_{ij}\neq0$.

> **Key observation:**  
> For the product $\mathbf{B}_m\cdots \mathbf{B}_1$ to be *dense* (i.e., every source can affect every target), **every node in the first level must have at least one path to every node in the last level**. In graph terms, we need *dense connectivity* across the whole grid.

In this viewpoint:

* **Trainable parameters** ↔ *non‑fixed edges* (the non‑zero entries that we actually learn).  
* **Orthogonality constraint** ↔ each factor must be an orthogonal transformation (so each intermediate bipartite graph is a perfect matching plus optional rotations within matched pairs).

Thus the problem of designing a parameter‑efficient orthogonal matrix becomes: **choose sparse edge sets on each level that guarantee dense end‑to‑end connectivity while respecting orthogonality**.

---

## 3️⃣ What does sparsity look like on the graph?  

### Block‑diagonal OFT  
If $\mathbf{R}$ is block diagonal with blocks of size $b$, each factor is simply an identity map across blocks—there are no cross‑block edges. In graph terms this creates *disconnected sub‑graphs*: sources belonging to block 1 can never influence targets belonging to block 2. While it saves parameters ($\mathcal{O}(bd)$), it inevitably reduces expressivity because dense connectivity is impossible without adding many more blocks.

### Butterfly factorization  
Enter the **butterfly network**, familiar from the Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform. A butterfly graph systematically couples pairs of nodes across stages so that after only $\log_2 d$ stages every source has reached every target.  

Concretely, each butterfly factor $\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)$ (with block size $k$) looks like:

\[
\tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)= \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\underbrace{\mathbf B^F_1(k),\,\dots,\,
\mathbf B^F_{d/k}(k)}_{d/k\text{ blocks}}\bigr),
\]
where each $\mathbf B^F_i(k)$ is a *butterfly factor*

\[
\mathbf B^F(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_1) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_2) \\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_3) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_4)
\end{bmatrix}, 
\qquad 
\mathbf d_j \in \mathbb R^{k/2}.
\]

If we stack $\log_2 d$ such factors with decreasing block sizes ($k = d, d/2,\dots,2$), the resulting graph has exactly **$\mathcal O(d \log d)$ edges**, yet every source still reaches every target—fulfilling dense connectivity with far fewer trainable parameters than $\mathcal O(d^2)$.

In short:

| Design | Edges (≈ parameters) | Connectivity |
|--------|----------------------|--------------|
| Full dense orthogonal | $d^2$ | Trivial |
| Block-diagonal (size $b$) | $bd$ | Only within blocks |
| Butterfly ($m=\log_2 d$, $b=2$) | $d\,\log d$ | Fully connected across all levels |

The graph view makes these trade‑offs instantly visible: you either add many intra–block edges (block diagonal) or few carefully arranged inter‐block edges (butterfly).

---

## 4️⃣ Why does this matter for finetuning?  

### 4.1 Parameter efficiency without sacrificing expressivity  
Because each butterfly component can be made orthogonal using a simple Cayley transform (or directly parameterized as a 2×2 rotation), BOFT (the **B**utterfly **O**rthogonal **F**inetuning method) achieves an orthogonal matrix with only $\frac12(b-1)dm = \mathcal O(d \log d)$ learnable scalars. Yet empirical results in the paper show that BOFT matches or outperforms both LoRA and block‑diagonal OFT on vision transformers, large language models, and diffusion models—demonstrating that dense connectivity is essential for preserving pretrained knowledge while still allowing task adaptation.

### 4.2 Clear intuition for sparsity patterns  
Seeing factors as bipartite graphs helps practitioners reason about where information flow may be bottlenecked. If you notice poor performance on certain downstream tasks, you can directly inspect whether some source–target pairs never share a path and adjust the topology accordingly—something far harder when looking only at matrix entries.

### 4.3 A fertile ground for new designs  
The information transmission framework abstracts away from specifics of Fourier transforms; any network topology that guarantees all‑pairs reachability with few edges becomes a candidate factorization scheme. For example:

* **Benes networks** – widely used for switching fabrics—provide optimal rearrangeable routing with just $2\log_2 d - 1$ stages.
* **Hypercube / Mesh topologies** – offer logarithmic diameter and could be expressed via block permutations.
* **Expander graphs** – have strong connectivity properties using linear numbers of edges.

Adapting such topologies into orthogonal sparse factors could further reduce parameters or improve training stability while keeping the dense end‐to‐end effect.

---

## 5️⃣ From Graphs Back to Orthogonal Matrices  

To close the loop, let’s recall how we enforce orthogonality on each factor:

1. **Cayley transform:** any skew‑symmetric matrix $\mathbf Q = -\mathbf Q^\top$ yields an orthogonal matrix
   \[
   \mathbf R = (\mathbf I + \mathbf Q)(\mathbf I - \mathbf Q)^{-1}.
   \]
   By restricting $\mathbf Q$ to have nonzero entries exactly where our graph has edges, we preserve both sparsity and orthogonality.
2. **Small rotations:** For blocks of size 2×2 we can simply use
   \[
   \begin{bmatrix}
   \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\
   \sin\theta &  \cos\theta
   \end{bmatrix},
   \]
   where $\theta$ is learnable. Larger block sizes ($b>2$) can be built from Kronecker products or via higher‑dimensional rotation parameterizations.
3. **Identity initialization:** All factors start as identity matrices so that initially $\mathbf R = \mathbf I$, guaranteeing zero deviation from the pretrained model—a common practice in PEFT methods.

During training BOFT multiplies all factors together forward‐propagating through them just like any other linear layer; after finetuning they can be folded into one dense orthogonal matrix and baked into the model weights, incurring *no extra inference cost*.

---

## 6️⃣ Looking Ahead – Network‑Topology Inspired Finetuning  

The authors point out two open questions:

* **Runtime overhead:** Multiplying several sparse factors incurs extra FLOPs during training compared with a single block diagonal matrix.
* **Optimal topology:** Is butterfly truly optimal for transmitting information under an orthogonality constraint?

Because our perspective reduces matrix design to *graph design*, we can tap into decades of research from computer networking and parallel algorithms:

| Potential topology | Edge count | Known benefits |
|--------------------|------------|----------------|
| Benes network      | $(2\log_2 d - 1)d$ | Rearrangeable routing; minimal depth |
| Clos network       | $\approx 4d\,(\log_2 d)$ | Scalable hierarchical interconnect |
| Random expander    | $c\,d$, small constant $c$ | High spectral gap → robust mixing |

Exploring these alternatives could lead to even leaner PEFT schemes where training time stays low while still delivering dense end‑to‑end connectivity.

## 7️⃣ Takeaways  

*Treating an orthogonal matrix factorization as information flow over a multipartite graph turns an abstract algebraic problem into something you can draw and reason about.*  

* The graph demands **dense connectivity** → ensures any pretrained neuron can affect any downstream dimension after finetuning.
* By carefully arranging sparse edges—e.g., via butterfly layers—we get **$\mathcal O(d \log d)$ parameters**, dramatically cheaper than full $\mathcal O(d^2)$ while preserving expressivity.
* This viewpoint naturally links PEFT design to classic network topologies, suggesting many unexplored avenues (Benes, expander graphs…) for future research.
* Practically, Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) inherits OFT’s stability (spectral norm preservation), gains LoRA‑level parameter efficiency, and incurs no inference overhead once training finishes.

If you work on adapting large models—whether language models, vision backbones, or diffusion generators—consider visualizing your weight reparameterizations as graphs first.
