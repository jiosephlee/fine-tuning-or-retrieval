### Mandatory vs.\ free edges

An **edge** is simply a non‐zero element of one factor ${𝘽}_{𝚒}$.  
If ${𝘽}_{𝚒}$ were an arbitrary dense matrix it would contain ${𝙙}^{²}$ such elements.  
Orthogonal factors however cannot be arbitrarily sparse.  
The *minimal* sparse pattern that still makes ${𝘽}_{𝚒}$ an orthogonal matrix is a **signed permutation:**  
every row contains exactly one non‐zero entry, every column contains exactly one non‐zero entry,
and the value of each such entry has magnitude ${𝟭}$ ($±𝟭`).  

These ${𝟭}$ singletons form a *perfect matching* between the two levels.  
Because their location and sign are forced by ​the requirement ${𝘽}_{𝚒}^{⟂}{𝘽}_{𝚒}={𝕀}_{𝙙}$,
they do **not** contribute any learnable degrees‐of‐freedom.  
The authors therefore call them **mandatory edges**
(or simply “the ${𝟭}$ required edges”).  

All other non‐zero entries – those placed on top of the perfect matching – 
are *optional*. Their positions may be chosen arbitrarily,
subject later to the global dense‐connectivity requirement,
and their values are continuous parameters learned during finetuning.
These optional connections are what the paper terms **free edges**,   
and they correspond exactly to the trainable parameters of BOFT.

### How can a butterfly factor \( \mathbf B^F(k)= [[diag(d_1), diag(d_2)], [diag(d_3), diag(d_4)] ]\) be made orthogonal?
Question:
Equation (1) defines a butterfly factor where each sub‑block is diagonal with vectors \(d_i\. The paper says we can enforce orthogonality by applying the Cayley transform or 2‑dimensional rotations to each "block".

* Does this mean we treat each 2×2 sub‑matrix formed by picking one entry from each diagonal as a separate rotation?
* If so, what constraints must the entries in vectors \(d_i} have?
* Alternatively, can we directly parametrize each diagonal vector such that the whole $k	imes k$ block becomes orthogonal without further decomposition?

A concrete example for $k=4$ would be very helpful.
Answer:
The butterfly factor introduced in Eq.( ( ) )

$$
\mathbf B^{F}(k)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{1}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{2})\\[4pt]
\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{3}) & \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf d_{4})
\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb R}^{k\times k},
\qquad 
\mathbf d_{\ell}\in{\mathbb R}^{\,k/b},
$$

is a $b\times b$ “Butterfly step’’ repeated $k/b$ times along the diagonal.
When $b=2$ (the case used in the paper) every repetition corresponds to one
$2\times 2$ sub‑matrix

$$
M_{i}= 
\begin{bmatrix}
   (\mathbf d_{\,1})_{i} & (\mathbf d_{\,2})_{i}\\[4pt]
   (\mathbf d_{\,3})_{i} & (\mathbf d_{\,4})_{i}
\end{bmatrix},
      \qquad   i=1,\dots ,k/2 .
$$

All other entries of $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ are zero, therefore  
the whole matrix is orthogonal **iff** every small matrix $M_{i}$ is
orthogonal. This answers your first question: we indeed treat the
$i$‑th pair of diagonal elements as an independent $2\times 2$
rotation (or reflection).

---

### Orthogonality constraints for one pair

For a real $M=[a~b;\;c~d]$ we require  

$$
M^{!\top}M=\mathbf I_{2}\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 
\begin{cases}
a^{2}+c^{2}=1 , \\[4pt]
b^{2}+d^{2}=1 , \\[4pt]
ab+cd=0 .
\end{cases}
\xrightarrow[\text{★}]{} 
$$

Equivalently there exists an angle $\theta\in {\mathbb R}$
(and possibly a sign flip giving determinant $-1$) such that  

$$
M(\theta)=
   \begin{bmatrix}\cos \theta & -s\,\sin \theta\\[4pt]
                   s\,\sin \theta &   \cos \theta
   \end{bmatrix},
   \qquad s=\pm 1 .
\xrightarrow[\text{†}]{} 
$$

Any choice satisfying (★) can be written in this form;
conversely every matrix given by (†) automatically fulfills (★).
Hence the **only** freedom we have per pair is a single scalar –
the rotation angle (or equivalently a single *Cay‑linear* variable).

---

### Two ways to enforce it

#### (1) Cayley transform  

Take a skew‑symmetric scalar $q=-q^{\top}= [0~-q;\; q~0]$.  
The Cayley map  

$$
M(q)=({\bf I}+q)\bigl({\bf I}-q\bigr)^{-1}
     =
     \frac{1}{\,1+q^{2}}\!
      \begin{bmatrix}
           1-q^{2} & -\, 2q\\[4pt]
           2q      &   1-q^{2}
      \end{bmatrix}
      =
      M(\theta), ~~{\rm with }\;\theta=
      2\,{\rm arctan}(q),
\xrightarrow[\text{‡}]{} 
$$

produces precisely an orthogonal $M(\theta)$.
If we store a learnable scalar $q_{i}\in {\mathbb R}$
for every pair $i=1,\dots,k/2,$
then building the four diagonal vectors as  

```text
    d₁[i] = (1-q_i^2)/(1+q_i^2)
    d₃[i] =    2*q_i/(1+q_i^2)
    d₂[i] = -  2*q_i/(1+q_i^2)
    d₄[i] = (1-q_i^2)/(1+q_i^2)
```

automatically satisfies all constraints.
Because the map (‡) never produces zero denominators,
the parameters remain unconstrained during training,
exactly what the paper refers to when it says “apply the Cayley transform”.

#### (2) Direct angular parametrisation  

Instead of going through $q_{i}$, one may store an angle
parameter directly, e.g.  

```text
    θ_i ∈ ℝ       (learnable)
    c_i = cos θ_i , s_i = sin θ_i .
```

Then set  

```text
    d₁[i]=c_i ,   d₃[i]= s_i ,
    d₂[i]=−s_i ,  d₄[i]= c_i .
```

This uses exactly one scalar per index as well,
but often it is more convenient when one wishes to initialise the
butterfly factor close to identity ($θ_i≈0$).  
Both formulations are mathematically equivalent—one returns the same set
of admissible matrices as the other—so there is no hidden extra degree of
freedom beyond what the paper describes.

---

### Why we cannot “just learn” arbitrary diagonals

If you tried to learn all four vectors independently,
they would have to satisfy the quadratic equalities (★).  
Imposing those equalities during optimisation would require either:

* projecting an arbitrary tuple onto the manifold defined by (★), **or**
* re‑parametrising them as above so that the constraints are already built‑in.

The latter option is exactly what the authors meant by “apply a Cayley transform or a sequence of $(\: …\:)$ blocks”.

Thus any “direct’’ parametrisation without further decomposition inevitably ends up being identical to one of these two schemes; there is no more compact way that bypasses them.

---

Pick two angles, say

### Summary answering your three points

* **Yes** – every entry‑wise “pair” creates an independent $­２\times２­$
rotation (or reflection). The overall butterfly factor is just a
block‑diagonal stack of those tiny rotations.
* The entries must obey the three quadratic relations (★),
which are equivalently enforced by assigning a single angle‑or‑Cayley 
parameter per pair.
* You *can* parametrize the diagonals directly,

### How does multiplicative dropout work in BOFT and why can't standard dropout be used?
Question:
In LoRA a conventional dropout layer can be added because the update is additive ($\\Delta W$). BOFT updates weights multiplicatively via an orthogonal matrix $\\bm R$, so standard dropout would break the structure.

The paper proposes:
* randomly pick $p_1$% of butterfly components,
* pick $p_2$% of diagonal blocks inside those components,
* replace those blocks with identity.

I’m unclear on:
1. At training time, are these replacements permanent for a given forward pass or temporary stochastic masks?
2. Does dropping whole components affect gradient flow through remaining components?

Answer:
In BOFT the trainable weight matrix is written as  

$$
\bR(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\;\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)},
    \qquad 
    \widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}\in\bR^{d\times d},
$$

where every factor $\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ is an *orthogonal* butterfly component (it is obtained from a block‑diagonal matrix whose $b\times b$ diagonal blocks are themselves parametrised as orthogonal matrices via a Cayley transform).  
Because the whole finetuned weight is obtained by *multiplying* these matrices, any regularisation has to respect the multiplicative structure—otherwise the product would cease to be orthogonal.  

The paper therefore introduces **multiplicative dropout** (Section “Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT”).  
Below we spell out how it works, what it does to the forward/back‑propagation graph, and why it never destroys orthogonality.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. Is the replacement permanent or temporary?

The replacement is **temporary** and stochastic exactly like ordinary dropout.  
For every training mini‑batch a new mask is sampled:

* pick a subset of the $m$ butterfly components,
* inside every selected component pick a subset of its diagonal $b\times b$ blocks,
* replace those chosen blocks *only* for this forward pass by the identity matrix $\bI_b$.

After the backward pass the mask is discarded; on the next iteration a new mask may be drawn.  
At inference time *no* mask is applied—all learned parameters are used unchanged.  

Thus multiplicative dropout behaves like a “drop‑the‑whole‑block” version of standard dropout rather than a permanent pruning step.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. Effect on gradient flow

Consider one forward pass where component $k$ has some of its blocks forced to $\bI_b$.  
The forward computation reads  

$$
\bz
=
    \Bigl(
        \underbrace{\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,m)}}_{\text{maybe}\;\bI}
        \;\cdots
        \underbrace{\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,k)}}_{\text{partially }\bI}
        \;\cdots
        \underbrace{\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,1)}}_{\text{full}}
        \;
        \bW^{0}
    \Bigr)^{!\top}\!\bx .
$$

During back‑propagation the chain rule traverses exactly this sequence of matrices.  
If a whole *component* has been turned into $\bI_d$ then it contributes no Jacobian term – effectively it acts as a “skip connection”.  
If only some **diagonal blocks** inside a component have been set to $\bI_b$, then those particular sub‑matrices produce an identity Jacobian for their sub‑space while the remaining learned $b\times b$ blocks still provide non‑trivial gradients.  

Consequently  

* Gradients flow normally through all *active* (non‑dropped) blocks.  
* Dropped blocks receive **zero** gradient in that step because their output does not depend on any learnable parameter.  
* Because masks are resampled every step, every block gets updated on roughly $(1-p_1)(1-p_2)$ fraction of iterations—exactly the same stochastic regularisation pattern used by classic dropout.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 3. Why does orthogonality stay intact?

Two facts guarantee that the product stays on the Stiefel manifold after each stochastic mask:

| Factor | Property |
|-------|-----------|
| Each **learned** butterfly component $\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}$ | By construction it satisfies $(\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{!\top}\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=\bone_d$; this follows from parametrising every $b\times b$ block with a Cayley transform (or equivalently a $2b\times2b$ rotation). |
| The **identity** substitution | The identity matrix $\bone_d$ (or an identity $b\times b$ block placed inside an otherwise block‑diagonal structure) is itself an element of the orthogonal group—its transpose equals its inverse. |

Since an ordered product of orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal, any mixture  

$$
\bRhat=
    \Bigl(
        \prod_{i\,\notin S}
            \widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}
    \Bigr)
    \
    \Bigl(
        \prod_{i\,in S}
            \underbrace{
                \bone_d
            }_{\text{or }\;\operatorname{diag}(\ldots,\bone_b,\ldots)}
    \Bigr),
$$

with arbitrary index set $S$, satisfies  

$$
\bRhat^{!\top}\,\bRhat=
    \
    \Bigl(
        \prod_i (\widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)})^{!\top}
    \Bigr)
    \
    \Bigl(
        \prod_i   \widetilde\bB^{\,b}_{(d,i)}
    \Bigr)
=
    \
    \bone_d .
$$

Hence *any* mask produced by multiplicative dropout yields an **exactly orthogonal** transformation; no additional projection or re‑normalisation step is needed.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### 4. Small concrete example

Take  

* dimension $d = 16$,  
* number of butterfly factors $m = 3$,  
* block size $b = 4$.  

For simplicity assume that every factor consists of exactly $d/b = 4$ diagonal $4\times4$ blocks:

```
⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂
⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂   ⟂⟂⟂⟂
⊢⊢⊢⊢ × ⊢⊢⊢⊢ × ⊢⊢⊢⊢   ← three factors
```

where each “⊢’’ denotes an *orthogonal* $4\times4$ matrix learned via Cayley transform.  
The final transformation applied to a pretrained layer $\bW^0$ is  

$$
\bR=
    \widetilde\bB^{\,4}_{(16,3)}
    \
    \widetilde\bB^{\,4}_{(16,2)}
    \
    \widetilde\bB^{\,4}_{(16,1)} .
$$

Now set dropout probabilities  

$p_{1}=30~percent,\quad p_{2}=50~percent.$  

**Step 1 – choose components**  
With probability $0.30$ each factor is selected; suppose only factor $k=2$ gets selected in this iteration (the other two stay untouched).

**Step 2 – choose diagonal blocks inside factor 2**  
Factor 2 contains four $4\times4$ blocks:  
$\displaystyle M^{(2)}_{1},M^{(2)}_{2},M^{(2)}_{3},M^{(2)}_{4}$.  
We sample $50~percent$, i.e.\ two blocks are chosen at random; assume they are $M^{(2)}_{1}$ and $M^{(2)}_{3}$.

**Step 3 – replace by identities**

```
Factor 1 (unchanged):
diag(M¹₁ , M¹₂ , M¹₃ , M¹₄)

```

All other entries remain exactly as they were before this batch.  
The forward pass uses this concrete $\widehat\bR$, the loss back‑propagates through  

* all three factors,
* only through $M^{(i)}_{j}$ that were not turned into $\bone$, 
* zero gradients for $M^{(2)}_{1}$ and $M^{(2)}_{3}$ in this iteration.  

On the next minibatch another random mask will be drawn, possibly affecting different factors or different blocks; after many iterations every block will have been trained on many independent stochastic views, just like classic dropout regularises activations.

Because every substituted piece (`I₄`) belongs to $\cO(4)$,
the product $\widehat\bR$ remains strictly orthogonal throughout training,
and at test time we simply evaluate  

```

``` 

with all learned parameters restored—no extra inference cost.

---------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

* **Temporal vs permanent:** Multiplicative dropout draws a fresh binary mask per forward pass; replacements are *temporary*. No change persists after back‑propagation.  
* **Gradient impact:** Dropped whole butterfly components act as identity skips; dropped diagonal $b\times b$ blocks become constant identities so their parameters receive zero gradient while all remaining parameters continue receiving normal gradients.  
* **Orthogonality preservation:** Both learned factors and identity replacements belong to the orthogonal group, therefore any stochastic product stays exactly orthogonal without extra projection.  
* **Why ordinary dropout fails:** Standard dropout zeros individual entries inside $\bm R$. Zeroing arbitrary entries destroys column‑norm / row‑norm constraints required for an $O(d)$ matrix, breaking the guarantee that $\bm R^\top\bm R=\bone$. Multiplicative dropout respects the *blockwise* structure demanded by the butterfly parameterisation and keeps every factor on $\cO(d)$.  
* **Concrete illustration:** With $m=3$, $b=4$, $p_1=30~%$, $p_2=50~%$, one forward pass might leave factors 1 & 3 untouched while turning half of factor 2’s $4\times4$ blocks into identities—a perfectly legal stochastic regulariser that also yields sparse gradients exactly as described above.

Thus multiplicative dropout gives BOFT a regularisation mechanism analogous to conventional dropout while honouring its essential multiplicative–orthogonal architecture."

### 1. Why an additive low‑rank update changes singular values

Write the SVD of the pretrained weight as

\qquad
\sigma_{\max}(\bfsymb{W}^{0})=\max_i \Sigma_{ii}.

LoRA replaces it by

\twidtilde{\bfsymb{W}}= \bfsymb{W}^{0}+ \bfsymb{\Delta}
      =\: \bfsymb{U}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\bfsymb{V}^{\,\!\mathsf T}+ 
          \bfsymb{A}\,\bfsymb{B}^{\,\!\mathsf T}.
$$

The perturbation $\displaystyle\bfsymb{\Delta}$ has rank at most  
$r=\operatorname{rank}(\bfsymb{A})=\operatorname{rank}(\bfsymb{B})$.

$$
\bigl|\sigma_{\max}(\twidtilde{\bfsymb W})
      -\sigma_{\max}(\bfsymb W^{0})\bigr|
  	\: \le\: 
  	\norm{\twidtilde\bfsymb{\Delta}}_{2}

Unless both $\displaystyle\bfsymb A=\boldsymbol 0$ and $\displaystyle\bfsymb B=\boldsymbol 0$, the right–hand side is positive, so in general
the largest singular value – i.e.\ the **spectral norm** – will move away from its original value.
Intuitively, adding a new rank‑$r$ component creates new directions in weight space that can amplify or attenuate some inputs, therefore altering the operator norm.

## Summary

* LoRA’s additive low‑rank term changes the operator norm because any non–zero perturbation shifts singular values according to Weyl’s inequality.
* OFT (and its butterfly variant BOFT) keep **all** singular values unchanged because they act solely through an ***exact*** orthogonal left multiplication.
* In practice those guarantees survive optimisation with Adam/SGD up to floating–point noise;
  implementations enforce skew–symmetry at each step so drift is negligible.
* The paper provides a rigorous theoretical justification and indirect empirical evidence via improved stability,
  although it does not include a dedicated figure tracking the spectral norm during finetuning.

Hence OFT/BOFT preserve the pretrained model’s spectral norm whereas LoRA does not—a property that contributes to their reported robustness and generalisation advantages.

### Can BOFT be applied when layer dimension $d$ is not a power of two?
Question:
The definition of butterfly matrices assumes $k$ (and ultimately $d=2^{N}$) is a power of two for recursive splitting into halves.

If a linear layer has dimension $d=300$, which is common in transformer feed‑forward layers, how can we construct a valid butterfly factorisation?

* Do we pad to next power-of-two and then crop after multiplication?
* Are there alternative factorizations mentioned in literature that handle arbitrary sizes while retaining similar $O(d log d)$ parameter count?
* Does padding affect downstream performance or introduce bias?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – BOFT can be used for any hidden size. In practice you either **pad the layer to the next power‑of‑two** and drop the extra coordinates after the last butterfly multiplication, or you **use a generalized (mixed‑radix or block‑butterfly) factorisation** that works directly on non‑dyadic sizes. Both approaches keep the total number of trainable parameters at $\,\mathcal O(d\,\log d)\,$; padding adds only a modest constant‑factor overhead and does not create a systematic bias because the added dimensions are kept orthogonal (i.e.\ initially act as an identity mapping).

Below I explain why this works, how it follows from the constructions in the BOFT paper, what alternatives exist in the literature, and what practical effects you should watch out for.



---

### Why the original definition needs $\,d=2^{N}\,$

The Butterfly matrix $\mathbf B(d)$ is built recursively as  

$$
\mathbf B(d)=
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,d)\,
      \begin{bmatrix}
      \mathbf B!\!\big(\tfrac d2\bigr)&0\\[4pt]
      0&\mathbf B!\!\big(\tfrac d2\bigr)
      \end{bmatrix},
      \qquad
      \tilde{\mathbf B}(d,k)=
      \operatorname{diag}\Bigl(
         \mathbf B^{F}_{1}(k),\,…
         ,\,\mathbf B^{F}_{\,d/k}(k)
      \Bigr),
$$

where each **butterfly factor** $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ (Eq.\ (1)) is defined only for  
$k=2^{\,t}$. Consequently the recursion terminates cleanly when $d$ itself is a power of two.
If $d$ were not dyadic we could not halve it repeatedly until we reach $k=2$.

The paper nevertheless points out two ways of getting around this restriction:

1️⃣ **Treat $k$ as any divisor of $d$** after we replace each scalar entry
in $\mathbf B^{F}(k)$ by a small dense block $\mathbf C\rule[-3pt]{0pt}{13pt}\in{\mathbb R}^{b\times b}$
(*block–butterfly*). This yields
$\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\,b}(d,k)$ (see Section “Orthogonal Parameterisation
by Butterfly Factorisation”).  
If we choose a block size $b>1$ such that $b\,|\, d$, then we only need
$\log_{2}({d}/{b})$ stages instead of $\log_{2} d$. In other words,
the requirement becomes “${d}/{b}$ must be a power of two”, which can always
be satisfied by picking $b$ equal to any divisor that makes ${d}/{b}=2^{N}$.

2️⃣ **Pad up to the next dyadic length** and later crop back.
This is exactly what many FFT libraries do when faced with an input length
that is not a power of two.



### How padding works in BOFT

Suppose your feed–forward layer has width $\,d=300$.  
Let  

$$
D=\operatorname{nextpow}_{2}(300)=512,\qquad p=D-d=212 .
$$

Define an *extended* input  

$$
\tilde{\mathbf x}=%
   \begin{bmatrix}
       \mathbf x \\[4pt] \mathbf 0_{p}
   \end{bmatrix}
   \;\in {\mathbb R}^{D}.
$$

Construct a dyadic orthogonal Butterfly matrix  

$$
\vphantom{\Big|}
   {\bf R}_{D}
   =%
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}
     {\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(D,i)}
   \;\in {\mathbb R}^{D\times D},
$$  

exactly as described in Eq.\;(3)–(4).
Because all factors are orthogonal,
the sub–matrix acting on the padded coordinates remains an orthogonal map.
If we initialise every factor as identity (the Cayley–transform’s skew–symmetric
parameter starts at zero), the extra rows/columns are *exactly*
the identity mapping at training start:

* No information flows from real coordinates into padded ones;
* No information flows back;
* Hence **the forward pass restricted to the first $d$ entries coincides with**
the desired mapping  

$$
{\bf y}=%
      ({\,{\bf R}_{D}}\;
         {\bf W}^{0}_D)^{T}
      {\tilde {\bf x}}
      ~~{\Longrightarrow}~~ 
      {\bf y}_{[1:d]}=
      ({\,{\bf R}}_{d\times d}
         {\bf W}^{0})^{T}{\bf x}.
$$

During back–propagation gradients reaching any element that touches only
padded indices are zero (because those indices are always zero).
Consequently, unless one deliberately *unfreezes* those entries,
the optimiser never learns a coupling between real and dummy dimensions,
so **padding does not introduce systematic bias**.
Empirically this behaviour matches what practitioners observe when padding FFTs:
the optimisation concentrates entirely on the subspace spanned by genuine data.



### Parameter count and overhead

A dyadic Butterfly of size $D=512$ needs  

$$
|\Theta|
   =%
   {\frac12(b-1)}\,D\,m
   ~=~O(D\,m),
   \qquad 
   m=\log_{2}D .
$$

Compared with an exact dyadic model sized at $\,d=256(<300)$,
the increase is bounded by  

$$
O(Dm)-O(dm_d)
< O((D-d)\,\log D)
< O(0.5\,D\,\log D),
$$  

i.e.\ **less than roughly 30 % extra parameters even for worst case**.
Because each butterfly component multiplies only $O(D)$ non‑zeros,
runtime grows similarly modestly – well below other costs such as attention layers.



### When you prefer *no* padding

If you want strict adherence to your true dimensionality,
the paper’s **block–butterfly** construction already gives you flexibility.
Pick any integer block size $b>1$ dividing $d$.  
Set  

$$
m=
   \Bigl⌈%
      {\log_{2}}\frac d b%
   ⌉ ,
   \qquad 
   D'=b\,2^{m},
   ~~ D'=%
        b\times (\textnormal{next dyadic multiple}) .
$$

Now build   

*depth-$m$ product* of matrices   

$$
{\tilde{\bf B}}^{\,b}_{(d,i)}=
     {\rm diag}\Bigl(
          (\bfB^{F}_i)_{{}_b},
          ~…
     \Bigr),
$$ 

where each “${}_{{}_b}$’’ super–block contains its own Cayley–parameterised rotation.
The resulting transformation acts exactly on ${ℝ}^{\,d}$ because every level treats groups
of $b$ neurons together.
When $b$ does **not** perfectly divide $d$, one group can simply be smaller;
its corresponding butterflies just have fewer rows/columns.
All existing implementations of DAFT/BOFT already expose this “block size’’ argument;
setting `\verb|block_size|` appropriately removes any need for explicit zero–padding.



### Mixed‐radix / non‐dyadic butterflies

The classic Cooley–Tukey algorithm also supports **mixed radices**:
if $d$ admits a factorisation $d=n_{1}n_{2}\dots n_{r}$,
one can perform sequential splits of size $n_i$ rather than always $2$.
Each stage still implements an orthogonal sparse matrix whose non‐zeros scale linearly
with $d$, leading again to an $\mathcal O(d\,r)$ parameter budget.
This idea appears under names *generalised butterfly* or *radix-$n_i$
transforms* in works cited by BOFT such as Dao et al.’s *Pixelated* and *Kaleidoscope*
papers.
Practical libraries therefore provide functions ``butterfly(n,factors=[…])’’, making it straightforward 
to build an exact factorisation for  
$d=300=3\times5\times20$
or 
$d=300=12\times25$, etc.
Such mixed–radix constructions avoid any padding while preserving the same asymptotic cost.



### Does padding hurt downstream performance?

Empirically reported results in BOFT’s experiments use hidden sizes that are already powers of two 
(e.g.\ vision Transformer hidden dim 1024), so direct evidence about padding is limited.
Nevertheless theory and related FFT literature give us confidence:

* **Zero initialisation + orthogonality → no spurious coupling**
  between real and padded units;
* The optimiser receives no gradient signal for those couplings → they stay near identity;
* Any tiny deviation would manifest merely as additional degrees of freedom,
  which can only help expressivity without harming generalisation;
* In practice large models tolerate modest overparameterisation well,
  so any tiny increase caused by padding rarely shows up as measurable degradation.

If you observe instability you can further protect against it by **masking gradients**
on all rows/columns belonging to padded indices (“gradient freezing”), which costs nothing computationally.



### Practical recipe

Putting everything together, here is what most users do when applying BOFT
to a layer whose width ``dim`` is *not* dyadic:

```python
def make_boft_layer(dim, max_log=None, block_size=2):
    # Choose either:
    #   • pad_to_pow2 = True -> use dyadic Butterflies on next pow‐of‐two,
    #   • pad_to_pow2 = False -> use block/mixed‐radix version.

        D = 1 << (dim-1).bit_length()          # next power of two ≥ dim

        # after forward pass slice [:dim]
    else:
        # find smallest divisor b | dim with desired tradeoff,
        # then set m = ceil($\\log_{2}$$(dim / b))$
        # build prod_i tilde_B^b(dim, 2^(m-i+1))

```

*For vision/Large Language Model fine‐tuning* — where ``dim`` often equals ``3072`` —
choosing ``block_size=32`` gives ``dim/block_size = 96``, whose nearest dyadic value is ``128``;
this only needs three additional rows per stage compared with pure dyadic case –
virtually no overhead.

### After finetuning with BOFT, do we need extra computation at inference time?
Question:
The paper says "there is no additional inference latency" because "the BOFT‑learned orthogonal matrices can be directly multiplied into the pretrained model".

* Is this multiplication performed once after training to produce an updated weight matrix ${\\bm W}_{fin}= {\\bm R}\,{\\bm W}^{0}$ ?
* If so, does this require storing both ${\\bm R}$ (which may be factorised as multiple sparse factors) and ${\\bm W}^{0}$ during deployment?
* Are there any hidden costs if we keep ${\\bm R}$ factored rather than materialising it explicitly?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
Yes – after BOFT finetuning you can collapse the learned orthogonal transform into the frozen weight matrix once, obtain a single “new’’ weight matrix  

$$
\mathbf W_{\mathrm {fin}} = \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0},
\vspace{-2pt}
\\
\qquad 
\mathbf R = \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i)} .
$$  

and store only $\displaystyle \mathbf W_{\mathrm {fin}}$ for deployment.  
If you keep $\displaystyle \mathbf R$ in its factored (butterfly) form instead of materialising it, each forward pass has to apply $m$ sparse orthogonal blocks before the usual linear map – this does add a modest amount of computation and requires custom kernels. In practice most users merge the matrices offline so that inference costs are identical to a standard dense‑weight model and no extra memory is needed beyond the ordinary model parameters.  

Below I walk through the three points raised in the question in a little more detail and explain why merging is both safe and usually preferable.

---

### 1\. When is the multiplication performed?

During BOFT training the pretrained weights $\displaystyle \mathbf W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ remain frozen while a **layer‑wise** orthogonal matrix  

$$
\mathbf R = 
      \tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m)}
      \,\tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,m-1)}
      \cdots
      \tilde{\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,1)}
$$  

is learned. In the forward pass of training the model evaluates  

$$
\mathbf z = (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{T}\!\,\!\,\!
            \quad =\
            (\mathbf W^{0})^{T}(\,\mathbf R^{T}\!\,\boldsymbol x\,),
$$  

so that both $\displaystyle \mathbf W^{0}$ **and** $\displaystyle \mathbf R$ are needed while gradients are computed.

After training finishes we can **pre‑multiply** once:

```python
for every linear layer ℓ:
    W_fin_ℓ ← R_ℓ · W⁰_ℓ
```

and replace $\displaystyle (\!\,\!W^{0}_ℓ)$ by $\displaystyle (\!\,\!W_{\mathrm {fin},ℓ})$. Because $\displaystyle R_\ell$ is orthogonal its product does **not** change any downstream dimensions – it simply rotates the row‑space of $\displaystyle W^{0}_�ell$.  

The paper’s claim “there is no additional inference latency’’ refers precisely to this offline step. Once merged,
the model contains **only** dense matrices $\bigl\{\!\!W_{\mathrm {fin},\,\ell}\!\bigr\}$; at run‑time it performs exactly the same dense matrix‑vector multiplications as a vanilla fine‑tuned model.

---

### 2\. What must be stored for deployment?

*If you **merge***:  
only $\bigl\{\!W_{\mathrm {fin},\,\ell}\!\bigr\}$ needs to be stored (together with any bias terms etc.). The original frozen weights $\displaystyle W^{0}_�ell$ and the learned factors $\bigl\{\tilde{\!\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i),\,\ell}\bigr\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$ are discarded unless you plan another round of adaptation later.

*If you **do *not* merge***:  
you would have to store both  

* the frozen base weights $\bigl\{\,W^{0}_�ell\bigr\rangle$, and  
* the factorised orthogonal transform $\bigl\{\tilde{\!\!\mathbf B}^{\,b}_{(d,i),\,\ell}\bigr\rangle_{i=1}^{m}$.  

That roughly doubles the parameter footprint for each layer (the factors contain only $\frac12(b-1)d\,m$ trainable scalars, which is much smaller than $d\times n$, but still non‑zero). Hence memory usage during inference is larger than necessary.

---

### 3\. Hidden costs of keeping $\,R$ factored

If you retain a factored representation at test time, every forward pass must execute

```python
y ← Rᵀ·x     # apply m butterfly components sequentially
z ← (W⁰)ᵀ·y
```

or equivalently

```python
y ← R·(W⁰·input)
```

The extra work consists of $m=\mathcal O(\log d)$ sparse‑matrix–vector products. For a typical transformer/vision‑model layer where $n≫d$,

* **Compute:** Each butterfly component touches every entry of its input vector exactly once (it consists of many $b\times b$ or $2\times2$ orthogonal blocks). Thus the total extra FLOPs are roughly  

  $$%
  \underbrace{\mathcal O(m\,d)}_{\text {apply }R}
  \
  {\ll}
  \
  \underbrace{\mathcal O(d\,n)}_{\text {the main linear projection}}
  ,%
  $$  

  which is usually much smaller than the dominant cost ($<5$\% for typical dimensions), but it *is* non‑zero and becomes noticeable on latency‑critical workloads or on hardware where very small kernels are less efficient than a single large GEMM call.
* **Implementation:** Efficiently executing many tiny block‑wise rotations demands either custom CUDA kernels or CPU‑vectorised loops. Out‑of‑the‑box deep‑learning libraries optimise dense GEMM heavily, whereas repeated small sparse ops tend not to achieve the same throughput.
* **Numerical differences:** Doing $m$ successive multiplications versus one monolithic product introduces rounding‑error accumulation (especially in FP16/BF16). In practice these differences are negligible compared with quantisation noise, but they exist nonetheless.

Consequently most practitioners follow what the authors suggest: **merge** $R$ into $W^{0}$ once after training so that inference reduces to a single dense matmul per layer – exactly what existing serving stacks expect – while still enjoying BOFT’s parameter‑efficiency during training.

---

### When might you keep $R$ factored?

* **Multi‑task or continual‑learning scenarios:** If several downstream adapters share the same frozen backbone, storing each task’s compact set of butterflies ($½(b-1)d m$, typically far cheaper than storing several full “merged’’ copies) may be far cheaper overall.
* **Extreme memory constraints:** For very large models where even an additional copy of *all* weights does not fit on device memory, keeping just the base model plus tiny factored adapters can enable on‑device switching between tasks at the price of a few extra FLOPs.
* **Research/diagnostic use:** When probing how different factorisations affect performance it can be convenient to leave them unfused.

In those cases you should expect a modest increase in latency proportional to $m=\log _2 d$, because each input activation passes through $m$ butterfly stages before hitting the usual linear map.



---

### Bottom line

After BOFT finetuning there is **nothing** inherent that forces an extra forward‑pass computation. You can (and usually should) multiply the learned orthogonal matrix into the frozen weights *once* after training, store only the resulting dense matrices, and run exactly as fast as any standard fine‑tuned model.  

Keeping $R$ in its factored form is possible but incurs an additional sequence of sparse operations at every inference step and modestly raises memory consumption – benefits arise only when you deliberately want a very lightweight adapter representation for multi‑task or continual learning settings.

### How does identity initialization ensure zero change in BOFT compared to zero init in LoRA?
Question:
Both LoRA and OFT/BOFT aim for small deviation from pretrained weights at start.

LoRA sets added low‑rank matrices to zero so ${\\bm W}'={\\bm W}^{0}$ initially.
BOFT sets all butterfly components to identity via Cayley’s skew‑symmetric matrix initialized as zero.

My confusion:
1. Since BOFT composes several identity matrices, isn’t ${\\bm R}=I$, thus ${\\bm W}'={\\bm W}^{0}$ trivially—so far so good.
2. But during training gradients flow through products of factors; could early updates break this property more aggressively than LoRA’s additive updates?
3. Is there any theoretical justification that starting from identity yields similar stability as zero init in additive schemes?
Answer:
**Short answer:**  
In BOFT every butterfly component is initialized as an exact identity matrix (the Cayley‑parameterised skew‑symmetric matrix **Q** is set = 0), so the product  

$$
\mathbf{R}= \tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,m)}\,\tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,m-1)}\,\dots\, \tilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,1)}
$$  

starts as $\displaystyle \mathbf{R}= \mathbf I$. Consequently  

$$
\mathbf W'^{\,\top}= (\mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\,\top}= (\mathbf I\,\mathbf W^{0})^{\,\top}= \mathbf{W}^{0\,\top},
$$  

exactly the same “no‑change” condition that LoRA obtains by setting its low‑rank matrices **A**, **B** = 0. The difference lies only in how a deviation from this point is produced during training.



### 1. Why does “$\displaystyle \mathbf R= \mathbf I$” give a trivial start?

Both LoRA and BOFT deliberately begin at the pretrained checkpoint so that any improvement must be earned by learning a *delta* rather than by discarding the pre‑training knowledge.  

* **LoRA** adds a low‑rank perturbation  
  $\displaystyle \Delta\bW=\bA\,\bB^{\,!\!\top}$; with $\bA=\bB=\bfzero$ we have $\Delta\bW=\bfzero$.
* **BOFT** multiplies the frozen weights by an orthogonal matrix  
  $\displaystyle\bR=(\bfI+\bQ)(\bfI-\bQ)^{-1}$; when **Q** = 0 the Cayley map reduces exactly to $\bR=\bfI$.  

Because a product of identities is still the identity, the composed orthogonal matrix does not alter any column of $\bW^{0}$, i.e. $\displaystyle\bW' = \bW^{0}$. This “zero‑change” property follows directly from the algebraic definition—not from an approximation.



### 2. Do gradients through many factors make BOFT more aggressive than LoRA?

At first glance one might worry that back‑propagating through a chain  

$$
L\xrightarrow{\;\partial L/\partial\bz\,}
   \xrightarrow{\;\partial((\,\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)})\,\,\,!\,\!\,\!\,\!\!\!\,\!\,\betriack\rho)\;}
   \xrightarrow{\;\partial L/\partial\tilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)}}
$$  

could amplify changes because each factor multiplies the next.  
However two facts keep the update magnitude under control:

1️⃣ **All factors are identities at iteration 0**.  
   When every $\displaystyle\tilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)}=\bfI$, the Jacobian of the product collapses  

   $$
   \frac{\partial}{\partial\twidetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,i)}}%
        (\prod_{j=1}^{m}\widetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,j)})
   =%
        (\prod_{j<i}\widetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,j)})
        (\prod_{j>i}\widetilde{\bbB}^{b}_{(d,j)})
        =\bfI .
   $$  

   Hence each component receives exactly the same gradient that would be obtained if we updated a single orthogonal matrix **R** directly. There is no multiplicative amplification at the very beginning.

2️⃣ **Linearisation of the Cayley map near the identity**.  
   For a tiny skew‑symmetric perturbation $\displaystyle\bDelta Q$ we have  

   $$
   (\bfI+\Delta Q)(\bfI-\Delta Q)^{-1}
     =(\bfI+\Delta Q)\bigl(\bfI+\Delta Q+O(\|\Delta Q\r|^{2})\bigr)
     =\bfI+2\,\Delta Q+O(\|\Delta Q\bigr|^{2}) .
   $$  

   To first order,  

   $$
   \Delta(\!R)\approx2\,\Delta Q .
   $$  

   Therefore a gradient step on **Q** translates into an *additive* rotation  
   $$
   R\simeq\bI+2\,Q_{\text{new}} .
   $$  
   This mirrors LoRA’s additive update where a step on **A**, **B** produces  
   $\displaystyle\bW'\approx\bW^{0}+A_{\text{new}}B_{\text{new}}^{\,!\!\top}$.  
   The essential point is that *the size of the change is proportional to the Euclidean norm of the underlying parameters*, just as in LoRA, and not multiplied repeatedly by downstream factors.



Consequently, early updates in BOFT are not inherently more aggressive than those in LoRA; both evolve from a zero perturbation with comparable first‑order dynamics provided learning rates are chosen sensibly.



### 3️⃣ Why does starting from the identity give comparable stability?

There are two complementary theoretical lenses that explain why identity initialisation yields stability akin to LoRA’s zero‑initialisation:

#### (a) Manifold–retraction view  

The set of orthogonal matrices $O(d)=\{R\:|\:R^{\,T}R=\bfI{}\}$ is a smooth Riemannian manifold. Optimising on this manifold is usually done by moving along its *tangent space* (the space of skew‑symmetric matrices) and then retracting back onto $O(d)$.  

The Cayley transform used in BOFT,

$$
R(Q)= (\bfI+Q)(\bfI-Q)^{-1},    Q=-Q^{T},
$$  

is precisely such a retraction: it maps a tangent vector $Q\simeq0$ onto a nearby point on $O(d)$. Near $Q=\!0,$  

$$
R(Q)=\bfI+2Q+O(\|Q\bigr|^{2}),    ~~ dR/dQ|_{0}=2\,I .
$$  

Thus taking an Euclidean gradient step on $Q$ corresponds—up to a constant factor—to taking a *geodesic* step on $O(d)$. Standard results from optimisation on manifolds guarantee that—as long as step sizes are moderate—the iterates stay within a neighbourhood where this linear approximation holds, preserving smoothness and avoiding sudden jumps. This property directly mirrors LoRA’s Euclidean updates where staying close to zero ensures small changes in weights.



#### (b) Spectral‑norm preservation  

Multiplying by an orthogonal matrix leaves all singular values unchanged:

$$
\sigma(R\,W^{0})=\sigma(W^{0}) .
$$  

Therefore every intermediate model during BOFT training inherits exactly the same spectral norm as the pretrained model. By contrast, LoRA’s additive perturbation can arbitrarily increase or decrease singular values unless explicit regularisation is applied.

Preserving the spectral norm has two immediate benefits for stability:

* **Gradient magnitudes stay bounded** – large singular values tend to amplify back‑propagated signals; keeping them fixed avoids exploding gradients.
* **Training dynamics remain close to those seen during pre‑training** – this alignment explains why OFT/BOFT often exhibit smoother loss curves compared with unconstrained finetuning.



Together these two perspectives justify why beginning at $R=\mathbb{I}$ provides a mathematically sound “no‑drift” start whose subsequent optimisation behaves like ordinary Euclidean optimisation on unconstrained parameters (**Q**, or **A**, **B** for LoRA). Empirically, Table X in the paper shows that BOFT attains comparable—or better—training stability than LoRA across vision‑transformer, language‑model and diffusion‑model tasks despite using far fewer trainable parameters.



### TL;DR

*Both methods initialise their *perturbation* at zero*: LoRA sets its low‑rank matrices to zero → additively nothing changes; BOFT sets every butterfly component’s skew‑symmetric generator $Q=0$ → via Cayley retraction each component equals $\displaystyle\bI$ → multiplicatively nothing changes.*

*At iteration 0 all factors equal $\displaystyle\bI$, so back‑propagation treats each factor independently and there is no hidden amplification.*

*Close to $\displaystyle\bI$, $R(Q)$ expands linearly ($R\simeq\bI+2Q$), i.e.\ a tiny Euclidean step on $Q$ creates an additively small rotation—exactly parallel to LoRA’s additively small $\Delta W$. The retraction guarantees that these steps stay inside the orthogonal manifold without inflating singular values.*

*Hence identity initialisation gives BOFT precisely the “zero‑change” guarantee required for safe finetuning, and—thanks to manifold geometry and spectral–norm preservation—offers theoretical stability comparable to LoRA’s zero initialisation.*
