Chapter 2: Geometry‑Preserving Updates in Neural Networks  

# Linear layers as sets of neuron vectors  

A fully‑connected linear layer maps an input vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ to an output vector $\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ via a weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$.  In most modern deep‑learning libraries the forward computation is written as  
\[
\mathbf{z}=({\mathbf{W}^{0}})^{\!\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]  
Each column $\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ (for $i=1,\dots,n$) therefore represents the **neuron vector** associated with the $i$‑th output unit.  Geometrically, these vectors live in the same $d$‑dimensional space as the input features and can be viewed as directions along which $\mathbf{x}$ is projected before being summed into an activation.  The collection $\{\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\}_{i=1}^{n}$ thus forms a point cloud on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ that encodes how information is extracted from $\mathbf{x}$ by the pretrained model.  From a geometric perspective, any modification of $\mathbf{W}^{0}$ can be understood as moving these points around or reshaping their configuration.  Hence, when we adapt a pretrained model to a downstream task we would like to keep this configuration **stable**—or at least change it in a controlled way—so that knowledge learned during pretraining does not vanish abruptly.



# Pairwise angular relationships and hyperspherical energy  

The most fundamental geometric invariant among neuron vectors is their **pairwise angle**.  For any two neurons $\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}$ let  

\[
\theta_{ij}
= \arccos\!\bigg(\frac{\langle \mathbf{w}_{i}^{0},\,\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}^{0}\|_2\,\|\mathbf{w}_{j}^{0}\|_2}\bigg)
\]  

denote the angle between them.  The full set of angles $\{\theta_{ij}\}_{i<j}$ captures how “spread out’’ or “clustered’’ the neurons are on a sphere of radius equal to their norms.  Recent work has shown that this spread correlates with generalization ability: when neurons are more uniformly dispersed on a hypersphere—an effect quantified by **hyperspherical energy**—the model tends to be more robust after fine‑tuning~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal}.  In this context, hyperspherical energy is a scalar functional that penalizes configurations with many small angles (i.e., clusters) and rewards those where angles are large and diverse.  

Preserving pairwise angles during adaptation therefore serves two purposes.  First, it guarantees that the relative orientation learned from massive pretraining data remains intact; second, it indirectly keeps hyperspherical energy low, which has been empirically linked to improved stability and generalization on downstream tasks~\cite{qiu2023controlling}.  Consequently, many recent fine‑tuning paradigms have been designed explicitly so that they do **not** perturb these angular relationships.



# Definition and key properties of orthogonal matrices  

A real square matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is called **orthogonal** if  

\[
\mathbf{R}^{\!\top}\,\mathbf{R}= \mathbf{I}_d,
\]  

where $\mathbf I_d$ denotes the identity matrix of size $d$.  This compact condition entails several important geometric consequences:

* **Norm preservation** – For any vector $\mathbf v$, $\|\mathbf R \mathbf v\|_{2}= \|\mathbf v\|_{2}$.  Hence scaling factors are never introduced.
* **Inner‑product preservation** – For any pair $(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)$,
  \[
  \langle \mathbf R \mathbf u,\;\mathbf R \mathbf v\rangle
   = \langle \mathbf u,\;\mathbf v\rangle .
  \]  
  Because inner products determine cosines of angles, every angle between any two vectors stays unchanged after left‑multiplication by an orthogonal matrix.
* **Determinant magnitude one** – $\big|\det(\mathbf R)\big|=1$, guaranteeing that orientation is either preserved ($+1$) or reflected ($-1$) without volumetric expansion or contraction.
* **Invertibility via transpose** – The inverse of an orthogonal matrix coincides with its transpose: $\mathbf R^{-1}= \mathbf R^{\!\top}$. 

These properties imply that an orthogonal transformation rotates—or possibly mirrors—the space without distorting distances or angles.  When such a transformation is applied uniformly to all neuron vectors in a linear layer (e.g., $\widetilde{\mathbf W}= \mathbf R\,\mathbf W^{0}$), every pairwise angle $\theta_{ij}$ is exactly preserved:

\[
\theta_{ij}
= \arccos
      \frac{\langle \widetilde{\!\!\;\!\!{\bf w}}_i , 
                 \widetilde{\!\!\;\!\!{\bf w}}_j\rangle}
           {\|\widetilde{\!\!\;\!\!{\bf w}}_i 
            \|_2\, 
            \|\widetilde{\!\!\;\!\!{\bf w}}_j
            \|_2}}
=
    \arccos
      \frac{\langle {\bf w}_i , 
                 {\bf w}_j\rangle}
           {\|{\bf w}_i 
            \|_2\, 
            \|{\bf w}_j
            \|_2}},
\]
where ${\bf w}_i={(\bf W^{0})}_{:,i}$.  Therefore an orthogonal matrix offers precisely the geometric guarantee needed for *geometry‑preserving* finetuning.



Because directly optimizing over all orthogonal matrices would involve handling a manifold constraint (\refeqn{$\bf R^{T} R = I$}), most practical implementations employ **Cayley parameterization**~\cite{liao2022cayley}:

\[
    \boxed{
    {\bf R}= (\bf I + {\bf Q})\,(\bf I - {\bf Q})^{-1}},
    \qquad
    {\bf Q}=-{\bf Q}^{\,\!\top}.
\]

Here ${\bf Q}$ is a *skew‑symmetric* matrix whose entries are unrestricted real numbers; any such ${\bf Q}$ yields an orthogonal ${\bf R}$ (provided ${\bf I}-{\bf Q}$ remains invertible).  During training one back‑propagates through ${\bf Q}$, thereby automatically enforcing orthogonality without requiring explicit projection steps.



# Multiplicative versus additive updates under geometric constraints  

Fine‑tuning methods differ mainly in how they modify the original weights $\mathbf W^{0}$. Two broad families can be distinguished:

### Additive updates  

The most widely adopted paradigm adds a *correction* term,
  
\[
    \widetilde{\bf W}= {\bf W}^{0}+{\Delta},
    \qquad
    {\Delta}= {\bf A}{\bf B}^{\,\!\top},
\]
where ${\bf A},{\bf B}\in\mathbb {R}^{d\times r }$ are low‑rank factors ($r< d$).  
This scheme underlies approaches such as LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}. Because ${~}{+\Delta}{~}$ impacts each column independently and does not enforce any global relationship among columns, there is no guarantee that pairwise angles will stay constant after adaptation; indeed one may observe substantial distortion of both norms and inner products.  In practice additive updates are cheap—few parameters suffice—but they trade away geometric fidelity for flexibility.

In practice one may impose additional sparsity patterns on ${R}$—for instance block–diagonal structures used in early OFT~[qiu2023controlling] or butterfly factorizations introduced later—to reduce trainable parameters without breaking orthogonality.


### Trade‑offs  

Additive methods manipulate weight space freely at low cost but risk breaking angelic structure and inflating spectral norm—both phenomena have been linked empirically to unstable training when adapting large foundation models~[qiu2023controlling]. Multiplicative methods preserve geometry almost by construction yet originally required storing dense orthogonal matrices ($O(d^2)$ parameters). By factorizing ${R}$ into sparse components (block–diagonal or butterfly), recent works achieve *parameter efficiency* while retaining multiplicative benefits.


# Prior work leveraging geometry in fine‑tuning  

The idea that *geometry matters* for fine‑tuning has surfaced repeatedly across recent literature:

* **Small learning rates** – Classical finetuning often adopts tiny learning rates precisely because they limit how far parameters move from their pretrained values~[raffel2020exploring]. Although heuristic, this practice implicitly aims at preserving geometric relations encoded in weights.
* **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** – Proposed by Qiu et al.~[qiu2023controlling], OFT introduces an explicit *orthogonality constraint*: each layer’s weights are updated via left multiplication by an learnable orthogonal matrix ${R}$. By virtue of Lemma 1 (the inner product preservation property), OFT guarantees exact preservation of every pairwise angle among neuron vectors.
* **Block–diagonal OFT** – To reduce storage cost one may restrict ${R}$ to block‑diagonal form (`diag(R₁,…,Rᵣ)` where each block $R_i$ acts on a small subspace). This yields far fewer trainable parameters ($O(b d)$ for block size $b$) at the expense of imposing an artificial grouping on dimensions—a sparsity pattern whose inductive bias may limit expressiveness.
* **Butterfly‑based Orthogonal Finetuning (BOFT)** – Building upon OFT’s geometric argumentation, BOFT replaces block diagonal sparsity with *butterfly factorization*. Inspired by fast Fourier transform graphs~[cooley1965algorithm], BOFT constructs ${R}$ as a product of $\log d$ sparse orthogonal components each having only $O(d)$ non­zero entries. This achieves parameter complexity $O(d\,log d)$ while still guaranteeing dense connectivity among all rows/columns—a much richer hypothesis space than block diagonal OFT.
* **Low‑rank additive schemes (e.g., LoRA)** – Although not explicitly framed around geometry preservation, rank-constrained additive methods implicitly alter angular relations because they introduce new directions into weight space without enforcing inner–product constancy.
* **Prompt tuning & adapters** – These families augment pretrained models rather than changing core matrices directly; consequently they largely bypass any direct alteration of neuron geometry inside existing layers.

All these approaches share a common motivation: keep *as much useful structure from pretraining as possible* while still providing enough flexibility for downstream specialization. Orthogonality offers one mathematically clean way to realize this goal—by rotating rather than reshaping—and recent advances such as BOFT demonstrate how careful structural design can reconcile geometry preservation with strict parameter budgets.


---  

In summary, treating each linear layer as a cloud of neuron vectors makes clear why their relative orientations matter for downstream performance. Pairwise angles encode essential relational knowledge; maintaining them helps keep hyperspherical energy low and stabilizes training. Orthogonal matrices naturally enforce angle preservation through norm- and inner‐product invariance; consequently multiplying pretrained weights by an orthogonal transform constitutes a *geometry‑preserving* update. Compared with additive schemes—which risk distorting these invariants—the multiplicative protocol enjoys strong theoretical guarantees at modest computational overhead when sparsity‐aware factorizations like block diagonal or butterfly structures are employed. The evolution from naïve OFT toward BOFT exemplifies how modern research leverages these geometric insights to devise parameter‑efficient finetuning strategies suitable for today’s ever larger foundation models.
