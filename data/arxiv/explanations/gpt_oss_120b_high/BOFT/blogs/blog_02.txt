\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# When Flexibility Meets Cost: The Parameter Bloat Problem in OFT  

*Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) promises geometry‑preserving adaptation of huge foundation models, but its original formulation carries a hidden price tag – millions of extra trainable weights. In this post we unpack why the full‑dimensional orthogonal matrix blows up the parameter budget and why the naïve block‑diagonal fix trades off expressiveness for savings.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why Orthogonal Finetuning Looks So Attractive  

Modern vision transformers, language models, or diffusion backbones share a common pattern: each linear layer can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) is **frozen** after pre‑training and \(\mathbf{x}\) is the layer input.  
OFT proposes to modify the forward pass by inserting a learnable **orthogonal matrix** \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) on the left:

\[
\mathbf{z}= (\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{0})^{\top}\mathbf{x}.
\]

Because \(\mathbf{R}\) satisfies \(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}_{d}\), *pairwise angles between neuron weight vectors are preserved*. In other words, the geometric knowledge baked in \(\mathbf{W}^{0}\) stays intact while the model still enjoys a flexible linear transformation.

Mathematically this is neat, but it comes with a concrete engineering cost: **how many parameters does \(\mathbf{R}\) contain?**  

---

## 2️⃣ The Full‑Dimensional Orthogonal Matrix = \(\mathcal{O}(d^{2})\) Parameters  

An arbitrary orthogonal matrix has \(d^{2}\) entries, but its degrees of freedom are only \(d(d-1)/2\). In practice we store it implicitly (e.g., via a skew‑symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\) in the Cayley transform \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\)), still requiring **\(d^{2}\)** scalars to be learned and updated during finetuning.

Consider a typical transformer feed‑forward layer with hidden size \(d=4096\). A full orthogonal \(\mathbf{R}\) would need roughly

\[
4096^{2} \approx 1.68\times10^{7}
\]

trainable numbers – *17 million* extra parameters for just one layer!  
When you stack dozens of such layers (as in GPT‑style language models or large vision transformers), the total **parameter bloat** easily reaches hundreds of millions, dwarfing classic PEFT methods like LoRA that add only a few thousand per layer.

Such a blow‑up hurts:

* **Memory** – each gradient step must store the full dense \(\mathbf{R}\), increasing GPU RAM usage.
* **Compute** – multiplying a dense \(d\times d\) matrix per forward pass adds an \(O(d^{2})\) overhead.
* **Optimization stability** – more degrees of freedom can make training noisier unless carefully regularized.

---

## 3️⃣ Block‑Diagonal — A Shortcut That Cuts Parameters

To tame the memory explosion, the original OFT paper introduced a **block‑diagonal** structure:

\[
\mathbf{R}= \operatorname*{diag}(\underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{1}}_{b\times b},
                                 \underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{2}}_{b\times b},
                                 \dots,
                                 \underbrace{\mathbf{R}_{r}}_{b\times b}), 
\qquad br = d .
\]

Each block \(\mathbf{R}_{i}\) remains orthogonal, but now only \(b\,d\) trainable scalars are needed (actually \((b-1)\frac{b}{2}r = \frac12(b-1)d\)). For the same \(d=4096\), picking \(b=64\) reduces parameters from ~17 M down to roughly

\[
64\times 4096 = 262\,144,
\]

a **~65× reduction**!

The savings are compelling—this “sparsity pattern” lets OFT compete with LoRA on memory while still employing an orthogonal transform.

---

## 4️⃣ What We Lose When We Go Block‑Diagonal  

The elegance of the full orthogonal group lies in its *unconstrained mixing* of all input dimensions. The block‑diagonal approximation imposes two major restrictions:

### 4.1 No Cross‑Block Interaction  

Each block only sees its own slice of the activation vector \(\mathbf{x}\). If we split a 4096‑dimensional vector into 64 blocks of size 64, dimension 5 in block 1 never talks to dimension 200 in block 3 during the orthogonal transformation. Consequently:

* Information that could have been re‑distributed across the whole feature space stays trapped within its block.
* Certain useful linear transforms—e.g., discrete Fourier transform (DFT), Hadamard transform, or any operation requiring global mixing—cannot be realized with a purely block‑diagonal orthogonal matrix.

### 4.2 Artificial Grouping By Index  

The division into blocks is driven purely by index order (`[1:b], [b+1:2b], …`). There’s no guarantee that neurons sharing an index segment have any semantic relationship after pretraining. By enforcing independent rotations per block we impose an *inductive bias* that may clash with the learned representation geometry.

### 4.3 Reduced Expressivity → Potentially Weaker Adaptation  

From a hypothesis‑space viewpoint, full OFT can explore any point on the orthogonal group \(\mathcal{O}(d)\). A block‑diagonal restriction restricts us to the subgroup

\[
\mathcal{O}(b)\times \mathcal{O}(b)\times\dots\times \mathcal{-O}(b),
\]

a dramatically smaller set when \(b \ll d\). Empirically this translates into:

* Fewer degrees of freedom to align pretrained features with downstream task specifics.
* Higher risk that some tasks simply cannot be represented well within this limited subspace.

The original paper notes precisely this trade‑off: *“the block-diagonal sparsity pattern may introduce undesirable inductive biases … and cannot approximate classic linear transforms.”*

---

## 5️⃣ A Quick Numerical Illustration  

| Layer size \(d\) | Full OFT params (\(=\mathcal O(d^{2})\)) | Block size \(b\) | Block‑diag params (\(\approx b d/2\)) | Parameter reduction |
|------------------|----------------------------------------|-------------------|--------------------------------------|---------------------|
| 1024             | ~1 M                                   | 32                | ~16 K                                | ~62×                |
| 2048             | ~4 M                                   | 64                | ~65 K                                | ~62×                |
| 4096             | ~17 M                                  | 128               | ~262 K                               | ~65×                |

Even when we choose relatively large blocks (e.g., \(b=128\)), we still shave off more than **two orders of magnitude**. The price is exactly what we described above: each row/column can only interact inside its own block.

---

## 6️⃣ Why This Matters – Setting Up for Better Solutions  

Understanding where OFT’s parameter bloat originates helps us see why later works propose *structured factorizations* (butterfly matrices, low‑rank updates, etc.) instead of simple sparsity patterns:

* **Goal:** retain dense connectivity (every source can reach every destination) while keeping trainable parameters near \(O(d\,\log d)\).
* **Approach:** factorize a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal factors—each factor cheap to store but together capable of global mixing.
* **Result:** recover much of full OFT’s flexibility without paying the quadratic cost; at the same time avoid block‑diagonal’s expressivity ceiling.

These ideas culminate in BOFT (Orthogonal Butterfly), which builds exactly such factorizations using butterfly graphs from fast Fourier transforms. But before diving into BOFT we needed to clearly diagnose *the problem*: full‑dimensional orthogonal matrices are too heavy and naïve sparsity kills too much expressivity. That diagnosis is what we’ve walked through here.

---

## 7️⃣ Takeaways  

1. **Full OFT’s strength—geometric preservation—comes from using an unrestricted \(d\times d\) orthogonal matrix**, whose storage scales quadratically with hidden dimension.
2. **A dense orthogonal matrix quickly becomes impractical** for modern backbones where \(d=1024–4096\): millions of extra learnable parameters strain memory and compute budgets.
3. **Block‑diagonal OFT chops parameters down** dramatically by restricting transformations to independent subspaces defined by index-based blocks.
4. **Such restriction sacrifices global mixing**, hindering approximation of classic linear transforms and potentially limiting downstream performance because neurons can no longer communicate across blocks.
5. The tension between *flexibility* (full connectivity) and *cost* (parameter budget) fuels research into smarter factorizations—an avenue explored by BOFT and related methods.

Understanding this trade‑off equips practitioners with a clear mental model when they decide whether plain OFT fits their hardware constraints or whether they should reach for more sophisticated, butterfly‐based schemes that promise “the best of both worlds.”
