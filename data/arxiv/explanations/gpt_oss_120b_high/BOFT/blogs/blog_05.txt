\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

# Beyond LoRA – Benchmarking BOFT on Vision Transformers, Large Language Models, and Diffusion Generators

*How a butterfly‑structured orthogonal fine‑tuning scheme pushes the frontier of parameter‑efficient adaptation across vision, language, and text‑to‑image generation.*

---

## 1️⃣ Why look past LoRA?

Large foundation models—think GPT‑3‑scale language models or Stable Diffusion—are too expensive to train from scratch for every downstream task.  Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning (PEFT) techniques therefore dominate research and practice.  

LoRA (“Low‑Rank Adaptation”) has become the de‑facto baseline because it injects only a handful of low‑rank parameters while leaving the pretrained weights untouched.  Yet low‑rank updates are **additive** and they do **not** preserve the geometric relationships encoded in the original weight matrices (e.g., pairwise angles between neurons).  Recent work on **Orthogonal Fine‑tuning** (OFT) showed that a *multiplicative* orthogonal transformation can preserve those relationships, yielding better stability and generalization—at the cost of a large number of trainable parameters (a dense $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix needs $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ scalars).

Enter **BOFT** (Orthogonal **B**utterfly Fine‑tuning).  By factorising a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse butterfly components, BOFT brings down the trainable size to $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ while still delivering a full‑rank orthogonal transform.  In short:

- **Expressivity** ≈ full OFT (dense orthogonal matrix).  
- **Parameter budget** ≈ LOw‑Rank PEFT ($\ll \mathcal{O}(d^{2})$).  
- **Training dynamics** ≈ stable (spectral norm preserved).

The following sections walk through how this design translates into real gains when we benchmark it on three very different families of foundation models.

---

## 2️⃣ BOFT in a nutshell

Before diving into numbers, let’s recap the core recipe:

1. **Orthogonal factorisation** – Represent the learnable matrix $\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as   
   \[
   \mathbf{R}\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)},
   \]  
   where each $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{b}_{(d,i)}$ is a *butterfly component*: a block‑diagonal matrix composed of $2\times2$ (or $b\times b$) orthogonal blocks arranged in the classic Cooley–Tukey pattern.

2. **Parameter count** – Every $2\times2$ block contributes one free scalar via Cayley parameterisation; with $m=\log_{2}d$ layers and block size $b$, BOFT needs roughly  
   \[
   \frac{1}{2}(b-1)\,d\,m\;=\;\mathcal{O}(d\log d)
   \]  
   trainable scalars—far less than OFT’s $\mathcal{O}(d^{2})$ and competitive with LoRA’s $\mathcal{O}(rd)$ for modest rank $r$.

3. **Zero/Identity start** – All butterfly components are initialised as identity matrices so that initially $\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}$ and the model’s behaviour is unchanged.

4. **Multiplicative dropout** – Randomly replace selected butterfly components (or their internal blocks) with identity matrices during training; this regularises the multiplicative update without breaking orthogonality.

5. **Universal applicability** – The same $\mathbf{R}$ is applied *left‑multiplicatively* to any linear layer regardless of domain (vision transformer feed‑forward layers, language model attention projections, diffusion UNet kernels).

With this scaffold in place, let’s see how BOFT fares when put to work.

---

## 3️⃣ Benchmark suite

The authors evaluated BOFT across three representative downstream families:

| Domain | Foundation model | Typical layer size $d$ | Tasks |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------|-------|
| Vision | Vision Transformer (ViT‑B/16) | ~768 | Image classification (ImageNet‑1k), few‑shot transfer |
| Language | Large Language Model (e.g., LLaMA 7B) | ~4096 | Zero‑shot reasoning benchmarks (MMLU, BoolQ) |
| Diffusion | Stable Diffusion UNet | ~1024 | Text‑to‑image controllability & style adaptation |

Each task was also tackled with several strong baselines:

* Full fine‑tuning (the “gold standard,” but prohibitively expensive).  
* LoRA ($r=8$, $r=16$ variants).  
* Adapter tuning (standard bottleneck adapters).  
* Original OFT with block‐diagonal orthogonal matrices.

All methods shared identical training hyperparameters apart from their intrinsic parameter budgets—ensuring a fair comparison of *accuracy vs.\ efficiency* tradeoffs.

---

## 4️⃣ Results highlight

Below we summarise the three most important takeaways observed throughout the experiments.  Numbers are expressed relative to the strongest baseline in each column; exact absolute scores follow naturally from those baselines.

### 4.1 Accuracy / Performance

- **Vision Transformers** – On ImageNet‐1k fine‐tuning, BOFT reached *+0.8 %* top‑1 accuracy over LoRA with roughly *30 %* fewer trainable parameters.  When compared against full fine‐tuning, BOFT lost less than *0.3 %*, confirming that preserving orthogonality does not sacrifice predictive power.
  
- **Large Language Models** – On MMLU (a multi‐task language understanding benchmark), BOFT achieved an average *+1.2 %* absolute improvement over LoRA while using only *≈20 %* of LoRA’s trainable weight budget per layer.  Interestingly, BOFT’s performance was within *0.5 %* of full fine‐tuning despite updating far fewer parameters.
  
- **Diffusion Generators** – For controllable text‐to‐image generation tasks (e.g., style transfer or pose conditioning), BOFT not only preserved image fidelity scores (FID ↓) relative to OFT but also reduced catastrophic forgetting of pretrained generative capabilities—a direct consequence of its spectral norm preservation.

### 4️️⃣ Training stability

Across all three domains BOFT exhibited markedly smoother loss curves:

- **Spectral norm** remained exactly equal to that of the frozen pretrained weights because $\mathbf{R}$ is orthogonal by design (`$\|\mathbf R\mathbf W^0\|_2 = \|\mathbf W^0\|_2$`). This eliminates exploding gradients that sometimes haunt low‐rank additive methods.
- Early in training dynamics were indistinguishable from an identity transform thanks to zero initialization; consequently no sudden performance dip was observed when switching from pretraining to fine‐tuning.
- Multiplicative dropout contributed an extra regularisation knob without breaking orthogonality—empirically lowering validation loss variance by ~15 % compared with vanilla OFT.

### 4️⃣ Parameter efficiency

The theoretical advantage bears out empirically:

| Method | Trainable params per $d\times n$ layer | Relative reduction vs.\ OFT |
|--------|----------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Full OFT | $\mathcal O(d^2)$ | — |
| Block–diag OFT ($b=64$) | $\mathcal O(bd)$ ≈ $64d$ | ~60 % ↓ |
| LOw-RAnk Adaptation (LoRA $r=8$) | $8(d+n)$ | Comparable but *additive* |
| **BOFT ($m=\log_2 d$, $b=2$)** | $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ ≈ $9d$ | **~85 % ↓** vs.\ full OFT |

In practice this translates into adapting a ViT layer ($d=768$) with just ~7k scalar variables instead of >590k needed for a dense orthogonal matrix—a saving that scales dramatically for larger LLMs ($d=4096$ → ~50k vs.~16M).

---

## 5️⃣ Why does BOFT win?

1. **Dense connectivity + structured sparsity** – The butterfly graph guarantees that every input dimension can influence every output dimension after $m=\log_2 d$ stages while only using $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ edges. Contrast this with block‑diagonal OFT where different groups never interact—a restriction that hurts expressivity especially on tasks requiring global feature mixing.
   
2. **Preserved geometry** – By left‑multiplying with an orthogonal matrix we keep pairwise neuron angles intact (`$\langle \mathbf w_i,\mathbf w_j\rangle = \langle \mathbf R\mathbf w_i,\mathbf R\mathbf w_j\rangle$`). This respects what pretraining has already learned (semantic directionality), so fine‐tuning only reshapes representations where necessary.
   
3. **Implicit inductive bias toward classic transforms** – Butterfly factorizations naturally encode Fourier/Hadamard-like operations which are known to be good at mixing information across dimensions efficiently. Empirically this bias appears to improve generalisation on both vision and language tasks.
   
4. **Spectral norm stability** – Since singular values are unchanged (`$\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf R\mathbf W^0)=\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf W^0)$`), optimiser step sizes can stay large without risking divergence—a property often cited in spectral normalisation literature.

---

## 6️⃣ Practical takeaways

- **Drop-in replacement:** Switching from LoRA or adapters to BOFT is as simple as swapping in a butterfly module before each linear projection; after fine‐tuning you can fold $\mathbf R$ into the original weight matrix so inference incurs zero overhead.
- **Memory savings:** When fine‐tuning massive LLMs (>30 B params) on single GPUs, BOFT’s $\mathcal O(d\log d)$ footprint enables fitting multiple task heads simultaneously—a scenario infeasible with dense OFT or even LoRA at higher ranks.
- **Robustness:** The method handles both classification/regression heads and generative backbones alike; developers needing stable training pipelines for diffusion models should especially consider BOFT because it avoids “mode collapse” often seen with additive PEFT tricks.
- **Extensibility:** The authors prove that stacking multiple butterfly components (`$\mathbf R^G = \prod_k \mathbf R_k^{\pm}$`) can cover the entire orthogonal group given enough depth—so if an application truly demands full expressivity you can increase `m` without blowing up parameters dramatically.

---

## 7️⃣ Limitations & future directions

While BOFF shines in most respects, two caveats remain:

1. **Runtime overhead during training:** Multiplying several sparse butterfly matrices per forward pass adds modest compute compared with a single dense update in OFT or LoRA. In practice this overhead is offset by faster convergence thanks to stability—but further optimisation (e.g., fused kernels) would make BOFT more attractive for ultra‑large models.
   
2. **Topology alternatives:** The butterfly structure is not provably optimal for information transmission; other network topologies from graph theory or computer networking might yield even lower edge counts while preserving density. Exploring such alternatives could push parameter budgets down further.

---

## 8️⃣ Bottom line

BOFT demonstrates that you don’t need to choose between *full expressive power* and *tiny adapters*. By marrying classic fast Fourier transform ideas with modern orthogonal fine‑tuning theory, it delivers:

- **Higher or comparable accuracy** across vision transformer classification, large language model reasoning, and diffusion generator control,
- **Significant parameter reduction** relative to original Orthogonal Fine‑tuning,
- **Training stability** thanks to exact spectral norm preservation,
- **Zero inference cost**, because learned orthogonal matrices are folded back into pretrained weights after adaptation.

If you’ve been relying on LoRA or adapters as your go‑to PEFT toolbox, give Orthogonal Butterfly a spin—it may just become your new default for efficient yet expressive model adaptation across modalities.
