\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### Why do scaling‑law plots show xLSTM having a lower offset than Transformers despite linear time complexity?
Question:
Figure 5 suggests xLSTM models start at a lower perplexity baseline than comparable Transformers when plotted against model size.

* Is this offset mainly due to better utilization of parameters (e.g., richer memory), or because of architectural differences such as residual stacking?
* How much does constant‑memory complexity contribute indirectly through larger feasible batch sizes during training?
* Could future optimizations narrow this gap further?
Answer:
The lower‐perplexity “offset’’ that you see for xLSTM in Figure 5 is not a mysterious artefact of fitting procedure – it reflects real differences in how efficiently two families turn parameters into useful representations.​  

In a scaling‑law fit **the validation loss is usually modelled as**

$$
\mathcal{L}(N)=a\,N^{-c}+b,
$$

where $N$ is number of trainable parameters, $c$ is *slope* capturing how quickly performance improves with size,
and $b$ is *offset* telling you how far given architecture lies from ideal loss even if made arbitrarily large.​  
The experiments in §5.2 (15 B‑token training) and §5.4 (300 B‑token training)
use exactly same pre­LayerNorm residual backbone for every method,
so **the only systematic architectural differences able to move**
$b$
are those introduced by X­ LSTMs themselves.​

### Why does X­ LSTM have a lower $b$

The paper isolates two sources of gain in Table 9 (ablation):
exponential gating **and** matrix–memory of m­ LSTMs.​  
Exponential gates allow an input or forget gate value far above 1,
dramatically enlarging dynamic range of additive updates referenced 
in Eq.~(2).
This lets very few parameters store information over many steps without saturating –
a form ­of “parameter­efficient memory”.  
Matrix–memory replaces scalar cell state $c_{t}$  
by **a full**
$d\times d$
associative matrix $\mathbf{C}_{t}$.  
Retrieval occurs by multiplying $\mathbf{C}_{t}$ with query vector
(Eq.~(7)),
giving every parameter an extra degree­of­freedom   
for encoding key–value pairs.​   
In practice this means an X­ LSTM with say **350 M**
parameters already reaches storage capacity 
$\displaystyle{\mathcal O(d^{2})}$,
whereas Transformers require ${}\displaystyle{\mathcal O(d)}$
hidden dimensions plus many attention heads
to achieve comparable expressivity.​    
Consequently each parameter contributes more toward reducing prediction error,
moving whole curve downward – i.e.\ lowering $b$.​

Residual stacking helps only insofar as it makes families comparable –
all models share same pre­LayerNorm residual blocks (§3.4).
Ablations show swapping one LST M layer 
for X­ L STM block while keeping stack unchanged drops baseline loss
by roughly 0·2––0·3 ppl on Wikitext­103 scale tasks (Table 9).
Hence offset isn’t artefact fo deeper/shallower stacks,
but stems from richer internal dynamics.​
