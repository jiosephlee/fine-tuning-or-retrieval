xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory – Introduction & Motivation  

# Historical Timeline of Sequence Modeling Architectures  

The study of sequential data has progressed through several distinct generations of neural architectures. In the early 1990s researchers identified that standard recurrent neural networks (RNNs) suffered from vanishing gradients when back‑propagated through many time steps \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:00book}. To address this fundamental difficulty, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber introduced two complementary ideas that became the cornerstone of the Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) network \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:97nips,Hochreiter:97}. The first idea was the *constant error carousel*, an additive recurrence for a cell state $c_t$ that permits gradients to flow unchanged across long time horizons:

\[
c_t \;=\; f_t \, c_{t-1} \;+\; i_t \, z_t ,
\qquad
h_t \;=\; o_t \,\psi(c_t) .
\tag{1}
\]

Here $i_t$, $f_t$, and $o_t$ are sigmoid gates controlling write, forget, and read operations respectively, $z_t$ is the candidate cell input, and $\psi$ is a squashing function such as $\tanh$. This formulation dramatically reduced the depth‑related degradation that plagued earlier RNNs and opened a practical path toward learning long‑range dependencies.

For more than two decades LSTMs dominated sequence modelling research because they could be trained reliably on a wide variety of tasks. In 2017 a fundamentally different architecture was proposed—the Transformer \citep{Vaswani:17}. Instead of recurrent connections it employed *self‑attention*, an operation that can be evaluated in parallel across all positions in a sequence. The Transformer’s combination of high computational throughput and powerful context integration quickly made it the default choice for large‑scale language modelling.  

# Key Successes of Classical LSTMs Across Domains  

Even after the emergence of Transformers, LSTMs continued to deliver state‑of‑the‑art results on many benchmark problems. In natural language processing they were used for text generation \citep{Graves:13,Karpathy:15blog}, machine translation \citep{Sutskever:14nips}, image captioning \citep{Karpathy:15,Hossain:19}, program evaluation \citep{Zaremba:14arxiv}, and source‑code synthesis \citep{Karpathy:15blog}. Outside pure NLP they have been applied to scientific domains such as hydrological forecasting \citep{Kratzert:18,Kratzert:19,Nearing:24} and reinforcement learning agents that excel at complex games—for example AlphaStar for StarCraft II \citep{Vinyals:17short} and OpenAI Five for Dota 2 \citep{OpenAI:19}. Across these tasks an important qualitative observation emerged: LSTMs were able to *abstract* semantic information into distributed cell states—often termed “semantic neurons” or “sentiment neurons” – a phenomenon documented in analyses of trained models \citep{Karpathy:15blog,Lakretz:19,Bau:19,Radford:17arxiv}. These successes cemented LSTMs as versatile sequence learners capable of both short‑term processing and long‑range abstraction.  

# Rise of Transformer Models and Their Advantages  

The Transformer’s self‑attention mechanism replaced recurrent updates with direct pairwise interactions between tokens:

\[
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)\;=\;\operatorname{softmax}\!\bigl(\tfrac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\bigr)V .
\]

Because each token’s representation can be computed from all others simultaneously, training time scales *linearly* with respect to model size but *quadratically* with respect to sequence length—a trade‑off that proved acceptable when massive parallel hardware was available. Moreover, attention layers can be stacked deeply while preserving stable gradients thanks to layer normalisation \citep{Ba:16b}, enabling language models with billions of parameters such as GPT‑3 \citep{Brown:20short} or PaLM \citep{Chowdhery:22}. The resultant models set new records on virtually every benchmark used in natural language processing. Consequently many recent research efforts shifted focus away from recurrence toward pure attention architectures.  

# Limitations Prompting a Re‑Examination of Recurrent Models  

Despite their historic importance, classic LSTMs exhibit three concrete shortcomings that become increasingly pronounced at large scale:

1. **Inability to Revise Storage Decisions** –  
   When an input sequence encodes a “nearest neighbor search” problem (i.e., scanning until finding the most similar vector), an LSTM must decide at each step whether to store the current candidate or retain an older one. Experiments show that standard sigmoid gates cannot effectively overwrite a previously stored value once its forgetting factor has already diminished it. This rigidity leads to high mean squared error on such tasks (see Figure 1 left panel).  

2. **Limited Storage Capacity** –  
   Traditional cells keep only a *scalar* $c_t$ per hidden dimension. Consequently information must be heavily compressed before being written into memory. On language modelling benchmarks like Wikitext‑103 this manifests as poorer perplexity on rare tokens—words that appear infrequently cannot be represented accurately in low‑dimensional scalars (Figure 1 right panel).  

3. **Lack of Parallelizability** –  
   The hidden‐to‐hidden recurrence embodied by $h_{t-1}$ feeding forward into $z_t$, $i_t$, $f_t$, $o_t$ forces strict sequential evaluation. Unlike attention’s data‑parallel operations, this serial dependency hinders exploitation of modern GPU pipelines for very long contexts.

These issues motivated a systematic re‑examination of recurrent models under contemporary scaling regimes—specifically asking whether modern engineering tricks could alleviate these bottlenecks while retaining recurrence’s intrinsic benefits for state tracking and abstraction.

# Research Question – Performance Limits When Scaling LSTMs  

The central inquiry posed by the authors is:

> *If we scale Long Short‑Term Memory networks to billions of parameters while integrating recent advances from large language model training—yet explicitly address their known limitations—how far can we push language modelling performance?*  

In other words, do the architectural constraints intrinsic to classic LSTMs fundamentally bound their scalability, or can revised gating mechanisms and richer memories close the gap with Transformers and State‑Space Models? This question frames all subsequent contributions in the paper.

# High‑Level Overview of xLSTM Innovations  

To answer the above question the authors introduce **Extended Long Short‑Term Memory (xLSTM)**—a family comprising two novel recurrent cells together with a residual block backbone:

## Exponential Gating with Normalisation and Stabilisation  
Traditional gates use sigmoid activations bounded between 0 and 1. xLSTM replaces—or augments—the input gate $i_t$ (and optionally the forget gate $f_t$) with an *exponential* activation $\exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ (or $\exp(\tilde{f}_t)$). This equips the network with multiplicative dynamics capable of *amplifying* storage decisions when needed—enabling rapid revision of previously written values during tasks such as nearest‑neighbor search.

Because exponentials can explode numerically, xLSTM introduces two protective mechanisms:
* A **normalizer state** $n_t$ that aggregates future forget contributions,
* A **stabiliser state** $m_t = \max (\log f_t + m_{t-1},\, \log i_t)$ which rescales raw exponentials back into a numerically safe range without altering downstream gradients (see Equations (2)–(4)).  

These additions preserve training stability while granting gates additional expressive power.

## New Memory Structures  

### sLSTM – Scalar Memory With Mixing  
The first variant retains a single scalar cell per hidden dimension exactly as Equation (1) describes but augments it with its own normalizer:

\[
c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t , 
\qquad 
n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t .
\]

The hidden output is now normalised by dividing by $n_t$, i.e., $\tilde h_t = c_t / n_t$. Crucially sLSTM permits **memory mixing** via recurrent weight matrices $\mathbf R_{\mathbf z}, \mathbf R_{\mathbf i}, \mathbf R_{\mathbf f}, \mathbf R_{\mathbf o}$ that connect hidden states across cells within each “head”. This mixing enables sophisticated state tracking akin to classic multi‑cell LSTMs but now enhanced by exponential gating.

### mLSTM – Matrix Memory With Covariance Update  
The second variant lifts the cell from scalar values to a full matrix $\mathbf C \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}$:

\[
\mathbf C_{t} = f_{t}\,\mathbf C_{t-1} + i_{t}\,\mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{\!\top},
\qquad 
\mathbf n_{t}=f_{t}\,\mathbf n_{t-1}+ i_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}.
\]

Here $\mathbf k_{t}$ (“key”) and $\mathbf v_{t}$ (“value”) are linear projections of the input $\mathbf x_{t}$ analogous to Transformer terminology. The outer product $\mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{\!\top}$ implements the classic **covariance update rule** used in Bidirectional Associative Memories \citep{Kohonen:72,...}. In this formulation:
* The forget gate plays the role similar to a decay rate,
* The input gate acts like a learning rate,
* The output gate scales readout vectors retrieved via $\tilde{\mathbf h}_t = (\mathbf C_{t}\,\mathbf q_{t}) / \max(|\, \mathbf n_{t}^{\!\top}\! \mathbf q_{t}\!| ,\,1)$ where $\mathbf q_{t}$ is another linear projection (“query”).

Because every step’s update is expressed solely through matrix multiplications without hidden–to–hidden recurrences, **mLSTM admits full parallelisation** akin to FlashAttention or Gated Linear Attention methods—allowing simultaneous processing across time steps after suitable unrolling tricks described in Appendix C.

Both variants inherit exponential gating so they share improved revision ability while offering complementary trade­offs:
* sLSTM retains recurrent mixing capability but is not fully parallelisable.
* mLSTM sacrifices mixing for matrix capacity and massive parallelism.

## Integration Into Residual Blocks → xLSTM Architecture  
Each cell type can be embedded inside one of two residual block designs:

* **Post up‑projection block** – typical for sLSTM where hidden representations are first computed in native dimensionality then linearly expanded before nonlinearity.
* **Pre up‑projection block** – suitable for mLSTM because its matrix memory benefits from operating directly in high dimensional space before contraction.

Stacking these blocks under pre–LayerNorm residual backbones yields deep **xLSTM architectures**, directly comparable in depth/parameter budget to current large language models while preserving linear time complexity ($O(T)$) with respect to sequence length $T$. Figure 2 visualises this pipeline.

Overall these design choices endow xLSTM with three anticipated advantages over vanilla LSTMs:
1. Ability to revise stored information quickly via exponential gates;
2. Increased representational capacity by moving from scalars to matrices;
3. Potential for parallel execution through mLSTM’s lack of hidden–hidden dependencies.

Foundations of Recurrent Neural Networks and Long Short-Term Memory  

# Recurrent Neural Network Definitions  

Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are a family of models that process sequential data by maintaining a hidden state that evolves over discrete time steps. At each step $t$, an RNN receives an input vector $\mathbf{x}_t$ and updates its hidden representation $\mathbf{h}_t$ as a function of both $\mathbf{x}_t$ and the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{t-1}$. In its most elementary form this update can be written as  

\[
\mathbf{h}_t \;=\; \phi\!\big( \mathbf{W}_x \mathbf{x}_t \;+\; \mathbf{R} \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{b}\big),
\]  

where $\phi$ denotes a point‑wise non‑linearity (often $\tanh$), $\mathbf{W}_x$ are input weight matrices, $\mathbf{R}$ are recurrent weight matrices, and $\mathbf{b}$ is a bias term. The recurrence “ties together’’ all time steps so that information can propagate forward through arbitrarily long sequences. Because the same parameters are reused at each step, an RNN can be applied to inputs of any length while keeping its parameter count fixed. In this way RNNs provide a natural computational abstraction for time series, language, or any data where ordering matters.  

# Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)  

Training an RNN requires computing gradients of a loss $L$ with respect to all parameters that appear at every time step. This is performed by unrolling the recurrence for $T$ steps and applying the chain rule across the unrolled graph—a procedure known as backpropagation through time (BPTT). Concretely, if we denote by $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}$ the gradient flowing into hidden state $\mathbf{h}_t$, then for a vanilla recurrent update one obtains  

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}} 
   = 
   \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}\,
   \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}},
\]  

and this relation recurses backward from $t=T$ down to $t=1$. The Jacobian term $\frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_t}{\partial \mathbf{h}_{t-1}}$ contains the recurrent weights $\mathbf{R}$ multiplied by derivatives of the activation function $\phi$. Because BPTT repeatedly multiplies these Jacobians across many time steps, the magnitude of gradients can either shrink to near zero or explode to very large values depending on the spectral properties of $\mathbf{R}$. This phenomenon is formally known as **vanishing** or **exploding** gradients and directly limits how far standard RNNs can transmit information backward during learning.  

# Gradient Stability Issues in Vanilla RNNs  

When gradients vanish, early parts of a long sequence receive negligible learning signals; consequently a vanilla RNN cannot learn long‑range dependencies such as “the subject introduced ten words ago agrees with a verb later in the sentence.” Conversely, exploding gradients lead to numerical instability and make optimization erratic unless aggressive gradient clipping is applied. Both issues stem from repeatedly applying a bounded non‑linear function (e.g., $\tanh$) together with linear transformations whose eigenvalues may lie inside or outside the unit circle. Early work recognized these problems as fundamental obstacles for training deep or long recurrent networks \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:00book}. Overcoming them required architectural changes that preserve gradient magnitude across many time steps while still allowing flexible modulation of information flow.  

# Original LSTM Equations  

The Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) architecture was introduced precisely to solve the vanishing‑gradient problem by embedding an additive “constant error carousel’’ inside its memory cell \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:97nips,Hochreiter:97}. The core equations governing an LSTM at time $t$ are  

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t ,\qquad
h_t = o_t\,\tilde h_t ,
\qquad
\tilde h_t = \psi(c_t) .
\tag{1}
\]  

Here $c_t$ is the **cell state**, which acts as an internal memory; $h_t$ is the **hidden state** that is emitted outward; $z_t$ denotes a transformed version of the external input; $i_t$, $f_t$, and $o_t$ are respectively an **input gate**, **forget gate**, and **output gate**; and $\psi$ is a squashing function (commonly $\tanh$) that normalizes $c_t$ before output gating.  
The gates modulate information flow using sigmoid activations  

\[
\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+\exp(-x)} ,
\]  

so that each gate output lies in $(0,1)$. Explicitly,

\[
\begin{aligned}
z_t &=
    \varphi(\tilde z_t), &
    \tilde z_t &= 
        {\mathbf w}^{\!\top}_{z}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{z}\, h_{t-1} + b_{z}, \\[4pt]
i_t &= 
    \sigma(\tilde i_t), &
    \tilde i_t &= 
        {\mathbf w}^{\!\top}_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{i}\, h_{t-1} + b_{i}, \\[4pt]
f_t &= 
    \sigma(\tilde f_t), &
    \tilde f_t &= 
        {\mathbf w}^{\!\top}_{f}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{f}\, h_{t-1} + b_{f}, \\[4pt]
o_t &= 
    \sigma(\tilde o _t), &
    \tilde o _t &= 
        {\mathbf w}^{\!\top}_{o}\,\mathbf{x}_t + r_{o}\, h_{t-1} + b_{o}.
\end{aligned}
\]  

The vectors ${\mathbf w}_{*}$ map from input $\mathbf{x}_t$, while scalars $r_{*}$ connect from the previous hidden state $h_{t-1}$ (the so‑called *hidden‑dependent* gating). Bias terms $b_{*}$ allow each gate to shift its operating region independently. The activation function for cell inputs and for normalizing $c$ is denoted by $\varphi$ and $\psi$, respectively—typically both set to hyperbolic tangent.  

### Peephole Connections and Multi‑Cell Vectors  

Later refinements extended this scalar formulation to **multiple parallel cell states** assembled into a vector $\mathbf c _ t \in \mathbb R^{d}$ (see Appendix ⟨reference⟩). In such multi‑cell variants each gate becomes a vector‐valued function and recurrent parameters become matrices $\mathbf R^\!_{\cdot } \in {\mathbb R}^{d\times d}$, enabling *memory mixing* across cells via hidden‑to‑cell connections. Although these extensions increase representational power dramatically—allowing richer interactions between different memory slots—the essential gating mechanisms ($i,f,o,\sigma$) remain unchanged. Peephole connections—that is direct links from cell state $c_{t{-}1}$ into gate pre‑activations—are also admissible within this framework but were not required for the basic LSTM presented above. 

# Interpretation of the Constant Error Carousel  

The first term on the right–hand side of Equation (1), namely $f_{t}\,c_{\, t-1}$, implements an **additive recurrence** instead of a purely multiplicative one typical in vanilla RNNs ($c_{\, t}=W\,c_{\, t-1}+...$). This additive pathway means that when forget gate $f_{t}\approx 1$, the gradient flowing back through $c_{\, t}$ satisfies  

\[
\frac{\partial c_T}{\partial c_{\tau}} 
   = 
   \prod_{\tau < t \le T} f_{\, t}.
\]  

If each forget gate remains near unity over many steps then this product stays close to one, guaranteeing that errors can propagate unchanged—hence *constant error*. Accordingly, even if later layers apply non‑linear compressions via $\psi$, backpropagation through many timesteps does not suffer exponential decay or blowup because information passes through an essentially linear channel governed by learnable gates rather than fixed weights alone. The *carousel* metaphor captures this idea: information circles around inside $c$, being refreshed selectively by incoming signals ($i_tx z_x$) while otherwise remaining intact if desired. Hence LSTMs empower networks to retain pertinent facts over arbitrarily long horizons while still allowing them to forget irrelevant details through small values of $f_{ t }$. 

# Empirical Impact of Gated Recurrence in Early NLP Tasks  

When first introduced in the early 1990s \citep{Hochreiter:91,Hochreiter:97}, LSTMs rapidly demonstrated superiority over plain recurrent architectures on sequence modelling problems where long‑range dependencies matter. Empirical studies reported breakthroughs in text generation \citep{Graves:13}, handwriting synthesis \citep{Graves:13}, sequence‑to‑sequence translation \citep{Sutskever:14nips}, program evaluation \citep{Zaremba:14arxiv}, image captioning \citep{Karpathy:15,Hossain:19}, source‑code generation \citep{Karpathy:15blog}, hydrological forecasting \citep{Kratzert:18,Kratzert:19,Nearing:24}, and reinforcement‑learning agents such as AlphaStar (StarCraft II) \citep{Vinyals:17short} and OpenAI Five (Dota 2) \citep{OpenAI:19}. Across these domains LSTMs were praised for their ability “to learn abstractions” – they naturally discovered neurons that encoded numbers \citep{Lakretz:19}, syntax \citep{Bau:19}, or sentiment \citep{Radford:17arxiv}. Even after Transformers became dominant around 2017 \citep{Vaswani:17}, LSTMs remained competitive on specialized tasks where compact recurrent representations were advantageous (e.g., real‑time control for nuclear fusion shown in \citet{Degrave:22short}). These early successes cemented gated recurrence as a cornerstone technique for handling ordered data before attention‐based models rose to prominence.

--- 

In summary, recurrent neural networks provide a natural way to process sequences but suffer from unstable gradients when trained with naïve recurrence. Backpropagation through time formalizes learning but reveals why gradients either vanish or explode in deep unrolled graphs. The Long Short‑Term Memory architecture resolves these issues by inserting an additive constant error carousel together with multiplicative gates whose sigmoid activations regulate when information should be stored (\(i_t,z_t)\), retained (\(f_t)\), or exposed (\(o_t)\). Later extensions such as multi‑cell vectors broadened representational capacity while preserving gated control mechanisms. Together these innovations enabled early natural language processing breakthroughs and continue to influence modern extensions like xLSTM explored later in this book.
