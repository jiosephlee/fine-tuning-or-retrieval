# mLSTM Unlocked: From Tiny Scalars to Full‑Size Matrix Memories  

*How turning a single number into a full matrix gives recurrent networks a built‑in key‑value store.*

---

## Introduction  

When Long Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) networks were introduced in the early 1990s, their “cell” was just one scalar value per hidden unit—a little bucket that could be filled or emptied over time. That scalar design worked wonders for many sequence tasks, yet it imposed an obvious bottleneck: all the information you wanted to remember had to be crammed into a single number per unit.  

The recent **xLSTM** paper asks whether we can push LSTMs into the same performance league as today’s large language models simply by giving them more expressive memories. The answer comes in two flavors:

1. **sLSTM** – keeps the scalar cell but introduces exponential gating and new mixing mechanisms.  
2. **mLSTM** – **expands the cell from a scalar to a full matrix**, turning every recurrent layer into an implicit key‑value store.

In this post we focus on **mLSTM** and unpack three ideas that make it powerful:

* A matrix‑valued memory **\(\mathbf C\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\)** that can accumulate many key‑value pairs.  
* The **covariance update rule**—the mathematical version of “adding a flashcard to your notebook”.  
* **Exponential gates** that act like learnable learning rates for these updates.

Together they give an RNN the capacity of a miniature associative database while still operating with linear time and constant memory cost per token.

---

## From Scalar Cell to Matrix Memory  

In a classic LSTM the recurrence looks like

\[
c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1} + i_t\,z_t ,
\qquad
h_t = o_t\,\psi(c_t),
\]

where \(c_t\) is the scalar cell state and \(f_t,i_t,o_t\) are sigmoid gates.  

**mLSTM replaces \(c_t\) with a matrix \(\mathbf C_t\).** Each time step now stores two vectors:

* **Key** \(\mathbf k_t \in \mathbb R^{d}\) – essentially an address.
* **Value** \(\mathbf v_t \in \mathbb R^{d}\) – the content you want to retrieve later.

The memory matrix accumulates outer products of these pairs, building up an associative map inside the network itself.

---

## The Covariance Update Rule – Adding Flashcards to Your Notebook  

Think of your notebook as an empty table with \(d\times d\) cells. When you encounter a new fact (a key–value pair), you write it down by adding its outer product:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf C_{t} = f_{t}\,\mathbf C_{t-1}
               + i_{t}\,\bigl(\mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{\!\top}\bigr)
}
\tag{1}
\]

* The term \(\mathbf v_{t}\,\mathbf k_{t}^{\!\top}\) is exactly what you get when you multiply a column vector (**value**) by a row vector (**key**)—a rank‑one “flashcard” that links the two.
* The **forget gate \(f_{t}\)** optionally decays older entries, acting like erasing faded ink.
* The **input gate \(i_{t}\)** scales how strongly you write this new flashcard into the notebook—it’s your learning rate for that particular update.

Because each update adds an outer product rather than overwriting, you can keep stacking many flashcards without losing earlier ones—provided you control forgetting with \(f_{t}\).

---

## Exponential Gates as Learning Rates  

Standard LSTMs use sigmoid activations for their gates (\(0<g<1\)). In mLSTM we switch the input gate—and optionally the forget gate—to **exponential activations**:

\[
i_{t}= \exp(\tilde i_{t}), 
\qquad 
f_{t}= 
   \begin{cases}
      \sigma(\tilde f_{t})\\
      \exp(\tilde f_{t}) 
   \end{cases}
\]

Why exponentials?  

* An exponential can produce values **larger than 1**, letting us *amplify* important updates rather than merely scaling them down.
* When interpreted through Eq (1), \(i_{t}\) directly becomes a *learnable step size*: large \(i_{t}\) writes a bright flashcard, small \(i_{t}\) writes a faint one.
* Similarly, an exponential forget gate can implement *growth* or *decay* beyond simple attenuation—a powerful way to model long‑range retention or rapid forgetting.

To keep numbers from exploding, mLSTM adopts the same stabilizer state used in sLSTM:

\[
m_t = \max\bigl(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1},\,\log(i_t)\bigr),
\]
followed by rescaling:
\(i'_t = \exp(\log(i_t)-m_t)\),   
\(f'_t = \exp(\log(f_t)+m_{t-1}-m_t)\).

These stabilized gates behave identically in forward pass and gradient flow, yet stay numerically safe during training.

---

## Why Matrix Memory Means Massive Capacity  

A scalar cell can store at most one real number per unit—effectively one bit of “information” after quantization. A full matrix memory has \(d^2\) parameters that evolve *without any learned weights*. Each new key–value pair contributes another rank‑one slice to this space, so after processing \(T\) tokens you have roughly \(T\) rank‑one components summed together (subject to forgetting).

The consequences are striking:

| Property | Scalar LSTM | mLSTM (matrix memory) |
|----------|-------------|----------------------|
| Storage dimension | \(d\) scalars | \(d\times d\) entries |
| Natural key‑value binding | None (implicit via gating) | Explicit via outer product |
| Ability to retrieve rare items | Limited – often overwritten | High – each flashcard persists until decayed |
| Scaling with hidden size |\(~O(d)\)|\(~O(d^2)\) effective capacity |

In language modeling experiments on **Wikitext‑103**, this extra capacity translates into markedly lower perplexities on low‑frequency tokens—precisely where traditional LSTMs stumble because they cannot keep enough distinct traces of rare words.  

---

## Parallelism and Practicalities  

At first glance adding a matrix recurrence seems expensive: each step involves two dense matrix multiplies (\(\mathbf v_k^{\!\top}\) construction and query read). However:

* There are **no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrent connections** beyond the matrix update. This eliminates sequential dependencies other than the simple scan over time.
* The whole scan can be expressed as parallel prefix operations—a technique used by FlashAttention and other recent linear attention kernels.
* In practice mLSTM’s forward pass runs comparably fast on GPUs when implemented with batched matrix ops despite its quadratic inner dimension cost.

The tradeoff is clear: **linear compute w.r.t sequence length** versus **extra per‑step matrix work** proportional to \(d^2\). For hidden sizes typical of current language models (\(d=1024–2048\)), modern GPUs handle this comfortably, especially when leveraging mixed precision training.

## Results at a Glance  

The xLSTM paper reports several head‑to‑head studies showing how mLSTM shines:

* On synthetic associative recall tasks with up to 256 key–value pairs and context lengths of 2048 tokens, an architecture mixing just one mLSTM block with several sLSTM blocks (**xLSTM[1:1]**) outperformed all non‑Transformer baselines.
* In large‐scale language modeling (300 B tokens), pure mLSTM stacks (**xLSTM[1:0]**) achieved lower validation perplexity than both GPT‐style Transformers (e.g., Llama) and strong State‑Space models (Mamba).
* Crucially, when tested on much longer contexts than seen during training (up to 16 k tokens), mLSTM kept its perplexity flat—evidence that its built‑in key‐value store truly preserves information across hundreds of steps.

These results suggest that simply “making the cell bigger” does more than increase parameter count—it endows the model with an *associative memory* that scales gracefully with data size.

---

## Takeaways  

1. **Matrix memories give RNNs an explicit associative store** without adding trainable parameters beyond standard linear projections.
2. **Covariance updates provide a clean algebraic recipe** for writing and reading key–value pairs—think of each outer product as adding a flashcard.
3. **Exponential gating turns those flashcards into learnable updates**, acting like dynamic learning rates (and decay rates when applied to forget gates).
4. Despite extra inner‐matrix work, mLSTM retains linear time complexity across sequence length and enjoys excellent parallelization on modern hardware.
5. Empirically, this design narrows—and in some regimes closes—the performance gap between LSTMs and today’s top Transformers and State‑Space models.

The next frontier will be scaling these architectures up further while tightening CUDA kernels for matrix updates—something that could make mLSTMs competitive *everywhere*, from language models running on cloud clusters down to edge devices needing efficient continual learning.
