Motivation for Efficient Large Language Models  

# Historical scaling trends in LLM size and performance  

Over the last few years there has been a striking escalation in both the scale and capability of large language models (LLMs). Early transformer‑based systems were built with millions of parameters and trained on relatively modest corpora, yet already exhibited impressive language understanding abilities. Subsequent generations pushed parameter counts into the billions while simultaneously increasing the number of training tokens to hundreds of billions—a regime exemplified by our own pre‑training protocol that consumes $100$ billion tokens for each model variant examined. Empirically, every increase in model size has been accompanied by systematic gains across a wide swath of downstream benchmarks such as ARC‑Easy, ARC‑Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA and BoolQ. This progression has been described as a “new era” where ever larger networks unlock new levels of reasoning, translation, coding assistance and many other natural–language tasks. The pattern is clear: more parameters combined with more data lead to lower perplexity on held‑out corpora such as WikiText2 or C4, which translates directly into higher zero‑shot accuracy on challenging evaluations. However this progress comes at a steep price—the computational demand grows roughly quadratically with hidden dimension when standard dense matrix multiplications dominate execution time because every layer largely consists of $\texttt{nn.Linear}$ operations performed at $16$‑bit floating point precision (FP16 or BF16). Consequently the community has observed an ever rising wall of resource requirements that threatens both practical deployment and continued scientific advancement unless new efficiency strategies are devised.

# Energy consumption and environmental concerns  

The dramatic increase in compute does not occur in isolation; it imposes a substantial burden on electricity consumption as well as associated carbon emissions. In modern silicon processes the primary limiter of throughput is power rather than raw transistor count—any operation that consumes many joules per arithmetic step will throttle overall speed regardless of nominal clock frequency. Standard dense linear layers rely heavily on floating‑point multiply–add units whose energy per operation far exceeds that of simple integer addition circuits. By replacing these multiplications with additions over ternary weights ($\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}$) — as done in BitNet architectures — the arithmetic workload becomes dominated by integer adders that consume orders of magnitude less energy per operation than their floating‑point counterparts.\newline  

Concrete estimates derived from our measurement framework reveal that when matrix multiplication dominates computation (as is typical for transformers), moving from FP16 multiplication/addition to INT8 addition yields an arithmetic‑operation energy reduction factor of roughly $71.4\times$ on modern $7\,$nm process technology. This theoretical saving translates into observed speedups because less power dissipation allows higher sustained clock rates without triggering thermal throttling.\newline  

Beyond raw computation, moving weights from DRAM into an on‑chip buffer such as SRAM incurs considerable bandwidth costs; high‐bandwidth memory accesses dominate power budgets during inference when large dense weight matrices must be streamed repeatedly through the compute fabric. By compressing every weight down to an effective storage requirement of approximately $1.\!58$ bits—a figure obtained because three possible values require $\log_2(3)\approx1.\!585$ binary bits—the total volume transferred per token drops dramatically relative to FP16 models whose weights occupy $16$ bits each.~This reduction curtails both dynamic power drawn by memory interfaces and total time spent waiting for data movement, thereby shrinking overall energy footprints further.\newline  

Collectively these considerations establish a solid motivation: any viable path toward larger language models must confront their escalating energy demands head‑on if researchers hope to keep development sustainable both ecologically and economically.

# Deployment constraints on cloud services and edge devices  

Even when one accepts higher operational costs in data centers, practical constraints still arise when attempting to serve massive transformer models at scale inside cloud environments. Contemporary GPUs allocate most of their silicon budget toward general purpose arithmetic units designed for dense FP16 matrix multiplications; however they also possess limited fast on‑chip memory—typically a few hundred megabytes per accelerator—while host DRAM runs into tens or hundreds of gigabytes per node.\newline  

Because full‑precision weights occupy sixteen times more space than their $1.\!58$‑bit counterparts, fitting a multi‐billion‐parameter network into GPU memory often mandates complex model parallelism schemes such as pipeline parallelism across multiple cards or offloading strategies that incur extra communication latency between devices.\newline  

In contrast, ternary‐weight networks shrink this demand proportionally so that a $70\,$B parameter model consumes roughly four times less GPU memory than an equivalent FP16 baseline.\footnote{Our measurements indicate a factor ranging from $3.\!32\times$ at $3.\!9\,$B parameters up to $4.\!1\times$ at $70\,$B parameters when employing a dedicated two‑bit kernel.] Consequently fewer physical accelerators are required merely to hold the model’s parameters—an advantage reflected directly in reduced capital expenditure for cloud providers.\newline  

The benefits extend far beyond traditional datacenter servers into truly constrained environments such as smartphones or embedded controllers where both RAM capacity ($\sim2\,$GB) and energy budget are minuscule compared with server GPUs.\newline  

Because activations are already cast into $\texttt{int8}$ form during inference—instead of retaining sixteen­‐bit formats—the total activation footprint per token also drops substantially (\emph{e.g.,} halving the amount needed for KV caches). This implies that sequence lengths can be doubled within identical memory budgets—a critical capability when handling long documents or code snippets without resorting to external cache management tricks.\newline  

All together these deployment considerations illustrate why shifting towards highly compressed representations is indispensable not just for shaving runtime seconds off cloud inference latencies but also for making any form of LLM usable outside massive infrastructure enclaves.

# The trade‑off between model quality and inference cost  

A central question confronting engineers working on low‑bit transformers is whether reducing numerical precision inevitably harms predictive performance.\newline  

Our empirical investigations provide a nuanced answer: When ternary weights are trained *from scratch* using an “absmean” quantization scheme—where each weight matrix is first normalized by its mean absolute magnitude before rounding onto $\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}$—the resulting model exhibits virtually indistinguishable perplexity compared with an identical architecture trained entirely in FP16.\newline  

Specifically, starting at a modest $3$\!B parameter scale both perplexity measured on WikiText2/C4 datasets and zero‑shot accuracies across benchmarks such as ARC\,EASY or Winogrande converge within statistical noise.\newline  
At this scale BitNet $b_{\,\!{\tiny 1}.58}$ achieves a latency reduction factor close to $2.\!71\times$ while consuming only about one third ($\sim0.\!28\times$) of GPU memory relative to its FP16 counterpart.\newline  
Scaling upward amplifies these advantages: A $70$\!B parameter version runs roughly four times faster than an equivalent FP16 baseline yet retains comparable—or even superior—skill scores because additional capacity compounds both semantic richness *and* efficiency gains arising from larger linear blocks where integer addition dominates execution time.~Thus moving towards extreme compression does **not** force practitioners onto an either/or choice between quality versus cost;\ instead it opens up a new Pareto frontier where both axes improve concurrently.—a front highlighted repeatedly throughout our experimental section where “BitNet $b_{\,\!{\tiny 1}.58}$ outperforms LLaMA FP16 3B while using fewer resources.”\newline  

These observations fundamentally shift how researchers think about GPU budget allocation: rather than deploying substantial monetary resources solely toward higher floating-point precision arithmetic units one can invest instead in richer networking topologies or higher batch throughput while maintaining—or improving—the underlying linguistic competence exhibited by legacy full precision models.

Transformer Fundamentals and Bottlenecks

# Self‑attention mechanism and feed‑forward network  

The Transformer architecture, which underpins virtually every modern large language model (LLM), consists of two tightly coupled computational pillars: the self‑attention mechanism and the position‑wise feed‑forward network (FFN). Both components are built from a series of linear transformations followed by simple non‑linearities, and together they enable the model to capture long‑range dependencies while preserving a high degree of parallelism across sequence positions.  

In self‑attention each input token is first projected into three latent spaces – queries ($Q$), keys ($K$) and values ($V$). Formally, given an input activation matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ where $n$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the hidden dimension, three weight matrices $W_Q,W_K,W_V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}$ produce  

\[
Q = X W_Q,\qquad K = X W_K,\qquad V = X W_V .
\]  

The attention scores are obtained by a scaled dot‑product between $Q$ and $K^{\top}$, yielding an $n\times n$ affinity matrix that quantifies how much each token should attend to every other token. After normalizing this matrix with a softmax operation, it is multiplied by $V$ to produce a context representation for each token:

\[
\text{Attention}(X) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V .
\]  

Crucially, all three projections ($W_Q$, $W_K$, $W_V$) are implemented as dense matrix multiplications using standard fully‑connected layers (\texttt{nn.Linear} in PyTorch). The subsequent product $\text{softmax}(\cdot)V$ also requires a matrix multiplication spanning all sequence positions. In practice these two stages dominate the runtime of a Transformer block because they involve $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ arithmetic operations – an amount that grows quadratically with sequence length.  

The FFN follows attention within each layer and operates independently on each token position. It typically consists of two linear layers separated by a nonlinearity such as GeLU or SwiGLU (the latter is used in BitNet b1.58). With hidden dimension $d$ and intermediate expansion factor $f$ (often 4), the FFN computes  

\[
\text{FFN}(x) = W_2\, \sigma(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2 ,
\]  

where $W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times f d}$ and $W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{f d\times d}$. Though there are only two projections per token rather than three as in attention, their total contribution remains comparable because they are executed for every token across many layers. Consequently, both attention and FFN reduce to repeated dense linear mappings – i.e., many calls to \texttt{nn.Linear} – making these layers the primary source of computational work in an LLM.

# Role of linear projections (nn.Linear) in attention and MLP blocks  

Within a standard Transformer layer each logical operation—query generation, key generation, value generation, attention output recombination, internal expansion of the FFN—maps directly onto an instance of \texttt{nn.Linear}. In framework terms this module executes a single dense matrix multiplication followed by an optional bias addition:

\[
Y = X W + b ,
\]  

where $X$ holds activations from either the previous layer or the embedding table, $W$ encodes learnable parameters, and $b$ is an optional bias term (the BitNet b1.58 design removes biases altogether). Because every token’s feature vector must be multiplied by every column of $W$, each call incurs $\mathcal{O}(n d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}})$ multiply‑accumulate operations.  

When scaled up to billions of parameters—typical for modern LLMs—the cumulative number of \texttt{nn.Linear} invocations becomes massive. For a 70 B parameter model with 96 layers, there may be on the order of several hundred thousand distinct linear transformations processed during a single forward pass on a modest sequence length. As highlighted in the “Era of 1‑bit LLMs” work, these dense multiplications form “the bulk of any LLMs” because they dominate both arithmetic intensity (number of FLOPs per byte transferred) and memory movement (the need to fetch weight matrices repeatedly from DRAM or cache).  

BitNet b1.58 directly targets this bottleneck by replacing every \texttt{nn.Linear} with \texttt{BitLinear}, a custom operator where weights are constrained to ternary values $\{-1,0,+1\}$ (“1.58 bits”). By eliminating multiplications entirely—replacing them with conditional additions or skips—the new operator reduces both computational latency and energy per operation while preserving functional equivalence when trained end‑to‑end from scratch.

# Floating‑point formats commonly used (FP16/BF16)  

Historically LLM training and inference have relied on reduced‑precision floating point formats such as FP16 (half precision) or BF16 (brain floating point). Both formats store numbers using 16 bits but allocate their exponent–mantissa fields differently: FP16 dedicates 5 bits to exponent and 10 bits to mantissa plus one sign bit; BF16 allocates only 8 mantissa bits while preserving an 8‑bit exponent identical to single‑precision FP32’s range. These choices strike a balance between numeric range—important for stable training—and storage efficiency—key for fitting massive weight matrices into GPU memory.\newline  

When an LLM operates under FP16/BF16 semantics every weight element occupies two bytes on memory buses or caches. Consequently a model containing 70 B parameters consumes roughly \(140\,\text{GB}\) solely for its weights if stored uncompressed—a figure far beyond what typical accelerators can hold on chip. Moreover during inference each multiply–accumulate step must read two such half‑precision operands from memory before feeding them into hardware tensor cores that perform fused dot products at high throughput.\newline  

The “Era of 1‑bit LLMs” paper emphasizes that transitioning from these floating-point regimes to low-bit integer representations directly confronts one root cause of inefficiency: floating‑point multiplication itself consumes significantly more energy than integer addition even when operating at half precision. By moving away from FP16/BF16 weight representations toward ternary weights stored effectively at 1.58 bits per parameter, BitNet b1.58 eliminates costly floating‐point multiplications while retaining comparable model expressiveness after appropriate training procedures.

# Memory bandwidth requirements during inference  

During inference an LLM repeatedly streams weight tensors from main memory (DRAM) into on‑chip compute units (e.g., SRAM or registers). Because transformer layers are dominated by \texttt{nn.Linear} calls whose operands are large dense matrices, the data movement volume quickly eclipses raw arithmetic work. In concrete terms, processing one batch token through a single linear projection requires loading all entries of its weight matrix—a matrix that may be several gigabytes for very large models—and simultaneously writing out activations back to memory.\newline  

Memory bandwidth therefore becomes a hard ceiling on attainable throughput: even if computational units could theoretically execute billions of operations per second, they will be stalled if required weights cannot be supplied fast enough from DRAM over PCIe or NVLink links. The “Era of 1‑bit LLMs” discussion highlights that 1‑bit architectures cut this bandwidth demand dramatically because each weight now occupies only \(1/8^{th}\) as many bits as an FP16 counterpart (\( \frac{2\,\text{bytes}}{0.\!1975\,\text{bytes}} \approx 10\times \)). Consequently fewer bytes travel across memory buses per forward pass.\newline  

Furthermore reductions in weight size shrink cache pressure: more parameters fit into higher levels of cache hierarchy before eviction occurs, which lowers miss rates and improves reuse locality for subsequent accesses within a layer or across successive layers (e.g., when reusing projection matrices across heads). This compression effect explains why BitNet b1.58 reports up to \(3.55\times\) lower GPU memory usage while achieving comparable latency relative to full precision baselines—the lower bandwidth burden translates directly into faster wall‑clock performance at fixed hardware budgets.

# “Multiply‑accumulate” cost as the primary bottleneck  

A dense linear transformation essentially computes many dot products:

\[
y_j = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{in}}} x_i w_{ij},
\]

where each term \(x_i w_{ij}\) represents one multiply–accumulate (MAC) operation—a multiplication followed by an addition into an accumulator register. In modern silicon processes MACs dominate power consumption because multiplication circuitry involves more transistors and higher switching activity than simple adders.\newline  

For half‑precision floating point formats typical MAC energy figures range from tens to hundreds of picojoules per operation on advanced nodes such as 7 nm technology~\cite{energycost}. By contrast integer addition costs orders of magnitude less—often sub‐picojoule per add—even when repeated billions of times.\newline  

Because transformers spend most cycles performing MACs inside \texttt{nn.Linear}, any reduction that replaces multiplication with cheaper alternatives yields outsized gains across both latency and energy budgets. This observation underlies BitNet’s design philosophy: ternary weights enable us to reinterpret each MAC as either adding \(+x_i\), subtracting \(x_i\), or omitting the term altogether when \(w_{ij}=0$. Thus the entire inner product collapses down to conditional integer adds without any floating-point multiplier involvement.\newline  

Empirically reported in the paper is a \(71.4\times\) reduction in arithmetic‐operation energy consumption for matrix multiplication on a 7 nm chip when moving from FP16 multiplications toward BITNET’s INT8-adder paradigm—a quantitative illustration that MAC removal is indeed the dominant source of savings rather than peripheral aspects such as control flow overhead or branching logic.

# Why reducing precision chiefly benefits matrix multiplication  

Matrix multiplication lies at the heart of every transformer layer because it synthesizes information across dimensions via repeated dot products between activation vectors and weight matrices—a pattern repeated thousands of times per forward pass. Precision reduction affects two distinct yet intertwined costs associated with this operation: data transport cost and compute cost.\newline  

From a data perspective lower‐precision weights shrink storage size dramatically (\(b_\text{weight}\) bits versus \(b_\text{fp}=16\)). Since each element must be fetched before it participates in any arithmetic step, cutting bitwidth directly scales down total bytes moved over memory channels irrespective of algorithmic optimizations such as tiling or prefetching.\newline  

From a compute perspective high‐precision multiply–accumulate units survive only because multiplying arbitrary floating-point numbers demands complex circuitry (exponent alignment, mantissa multiplication followed by rounding). When weights become confined to values $\{-1,0,+1\}$ as defined in BitNet b1.58,

\[
w_{ij}\in \{-1,\;0,\;+1\},
\]

the product \(x_i w_{ij}\) simplifies:

* If \(w_{ij}=+1,\) \(x_i w_{ij}=x_i,\)
* If \(w_{ij}=-1,\) \(x_i w_{ij}=-x_i,\)
* If \(w_{ij}=0,\) it contributes nothing.

Thus every multiplication collapses into either copying an activation value unchanged, negating it via a cheap sign flip (often realized without any arithmetic unit), or dropping it entirely—operations implementable purely through integer adders combined with cheap control logic handling sparsity (\(w=0\)). Consequently hardware can bypass costly multiplier datapaths entirely during inference.\newline  

Because most neural networks—including transformer LLMs—have very high fan-in/out dimensions (\(d_{\text{in}}\), \(d_{\text{out}}\)) rendering MAC throughput bound by raw silicon resources rather than algorithmic parallelism limits like Amdahl’s law ~[cite], any reduction that shrinks both operand width *and* eliminates multiplier stages yields superlinear speedups when aggregate workloads are large enough—as demonstrated by BitNet b1.58’s reported up-to‐4× speedups for models beyond 13 B parameters relative to full‐precision baselines.\newline  

In summary, lowering precision does more than just make individual numbers smaller; it fundamentally changes how those numbers interact inside matrix products—from costly multiplications over wide datapaths into lightweight accumulations over compressed representations—thereby addressing both dominant sources—memory bandwidth usage and MAC energy—that together constitute the primary bottleneck in modern transformer based language models.

Quantization Basics for Neural Networks  

# Fixed‑point versus floating‑point representations  

Large Language Models (LLMs) such as the standard LLaMA family store almost all learnable parameters in sixteen‑bit floating point formats—either FP16 or BF16—as noted in the introduction of *The Era of 1‑bit LLMs*. A floating‑point value consists of a sign bit, an exponent field and a mantissa field. This representation gives a very wide dynamic range while still preserving enough fractional precision for gradient‑based learning, which explains why most modern LLMs default to FP16/BF16 during training and inference.  

Fixed‑point representations adopt a very different philosophy. Instead of an exponent, every number is interpreted as an integer multiplied by a constant scale factor that is common across a tensor (or often across an entire model). In other words, a tensor $X$ stored in fixed point can be written as  
\[
X = s\;\cdot \;\hat X,\qquad \hat X\in\mathbb Z,
\]  
where $s$ is a real‐valued scalar (the *scale*) and $\hat X$ contains only integer values such as $-128,\dotsc,+127$ when eight‑bit fixed point is used. Because integers can be added or shifted without involving expensive floating‑point multipliers, fixed‑point arithmetic dramatically lowers both energy consumption and silicon area devoted to arithmetic units—an observation that underpins the entire motivation behind BitNet~\cite{bitnet}.  

When moving from FP16/BF16 toward lower precision (e.g., four‑bit or even one‐bit regimes) the dominant compute kernel—matrix multiplication—transforms from a sequence of FP16 multiplies followed by adds into essentially an integer addition problem once the weights have been constrained to small discrete sets such as $\{-1,0,+1\}$. This shift reduces the *arithmetic intensity*—the ratio between compute operations and data movement—in favor of more cache‑friendly operations and consequently yields large speedups on power‐limited chips.

# Uniform quantization pipeline: scale factor, zero point, rounding  

Uniform quantisation maps each continuous weight value $w$ onto one element of an evenly spaced discrete lattice defined by a scale $s$ and optionally a *zero point* $z$. In the setting presented by BitNet b\(^{\text{ } \! }\! {}\!{}\)​\(^{\text{\,} \! }\! {}\!{}_{\,\,\,\,5}\!{}_ {\!\!\!8}\!\!(=\,{\rm b} =\! {{}\!}{\,\,}{})​\( _{\!\!\!h}\!\!\!\!\)​${}$ The particular choice employed throughout *The Era of \(^{\text {\,}}${}$​*\(\rm \,lmm...\)* adopts an *absolute mean* normalisation prior to rounding:
\[
    \widetilde W~=~{\rm RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\,{-}1,\,+1\Bigr),
    \qquad
    {\rm RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))),
\]
with 
\[
    \gamma~=~\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}| .
\]  
Here $W$ denotes the full‑precision weight matrix (shape $n\times m$), $\epsilon$ safeguards against division by zero, and $\gamma$ captures exactly the average absolute magnitude across all entries. Dividing by $\gamma+\epsilon$ therefore rescales every entry so that most values lie near unity before discretisation occurs. The operator ${\rm RoundClip}$ then snaps each rescaled entry either downwards or upwards towards one of three possible integers : ${-}1$, $0$, or ${+}1$. Consequently every learned weight after this operation belongs firmly within the ternary set $\{-1,0,+1\}$ required by BitNet b\<sub>​​</sub>​₁·₅₈​.\footnote{
The name “b​$_{\text {  } }\hspace{-0pt}‎$_{\text {  } }\hspace{-0pt}\" (“b\"j”) reflects precisely this effective entropy per scalar – see Section “Bits per parameter.”}

For activations we follow exactly the same pipeline except that instead of first clipping activations into $[0,Q_b]$, we shift them symmetrically into $[-Q_b,Q_b]$. This eliminates any extra subtraction needed for “zero‐point” correction while retaining unbiased numerical behaviour—the authors report negligible impact on downstream performance when employing this simpler symmetric scheme – an engineering choice motivated primarily by ease of implementation on GPUs where two’s complement integer arithmetic is native.\footnote{
In practice \(Q_b =2^{b_{\rm act}}{-}~{}\nolimits{}???${}$ where ${b_{\rm act}}=8$, reflecting eight‐bit activation precision throughout all experiments.)}

Overall this uniform pipeline consists simply of
* measuring an appropriate scale ($\gamma$ or equivalent activation norm),
* dividing every tensor element,
* applying nearest‐integer rounding,
* clipping values into their allowed interval,
* storing the result using substantially fewer bits than their precursor.
Because all steps are differentiable almost everywhere except at discrete thresholds—and those thresholds become deterministic after straight­–through estimators—the same pipeline can be inserted directly into both forward passes during training (see Section “Post‑training …”) rendering “quantisation aware” optimisation straightforward.



# Post‑training quantisation versus quantisation‑aware training  

Post­training Quantisation (PTQ) attempts to shrink an already trained high–precision model into low precision after learning has concluded. As summarised early in *The Era of \(^{\,}_{\,}\)* “One approach … use post‑training … implementation … reduces fidelity.” Historically PTQ has produced impressive memory reductions—from sixteen down towards four bits—as demonstrated by methods such as SmoothQuant~\cite{smoothquant}, GPTQ~\cite{gptq}, QuIP~\cite{quip}, among others cited therein.\footnote{
These works typically keep activation ranges fixed while learning optimal clipping thresholds after pre–training completes.)  
Yet despite their practical popularity (“widely used in industry LLMs”) PTQ remains inherently suboptimal because it never had access to low‑precision gradients during optimisation—the resulting discrete weights often produce degradation relative to their full‐precision counterpart.\footnote{
The degradation manifests both in validation perplexity increase (Table ≈ [tab:ppl] shows small improvements once model size reaches ∼3 B param.)}

Quantisation‑Aware Training (QAT), alternatively known here simply as “training from scratch with low­–precision constraints,” embeds the same discretisation function described above inside every forward pass while learning proceeds under loss gradients computed against discreetly rounded parameters (§Quantisation Function). By doing so BP/LR updates consistently see only ternary perturbations rather than an underlying continuous shadow copy—a strategy already proven successful within `BitLinear` layers constituting BitNet b₁·₅₈.\footnote{
Effectively `BitLinear` replaces `nn.Linear` thereby guaranteeing that every linear operation obeys ternary weight rules throughout optimisation.)  

Empirically observed outcomes confirm this intuition: when models are trained bottom–up under these constraints they match full‐precision LLaMA baselines already at ⟨3 B⟩ parameters—as reported under “Results” (“BitNet b₁·₅₈ starts…to match full precision …from a 3 B size”). Moreover increasing model capacity continues shrinking any lingering gap between low–precision and fp16 performances across both perplexities and downstream task accuracies.\footnote{
This conclusion directly stems from Tables [tab:ppl], [tab:zero-shot] showing parity at ≥ 3 B parameters.)}


Thus PTQ provides rapid deployment routes for legacy models whereas QAT—exemplified by BitNet b₁·₅₈—delivers principled tradeoffs allowing compressed models without sacrificing predictive quality.



# Bits per parameter metric and its information–theoretic interpretation  

A natural way to gauge storage cost per learnable scalar is through **bits‐per‐parameter** (\(b_{\rm pp}\)). If a tensor assigns each entry one value out of \(S\) equally probable symbols then elementary information theory dictates
\[
    b_{\rm pp}= \log_{2} S .
    \tag{★}
\]  
For standard binary symbols \(S=2$, thus \(b_{\rm pp}= \log_{2}2 =  １　bits.\) In contrast **ternary** weighting introduces \(S=3,\) producing
\(b_{\rm pp}= \log_{2}3 ≃ １．５８５…≈１．５８.\)
Exactly this calculation motivates naming our new formulation **BitNet b₁·₅₈**, explicitly noting that “adding an additional value `0` … results in ≈ １．５８　bits” (\S“BitNet b $_{\text { }}… ”).

Why does this matter? First it provides a **fair yardstick** when comparing heterogeneous compression strategies—four–bit PTQ schemes hold \(S=2^4 = １６→b_{\rm pp}=４.\) Consequently any improvement observed at lower \(b_{\rm pp}\) yet attaining comparable test loss signals genuine architectural progress rather than mere benefit derived from larger model capacity alone.\footnote{
Figures [fig:latency-memory-energy] illustrate equivalence statements such as “13B　BitNet   b₁·₅۸ ≃  ×57×56… fewer resources than　３Ｂ　FP１６.”  

Second \(b_{\rm pp}\) connects directly **to scaling laws** introduced implicitly (“a new scaling law …”). Holding total token count constant while varying \(b_{\rm pp}\) yields predictable shifts along empirical performance curves—higher effective precision pushes models rightward along those curves whereas moving leftward requires compensatory increases in width/depth or token quantity.\footnote{
Observed matching performance at ≥3B parameters indicates nontrivial correctness cost captured entirely via ternary-valued wandering flows.)}  



In short,
*binary* models consume exactly one bit per parameter,
*ternary* models consume ≈１．５８ bits,
and any multinomial grid spells out higher costs through Eq.(★). Interpreting real hardware footprints through these numeric lenses clarifies why **BitNet b₁·₅₈ achieves Pareto improvements** over both binary(BitNet)≲original14以及 fully­precision baselines.

# Impact of quantisation on arithmetic intensity (add vs multiply)

i.e., >10⁸ ops / GB ⇒ compute bound; ≤10⁸ ⇒ bandwidth bound). Modern Transformers exhibit especially high intensity because dense linear layers dominate inference cost:
each output element requires `N_in × N_out` ~~floating-point multiplies plus adds (`FP16⊗FP16`).
