Motivation and Challenges in Scaling Large Language Models  

# Historical progress from early language models to modern multi‑billion‑parameter transformers  

The past several years have witnessed an unprecedented expansion in the scale of large language models (LLMs). Early transformer‑based systems were modestly sized, typically using a few tens of millions of parameters and trained with limited token budgets. As research communities embraced the transformer architecture, model designers began to increase both the depth (number of layers) and width (hidden dimension) of these networks while consistently employing half‑precision floating‑point formats such as FP16 or BF16 for their weights and activations. This trend gave rise to “vanilla” LLMs whose principal computational kernel is a dense matrix multiplication realized through conventional \texttt{nn.Linear} layers.  

Open‑source backbones such as LLaMA solidified a de‑facto standard by providing scalable designs ranging from a few hundred million parameters up to tens of billions of parameters \cite{llama, llama2}. In practical experiments described in this work, models were trained on the RedPajama dataset for $100$ billion tokens at sizes spanning $3$ B, $3.9$ B, $7$ B, $13$ B and even $70$ B parameters. Across this spectrum the same fundamental operations—half‑precision matrix multiplications and associated attention mechanisms—dominate both training and inference workloads. Consequently, each successive jump in parameter count brings measurable improvements in perplexity on benchmark corpora such as WikiText2 or C4, as well as higher zero‑shot accuracy on tasks ranging from ARC‑Easy to BoolQ.\n\nWhile raw predictive quality benefits clearly from larger models, the accompanying computational demands rise dramatically because every additional weight participates in every forward pass during inference. Understanding why scale brings both opportunity *and* challenge therefore requires an examination of how model size interacts with hardware realities.\n\n# Why model size matters: performance vs compute trade‑offs  

Empirical scaling laws observed across many transformer families indicate that increasing model capacity—measured by total parameter count—correlates with lower perplexity and higher downstream task accuracy when other factors such as data quantity are held constant \cite{llama}. In the experiments reported here this relationship persists until roughly $3$ B parameters for half‑precision baselines, after which further scaling continues to tighten performance gaps between full‑precision LLaMA baselines and the ternary {\bf BitNet b1.58} variant.\n\nHowever this performance gain does not come for free. The dominant operation during inference is dense matrix multiplication inside each \texttt{nn.Linear} call. In an FP16 network each multiplication requires a floating‑point multiply ($\times$) followed by an addition ($+$), both incurring substantial silicon energy per operation due to the need for precise mantissa handling and exponent alignment.\n\nIf a model’s weight matrix has dimensions $n\\times m$, then each forward pass performs roughly $n\\times m$ multiply–add pairs per token per layer. When parameter count grows by a factor $c$, both $n$ and $m$ typically increase proportionally, inflating the total number of arithmetic operations by approximately $c^{2}$. Thus larger models generate superlinear increases in computational workload even though their *accuracy* improves only sublinearly.\n\nConsequently designers confront a classic trade‑off curve: moving rightward on the axis of model size yields diminishing returns in predictive quality while accelerating demand for compute resources, memory bandwidth, and power delivery.\n\n# Hardware bottlenecks in inference—memory bandwidth, DRAM↔SRAM transfers  

Even if raw arithmetic were free, inference would still be throttled by data movement across memory hierarchies. In contemporary GPUs an LLM’s weight tensors reside primarily in off‑chip DRAM while activation buffers occupy fast on‑chip SRAM or registers during execution.\n\nThe process of streaming hundreds of megabytes—or even gigabytes—for a single forward pass incurs two distinct costs:\n\n* **Bandwidth limitation** – The rate at which DRAM can feed data into computation units is bounded by external memory bandwidth (e.g., PCIe or HBM). When weights are stored at half precision ($16$ bits), each parameter contributes twice the amount of transfer traffic compared with an unsigned eight‑bit representation.\n* **Transfer latency** – Moving weights between DRAM and SRAM incurs latency that adds directly to per‑token inference time because kernels cannot commence matrix multiplication until relevant tiles are resident on chip.\n\nBitNet’s reimagining of linear layers into {\bf BitLinear} mitigates both concerns simultaneously:\n\n* By constraining every weight to one of three ternary values $\{-1,\ 0,\ +1\}$ (a “$1.58$‑bit” representation), the storage requirement per parameter shrinks dramatically relative to FP16’s two bytes per entry.\n* Smaller weight footprints mean fewer bytes travel over the DRAM↔SRAM link per token, directly cutting bandwidth consumption and reducing transfer latency.\n*\nEmpirically we observe that at larger scales—for example a \\(70\\) B‐parameter {\bf BitNet b1.58} model—the latency advantage grows above fourfold relative to its FP16 counterpart because the proportion of runtime spent inside \texttt{nn.Linear} grows faster than any other component whose memory footprint remains unchanged.\n\n# Energy consumption patterns of floating‑point arithmetic on modern silicon  

Energy cost is often dominated by arithmetic rather than data movement when error‐tolerant computation is performed at high throughput rates on modern process nodes (e.g., $7\,\\text{nm}$). Prior work cited by this study estimates that a half‑precision multiply–add pair consumes roughly two orders of magnitude more picojoules than an integer addition alone \cite{energycost,pokebnn}. Consequently any architectural change that replaces floating­point multiplications with plain integer additions yields enormous savings.\n\nBitNet b1.58 accomplishes exactly this replacement:\n\n* Matrix multiplication becomes an accumulation of signed integer products where each product collapses to either $+w$, $0$, or $-w$, given that quantized weights are $\pm 1$. Since multiplying by $\pm 1$ can be implemented as conditional sign flips rather than true arithmetic multiplications, most hardware units execute only an addition per term.\n* The residual operations—normalizations (RMSNorm), activation functions (SwiGLU), rotary embeddings—continue using reduced precision activations ($8$–bit), so their contribution remains modest compared with full‐precision counterparts.\n\nQuantitative analysis provided shows that BitNet b1.58 saves approximately \\(71.4\\times\\) energy per matrix multiplication operation when mapped onto a \\(7\\) nm chip—the dominant component accounting for most total system energy during inference.\n\nThese savings compound with increasing model size because a larger proportion of total cycles is spent inside linear layers where BitNet replaces expensive ``FP16 mul+add’’ blocks with cheap integer adds.\n\n# Economic and ecological motivations for more efficient inference  

High energy demand translates directly into higher operational expenditure (OPEX) for cloud providers or enterprises deploying large conversational agents at scale. A typical deployment serving millions of requests daily may consume megawatt­hours of electricity solely for inference cycles governed by half‑precision arithmetic.\n\nFrom an ecological standpoint such power draw produces commensurate carbon emissions unless mitigated through renewable sources—a nontrivial consideration given global concerns about AI’s environmental impact.\n\nBy compressing model weights into ternary form (\{-1,\ 0,\ +1\}), BitNet b1.58 reduces GPU memory footprints dramatically—in experimental settings five times less GPU RAM was required for comparable performance at \\(3\\) B parameters—and simultaneously accelerates token generation time by more than twice relative to FP16 baselines.\n\nThese improvements open new business cases:\n\n* **Cost reduction** – Lower GPU memory consumption allows higher batch sizes per device (as demonstrated by an elevenfold batch increase on two \\(80\\) GB A100 GPUs), yielding higher throughput without additional hardware spend.\n* **Energy efficiency** – As shown above, operation counts translate into substantially lowered joule usage per generated token; aggregated over massive deployment volumes this yields appreciable reductions in electricity bills.\n* **Accessibility** – Compact models become viable on edge or mobile platforms where DRAM capacity is limited and power budgets are strict, extending AI capabilities beyond data centers into consumer devices without prohibitive cost overheads.\n\nTaken together these economic incentives align tightly with environmental stewardship goals; decreasing per‑token power draw while maintaining state‑of‑the‑art language understanding embodies a principled response to growing demands placed upon ever larger LLMs.\n\nIn summary, rapid scaling has delivered powerful language abilities at ever greater computational expense. The intertwined challenges—rising compute intensity, memory bandwidth constraints, soaring energy budgets—make it imperative to revisit foundational design choices such as numeric precision and operator composition. The emergence of {\bf BitNet b1.58}, with its ternary weights operating near ``$1{\\rm .}58$`` bits while achieving parity with full‐precision baselines from \\(3\\) B parameters upward, exemplifies how revisiting these choices can mitigate hardware bottlenecks and unlock more sustainable pathways for future generations of large language models.

Foundations of Model Quantization for Neural Networks  

In recent years large language models have grown dramatically in size and capability, yet their deployment is limited by the high memory bandwidth, latency, and energy demands incurred by standard numerical representations such as half‑precision floating point (FP16 or BF16). Reducing the number of bits required to store each weight or activation—a process known as **quantization**—offers a principled way to curb these costs while preserving model quality. This chapter surveys the essential concepts behind numerical precision in deep learning, contrasts common approaches used today, and draws insight from recent work on extreme low‑bit LLMs—particularly BitNet b1\.58—to illustrate why careful quantization-aware design can deliver a Pareto improvement over traditional floating‑point baselines.



# Floating‑point formats (FP16/BF16) versus integer formats  

Standard transformer language models store almost all learnable parameters as half‑precision floating point numbers. Two dominant half‑precision standards are **FP16**, which uses a signed mantissa of ten bits together with a five‑bit exponent, and **BF16**, which preserves nine bits for exponentiation while allocating only seven bits for mantissa precision. Regardless of minor differences, both representations consume sixteen bits per scalar value and support the full dynamic range required during stochastic gradient descent and subsequent inference.


The computational heart of any transformer consists predominantly of dense matrix multiplications performed by linear layers (`nn.Linear`). Each multiplication historically involves two floating‑point operations per pairwise product: one *multiply* followed by one *addition*. On modern silicon these operations dominate power draw because mixed‐precision floating point units still allocate substantial silicon area to exponent handling and mantissa alignment despite reduced bit widths.


Replacing these operations with integer arithmetic can dramatically shrink both compute time and energy consumption. Integer addition alone costs orders of magnitude less power than a single floating‑point multiply–add cycle on contemporary process nodes such as 7 nm \cite{energycost,pokebnn}. Consequently an architecture whose linear layers operate purely on integer data—by constraining weights to ternary values $\{-1,\!0,\!+1\}$—can remove virtually all floating‑point multiplications while retaining dense connectivity.


From a storage perspective a half‑precision scalar occupies two bytes while an unsigned eight‑bit integer occupies one byte; a ternary value can be encoded even more compactly using just two bits per element if packed efficiently (the “1.58 bits” figure reflects an average code length after accounting for sparsity induced by zeros). Thus moving from an FP16 backbone toward integer‐centric kernels reduces raw model size by roughly a factor three or more.


# Post‑training quantization (PTQ) versus quantization‑aware training (QAT)  

**Post‑training quantization** refers to converting a fully trained network into a lower precision format without further adjustment of its parameters. This approach has become prevalent because it requires no additional data‐centric retraining steps; practitioners merely apply static scale factors learned from calibration data sets \cite{smoothquant,gptq,quip,quip_sharp}. The resulting models often achieve substantial reductions in memory bandwidth when inference runs on commodity GPUs or specialized accelerators.



Although convenient, PTQ is intrinsically *sub‑optimal*. Because the full set of model parameters was optimized under high‐precision arithmetic during pretraining, forcing them into a narrow discrete set afterwards inevitably introduces approximation error that cannot be remedied without modifying the underlying weights themselves.



**Quantization‑aware training**, by contrast, integrates low‑bit constraints directly into the optimization loop so that network parameters learn representations compatible with constrained arithmetic from the outset. In practice this involves replacing standard linear operators (`nn.Linear`) with custom kernels (`BitLinear`) whose forward pass projects raw weights onto a discrete set before performing any computation \cite{bitnet}. Gradient backpropagation proceeds through this projection step using straight‑through estimators so that learning continues despite non‐differentiable rounding.



The BitNet b1\.58 family embodies this philosophy by **training from scratch** with binary sign weights augmented by an explicit zero state (\{-1,\!0,\!+1\}). Throughout pretraining each weight matrix $W\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}$ undergoes an *absmean* projection defined mathematically as  

\[
\widetilde{W}
=
\operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\,+1\Bigr),
\qquad
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)\!=\!\max\!\bigl(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))\bigr),
\]  

with  

\[
\gamma
=
\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|
\]  

and $\epsilon$ a small constant preventing division by zero.\footnote{All symbols follow conventional notation introduced in Section 2 of the source manuscript.\cite{bitnet}}  
After projection every entry $\widetilde W_{ij}$ belongs strictly to $\{-1,\!0,\!+1\}$.\footnote{$0$ provides an explicit “feature filter’’ enabling selective deactivation without sacrificing signed connectivity.\cite{bitnet}}  

Because this projection occurs *during* each forward pass, gradients push raw parameters $W$ toward configurations whose discretized counterparts $\widetilde W$ already yield good loss values—a fundamentally different paradigm from post–training clipping strategies.\footnote{Standard PTQ methods do not alter learning dynamics after training is complete.\cite{smoothing}}  



In summary, PTQ applies static post hoc discretisation while QAT embeds discretisation into learning itself; BitNet b1\.58 demonstrates empirically that QAT allows extreme compression down to an average “bit depth’’ of $\,b = 1.58\, $bits without sacrificing perplexity or downstream task performance once model scale exceeds roughly three billion parameters.



# Common low‑bit schemes: 8‑bit uniform quantisation, 4‑bit techniques (SmoothQuant, GPTQ)  

A pragmatic first step towards reducing inference expense has been **uniform eight‑bit integer quantisation** of both weights and activations across all layers \cite{smoothing,gptq}. This approach maps each real value $x$ onto an unsigned eight–bit code via an affine transformation  
$x_q = \textstyle{\operatorname{round}}\!\bigl(x / s\bigr)+z$,  
where $s$ denotes a per–tensor scale factor and $z$ an optional zero point chosen so that $x_q\,∈\, \bigl[0,Q_{b}\bigr]$. Uniform eight–bit kernels preserve most linear algebraic relationships while halving memory bandwidth relative to FP16.



To push memory footprints further many researchers have explored **four–bit representations** achieved through sophisticated post–training procedures such as **SmoothQuant** \cite{smoothquant} and **GPTQ** \cite{gptq}. These methods typically perform per–channel scaling coupled with layerwise optimisation procedures that minimise reconstruction error when mapping high precision matrices onto four–bit codebooks.\footnote{GPTQ builds a greedy greedy approximation over groups of rows while SmoothQuant smooths out outlier activations before static calibration.\cite{smoothing}}  They often resort either to symmetric int4 encodings or asymmetric ones that preserve signed ranges via learned zero points.\footnote{$[-2^{b-1},\,2^{b-1}-1]$ intervals are common choices.\cite{smoothing}}  


Although four–bit PTQ substantially lowers storage demands—by another factor two comparedwith int8—it does not eliminate *multiplications* inside matrix products because even four–bit integers require multiplication hardware when paired against eight–bit activations during inference.\footnote{$a_i · b_j$ remains necessary regardless of operand width unless operands are binary or ternary.\cite{energycost}} As a result energetic gains plateau once below eight bits unless further architectural modifications occur.



The **BitNet b1\.58** design takes another radical step away from multi­precision matrix multiplications altogether: its weight matrices are ternary (${-}​$sign plus optional zeros) which permits representing each product simply as either addition or subtraction—or omission altogether when $0$ appears—followed exclusively by **INT8 addition** pipelines \( W_{ij}\cdot X_{j} \equiv (\pm X_{j}) + (\textbf0)\). Consequently its core kernel comprises no explicit multiplications nor accumulations beyond simple signed accumulation across rows—a property directly leveraged by specialised two‐bit kernels contributed by Ladder~\cite{ladder}.



Hence modern low‐bit schemes occupy a spectrum ranging from naïve eight‐bit uniform mappings through increasingly aggressive four­‐bit PTQs toward fully binarised / ternarised architectures where computation itself transforms underpinning arithmetic patterns.

Binary and Ternary Neural Networks: Theory and Benefits

The last decade has witnessed an unprecedented scaling of large language models (LLMs). While their capabilities have grown dramatically, so have their demands for memory bandwidth, compute cycles, and power consumption. A natural response has been to search for more economical representations of model parameters without sacrificing accuracy. In the extreme case, one may ask whether an LLM can be built whose weights take only one bit of information each. This question motivated the development of **BitNet**—the first architecture that trains Transformers with strictly binary ( ± 1 ) weights—and its subsequent extension **BitNet b1.58**, which augments the binary set with a third value, zero. In what follows we unpack the theoretical underpinnings of representing weights with roughly one and a half bits, explain why the inclusion of zero benefits both expressivity and sparsity, review how this line of research builds upon earlier binarized neural network efforts in vision and natural‑language processing, examine why eliminating multiplications yields massive energy savings on modern silicon, and finally acknowledge the limitations inherent to such low‑precision representations.

---

# (a) Information Content Calculation Leading to the 1.58‑bit Figure

When a network’s weight matrix is constrained to a *finite* alphabet, each element conveys only a limited amount of information. The classical measure from information theory is Shannon entropy,
\[
H \;=\; -\!\sum_{i} p_i \log_2 p_i,
\]
where \(p_i\) denotes the probability of symbol \(i\). If we assume the three admissible values \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) are used uniformly—a reasonable first‑order approximation when no further bias is imposed—the probabilities are all \(p_i = \frac13\). Substituting yields
\[
H 
   = -3\!\left(\frac13\right)\log_2 \!\left(\frac13\right)
   = \log_2 3
   \approx 1.585\;\text{bits}.
\]
Thus an *ideal* ternary weight distribution carries roughly **1.58 bits** of information per parameter rather than two full bits required by a naïve binary encoding (e.g., “00”, “01”, “10”, “11”). This observation justifies denoting models that employ ternary weights as **“1‑bit LLMs”** although strictly speaking each weight occupies slightly more than one bit when stored efficiently—hence the term **“BitNet b1.58”** used throughout this work.

The concrete quantization scheme employed by BitNet b1.58 reflects this constraint directly on every linear layer’s weight matrix \(W\). First an *average absolute* magnitude,
\[
\gamma
     \;=\;
     \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|,
\]
is computed across an \(n\times m\) matrix; then each entry is scaled by \(\gamma^{-1}\) (with a tiny stabilizer \(\epsilon\)) and rounded/clipped into \{-1,0,+1\}:
\[
\widetilde{W}
   = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\biggl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\, -1,\, +1 \biggr),
   \qquad
   \operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)
   = \max(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))).
\]
Because every element now belongs to a three‑symbol alphabet whose effective entropy equals \( \log_2 3\), it follows mathematically that each stored weight contributes only about **\(1.58\)** bits toward model size—a dramatic compression relative to standard FP16 (\(16​​\,\,bits/weight\)) or even BF16 representations.

---

# (b) Role of the Zero Value as an Implicit Feature Filter / Sparsity Promoter

The presence of **zero** among permissible weights confers two complementary benefits that go beyond pure compression:

### Implicit Feature Suppression

In any linear transformation \(y = Wx + b\), each column of \(W\) determines how an input feature influences downstream activations. When an entry becomes exactly zero, that particular input–output coupling disappears altogether—no multiplication nor addition takes place during inference. Consequently zeros act as *hard gates* that prevent certain features from propagating forward. By allowing the optimizer to set weights precisely at zero during training, BitNet b1.58 gains an **explicit mechanism for feature filtering** without resorting to external sparsity regularizers or pruning heuristics.

### Encouragement of Sparse Connectivity

Because zero costs nothing when executing integer arithmetic—a weight value of zero contributes no addition—the network naturally gravitates toward connections that cannot be removed without hurting performance less than removing beneficial ones needs more training data or larger capacity elsewhere in the model. Empirically this effect manifests as slightly higher accuracy than pure binary models when trained from scratch at comparable sizes: with only three symbols instead of two, BitNet b1.58 *matches* full‑precision baselines starting at three billion parameters while retaining lower memory footprints and latency budgets.
In practice this means that while every parameter occupies roughly \(1.58​ bits\), many entries effectively cost **zero compute cycles** during inference because they resolve into pure skips over addition operations.

These properties are key drivers behind why BitNet b1.58 outperforms its strict binary predecessor despite using similarly compact encodings—the extra symbol tailors both representational expressivity and computational economy through targeted sparsity.

# (c) Prior Work on Binarized Networks in Computer Vision and NLP

Low‑bit neural networks are not novel per se; they trace back to early attempts at extreme compression for *computer‑vision* workloads where convolutional layers dominate compute budgets:

* **BinaryConnect** introduced training directly with binary weights (\(\pm 1\)) while keeping activations high precision.
* Subsequent works such as **XNOR‑Net** extended this idea to both weights and activations together via clever scaling factors.
* A large corpus of research subsequently explored variance reduction techniques (e.g., straight‑through estimators), loss scaling tricks, or hybrid schemes where subsets of layers remain higher precision—all with mixed success on ImageNet‑scale tasks.

In natural‑language processing these ideas migrated more cautiously because language models rely heavily on large transformer matrices whose statistical properties differ from convolutional filters trained on images:
* Early attempts mainly applied **post‑training quantization** techniques such as *SmoothQuant*, *GPTQ*, *QuIP*, and *QuIP‑Sharp*—procedures that compress an already trained FP16/ BF16 checkpoint down to four or fewer bits without altering training dynamics.
* While these methods dramatically reduced memory bandwidth during inference—allowing successful deployment of GPT‑style models on commodity GPUs—they remained “sub‑optimal” according to empirical observations because they could not fully recover baseline perplexities after aggressive bit reductions.
* The seminal **BitNet** architecture lifted binarization from post‑hoc compression into the **training loop itself**, thereby allowing gradient updates to adapt directly under ternary constraints from scratch—an approach akin to earlier BinaryConnect works yet repurposed for transformers.
* By demonstrating parity with full precision starting at modest scales (~3 B parameters), BitNet established a proof point that binarized *LLMs* could be built without surrendering perplexity or downstream task accuracy—a milestone unattained by previous vision‑oriented binarization papers or NLP post‑training schemes alike.

Consequently BitNet b1.58 stands atop this lineage: it inherits insights from early vision binarizations concerning gradient handling under discrete constraints while integrating modern transformer engineering choices—RMSNorm, SwiGLU activations, rotary positional embeddings—and open‑source-friendly components adopted from LLaMA families so that it can be dropped into contemporary huggingface / vLLM pipelines without extra plumbing effort.

---

# (d) Advantages in Arithmetic Energy Cost When Multiplications Disappear

At first glance reducing weight precision seems primarily about shrinking storage footprints; however its most profound implication lies in **energy consumption** during matrix multiplications—the computational heart of transformers.

### From Floating‐Point Multiply–Add to Integer Add

In standard FP16 transformers each product \(w_{ij} x_j\) requires:
* A floating‐point multiplication (\(w_{ij} \times x_j\)) which consumes substantially more gate switching energy than integer addition.
* A subsequent floating‐point addition accumulating across columns.
By contrast, when all weights belong to \{-1,\!0,\!+1\} then:
* Multiplication collapses into either copying (\(+x_j\)), negating (\(-x_j\)), or **skipping** (\(0\times x_j=0\)). In hardware these copies/negations can be implemented via simple sign flips or wiring changes without any arithmetic unit activation.
* Accumulation becomes purely **integer addition**, typically carried out over INT8 operands because activations remain eight‑bit after quantization.
Thus product formation no longer requires any multiply operation—a fundamental change known colloquially as “the almost‑no‐multiplication paradigm”.

### Quantitative Energy Savings

Empirical estimates anchored on silicon‐level energy models reveal dramatic reductions:
* On a contemporary 7 nm process node spanning both SRAM alloys and logic gates—as used by NVIDIA’s Hopper GPUs—the energy cost per INT8 addition falls roughly two orders of magnitude below that required by an FP16 multiply–add pair.
* Figure 5 in the study reports a **71.4×** decrease in arithmetic‐operation energy consumption for matrix multiplication when moving from FP16 LLaMA baselines toward BitNet b1.58’s int8 add pipeline.
Moreover because linear layers dominate total ops count—increasing proportionally with model depth—the overall end‑to‑end energy advantage compounds at larger scales (13 B → 70 B parameters), making high‑capacity LLMs practically viable under tight power budgets typical for data-center inference servers or edge devices.

### System-Level Consequences

Eliminating floating-point multipliers reduces pressure on chip floorplan design:
* Memory bandwidth becomes dominant since data movement across DRAM → SRAM now carries merely one extra bit per weight rather than sixteen.
* On-chip dataflow engines can exploit ternary encoding via custom logic gates designed expressly for add‐only kernels—as demonstrated through integration with Ladder’s “2‑bit kernel”.
Consequently latency shrinks dramatically—empirically up to **4× faster** for a seventy‑billion parameter model—with memory usage dropping by roughly **$3\times$**, aligning perfectly with real-world throughput requirements documented across assorted benchmarks (ARC‑Easy/Easy Challenge sets etc.).

---

# (e) Potential Downsides Such as Reduced Representational Capacity

While ternary encodings engender compelling gains in speed and power efficiency it inevitably introduces *constraints on expressive power*. Three interrelated concerns merit attention:

### Limited Weight Granularity

A full-precision weight can occupy any point within a continuous real interval—a freedom necessary when modeling fine-grained relationships between tokens or visual features alike.
By forcing all connections into just three discretized levels (\(-1,\!0,\!+\,\,)\), certain delicate variations become impossible to capture directly.
Empirically this manifests most strongly at smaller scales: models below three billion parameters still lag behind their FP16 counterparts both in perplexity and downstream zero-shot accuracies despite otherwise identical token budgets and architectural designs.
Hence there appears indeed a threshold where capacity—and therefore scale—must compensate for coarser granularity.

### Training Instability & Gradient Approximation Errors

When updates must push continuous gradients onto discrete sets,
optimizers resort to straight-through estimators (“pretend” rounding during backpropagation), which introduces bias between true loss gradients and those perceived by parameters.
If not carefully calibrated—for instance through appropriate scaling factor $\gamma$ in Eqn.\;(2)—the training dynamics may either stall prematurely or wander toward suboptimal local minima where many weights saturate at ± 1 unnecessarily.
Although BitNet’s absolute-mean scaling ameliorates this issue compared with naive sign functions,
it nevertheless remains more delicate than optimizing unrestricted FP16 parameters where gradient flow remains exact throughout training steps.

### Hardware Compatibility Hurdles

Even though integer addition circuitry is ubiquitous today,
most general-purpose accelerators—including GPUs widely used today—still devote substantial silicon area toward floating-point units because most deep learning workloads still rely heavily upon them.
Therefore exploiting BitNet’s advantages fully often requires custom kernels (e.g., Ladder’s “2-bit kernel”) or even dedicated hardware designs akin to Groq’s LPUs described later.
If such kernels cannot be efficiently compiled across all target platforms—to say nothing about existing compiler pipelines—the realized speedup may fall short relative to theoretical expectations noted in our tables.


In summary these drawbacks imply that while ternary models open an exciting avenue towards ultra-low-cost LLMs,
designers must jointly consider *model size*, *training stability*, and *deployment hardware*. Practitioners typically address these concerns by scaling up parameter count beyond minimal thresholds (≥ 3B), employing robust quantization-aware training pipelines guided by absolute-mean scaling rules outlined above, or targeting hardware accelerators explicitly engineered around add-only matrix products.

The Original BitNet Architecture
=================================

In this chapter we examine the first generation of “Bit‑only” large language models, known as **BitNet**.  BitNet demonstrated that it is possible to replace every dense matrix multiplication inside a Transformer with an operation that uses **only integer additions** while preserving most of the predictive power of full‑precision models.  We first review the Transformer components that were affected by this redesign, then describe the **BitLinear** layer that implements addition‑only matrix products, outline the training pipeline employed by the authors, report the empirical gains observed over conventional FP16 baselines, and finally discuss the shortcomings that motivated a move toward a ternary (“1‑bit + zero”) variant introduced later as *BitNet b1.58*.

---

# Standard Transformer Components Relevant to BitNet  

Large language models are built on the canonical **Transformer** architecture introduced by Vaswani *et al.* A typical decoder‑only Transformer consists of repeatedly stacked layers, each layer containing three principal sub‑modules:

1. **Multi‑Head Self‑Attention (MHSA)** – For an input token sequence represented as a matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}$ ($L$ tokens, $d$ hidden dimension), attention first computes three linear projections:
   \[
   Q = XW_Q,\qquad K = XW_K,\qquad V = XW_V,
   \]
   where $W_Q,W_K,W_V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_h}$ are learned weight matrices ($d_h=d/\text{num\_heads}$).  
   The attention scores are then formed by scaled dot‑products $QK^\top$, followed by a softmax weighting applied to $V$.  
   In standard implementations each projection uses an **nn.Linear** module realized through dense floating‑point matrix multiplication ($\text{FP16}$ or $\text{BF16}$).

2. **Feed‑Forward Network (FFN)** – After attention, each token passes through a two‑layer MLP:
   \[
   \text{FFN}(x)=\sigma(xW_1+b_1)W_2+b_2,
   \]
   where $W_1\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d_{\text{ff}}}$ expands the dimension ($d_{\text{ff}}\approx4d$), $\sigma$ denotes an activation such as GELU or SwiGLU~\cite{swiglu}, and $W_2\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}\times d}$ projects back down.  
   Both $W_1$ and $W_2$ again rely on dense floating‑point linear layers.

3. **Normalization & Residual Connections** – Each sub‑module is wrapped with a residual connection followed by a normalization step (originally LayerNorm~\cite{layernorm}, here RMSNorm~\cite{rmsnorm}).  
   Bias terms are absent in BitNet’s implementation – all linear projections are bias‑free – which simplifies quantization without harming performance appreciably.

These three building blocks dominate inference cost because each forward pass repeatedly evaluates large matrix multiplications whose operand sizes grow quadratically with model width. Consequently any reduction in the arithmetic complexity of these multiplications directly translates into latency, memory bandwidth, and energy savings—a fact capitalized upon by BitNet’s redesign.

---

# BitLinear Layer Implementation Details – Integer‑Addition‑Only Matrix Multiplication  

The core contribution of BitNet is **BitLinear**, a drop‑in replacement for PyTorch’s `nn.Linear`.  Rather than storing arbitrary floating‑point weights $W$, each weight tensor is constrained to **binary values**:
\[
\widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,+1\}.
\]
The conversion from real‐valued parameters $W$ (the “shadow” weights maintained during training) into their binary counterpart follows an *absmean* scaling strategy:
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bigl|W_{ij}\bigr|,
\qquad
\widetilde{W}= \operatorname{Sign}\!\bigl(\tfrac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon}\bigr),
\]
where $(n,m)$ denote the dimensions of $W$, $\varepsilon$ guards against division by zero, and `Sign` rounds every entry toward its nearest integer among $\{-1,+1\}$.  
The scalar $\gamma$ captures the average magnitude of $W$ so that after binarization we retain an approximation
\[
W \approx \gamma\, \widetilde{W}.
\]

In a forward pass `BitLinear` computes
\[
Y = X\,(\gamma\, \widetilde{W})^\top + b,
\]
but crucially this expression can be rearranged so that **no floating‑point multiplications appear**:
* Multiplying by $+1$ corresponds simply to adding the input column,
* Multiplying by $-1$ corresponds simply to subtracting it,
* The global scale factor $\gamma$ is applied once after accumulating all signed contributions,
* Adding bias $b$ remains an integer addition under low‐bit activation representation (see next section).

Thus each output element reduces formally to an **integer sum**:
\[
y_k = \gamma\,\Bigl(\sum_{j:\,\widetilde{w}_{kj}=+1}x_j - 
                  \sum_{j:\,\widetilde{w}_{kj}=-1}x_j \Bigr) + b_k .
\]
Because $\widetilde{w}_{kj}\in\{-1,+1\}$ their sign can be stored compactly using one bit per weight; all subsequent arithmetic operates on unsigned or signed integers (typically int8 or int32 accumulators).  

From an implementation perspective this eliminates costly floating‐point multiply–accumulate units for every Linear operation inside MHSA‐projections and FFNs—a decisive gain given that >90 % of total FLOPs in modern decoder‐only LLMs arise from such dense linear maps.

---

# Training Pipeline Used in the Original BitNet Paper  

Training Binary‐weight LLMs requires retaining differentiability despite discrete parameters during forward propagation.  The authors adopt a conventional pretraining regimen akin to other open–source models:

* **Dataset & Token Budget** – Models were trained on the RedPajama collection~\cite{redpajama}, comprising diverse web crawls filtered for quality English text.  All experiments use a fixed budget of **100 billion tokens**, guaranteeing commensurate exposure across model sizes.
* **Model Initialization** – Real‐valued shadow weights $W$ are initialized using standard schemes (e.g., Xavier/Glorot), ensuring balanced magnitudes before binarization.
* **Forward Pass** – Before each forward evaluation the shadow weights are projected onto their binary equivalents $\widetilde W$ via the absmean quantizer described above.
* **Backward Pass & Gradient Approximation** – Gradients flow through $\widetilde W$ onto $W$, typically employing a straight‑through estimator whereby $\partial \widetilde W/\partial W\simeq 1$ wherever $|W|<(\gamma+\varepsilon)$. Although such tricks are common in low–precision literature, their exact formulation follows directly from employing `RoundClip` during binarization.
* **Optimizer & Hyperparameters** – A standard Adam‐style optimizer drives updates on $W$, while learning rates follow cosine decay schedules identical to those employed when training full precision baselines.
* **Activation Quantization** – While weights remain binary throughout training, activations are represented using **8‑bit integers**, matching prior work on low‑bit Transformers~\cite{bitnet}. This choice balances reduced bandwidth against sufficient dynamic range for backpropagation stability.
* **Training Infrastructure** – Experiments use NVIDIA GPUs equipped with CUDA kernels optimized for integer additions (“2‑bit kernel” from Ladder~\cite{ladder}) integrated into NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer library~\cite{FasterTransformer} for efficient inference throughput measurement during validation steps.\footnote{Although inference kernels differ marginally from pure training kernels, they share identical arithmetic semantics because both rely solely on addition operations after binarizing weights.}

Overall, aside from swapping `nn.Linear` → `BitLinear`, every other aspect—including data parallelism strategy, learning rate schedule, dropout configuration—mirrors pipelines established for full precision LLM pretraining.

# Observed Speedup and Memory Savings Compared With FP16 Baselines  

Because matrix multiplications dominate both compute time *and* memory traffic, eliminating their multiplications yields measurable advantages across several axes:

### Latency Improvements  
For models sized **3 B parameters**, empirical measurements showed:
* Time per generated token decreased by roughly **2.7 ×** relative to an FP16 “LLaMA” reference trained under identical conditions.
* As model width grows larger—e.g., **70 B parameters**—the speedup rises further (**≈ 4 ×**) because more linear layers become active learners and thus benefit proportionally from addition‐only kernels.
These gains stem mainly from two sources:
* Integer adders incur far fewer gate delays than floating point multipliers;
* Memory bandwidth becomes less limiting when each weight occupies merely one bit instead of sixteen.



### Memory Footprint Reduction  
Storing binary masks instead of half‑precision floats cuts raw weight storage size by a factor close to **16 ×**, modulo overhead required for per‑matrix scaling factors ($\gamma$) which constitute negligible bytes compared with billions of weights.
Consequently:
* Peak GPU memory consumption drops by roughly **3–4 ×**, depending on model size;
* The reduced footprint frees additional capacity for larger batch sizes or longer context windows during inference.



### Throughput Gains  
When deploying two NVIDIA A100 GPUs (**80 GB each**) under pipeline parallelism:
* A 70 B parameter *BitLinear* model accommodated batch sizes up to **11 times larger**, delivering an end–to–end throughput improvement close to **9 ×** over its FP16 counterpart;
* The lower memory demand also cuts out frequent off–chip DRAM traffic when loading checkpoints.


### Energy Savings  
A simplified energy model based on chip‐level operation costs~[~energycost~,~pokebnn~] shows:
* An INT8 addition consumes orders of magnitude less energy than an FP16 multiply–add unit;
* Over a full generation pass consisting mostly of `BitLinear`, total arithmetic energy drops by approximately **71 ×** compared against dense FP16 execution on modern 7 nm processes.


Collectively these results establish **BitNet** as a Pareto improvement over standard half­precision Transformers: it delivers comparable accuracy while dramatically reducing latency, memory pressure, and electricity usage.

Designing BitNet b1.58: Ternary Weights and Activation Strategies  

# AbsMean Weight Quantizer Formulation  

The central novelty of BitNet b1.58 lies in forcing every learnable parameter onto a ternary alphabet $\{-1,0,+1\}$.  Achieving this requires a deterministic mapping from a full‑precision matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}$ onto a discrete counterpart $\widetilde W$.  The authors adopt an “AbsMean’’ scheme that first normalizes $W$ by its average absolute magnitude and then applies a clipping round operation that never leaves the interval $[-1,\,1]$.  

Mathematically the mapping is written  
\[
\widetilde W=\operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\,-1,\,+1\Big),
\]  
where $\epsilon$ is a tiny constant introduced solely to avoid division‑by‑zero when $W$ contains only zeros.  The auxiliary function $\operatorname{RoundClip}$ enforces integer values while respecting hard bounds:
\[
\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\!\big(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))\big).
\]  
Because $\operatorname{round}(\cdot)$ returns an integer nearest to its argument, the combined effect produces three possible outcomes—$-1$, $0$, or $+1$—for each entry independently after clipping ensures that any overflow is pulled back into the permissible interval.\  

The scalar $\gamma$ determines precisely how aggressively $W$ must be scaled before discretisation:
\[
\gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\big|W_{ij}\big|.
\]  
This expression computes the arithmetic mean of absolute values across every element of $W$.  Intuitively $\gamma$ measures the typical magnitude present in the matrix; dividing $W$ by $\gamma$ rescales all entries so that their average absolute size becomes unity.  Consequently most entries lie near zero after scaling, making it likely that many become exactly zero after rounding whilst preserving those whose sign carries essential information._Zero_ values therefore emerge naturally rather than through an ad‑hoc pruning step._  

The presence of the explicit “zero’’ state has an important functional implication discussed elsewhere in the paper: it enables *feature filtering*. When a weight becomes exactly zero its contribution disappears from every subsequent dot product, effectively turning off a particular connection without incurring any arithmetic cost during inference—a property impossible in pure binary ($\{-1,+1\}$) schemes where every connection must either add or subtract a fixed amount._

Through repeated application during training—the real‑valued copy of $W$ receives gradient updates while $\widetilde W$, produced by the AbsMean rule above, drives forward passes—the model learns an internal representation compatible with extreme compression yet retains expressive power comparable to full‑precision baselines.



# Activation Quantization  

While weights are forced onto a ternary alphabet, activations remain higher precision because they convey richer intermediate information essential for attention mechanisms and feed‑forward layers.  In the original BitNet design activations were first scaled into an *unsigned* interval $[0,Q_b]$, where $Q_b$ denotes the maximal representable integer given an $b$‑bit format (e.g., $Q_8=255$).  A separate “zero point’’ would then be stored so that dequantisation could recover signed values later.\  

BitNet b1.58 replaces this two‑step procedure by applying a *signed* scale directly at token level:
each token’s activation vector is linearly rescaled into the symmetric range $[-Q_b,\,+Q_b]$.  No additional offset—the classic zero point—is required because negative integers already encode negative magnitudes intrinsically.\  

Formally let $a$ denote an unquantised activation tensor belonging to a single token’s computation graph.  The quantised version $\widehat a$ satisfies  
\[
\widehat a = \operatorname{clip}_{[-Q_b,Q_b]}\!\big(\operatorname{round}(s\,a)\big),
\]  
where $s>0$ denotes a token‑specific scaling factor chosen such that most entries fall inside $[-Q_b,Q_b]$, and $\operatorname{clip}_{[-Q_b,Q_b]}(\cdot)$ forces outliers back onto the interval borders.\  
Because every token enjoys its own scale factor there is no global overhead incurred when processing batches containing heterogeneous magnitudes—each token simply decides its own clipping threshold independently._This per‑token strategy simplifies hardware pipelines_: modern integer arithmetic units already operate naturally on two’s‑complement signed integers, eliminating “zero point subtraction’’ stages that would otherwise consume cycles and power.\  

Experimental evidence reported later confirms that moving from an unsigned interval with explicit zero points to this signed formulation incurs “negligible effects’’ on downstream perplexity and end‑task accuracy despite dramatically simplifying implementation details.



# LLaMA‑Alike Backbone Changes  

To make extreme quantisation practical without sacrificing architectural familiarity, BitNet b1.58 inherits most structural components from the open‑source LLaMA family—an intentional design decision meant to ease adoption across existing ecosystems.\  

### RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm  

Layer‐normalisation traditionally computes both mean subtraction and variance‐based scaling per token prior to applying learnable gain/bias parameters.  In contrast RMSNorm replaces variance estimation with a root–mean–square measure computed over hidden dimensions while omitting any additive bias term altogether._Because RMSNorm requires fewer arithmetic operations (essentially one reduction followed by reciprocal square root) it aligns naturally with low‐bit hardware where every extra addition translates into measurable latency._ Moreover eliminating learnable bias entries reduces parameter storage—a particularly valuable savings once every other parameter has been squashed down to three possible values._  

### SwiGLU Activation Function  

Feed–forward sublayers conventionally rely on simple ReLU or GeLU nonlinearities applied after linear projection.   SwiGLU—short for “Swish × Gated Linear Unit’’—interleaves two parallel linear projections whose elementwise product yields richer gating behaviour while still consisting solely of matrix multiplications followed by inexpensive elementwise operations (a sigmoid–like Swish unit applied componentwise).   When weights feeding SwiGLU are already ternary these multiplications collapse into simple additions/subtractions conditioned on gate activations, preserving expressive power despite aggressive compression._  

### Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE)  

Positional information remains indispensable for transformer attention despite extreme weight binarisation.   Rotary embeddings embed relative positions directly into query/key vectors through elementwise phase rotations rather than adding dedicated positional vectors.\  
Because rotation operations are mathematically equivalent up­to​a precomputation stage involving sine/cosine tables—all reproducible using integer approximations—the resulting attention scores retain high fidelity even when subsequently multiplied by ternary matrices.\  
Adopting RoPE therefore allows BitNet b1.58 to inherit LLaMA’s proven ability to extrapolate beyond trained sequence lengths without introducing extra learnable parameters that would otherwise defy ternarisation.__  

### Removal of All Bias Terms  

Every linear transformation in vanilla transformers contains an affine offset (“bias’’) added after matrix multiplication. Under full precision this cost is negligible relative to expensive floating‐point multiplications.​ In a setting where multiplications have been eliminated altogether—only integer additions remain—a bias would reintroduce an unavoidable add per neuron._Consequently **all** bias terms are stripped away in BitNet b1.58._ This omission yields three concrete benefits:
* It reduces memory traffic because fewer scalar values must be fetched from DRAM/SRAM;
* It removes an entire class of arithmetic operations from hot kernels;
* It dovetails perfectly with hardware designs focused exclusively on addition pipelines—a key premise behind modern “LPU’’ prototypes designed for binary nets._  

Collectively these four adjustments preserve most functionality exhibited by vanilla LLaMA models while abolishing components whose cost would otherwise dominate under a 1‑bit regime.



# Integration Pathways  

A primary goal articulated throughout the work is **compatibility**: developers should be able to swap into existing open–source stacks without substantial rewrites.\  

### HuggingFace Transformers  

Within HuggingFace’s `transformers` library every dense layer is instantiated as `nn.Linear`.   Replacing this class globally with `BitLinear`—a thin wrapper implementing Eq.~(above) on forward passes whilst preserving standard PyTorch autograd semantics—allows an entire LLaMA checkpoint written originally in FP16/BF16 format to run under BitNet b1.58 with just one import change._Because `BitLinear` adheres precisely to PyTorch’s module API (same constructor signature, same `.weight` attribute shape), downstream code such as pipelines for generation or evaluation continues unaltered._   Model checkpoints generated by training scripts automatically contain auxiliary tensors storing per‑layer scales $\gamma$, making loading trivial:\ 
```python
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("my_bitnet_3B", torch_dtype=torch.float16)
model = replace_linear_with_bitlinear(model)
```  
This workflow mirrors exactly what the authors report when “integrated…with minimal efforts.”  

### vLLM Inference Engine  

vLLM optimises transformer decoding through dynamic batch scheduling and fused kernels written in CUDA/C++.   The authors evaluate latency using NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer backend enriched by Ladder’s custom **2‑bit** kernel specifically tuned for matrices whose left operand consists solely of ternary values.^[Ladder kernel source https://github.com/NVIDIA/FasterTransformer].   By registering `BitLinear`’s matmul routine as an alternative implementation under vLLM’s kernel registry, requests processed through vLLM automatically benefit from the same speedups reported (`2×`–`4×` latency reductions) without any modification at inference time aside from loading the converted checkpoint._   

### llama.cpp Runtime  

On resource-constrained devices”—desktop CPUs or even ARM cores”—the community relies heavily on `llama.cpp`, which implements pure C/C++ inference capable of running entirely offline.~[llama.cpp repository https://github.com/ggerganov/llama.cpp].   
BitNet b1.58’s adherence to LLaMA’s module layout guarantees binary compatibility at checkpoint level once conversion scripts transform floating‐point tensors into packed ternary representations together with their associated scaling factors (`gamma`).   After conversion one simply compiles `llama.cpp` against the supplied weight files; no source code changes are required because `llama.cpp` treats all matrices uniformly during GEMM execution—and its optional INT8 kernels seamlessly accept ± µ scale factors introduced during quantisation._ The resulting executable inherits all memory savings documented in Table 2 (“3½× less GPU memory”) while preserving identical prompt APIs offered by vanilla LLaMA models.~   

Thus through three widely adopted ecosystems—PyTorch/HuggingFace on GPUs/TPUs, vLLM for high-throughput server deployments, and llama.cpp for edge CPUs—BitNet b1.58 offers an *out‑of‑the‑box* experience despite its radical reduction to **binary** weights plus signed‐8\-bit activations._

In summary, BitNet b1\.58 realizes an unprecedented tradeoff between model size and computational cost by imposing an analytically simple yet powerful AbsMean ternarisation pipeline on weights,
adopting signed per-token activation scaling,
and borrowing proven components from LLaMA while stripping away any redundancy—a combination that yields parity with FP16 baselines at merely one sixth of their memory footprint,
and up­to​ four times faster inference under identical hardware constraints.
These design decisions collectively embody what the authors refer to as **“the new scaling law”**, pointing toward future generations of large language models whose deployment will increasingly hinge upon bespoke bit–level architectures rather than raw floating point arithmetic alone.
