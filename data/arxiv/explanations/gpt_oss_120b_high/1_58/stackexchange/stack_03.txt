\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### What role does the "absmean" scaling factor $\gamma$ play in the weight quantizer?
Question:
The paper defines

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|,$$

and then computes ``RoundClip$(W / (\gamma + \epsilon), -\!\!\!

Why do they divide by *the average absolute value* rather than using other statistics such as ``max(|W|)`` or RMS?

I am also curious about how this choice influences:

* The sparsity pattern after rounding/clipping;
* Training stability across layers;
* The magnitude distribution fed into subsequent layers.
Answer:
In **BitNet b1.58** every learnable matrix $W∈ℝ^{n\times m}$ is kept in full‑precision during training and only its **ternarised** version  

$$
\widetilde W= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{γ+ε},\, -1,\, 1\bigr),
  γ=\frac 1 {nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
$$  

is used for the forward‑pass (the gradient flows through $W$ via a straight‑through estimator).  
The scalar $γ$ is therefore a **layer‑wise scale** that maps a “typical’’ magnitude of the raw weights onto the interval $[-1,\,1]$.

---

### Why the *average* absolute value?

* **Robustness to outliers** – The maximum $\max |W|$ is dictated by a single extreme entry. If one weight grows unusually large (which happens frequently during early training), $γ$ would become too big and almost every other entry would be divided down below $0.5$; after rounding almost all elements would turn into $0$. This makes the sparsity pattern erratic and destabilises learning.  

* **Preserving the central mass** – The mean absolute value $\displaystyle\rho=\frac 1 {nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|$ estimates the *typical* size of a weight under heavy‑tailed distributions that appear in deep nets. It ignores sign but captures scale without being dominated by rare large values, unlike an RMS  
$\displaystyle 
R=\sqrt{\frac 1 {nm}\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}^{2}},
$
which up‑weights large outliers even more strongly.

* **Simple and cheap** – Computing a sum of absolutes costs exactly the same order as any other statistic used during training and does not require a square–root operation.

Consequently $γ$ adapts smoothly as training progresses while keeping the effective dynamic range of $\widetilde W$ stable from layer to layer.

Thus an element becomes zero exactly when  

$$
|W_{ij}|<{\tfrac12} γ .
$$  

Since $γ$ equals the **average** absolute value, this threshold lies near the centre of the weight distribution. For a symmetric distribution whose density around zero is relatively high (e.g., Gaussian or Laplace), roughly one–third to one–half of the entries fall below $0.5 γ$; empirically BitNet reports a stable density $\approx 60\\%$ non‑zero across all layers and training steps. Using $\max |W|$ would push almost every entry below the $0.5 · \max |W|$ line → extreme sparsity; an RMS would push most entries even further below $0.5·{\rm RMS}$, again yielding far fewer active connections.

Therefore **abs‑mean scaling yields a predictable sparsity level** that is neither too aggressive (which would cripple model capacity) nor too dense (which would erase the benefits of ternarisation).

### Effect on training stability

Layer‑wise normalisation with $γ$ accomplishes two things that are crucial for stable optimisation:

1️⃣ **Controlled forward gain** – The pre‑activation at layer~$l$,  

$$
h^{(l)}=x^{(l-1)}\,{\widetilde W}^{(l)}
      =γ^{(l)}\,x^{(l-1)}S^{(l)},
      ~~S^{(l)}{}_{\! ij}\in{-1,\;0,\;+1},
$$  

has an expected magnitude proportional to $γ^{(l)}$. Because all matrices are normalised by their own average absolute value, the variance of $h^{(l)}$ stays roughly constant throughout depth, preventing exploding or vanishing activations—a problem often encountered when raw ternary matrices are used without scaling.

2️⃣ **Uniform gradient scale** – In straight‑through estimation the backward pass treats  

$$
\dfrac{\partial {\widetilde W}}{\partial W}\simeq\dfrac 1 {γ+ε}.
$$  

Hence gradients arriving at $W^{(l)}$ are automatically re–scaled inversely by its own typical magnitude, providing an effect similar to **weight normalisation** [Salimans & Kingma, 2016]. This self–regularising behaviour lets us keep standard learning–rate schedules across all layers without layer–specific tuning.

Because neither max nor RMS provide such a balanced re–scaling—max can make gradients vanish on most weights while RMS can make them excessively large—the abs–mean choice gives smoother optimisation dynamics throughout long LLM stacks.

---

### Magnitude seen by subsequent layers

After quantisation each effective weight equals either +$γ$, $0$, or ${-\!γ}$. Consequently any linear map behaves like a signed binary mask multiplied by a scalar gain $γ$.  

If we denote by 

$$
p=\Pr(|W|<0.5 γ)
$$  

the zero–probability, the second moment of $\widetilde W$ is  

$$
\E[\widetilde W^{2}] =(1-p)\,γ^{2}.
$$  

Since $p$/​is roughly constant across layers,
the variance injected into activations after each linear block stays close to
the variance produced by an unquantised matrix whose entries have standard deviation comparable to its mean absolute value.
Combined with RMSNorm—whose own normalisation removes any residual scale drift—the downstream modules receive activations of essentially unchanged magnitude compared with full‑precision training.



---

### Summary

Dividing by  

$$
γ=\frac 1 {nm}\sum _{{i,j}}|W_{ij}|
$$  

provides a simple yet robust per–tensor normalisation.
It matches the central bulk of the weight distribution without being swayed by occasional extremes (as *max* would be) or overly emphasizing large entries (*RMS*). This yields a **stable sparsity pattern**, keeps activation variances roughly identical from layer to layer, and automatically rescales gradients—all of which contribute to stable training of very deep transformers equipped with ternary weights.

The design mirrors other “scale‐by‐average’’ tricks used in post–training quantisation and weight normalisation literature, showing that even an ultra low–bit model such as BitNet b₁․58 can be trained from scratch with essentially the same hyper‐parameters employed for full‐precision LLMs.​
