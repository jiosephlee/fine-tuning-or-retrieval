# Scaling Law Redefined: Why a 13 B BitNet Beats a 3 B FP‑16 Model in Speed and Memory  

Large language models have taken us from dozens of millions of parameters to tens of billions—and now even beyond a hundred billion—while delivering impressive capabilities across almost every NLP benchmark. The downside? Storing and running these massive nets costs gigabytes of GPU RAM, massive amounts of energy, and latency that makes real‑time inference expensive or even infeasible on commodity hardware.

A recent preprint titled **“The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits”** puts forward a compelling answer: replace every floating‑point weight with a ternary value `{‑1, 0,+1}` while keeping activations at only eight bits (`int8`). The resulting architecture—called **BitNet b1.​58**—preserves accuracy yet slashes the resources needed for inference dramatically enough that a *larger* low‑bit model can run cheaper than an *older* half‑precision counterpart.

Below I unpack how the authors arrived at this “new scaling law,” walk through concrete numbers comparing a **13 B parameter** BitNet against a traditional **3 B FP‑16** LLaMA‑style model, and explain why the gap widens even more when you go up to tens‑of‑billions of parameters.



## A Quick Primer – Why Bits Matter

| Component | Typical FP‑16 implementation | BitNet b1.​58 |
|-----------|-----------------------------|----------------|
| Weight datatype | `float16` → **16 bits / weight** | Ternary `{‑1,0,+1}` → **effective ≈ log₂³ ≃ 1​.58 bits / weight** |
| Activation datatype | `float16` | `int8` (`8` bits) |
| Core operation | Matrix multiplication → many floating‑point multiplies + adds | Matrix multiplication → **only int8 adds** (no multiplies) |

Because training already normalises weights via the “absmean” quantiser  

\[
\widetilde W
     ~=~
     {\rm RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},~-1,\;+1\Bigr),
     \qquad
     \gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|,
\]

each linear layer ends up containing merely signed or zero entries—but never an actual floating‑point number.



### What Does “Effective ½‑bit” Mean?

A ternary alphabet carries three possibilities, whose Shannon entropy is  

\[
H_{\rm ternary}= \log_2 ³ \;\approx\; {\bf 1\.585}\ {\rm bits}.
\]

In practice the authors pack these symbols tightly (e.g., via bit‑plane compression), achieving an average cost reported as **“b​₁․₅₈”**, i.e., roughly one‐and‐a‐half bits per scalar instead of two bytes (`fp16`).  

Consequences are immediate:

* A ***13 B*** parameter net stores its weights in  
 \(13\times10^9\times{\bf 1\.6}\ {\rm bits}
   \;\approx\;26\times10^9\,{\rm bits}
   \;\approx\;{\bf 3.\!2\,GB}\)
  
* A ***3 B*** half‑precision net stores its weights in  
 \(   6\times10^9\,{\rm bytes}
   \;\approx {\bf 6.\!0\,GB}\).

Even before running any computation, the larger low‑bit model consumes **about half the VRAM** required by its much smaller full‐precision counterpart.



## The New Scaling Law – Bigger Low‑Bit Beats Smaller High‑Precision  

The authors distilled their empirical findings into three concrete equivalences (see Figure&nbsp;5 & Figure&nbsp;6 in the paper):

| Low‑bit Model | Equivalent or Better Than |
|---------------|---------------------------|
| **13 B** BitNet b​¹․⁵⁸ | Any **≥ 3 B** FP‑16 transformer |
| **30 B** BitNet b​¹․⁵⁸ | Any **≥ 7 B** FP‑16 transformer |
| **70 B** BitNet b​¹․⁵⁸ | Any **≥ 13 B** FP‑16 transformer |

In other words, once you cross roughly the *13–30–70 B* thresholds the gains compound—not only does latency drop and memory usage shrink compared with an equally sized half‑precision net, you also beat any *smaller* half‑precision net across three axes:

| Metric             | Quantitative gain observed |
|--------------------|----------------------------|
| **Inference latency** | Up to ***4×*** faster at ***70 B*** versus an equal‐size FP‑16 baseline |
| **GPU memory footprint** | Roughly ***4–5×*** lower when moving from FP‑16 → low‐bit at large scales |
| **Energy per token** | Multiplication costs disappear → estimated ***71×*** reduction in arithmetic‐operation energy on modern node‐processes |

Why does the advantage accelerate with scale? Matrix multiplications dominate both compute time (*nn.Linear*) and data movement (*weight fetches*) when models grow past ∼5–7 B parameters—the embedding layers become proportionally smaller and therefore contribute less overall bandwidth pressure. Since *BitLinear* replaces virtually all floating‐point multiplies with cheap int8 additions while slashing weight size by ≥ 90 %, every extra billions of parameters translates into almost linear savings rather than linear costs.



## Concrete Comparison – 13 B BitNet vs 3 B FP­‐16  

Below I fold together two strands supplied directly by the authors’ experiments:

### Memory Footprint

The paper reports absolute GPU RAM usage measured with NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer kernels (both models fed identical inputs).  

| Model                | Parameters | Peak GPU RAM |
|----------------------|------------|--------------|
| LLaMA (**FP₁₆**)    | `~ 3×10⁹` | `~ 6 GB` |
| **BitNet b¹․₅₈**     | `~ 13×10⁹` | `~ 12 GB` |

Even accounting for extra parameters (+310% ↑), total RAM increase stays below ***double*** instead of ⨉ 4 you’d expect from raw parameter count alone thanks to ternary packing.



### Latency Per Token

Using the same FasterTransformer setup plus Ladder’s custom “2‑bit” kernel employed for DeepSpeed/BitsandBytes kernels,

| Model                | Tokens / sec (single A100) |
|----------------------|----------------------------|
| LLaMA (**FP₁₆**)    | ≈ ​⟂ 130                 |
| **BitNet b¹․₅₈**, ‎13&nbsp; B | ≈ ​⟂ 500                |

That translates into roughly ***4×*** higher throughput (`tokens/s`) despite handling more than four times as many parameters.



### Energy Consumption

Figure&nbsp;7 isolates arithmetic energy costs associated exclusively with matrix multiplications—the dominant component (>60 %). By counting only INT8 additions versus combined FP₁₆ multiply–and–add operations, the authors estimate an average reduction factor of  

> $$E_{\rm Bitnet}\;\approx\;\frac{E_{\rm fp16}}{71}\;. $$  

When propagated through whole‐model inference this means generating ​512 tokens on an A100 consumes merely ‑­­­­­­­a few hundred millijoules versus several joules for the corresponding half‑precision run—a savings that scales upward as parameter count rises.



## Why Does Scale Amplify Advantages?

Three intertwined reasons make larger low‑bit nets ever more attractive:

> **Dominance of Linear Layers:** As models expand past ∼​7–15·​¹⁰⁹ parameters, almost every forward pass consists mostly of `nn.Linear`s (`Q·Kᵀ`, feedforward MLPs). In standard FP₁₆ those layers demand expensive multiply–add cycles (`FMA`). In *BitLinear*, each dot product collapses into an addition chain over `{− α,+ α}` signs—a hardware primitive that modern GPUs execute several orders faster per watt.

> **Memory Bandwidth Shrinks:** Bandwidth limits surface when loading billions of weights each step if stored uncompressed (`fp16`). Switching to ternary encodings cuts required traffic by nearly an order-of-magnitude (`Ω(maps)` → `Ω(maps)/9`). Consequently kernels spend far less time waiting on DRAM/SRAM transfers—a bottleneck that otherwise worsens quadratically with sequence length (`O(seq·dim)`).

> **Higher Utilisation Across Devices:** Because activations run all int8 already (the authors deliberately avoided zero-point offsets), pipelines such as vLLM or HuggingFace Transformers can treat tokens uniformly across layers without needing mixed-precision casts or special kernels—making deployment simpler on heterogeneous hardware ranging from GPUs up through custom LPUs patterned after Groq’s architecture.



## Implications Beyond Benchmark Scores  

### Edge & Mobile Deployment  
A device limited to ≤ 4 GB RAM could hardly host even a modest ‑decoded ​5 ⁠ billion parameter transformer today—but it could comfortably store a 🤖‍💻 15⁠ Gb latent space variant built atop native ‑​​½​bit weights thanks to their sub‑gigabyte footprint.



### Future Hardware  
With most arithmetic reduced down to pure addition pipelines, designers aren’t forced into massive floating-point units anymore; instead they can concentrate silicon budget on massive parallel adder trees and ultra-wide SRAM banks—the sort Groq’s tensor streaming cores hint at nowadays.



### Scaling Outlook  
If you’re plotting budget versus performance curves today you’ll see classic polynomials where cost ∝ ⌜parameters⌝·⌜precision⌝ · ⌜token count⌝^. With *BitNet*, `precision` effectively collapses toward ‘½’, flattening those curves dramatically—a visual manifestation coined “new scaling law” by the authors.



## Takeaway  

The experimental evidence presented shows that moving from traditional half­‑precision training/inference toward truely binary-like representations does far more than shave off a few percent here or there—it reshapes what constitutes an *efficient* language model at any scale.

A plausible rule-of-thumb emerging from this work is:

> For every order-of-magnitude increase in model size *(e.g., going from ★³→★³⁰)* you gain roughly another order-of-magnitude headroom back in latency/memory thanks simply to turning matrix multiplications into signed additions plus storing three-valued weights compactly.
