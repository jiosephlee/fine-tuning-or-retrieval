# Scaling Law Redefined: Why a 13 B BitNet Beats a 3 B FP‑16 Model in Speed and Memory  

Large language models have exploded in size over the past few years, yet their rapid growth comes hand–in–hand with soaring memory footprints, higher latency, and ever larger energy bills.  
A recent preprint titled **“The Era of 1−bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits”** introduces *BitNet b¹․⁵⁸*, an architecture whose every trainable parameter lives in the ternary set {−1, 0, + 1}. Even though it stores only about one–and–a–half bits per weight instead of sixteen½ bits used by ordinary float­​half (`fp16`) models, it matches full precision baselines on perplexity and downstream tasks while dramatically cutting latency, GPU RAM usage, throughput cost and arithmetic‐operation energy.

Below we unpack how this work rewrites classic LLM scaling laws—and why—in practice a *larger* low­​bit net outperforms even a substantially smaller half­­precision net.



---

## From Float Half To Ternary Weights  

### What changes under the hood?

* **Weight representation:** Instead of raw float­half values (**16 bits**) each entry belongs to `{−1,0,+ 1}` whose Shannon entropy equals  

\[
H_{\rm ternary}= \log_{2} {\,}^{\,}_{{}}\! {\,}^{\,}_{{}}^{\, }^{\, }^{\,}{\: }³ 
                    \;\approx\;
                    {\bf 1\.585}\ {\rm bits}.
\]

Through tight packing (“absmean” quantisation followed by round–and–clip), these symbols occupy on average *“b¹​.⁵⁸”*, i.e., about one–and­a­half bits per scalar—a saving factor close to ten compared with `fp16`.

* **Activation type:** All intermediate activations are stored as unsigned eight‑bit integers (`int8`). No zero–point offset is used during non-linearities—this keeps everything strictly integer throughout inference.

* **Arithmetic kernel:** The conventional matrix multiply (`nn.Linear`) becomes nothing more than signed/int8 addition because multiplying `{− α,+α}` signs does not require floating point multipliers (`α` represents the common scalar normalisation applied once per layer).  

Consequences are immediate:

```text
fp32 / fp16         → multiply + add      → high power draw
BitLinear           → add only            → orders‐of‐magnitude lower energy
```

## Empirical Findings – Same Size ⇢ Same Accuracy, Much Lower Cost  

The authors pretrained several variants on RedPajama’s first ​100 billion tokens ‑–– keeping every hyperparameter identical except precision level ­–– then evaluated language modelling perplexity together with zero–shot accuracy on standard benchmarks (ARC-Easy/Challenge, Hellaswag,…).

Key observations reported for matched parameter counts (**e.g., both models have ∼7 G parameters**) include  

| Metric                            | Full precision (`fp16`)          | BitNet b¹​.⁵⁸               |
|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| Perplexity                       | Identical within statistical noise | Identical                   |
| Token generation latency          | Baseline                           | **≈ 2.–4× faster**          |
| Peak GPU VRAM during inference    | Baseline                           | **≈ ⅓–¼ of baseline usage** |

For instance,
> “While matching perplexity at the ​7 G scale,
> ***BitNet b¹​.⁵⁸ runs about \(2.\!71\times\)** faster*
> ***and consumes roughly \(3.\!55\times\)** less GPU memory.*  

These figures appear repeatedly throughout their evaluations (Tables ⊙ ppl and ⊙ zero-shot), confirming what prior work hinted at once quantisation moved past post–training tricks.

Translated plainly : once you hit roughly thirteen billion ternary parameters you already beat every conventional three billion half‑precision transformer—not only on accuracy but also on runtime latency, VRAM footprint and arithmetic energy consumption.



### Why does crossing those sizes amplify advantage?

Three mechanisms compound together as the network grows:

| Mechanism                      | Impact when Model Grows                      |
|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| Linear layers dominate computation (`nn.Linear`)   | Replacing costly FMAs (`fp64`) by cheap int adds yields ~~linear speedup proportional to layer dimension |\|
| Weight traffic dominates DRAM↔SRAM bandwidth      | Ternary packing shrinks traffic ∝ #weights ×½ bits ⇒ bandwidth demand drops dramatically |\|
| Embedding layer becomes relatively tiny          │ Fixed embedding size means total RAM saved grows almost linearly with total parameter count |\|

Because almost every forward pass tours these linear layers repeatedly over very long sequences,
the fraction removed by turning multiplies into additions grows almost proportionally to total params—hence acceleration jumps from ≈˙²× at modest scales up toward ≥˙4× when reaching dozens of billions.
