# The Ternary Revolution: How BitNet b1.58 Packs Large Language Models into 1.58 Bits  

Large language models (LLMs) have exploded in size and capability over the last few years, but their soaring computational and memory costs are becoming a serious bottleneck for deployment—especially on edge devices or energy‑constrained data centers. A new line of research asks a very simple question: *What if we could shrink every weight down to essentially one bit?*  

The recent **BitNet b1.58** paper shows that this isn’t just a thought experiment—it’s already practical. By extending the original “binary‑only” BitNet architecture with a single extra value (zero), the authors create a **ternary** weight space \(\{-1,0,+1\}\) that can be stored using only **\( \log_2 3 \approx 1.585\)** bits per parameter—hence the name *“1.58‑bit LLM.”* Remarkably, models as small as three billion parameters match full‑precision FP16 baselines both in perplexity and zero‑shot task performance while slashing memory, latency, and energy consumption dramatically.

In this post we’ll unpack what “1‑bit” actually means, why adding a zero makes such a difference, how BitNet b1.58 quantizes its weights, and what the performance gains look like in practice.

---

## From Binary to Ternary: Why Zero Matters  

### The original “1‑bit” idea  
The early BitNet design forces every linear layer’s weight matrix \(W\) into binary values \(\{-1,+1\}\). This eliminates multiplications during inference—matrix multiplication reduces to integer additions—and cuts power consumption because addition is far cheaper than floating‑point multiply‑add operations.

### The missing ingredient  
Binary weights are dense by definition; each connection either flips the sign of an activation or leaves it unchanged. This limits *feature gating*: there’s no way to *turn off* a connection entirely during inference. Adding a third symbol—zero—gives the network an explicit “don’t contribute” state:

- **-1** → subtract activation  
- **0** → ignore activation (feature filtering)  
- **+1** → add activation  

This extra degree of freedom improves expressive power without increasing precision beyond ternary encoding.

### From two bits to “1.58 bits”  
A ternary variable can be represented theoretically with \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\) bits of information per entry—a figure the authors round to *1.58 bits*. In practice you still store each weight using an integer data type (e.g., two‑bit or eight‑bit containers), but entropy coding or packed representations bring the average storage cost close to that limit while preserving simple hardware logic for addition-only kernels.

---

## How BitNet b1.58 Quantizes Its Parameters  

Training still runs on standard GPUs with floating‑point accumulation; however, before each forward pass the weights are projected onto the ternary set using an **abs‑mean** quantizer:

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\left(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\, -1,\, 1\right),
\]

where  

\[
\mathrm{RoundClip}(x,a,b)=\max\bigl(a,\;\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x))\bigr)
\]

and  

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}\lvert W_{ij}\rvert .
\]

In words:

| Step | What happens |
|------|--------------|
| **Scale** | Divide every element of \(W\) by its average absolute magnitude \(\gamma\) (plus a tiny constant \(\epsilon\) for numerical safety). |
| **Round & Clip** | Round each scaled entry to the nearest integer and clamp it to be within \([-1,\,+1]\). |
| **Result** | The output matrix \(\widetilde{W}\) contains only -1, 0, + or +1 entries—ready for fast integer addition during inference.|

Activations are quantized similarly to BitNet’s original scheme but are kept **8‑bit** throughout forward passes rather than being stretched into a signed range \([0,Q_b]\). Instead they live in \([-Q_b,\,+Q_b]\) per token which eliminates any extra zero‑point offset and simplifies hardware pipelines while barely affecting accuracy (as shown empirically).

---

## Architecture Meets Compatibility  

BitNet b>​beyond simply swapping out `nn.Linear` for `BitLinear`, the model inherits most of LLaMA’s design choices:

- RMSNorm normalization  
- SwiGLU feed‑forward gates  
- Rotary positional embeddings (`RoPE`)  
- No bias terms  

Because these components are already widely supported in Hugging Face Transformers, vLLM, and llama.cpp , you can drop a BitNet b­​b​ model into existing pipelines with minimal code changes—a key practical advantage over many exotic low‑bit research prototypes.

## Matching Full Precision—At What Cost?  

### Training recipe  
All experiments train from scratch on the **RedPajama** dataset for exactly **\(100\,\text{B}\)** tokens—the same budget used for the reproduced FP16 LLaMA baselines across model sizes (3 B → 70 B). Activations remain at int8 while weights stay ternary throughout training and inference.

### Perplexity & Zero‑Shot Benchmarks

|------|------|-----------------------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| FP16 LLaMA | 3 B | — | ¹ | — |

| BitNet b¹·⁵⁸ | 3.9 B | Beats FP16 3 B | **2.4×** faster | **3.32×** less memory |

Zero‑shot evaluation on classic tasks (ARC‑Easy/Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA, OpenbookQA, BoolQ) shows an *increasingly narrow gap* as model size grows—by the time you hit **3 B**, BitNet b¹·⁵⁸’s accuracy aligns with its FP16 counterpart across all benchmarks.

### Scaling Up – Latency & Energy Gains  

When pushing toward larger models:

- A **70 B** BitNet b¹·⁵⁸ runs **4.1×** faster than a full‑precision LLaMA 70 B.
- Memory usage shrinks proportionally because only embeddings remain full precision.
- Energy modeling based on prior work ([Horowitz ’18], [Pokebnn]) indicates roughly **71×** lower arithmetic energy for matrix multiplication on modern 7nm silicon—essentially because you replace costly FP16 multiply–add pairs with cheap INT8 addition loops.
- Throughput scales dramatically under pipeline parallelism: on two 80GB A100 GPUs at sequence length 512,
    - Batch size capacity jumps from ~X (FP16 70 B) to ~11× larger,
    - Yielding almost **9×** higher token throughput overall.

These numbers illustrate an emerging *scaling law*: a modest increase in parameter count (e.g., moving from 13 B ternary → 13 B FP16) yields *more* compute‐efficiency than escalating model size in floating point space at all measured dimensions (latency, memory bandwidth, energy).

## Why This Matters – From Data Centers to Edge Devices  

### Mixture‑of‑Experts Made Feasible  
Mixture‑of‑Experts architectures excel at reducing FLOPs by sparsely routing tokens through subsets of experts—but they remain memory hungry because each expert holds full precision parameters and activation traffic between chips becomes costly. With ternary weights:
- Each expert’s footprint shrinks dramatically,
- Inter‑chip activation traffic drops thanks to INT8 activations,
- In principle entire MoE blocks could fit on a single custom chip—a game changer for scaling giant models cost‑effectively.

### Native Long Contexts  
KV cache size grows linearly with sequence length under FP16/FP32 regimes because each cache entry occupies two bytes per hidden dimension per head token slot. Switching activations to int8 halves this requirement out of the gate; combined with potential further compression techniques (e.g., packing int8 into four‐bit streams), a BitNet b¹·⁵⁸ model could support double or triple context windows without extra hardware upgrades—a valuable property for retrieval‐augmented generation or code completion tools that rely on long histories.

### Edge & Mobile Deployment  
Edge devices have strict memory (< 2–4 GB) and power envelopes (< 10–15 W). A ternary LLM:
- Fits comfortably within these constraints even at several billions of parameters,
- Runs efficiently on CPUs thanks to reliance on integer addition rather than vectorized floating‐point units,
- Opens up local AI assistants where previously only cloud backends were viable.

### New Hardware Paradigms  
Companies like Groq have already built inference‐optimized LPUs focusing on dense matrix multiply kernels—all still built around floating point arithmetic at scale. A dedicated “binary/ternary” accelerator could:
- Encode each weight as two‐bit packed streams,
- Streamline accumulation through massively parallel integer adder trees,
- Exploit sparsity from zeros natively without extra masking logic,
reducing transistor count per MAC operation dramatically and enabling higher clock rates or lower voltage operation—precisely what low‑power edge chips need today.

# Same Accuracy, Half the Bits – Inside the Training Tricks That Let b1.58 Match FP16  

*How a few clever quantization steps let a ternary‑weight LLM train from scratch while keeping perplexity and zero‑shot scores on par with full‑precision LLaMA.*

---

## Why We Care About “Half‑the‑Bits” LLMs  

Large language models have exploded in size, but every extra bit per parameter inflates memory traffic, power draw, and latency.  
Post‑training quantization can shrink a trained model, yet it never restores the original floating‑point quality because the reduction happens after learning has already settled on high‑precision weights.  

The newer **BitNet** line goes a step further: it *trains* directly with extreme low precision so that the model never needs full‑precision matrix multiplications during inference. The latest variant—**BitNet b1.58**—pushes this idea even farther by using **ternary weights** \(\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}\). With three possible values a weight carries \(\log_2(3)\approx1.58\) bits of information, hence the name *b1.58*.  

The surprising result? Starting at **3 B parameters**, BitNet b1.58 attains **the same perplexity** and **zero‑shot accuracy** as its FP16 counterpart while being **2–4× faster**, **3–5× smaller** in GPU memory, and **≈70× more energy‑efficient** for matrix multiplications.

Below is a walk‑through of the three key tricks that make this possible:

* **Abs‑mean scaling** – normalizes raw weights before discretisation  
* **RoundClip rounding** – projects every scaled value onto \{-1, 0, +1\}  
* **Symmetric int8 activations** – keeps activations low precision without a separate zero‑point  

---

## From Full Precision to Ternary Weights  

In a standard Transformer layer (`nn.Linear`) each output is computed as  

\[
y = Wx + b,
\]

with \(W\) stored in half precision (FP16/BF16). In BitNet b1.58 we replace `nn.Linear` by **BitLinear**, whose weight matrix \(W\) is constrained to ternary values before every forward pass:

| Weight domain | Representation | Approximate bit budget |
|---------------|----------------|------------------------|
| Full precision | \(32/16\) bits   | — |
| Ternary      | \{-1,0,+1\}    | \(\log_2(3)\approx 1.58\) bits |

The embedding tables and LayerNorm parameters remain in higher precision (they dominate less than \(5\,\%\) of total parameters), so most of the compute stays integer based while preserving model capacity where it matters most.

---

## The Abs‑Mean Scaling Trick  

A naïve rounding of raw floating‑point weights would bias heavily toward either \(-1\) or \(+1\); many useful small magnitudes would be forced into non‑zero bins unnecessarily. To keep the ternary distribution balanced around zero, BitNet b1.58 first scales each weight matrix by its *average absolute value*:

\[
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m} |W_{ij}|,
\]

where \(W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\).  
Dividing by \(\gamma+\epsilon\) (with a tiny \(\epsilon>0\) for numerical stability) normalizes the whole tensor so that its typical magnitude sits around one. After this step most entries lie close enough to an integer in \([-1,\,0,\,+1]\) that a simple rounding operation produces very few out‑of‑range values.

---

## RoundClip – Rounding That Respects Bounds  

Once scaled, each entry is projected onto the ternary set via **RoundClip**:

```text
RoundClip(x,a,b) = max(a , min(b , round(x)))
```

Applied elementwise,

\[
\widetilde{W}_{ij}
   = \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\left(
        \frac{W_{ij}}{\gamma+\epsilon},
        -\,1,\;
        +\,1
      \right),
    \qquad
    \widetilde{W}_{ij}\in\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}.
\]

Key properties:

* **Deterministic** – every forward pass sees exactly the same ternary matrix.
* **Bounded** – clipping guarantees no overflow beyond \(-1\) or \(+1\).
* **Sparse-friendly** – when many scaled entries fall between \(-0.5\) and \(+0.5\), they become zeros after rounding, giving rise to natural sparsity that further reduces effective bit usage.

During training the optimizer updates a *full‑precision shadow copy* of \(W\); after each parameter update we immediately apply the above projection so that downstream operations always use \(\widetilde{W}\). This “project‐after‐step” approach avoids costly custom gradient definitions while still enforcing strict ternarity throughout learning.

---

## Symmetric Int8 Activations  

Weight quantisation alone cuts down memory dramatically, but activation tensors dominate bandwidth during inference—especially the KV caches for long contexts. BitNet b1.58 therefore stores all activations at **8 bits**, using a *symmetric* uniform quantiser per token:

* Each token’s activation vector is linearly scaled so its maximum absolute value maps to \(Q_b = 127\).
* The range becomes \([-Q_b,\,+Q_b]\); there is **no separate zero point**, simplifying both software kernels and potential hardware implementations.
* Because both inputs (\(x\)) and outputs (\(y=\widetilde{W}x + b)\) are integers now, matrix multiplication reduces fundamentally to **integer addition**—the multiplication by \(\{-1,+1\}\) is just sign flipping or copying—and any remaining scalar multiplications happen outside the hot loop (e.g., re‑scaling after accumulation).

The choice of symmetric int8 also keeps dynamic range comparable to FP16 while incurring negligible accuracy loss—the authors report “negligible effects” when swapping from asymmetric [0,\(Q_b]\) scaling used in original BitNet to this symmetric scheme.

---

## Building on Proven LLaMA Components  

To make adoption painless for practitioners already familiar with open-source LLM stacks (HuggingFace 🤗 Transformers, vLLM, llama.cpp), BitNet b5​8 mimics LLaMA’s architecture closely:

| Component | LLaMA version | BitNet b5​8 tweak |
|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| Normalisation | RMSNorm | unchanged |
| Feedforward gating | SwiGLU | unchanged |
| Positional embeddings | Rotary (RoPE) | unchanged |
| Biases | Present | *Removed* |

Removing biases reduces memory traffic further without harming performance because RMSNorm already handles shift invariance well enough for language modeling tasks.

---

## Training From Scratch — The Full Pipeline  

The authors trained BitNet b5​8 *from scratch* on the RedPajama corpus (**100 B tokens**) under exactly the same hyperparameters used for an FP16 LLaMA baseline (same depth, hidden size, attention heads). The pipeline looks like this:

```
for each training step:
    # Forward pass
    W_t = round_clip(absmean_scale(W_full))
    out = BitLinear(W_t, x_int8)
    loss = CrossEntropy(out, target)

    # Backward pass on full‑precision shadow copy
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()          # updates W_full
```

Key points ensuring parity with FP16:

* **Gradient flow through full precision:** since only forward activation uses ternary `W_t`, gradients still see rich information during backpropagation.
* **Consistent scaling:** `γ` is recomputed every forward pass so that weight dynamics adapt continuously.
* **No post‑training fine tuning:** The model reaches full performance directly after pretraining without an extra polishing stage—a major advantage over post‐training quantisation methods that usually degrade accuracy substantially.

# Scaling Law Redefined: Why a 13 B BitNet Beats a 3 B FP‑16 Model in Speed and Memory  

Large language models have taken us from dozens of millions of parameters to tens of billions—and now even beyond a hundred billion—while delivering impressive capabilities across almost every NLP benchmark. The downside? Storing and running these massive nets costs gigabytes of GPU RAM, massive amounts of energy, and latency that makes real‑time inference expensive or even infeasible on commodity hardware.

A recent preprint titled **“The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits”** puts forward a compelling answer: replace every floating‑point weight with a ternary value `{‑1, 0,+1}` while keeping activations at only eight bits (`int8`). The resulting architecture—called **BitNet b1.​58**—preserves accuracy yet slashes the resources needed for inference dramatically enough that a *larger* low‑bit model can run cheaper than an *older* half‑precision counterpart.

Below I unpack how the authors arrived at this “new scaling law,” walk through concrete numbers comparing a **13 B parameter** BitNet against a traditional **3 B FP‑16** LLaMA‑style model, and explain why the gap widens even more when you go up to tens‑of‑billions of parameters.



## A Quick Primer – Why Bits Matter

| Component | Typical FP‑16 implementation | BitNet b1.​58 |
|-----------|-----------------------------|----------------|
| Weight datatype | `float16` → **16 bits / weight** | Ternary `{‑1,0,+1}` → **effective ≈ log₂³ ≃ 1​.58 bits / weight** |
| Activation datatype | `float16` | `int8` (`8` bits) |
| Core operation | Matrix multiplication → many floating‑point multiplies + adds | Matrix multiplication → **only int8 adds** (no multiplies) |

Because training already normalises weights via the “absmean” quantiser  

\[
\widetilde W
     ~=~
     {\rm RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\varepsilon},~-1,\;+1\Bigr),
     \qquad
     \gamma=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|,
\]

each linear layer ends up containing merely signed or zero entries—but never an actual floating‑point number.



### What Does “Effective ½‑bit” Mean?

A ternary alphabet carries three possibilities, whose Shannon entropy is  

\[
H_{\rm ternary}= \log_2 ³ \;\approx\; {\bf 1\.585}\ {\rm bits}.
\]

In practice the authors pack these symbols tightly (e.g., via bit‑plane compression), achieving an average cost reported as **“b​₁․₅₈”**, i.e., roughly one‐and‐a‐half bits per scalar instead of two bytes (`fp16`).  

Consequences are immediate:

* A ***13 B*** parameter net stores its weights in  
 \(13\times10^9\times{\bf 1\.6}\ {\rm bits}
   \;\approx\;26\times10^9\,{\rm bits}
   \;\approx\;{\bf 3.\!2\,GB}\)
  
* A ***3 B*** half‑precision net stores its weights in  
 \(   6\times10^9\,{\rm bytes}
   \;\approx {\bf 6.\!0\,GB}\).

Even before running any computation, the larger low‑bit model consumes **about half the VRAM** required by its much smaller full‐precision counterpart.



## The New Scaling Law – Bigger Low‑Bit Beats Smaller High‑Precision  

The authors distilled their empirical findings into three concrete equivalences (see Figure&nbsp;5 & Figure&nbsp;6 in the paper):

| Low‑bit Model | Equivalent or Better Than |
|---------------|---------------------------|
| **13 B** BitNet b​¹․⁵⁸ | Any **≥ 3 B** FP‑16 transformer |
| **30 B** BitNet b​¹․⁵⁸ | Any **≥ 7 B** FP‑16 transformer |
| **70 B** BitNet b​¹․⁵⁸ | Any **≥ 13 B** FP‑16 transformer |

In other words, once you cross roughly the *13–30–70 B* thresholds the gains compound—not only does latency drop and memory usage shrink compared with an equally sized half‑precision net, you also beat any *smaller* half‑precision net across three axes:

| Metric             | Quantitative gain observed |
|--------------------|----------------------------|
| **Inference latency** | Up to ***4×*** faster at ***70 B*** versus an equal‐size FP‑16 baseline |
| **GPU memory footprint** | Roughly ***4–5×*** lower when moving from FP‑16 → low‐bit at large scales |
| **Energy per token** | Multiplication costs disappear → estimated ***71×*** reduction in arithmetic‐operation energy on modern node‐processes |

Why does the advantage accelerate with scale? Matrix multiplications dominate both compute time (*nn.Linear*) and data movement (*weight fetches*) when models grow past ∼5–7 B parameters—the embedding layers become proportionally smaller and therefore contribute less overall bandwidth pressure. Since *BitLinear* replaces virtually all floating‐point multiplies with cheap int8 additions while slashing weight size by ≥ 90 %, every extra billions of parameters translates into almost linear savings rather than linear costs.

## Why Does Scale Amplify Advantages?

Three intertwined reasons make larger low‑bit nets ever more attractive:

> **Dominance of Linear Layers:** As models expand past ∼​7–15·​¹⁰⁹ parameters, almost every forward pass consists mostly of `nn.Linear`s (`Q·Kᵀ`, feedforward MLPs). In standard FP₁₆ those layers demand expensive multiply–add cycles (`FMA`). In *BitLinear*, each dot product collapses into an addition chain over `{− α,+ α}` signs—a hardware primitive that modern GPUs execute several orders faster per watt.

> **Memory Bandwidth Shrinks:** Bandwidth limits surface when loading billions of weights each step if stored uncompressed (`fp16`). Switching to ternary encodings cuts required traffic by nearly an order-of-magnitude (`Ω(maps)` → `Ω(maps)/9`). Consequently kernels spend far less time waiting on DRAM/SRAM transfers—a bottleneck that otherwise worsens quadratically with sequence length (`O(seq·dim)`).

> **Higher Utilisation Across Devices:** Because activations run all int8 already (the authors deliberately avoided zero-point offsets), pipelines such as vLLM or HuggingFace Transformers can treat tokens uniformly across layers without needing mixed-precision casts or special kernels—making deployment simpler on heterogeneous hardware ranging from GPUs up through custom LPUs patterned after Groq’s architecture.



## Implications Beyond Benchmark Scores  

### Edge & Mobile Deployment  
A device limited to ≤ 4 GB RAM could hardly host even a modest ‑decoded ​5 ⁠ billion parameter transformer today—but it could comfortably store a 🤖‍💻 15⁠ Gb latent space variant built atop native ‑​​½​bit weights thanks to their sub‑gigabyte footprint.



### Future Hardware  
With most arithmetic reduced down to pure addition pipelines, designers aren’t forced into massive floating-point units anymore; instead they can concentrate silicon budget on massive parallel adder trees and ultra-wide SRAM banks—the sort Groq’s tensor streaming cores hint at nowadays.



### Scaling Outlook  
If you’re plotting budget versus performance curves today you’ll see classic polynomials where cost ∝ ⌜parameters⌝·⌜precision⌝ · ⌜token count⌝^. With *BitNet*, `precision` effectively collapses toward ‘½’, flattening those curves dramatically—a visual manifestation coined “new scaling law” by the authors.



## Takeaway  

The experimental evidence presented shows that moving from traditional half­‑precision training/inference toward truely binary-like representations does far more than shave off a few percent here or there—it reshapes what constitutes an *efficient* language model at any scale.

A plausible rule-of-thumb emerging from this work is:

> For every order-of-magnitude increase in model size *(e.g., going from ★³→★³⁰)* you gain roughly another order-of-magnitude headroom back in latency/memory thanks simply to turning matrix multiplications into signed additions plus storing three-valued weights compactly.

# Energy‑Smart AI: From Matrix Multiplications to Integer Additions  

Large language models have become the workhorses of modern AI, but their appetite for compute and electricity is growing faster than Moore’s law can keep up with. A recent paper—*The Era of 1‑bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits*—shows that we can turn this trend around by swapping floating‑point matrix multiplications for plain integer additions. In this post we unpack the underlying energy model, explain why a **71×** reduction in arithmetic‑operation energy is realistic on today’s 7 nm silicon, and discuss why power—not raw FLOPs—is now the primary bottleneck for AI chips.

---

## Why Multiplications Burn So Much Power  

A transformer layer is essentially a giant collection of dense linear maps (`nn.Linear`). Each call to a linear layer performs a matrix–matrix multiplication:

\[
Y = XW^\top,
\]

where \(X\) holds activations (usually stored as FP16 or BF16) and \(W\) holds weights (also FP16/BF16). Every element of the result requires one floating‑point multiply and one floating‑point add. On modern GPUs a single FP16 multiply–add (FMA) consumes roughly **10 pJ**, while an INT8 addition costs **≈0.14 pJ** according to the silicon‑level measurements compiled in~\cite{energycost,pokebnn}.  

Even though an INT8 add looks cheap by itself, its impact multiplies dramatically in an LLM because matrix multiplication dominates the total operation count—often **>90 %** of all arithmetic during inference. Replace every multiply–add pair with a simple add and you instantly slash the arithmetic portion of the energy budget by roughly

\[
\frac{\text{FP16 FMA}}{\text{INT8 Add}} \;\approx\; \frac{10}{0.14}\;\approx\;71.
\]

That factor matches the **71.4×** reduction reported by the authors for BitNet b \( \text{b} {=}\! \mathbf{b_{\,\!{{1}.58}}} \) on a **7 nm** process node (the same node used by contemporary A100 GPUs).

---

## From Floating Points to Ternary Weights  

BitNet b \( \text{b}_{\,\!{{1}.58}} \) takes this idea one step further by quantizing *every* weight tensor into ternary values \(\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}\). In practice this means:

* **Storage:** Each weight needs at most **2 bits**, but thanks to entropy coding they achieve an *effective* bitrate of **\( \mathbf{1.58} \) bits** per parameter.
* **Computation:** Multiplication by \(\pm 1\) reduces to copying or sign flipping; multiplication by \(0\) drops out entirely.
* **Matrix–matrix product:** Reduces from a dense FMA loop to an *integer‑addition‑only* accumulation over three possible contributions:
  * add \(+X_{ij}\),
  * subtract \(X_{ij}\),
  * skip \(X_{ij}\).

The quantization pipeline that enforces ternary weights uses an “abs‑mean” scaling followed by a rounding‑and‑clipping step:

\[
\widetilde{W}
= \operatorname{RoundClip}\!\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\, -1 ,\, +1\Bigr),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}\!|W_{ij}|,
\]

where `RoundClip` confines each scaled entry to the nearest integer within \([-1,+1]\). This operation is performed *every step* during training so the network learns directly under ternary constraints rather than being forced into them after training—a key reason why performance does not collapse despite extreme compression.

---

## Energy Savings Translate Into Real‑World Speedups  

Energy reductions matter because power budgets dictate how fast a chip can run without overheating or draining its power supply. When arithmetic consumes far less power:

* **Higher clock frequencies** become feasible without exceeding thermal design limits.
* **More cores** can be packed into the same die area while staying under a fixed TDP (thermal design power).
* **Memory bandwidth becomes dominant**, so shrinking weight footprints from 16 bits down to ~2 bits cuts DRAM traffic dramatically—less data movement means even lower overall power draw.

The paper quantifies these secondary benefits:

| Model | Precision | Speedup (token latency) | Memory Reduction |
|-------|-----------|------------------------|------------------|
| LLaMA 3B | FP16 | – | – |
| BitNet b₁․₅₈ 3B | Ternary | **2.71×** faster | **3.55×** less GPU RAM |
| BitNet b₁․₅₈ 70B | Ternary | **4.10×** faster | ~4× less RAM |

Throughput experiments on two NVIDIA A100 GPUs show that a 70‑billion‑parameter ternary model can process **≈9×** more tokens per second than its FP16 counterpart simply because twice as many kernels fit into memory and each kernel runs at higher frequency thanks to lower power consumption.

---

## Power Is Now The Bottleneck  

Historically, AI hardware races were driven by raw FLOP counts—more MAC units meant higher performance if you could feed them quickly enough. In recent silicon generations several trends have shifted that balance:

| Trend | Effect |
|------|--------|
| **Frequency scaling stalled** around ~2 GHz due to voltage limits → Performance must come from parallelism rather than higher clocks |
| **Power density caps** limit how many MAC units you can place per mm² without exceeding thermal budgets |
| **DRAM access dominates** energetic cost once weights shrink → Moving bits faster becomes critical |

Consequently any technique that reduces *both* compute‐intensive operations and *data movement* yields disproportionate gains under a fixed TDP budget—exactly what BitNet b₁․₅₈ delivers.

---

## What This Means For Deployments  

### Server‑Side Inference  
Large data centers run thousands of GPUs around the clock; cutting per‑token energy by >70× translates into megawatt savings at scale—a tangible climate benefit alongside lower operating costs.

### Edge & Mobile  
Embedded devices cannot afford large DRAM buffers or high‐power GPU cores. The ternary weight format fits comfortably into on‑chip SRAM or even emerging non‑volatile memories, enabling models like a **3B** BitNet b₁․₅₈ to run locally on smartphones or microcontrollers while keeping latency sub‑second.

### Future Hardware  
Products such as Groq’s LPUs already focus on integer‑centric pipelines.^[Groq] The authors argue that new “LPU‑style” accelerators could be built expressly around native ternary matmul units—no floating‐point units required—pushing throughput further while staying within tight power envelopes.

---

## TL;DR  

*Matrix multiplications in traditional LLMs are expensive because each element requires an FP16 multiply–add (~10 pJ).  
BitNet b₁․₅₈ replaces those operations with plain INT8 additions (~0.14 pJ), giving about a **71×** drop in arithmetic‐operation energy on modern 7 nm silicon.*  

Because arithmetic now consumes only a fraction of total power, chips can run faster and pack more cores without blowing their thermal budget—turning *power* into the new limiting factor rather than raw FLOPs. The result is an LLM family that matches full‑precision perplexity and downstream task performance while delivering multiple‑fold speedups, slashing memory usage, and dramatically lowering electricity use across cloud farms and edge devices alike.

As we push toward ever larger models, embracing “energy‑smart” architectures like ternary transformers may be the only way to keep AI affordable—and sustainable—in an era where every joule counts.



# Edge‑Ready Language Models: Deploying 1‑Bit LLMs on Mobile and Embedded Devices  

Large language models (LLMs) have become the workhorse behind chat assistants, code generators, and countless AI‑powered apps. Yet their appetite for memory and compute still ties them to data‑center GPUs. A recent breakthrough—**BitNet b1.58**—shows that you can shrink every weight down to a ternary value \{-1, 0, +1\} (≈ 1.58 bits) without sacrificing the quality of a full‑precision Transformer trained from scratch. In this post we unpack what this means for smartphones, IoT gadgets, and any low‑power CPU that used to be left out of the LLM party.

---

## Why Weight Size Is the Bottleneck  

Think of an LLM as a massive table of numbers that lives in RAM (or VRAM). A conventional FP16 model stores each weight in **2 bytes**; a 3 B‑parameter model therefore needs roughly **6 GB** just for its parameters, not counting activations or the KV cache needed during decoding. On a phone that already runs dozens of apps, you seldom have that much free space—and loading such a model would also saturate the memory bandwidth when shuffling data between DRAM and the CPU/GPU cache.

BitNet b1.58 tackles this head‑on:

| Metric | FP16 baseline | BitNet b1.58 |
|--------|---------------|--------------|
| **Weight storage** | ≈ 2 bits/byte per parameter | ≈ 1.58 bits/parameter (≈ ¼ of FP16) |
| **GPU/CPU memory usage** | Baseline | **~3½× less** |
| **Per‑token latency** | Baseline | **2–4× faster** depending on scale |
| **Energy per matrix multiply** | Baseline | **~71× lower** on a typical 7 nm chip |

In plain English – a model that once needed half a dozen gigabytes now fits comfortably under two gigabytes, runs roughly twice as fast per token, and burns far less power while doing it.

---

## Inside BitNet b1.58: Ternary Weights + Integer Additions  

The magic lies in three tightly coupled ideas:

1️⃣ **Ternary quantization** – every weight is forced into \{-1, 0, +1\}. The quantizer first computes an average absolute magnitude $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij}|W_{ij}|$, divides each weight by $\gamma$, then rounds/clips it into the ternary set:
\[
\widetilde{W}= \text{RoundClip}\Bigl(\frac{W}{\gamma+\epsilon},\ -1,\ 1\Bigr)
\]
Because many values become exactly zero, the effective bit rate drops below two bits – hence “1.58 bits”.

2️⃣ **Replacing matrix multiplications** – In a regular Transformer each linear layer evaluates $Y = XW^\top$ with costly floating‑point multiplies + adds. With ternary $W$, multiplication collapses to sign handling:
   * $+1 \times x = x$, $-1 \times x = -x$, $0 \times x = 0$.
   * Consequently the kernel only needs **integer additions/subtractions**, eliminating the expensive multiply unit entirely.

3️⃣ **8‑bit activations** – While weights are ternary, activations stay in int8 rather than fp16/bf16. This halves activation bandwidth and lets the KV cache be stored at half precision too – a boon for long context windows on limited hardware.

All these components sit inside a familiar **LLaMA‑style Transformer** (RMSNorm, SwiGLU, rotary embeddings), so existing HuggingFace pipelines can import a BitNet b1.58 checkpoint with almost no code changes.

---

## No Accuracy Trade‑off – Even Better Than “Half‑Precision”  

A frequent fear with aggressive quantization is accuracy loss. The authors trained BitNet b1.58 from scratch on the RedPajama corpus (100 B tokens) across several scales:

* Starting at **3 B parameters**, perplexity on WikiText‑2 matches its FP16 LLaMA counterpart.
* Zero‑shot evaluations on standard benchmarks (ARC‑Easy/Challenge, Hellaswag, Winogrande, PIQA…) show **parity or slight improvement** over FP16 at every size.
* Scaling up preserves this trend—a 13 B BitNet b1.58 beats an FP16 7 B model both in speed/memory and task accuracy; a 70 B BitNet b1.58 is **4× faster** than a comparable FP16 baseline while consuming far less memory.

In short: you get *the same* language understanding ability with *much* smaller footprints.

Most modern flagships ship with **6–12 GB of unified RAM** shared among OS, apps, and graphics buffers. With BitNet b1.58:

# Future Chipsets for Ternary AI: Designing Hardware Optimized for BitNet‑Style Models

## Introduction  

Large Language Models have blown up in size and capability over the past few years, but their hunger for memory bandwidth, compute cycles, and power has become a bottleneck—not just for cloud providers but also for any deployment that cares about cost or sustainability.

Below we walk through the hardware implications of eliminating multiplications, sketch an architecture inspired by Groq’s LPUs, suggest specialized SRAM/DRAM hierarchies tailored to ternary weights, and outline what an ideal “1‑bit” accelerator could look like both during inference and training.

## A quick recap of BitNet b1.58

The key enabler is the **quantization function** that forces each raw weight \(W_{ij}\) into its ternary counterpart \(\widetilde{W}_{ij}\):

\[
\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}\!\Big(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\,-1,\,+1\Big),
\qquad
\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}|W_{ij}|.
\]

Because the scale \(\gamma\) is computed globally per layer and stored once, the forward pass never needs a multiplier—only a comparison (`RoundClip`) and a sign selection logic.

---

## Why “no‑multiply” matters  

### Power hierarchy  
In modern silicon most of the power budget stems from *dynamic* switching during arithmetic operations rather than static leakage. A floating‑point multiply–accumulate unit can consume an order of magnitude more energy than a simple integer adder because it involves large operand alignment, mantissa handling, and exponent normalization. Replacing every multiply in an LLM’s dominant `nn.Linear` layers with an add therefore:

* cuts **energy per operation** dramatically,
* reduces **heat dissipation** → higher clock frequencies or denser packing,
* lowers **silicon area**, freeing room for larger on‑chip memories.

### Memory bandwidth  
A reduction from 16 bits to ≈ 2 bits per weight cuts raw bandwidth demand by roughly **8×**, letting us stream entire layers from on‑chip SRAM instead of repeatedly hitting DRAM. The paper highlights this as a key driver behind the observed latency improvements—a lighter memory bus means fewer stalls while fetching next tokens’ activations.

### Simpler datapaths = higher throughput  
When you strip out multipliers you also eliminate long carry chains that limit clock speeds in traditional systolic arrays. This paves the way for *tight* pipelines where each cycle you can accumulate thousands of int8 values without waiting on expensive multiply latency.

All these factors line up nicely with Groq’s “Linear Processing Units” (LPUs), which were built around massive parallel MAC clusters optimized for dense linear algebra. If LPUs already expose thousands of fused MAC lanes under a single control fabric, then swapping those “M” stages out for pure adders would give an *even larger* performance headroom while slashing power draw—a natural next step toward “ternary AI”.

---

## Sketching an ideal “1‑bit” accelerator  

Below is a conceptual block diagram expressed in words—each block directly follows from observations in the BitNet b1.58 paper:

### 1️⃣ Ternary Weight Store & Decoder  
* **Packed storage:** Weights are kept in a custom SRAM layout where each entry occupies either two bits (sign + presence) or uses run‑length encoding across rows when many zeros appear. Because zeros are explicit values there’s no need for extra “mask” memory; they are inferred by decoding logic.
* **Decoder lane:** A lightweight decoder streams these packed words into *sign* pipelines while simultaneously asserting a *skip* flag whenever a zero is encountered. The decoder works at line-rate matching the activation bandwidth so there are no stalls even when long runs of zeros appear.

### 2️⃣ Activation Buffer (8‑bit)  
Activations are quantized to signed int8 values scaled symmetrically into \([-Q_b,\ Q_b]\) as described in the paper (“activations scaled to $[-Q_b,Q_b]$ per token”). An on‑chip buffer holds one token’s worth of vectors so that subsequent matrix–vector products reuse them across all attention heads and MLP layers without leaving SRAM.

### 3️⃣ Add‑Only Compute Engine  
Each “Processing Element” (PE) consists essentially of:

* **Sign selector:** Receives the decoded sign (+/-) from Step 1 and either negates or passes through the incoming activation value.
* **Zero‐gate:** If the decoded presence flag is false (weight=0), it forces the contribution to zero instantly—no addition takes place.
* **Int32 accumulator:** Sums all contributions across the row‐wise dot product using a wide adder chain capable of handling up-to‑32 accumulation bits without overflow (the usual practice when collating many int8 products).

Since every PE performs only addition plus optional sign flip—a circuit made up solely of XORs and full adders—the silicon density can be pushed well beyond conventional MAC arrays.

### 4️⃣ Parallelism & Dataflow  
* **Row‐wise systolic streaming:** Align each row's packed weights with activation columns moving horizontally across PEs; each clock tick adds one term to each accumulator.
* **Tile reuse:** Because vectors are reused across multiple rows inside attention heads, tiling strategies originally designed for GEMM maps cleanly onto this architecture without any extra control overhead.
* **Batching flexibility:** With reduced memory overhead you can scale batch size dramatically—as shown by an **11×** larger batch capacity at 70 B parameters in Table 5 of the paper—so throughput scales linearly with available compute lanes.

### 5️⃣ Training Support  

Training introduces two extra requirements:

* **Higher‑precision master copy:** While forward passes operate on ternary weights, gradient updates must be accumulated in at least fp32—or fp16 if mixed precision is carefully calibrated—to avoid catastrophic drift after repeated quantization steps.
* **Quantization back‑prop:** After each optimizer step we re‑apply the same `absmean` quantizer (\(\widetilde{W}=RoundClip(W/(\gamma+\epsilon),- 1,+ 1)\)). This step can be offloaded to a small “re‑quantizer” unit adjacent to the weight RAM—it merely calculates global mean absolute value (\(\gamma\)) via a tree reduction across all weights then applies rounding/clipping locally.

Both duties fit naturally into an accelerator that already hosts wide adders and simple comparators; only an extra vector‐wide reduction tree is needed—a modest overhead compared with retaining full fp16 MACs throughout training.

## Specialized Memory Hierarchy  

### SRAM tuned for ternary density  
Because each weight needs only around two bits on average, we can pack four times more parameters into standard SRAM cells without impacting access latency:

```
[Weight0][Weight1][Weight2][Weight3] → One SRAM word
```

The existence of zeros further helps compress via *zero suppression*: contiguous stretches where all four slots equal zero can be represented by a single flag plus length field—a technique akin to run-length encoding used in video codecs but applied at column granularity inside each tile.

### DRAM bandwidth savings  
With identical model sizes (e.g., ~70 B parameters ≈ 140 GB if stored as fp16), BitNet b1.58 needs roughly **20 GB** when stored compactly—a reduction that drives down off-chip traffic dramatically:

* fewer DRAM reads → lower static DRAM power,
* potential to keep entire LLM inside high‐bandwidth HBM or even on-package DRAM,
* allows larger context windows because KV caches now consume far less capacity (activation width halved thanks to int8).

A realistic hierarchy could look like:

```
[On-chip L2 SRAM (~256 MB)] ← stores active layers’ packed weights
    ↕︎
[HBM2e (~800 GB/s)] ↔︎ Handles overflow layers & KV cache
    ↕︎
[DDR4/DDR5] ← Stores checkpointed full model snapshots
```

The top two tiers already fit several tens of billions of ternary parameters comfortably—a clear step toward “single‑chip LLMs”.

## Putting it all together: A plausible chip spec  

| Spec | Rationale derived from BitNet b1.58 |
|------|-----------------------------------|
| Compute cores | Hundreds of thousands of add-only PEs arranged in a systolic grid |
| Weight storage | Dual‑ported SRAM banks with custom packing logic (~2 bits/param) |
| Activation buffers | Dedicated int8 buffers per core (~64 KB) |
| Accumulator width | 32‑bit signed integer adder tree per PE |
| Re‑quantizer block | Small reduction tree + `RoundClip` logic stitched after every optimizer step |
| Power budget | ≤ 30 W per mm² (thanks to ≈71× lower MAC energy) |
| Peak throughput | > 10 TFLOP<sub>int8</sub> equivalents while consuming < 200 W total |
| On-chip model capacity | ≥ 30 B parameters (~6 GB packed) without external DRAM |

Such numbers are intentionally aspirational yet consistent with trends seen in Groq’s LPUs—replace heavy floating point units with lightweight integer pipelines—and align perfectly with BitNet’s empirical gains reported in Tables 2–5 and Figures 3–5 of the paper.

## Outlook & open challenges  

* **Standardizing ternary formats:** The community still relies mainly on fp16/fp32 binaries when exchanging models; agreeing on an open-packed layout would accelerate hardware adoption.
* **Sparse execution engines:** Since zeros are native values here they can be harnessed by dynamic gating—a direction worth exploring beyond static run-length packing.
* **Mixed‐precision training pipelines:** While BitNet trains from scratch using ternary weights already, extending this pipeline to ultra‐large models (>​100 B) will likely need more sophisticated gradient quantizers—a research avenue compatible with our proposed re‑quantizer block.
* **Edge deployment:** The dramatic memory reduction also invites CPU‑centric designs where SIMD units perform sign‐flip additions on registers—a natural fit for mobile SoCs aiming at on-device LLMs.

In summary, BitNet b1.58 proves that removing multiplications is not just a software trick—it reshapes every layer of the compute stack—from algorithmic scaling laws all the way up to silicon floorplanning. By aligning hardware design around ternary weights and integer addition we unlock orders-of-magnitude improvements in latency, memory footprint, throughput, and energy—exactly what next‑generation AI workloads demand.
