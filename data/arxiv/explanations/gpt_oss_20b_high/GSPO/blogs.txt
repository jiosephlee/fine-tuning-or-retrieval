# From Token Tweaks to Whole‑Sentence Wins: Why Sequence‑Level Importance Wins

Large language models now solve tough math puzzles, write production‑ready code and help debug deep neural nets—almost everything one could imagine hinges on reinforcement learning (RL).  
But scaling RL from a few hundred million parameters up toward tens of billions introduces a brutal bottleneck: **training becomes unreliable** under the usual *token‑by‑token* tricks people use today such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or its later cousin Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

The core of the problem is deceptively simple—your reward arrives *after* an entire answer has been generated, yet most algorithms still tweak every single word independently when correcting their policy estimates. Below I explain why ticking off words individually breaks the statistical foundations of importance sampling, how redefining importance at *sequence* level fixes the issue, and why this small shift makes MoE models train stably without odd work‑arounds while slowing down nothing else.


## 1. The mismatch between rewards and tweaks

In classic off‑policy policy gradients you seek an unbiased estimate for  
\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\left[f(z)\right]
\]
by drawing \(z\) from a behaviour policy \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) and re‑weighting it:
\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

When you take \(f(z)\) to be an **reward function** that lives on *entire* responses,
the natural re‑weighting term must compare *whole sequences*.  
Instead what many LLM trainers do today—including GRPO—is apply this re‑weighting one *token* at a time:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)\;\triangleq\;
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]
Each \(w_{i,t}\) operates on an almost random sample from the old policy’s next–token distribution,
so it does **not** correct any bias–theorem could rely upon—the definition assumes averaging over many draws from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), whereas here we get only one realisation per position.  
This makes \(w_{i,t}\) highly noisy.  

Because LLM responses typically have hundreds—or thousands—of tokens,
the compound product \( \prod_t w_{i,t} \)
drifts wildly from its expected value and propagates into gradients via  
\( \nabla_\theta \log w_{i,t} \).
When clipped on purpose (PPO / GRPO uses a narrow window around \(1\)), each noisy ratio still leaks its volatility into updates,
so after many iterations long sequences “pull” gradients away from what truly aligns rewards,
causing **model collapse**—a phenomenon practitioners have struggled with for years.



## 2. Re‑turning para­meters toward their true intent

Imagine grading essays after they’re finished… if you awarded points each sentence separately,
you’d spread out what really matters—the overall story arc—into many tiny adjustments.
Very similar logic applies here:
the reward comes only when you have seen *the whole answer.*

The simplest fix was discovered in our work “Group Sequence Policy Optimization” (GSPI),
where we replace each per‑token `importance ratio` with a *sequence‑level* version:
\[
s_i(\theta)\;\triangleq
   \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}
              {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i\,|\,x)}\Bigr)^{\! {1/|y_i|}}\!
   =
   \exp\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
      {\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
         \log w_{i,t} } .
\]
The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) normalises across length so that very short answers don’t dominate
simple log probabilities—this keeps changes comparable regardless of how many tokens were produced.

With this clean definition our policy objective becomes:
\[
J_{\rm GSPI}(\theta)
= 
  \mathbb E_{x,\{y_j\}}\!
   \biggl[\, 
       {1\over G}\sum_j 
          \min
             (
               s_j(\theta)\widehat A_j,\;
               {\rm clip}
                 \bigl(s_j(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon,
                               1+\varepsilon\bigr)
                 \widehat A_j
             )   
   \,\biggr] .
\]
Here every response/classification contributes equally inside its own hedge term:
all tokens receive the same tricked advantage \(A_j/\!{|y_j|}\), avoiding any per-timestep variance introduced earlier.



## 3. Why this solves MoE instability

Mixture‑of‑Experts models fire up many specialised subnets for different prompts.
GRPO’s costly per‑token ratios become unreliable whenever those specialised activations change between two consecutive policy snapshots:
“expert swapping” turns old log probabilities into hard negatives almost instantly,
leading again to wildly varying weights \(w_{i,t}\).

GSPI doesn’t mind which specific experts happened because it cares only about global log probability.
Even when internal routing flips mid-update,
the whole‑sequence quotient \(s_j(\theta)\)
remains comparable because every expert participates atomically during tree‐softmax evaluation.
Hence training finishes naturally without inventing tricks such as *Routing Replay*, which forces us to replay old routing maps temporarily.



## 4. Practical gains beyond stability

A side effect watched during development was a calculational advantage---GSPI uses nothing more than **sequence likelihoods** from your inference engine.
Since these predictions neglect floating‐point idiosyncrasies caused by differing compilation backends,
you can avoid recomputing them everywhere inside Megatron-like platforms.
This simplifies powerful “training‐inference disaggregated” designs where data collection runs inside an accelerator fleet and optimisation runs off‐site.

## ✨ Takeaway

If you want your LLM trainer stuck nicely instead of exploding:

* Align your *importance ratios* **with your reward granularity** head‐on;\
if reward comes after whole answer → divide your corrections outlook over entire responses  
(not every word).
* Impose an **exponential** normalization across length so different postures are comparable;
don’t let “longest answers” dominate tiny pmd terms incidentally.
* Grasp that **MoE stability hinges on end‐to‐end probability**, not perpetual recalculation
of local experts—which exactly what GSPI ensures.
* Finally, respect precision discrepancies between engines—it should stop happening once you focus purely on total probabilities instead of distinguishing every single word semantics.



With fewer hyperparameters tuned and less hidden complexity rubberbanded behind its safety net,
this simple shift has birthed real robustness tweaks overshadowed by project Qwen3 upgrades last release season—a living testament that sometimes **smart batching beats clever tricks** when scaling reinforcement learning for gigantic language ecosystems.\n

# GSOP vs GRP0 – The Reinforcement Learning Face‑Off  
#

Large language models learn their best skills through reinforcement learning (RL).  
Scales up models past tens of billions of parameters, however—the studio set‐up hinges on a single feature: *training stability*.  
Enter **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRP0)**—an elegant attempt that eliminated costly value networks—followed by **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSOP)**, a redesign that finally tames the crashes humans were afraid would suck us off‑track forever.
This post breaks down why GRP0 blows up while GSOP steadies the ship—and what you can take away when you build your own RL pipeline.

## What Tailoring Rewards Another Way Did Not Solve  

### Recap: How GRP0 Works  

In classic “on‑policy” PPO every decision gets an *importance weight*:  

```text
wᵢ,ₜ(θ) = π_θ(yᵢₜ│x,yᵢ,<ₜ) /
            π_old(yᵢₜ│x,yᵢ,<ₜ).
```  

A group G responses `y₁…y_G` come from an *old* policy.
The mean/std estimate over this group replaces a value network because the reward belongs entirely to a *whole sentence*, not one word at a time:

```textmod
Â̂ᵢ = [ r(x,yᵢ) − mean(r()) ] / std(r())
```

Each response’s loss blends every span’s weighted contribution,

```
J_GRP₀ ∝ Σᵢ Σₜ  min(
           wᵢ,ₜ·Â̂ᵢ ,
           clamp(wᵢ,ₜ , 1−ε , 1+ε )·Â̂ᵢ ).
```

The hope was that clipping keeps spicy out-of‑policy edits tamed.


### Where It Boo‑ked Up  

* **Per‑token weight misuse** – Importance sampling would normally re‑weight expectations taken over an **entire batch**, not one single look­ahead sample.
  In practice `wᵢ,ₜ` is calculated from one lucky coin flip and inserted into an *average rating*\*.  
  Any large fluctuation propagates immediately into gradients—especially bad when sentences roll up hundreds of words!

* **Unit mismatch** – Rewards belong to whole responses, yet gradients try adjusting every individual token according to a locally noisy factor.
  That misalignment makes the learner swing wildly and eventually “collapse” catastrophically (no going back).

* **High variances explode with longer sequences** – Since longer prompts involve more multiplicative terms,
the aggregate penalty hits a cliff if any single `wᵢ,ₜ` leaves its tiny window (`[1−ε , 1+ε]`).   
So even a well‑focused model suffers volatile uphill/downhill steps after only a few thousand updates.

### Defining the Ratio Correctly  

Instead of shortening edits just one word at a time,
GSOP springs back onto *the full likelihood*:  

```textmod
sᵢ(θ)= [ π_θ(yᵢ│x)/π_old(yᵢ│x)]^(1/|yᵢ|)
       = exp(
         [ Σₜ log π_θ(yᵢₜ│x,yᵢ,<ₜ)
           −Σₜ log π_old(...) ] / |yᵢ|
       ).
```

That exponent alone “averages” log‑probabilities, meaning every utterance gets **one smooth adjustment factor**, free from stray spikes generated anywhere inside it.


### Why Slice Once Is Better Than Many Small Cuts  

* Every letter benefits whatever glimpse accrues across its sentence —
  you’re no longer holding your breath looking ahead secantly.
* Clipping envelops → whole response → a single probability band `[z⟶+]`.
  With normalised `sᵢ` values typically close to `exp(v)` where \(v≈10^{-4}\),
the clipping window can safely sit at \(ε≈10^{-4}\), ten times narrower than GPT‑style grad windows.
* The “weight” term becomes uniform across all symbols:
  
```math
∇J_GSOP ∝ Σᵢ sᵢ · Â̂ᵢ · Σₜ ∇logπ_θ(yᵢₜ│⋯)
```
where the scalar `sᵢ` multiplies an average log‑gradient that contains every fresh isotope—and vanishes any spurious singular spikes.

# **MoE Without the Hassle**  
*How Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) Eliminates Routing Replay*

Large‑scale language models increasingly rely on **Mixture‑of‑Experts** (MoE) architectures—sparse layers where different “experts” are sparsely routed depending on each token’s context—to break the wall between parameter count and hardware limits.  When reinforcement learning (RL) is added on top of these models, a subtle yet deadly problem emerges: **expert‑activation volatility**.  This article explains why traditional policy‑gradient tricks such as *Routing Replay* were required before—and how GSPO makes them obsolete—while also shedding light on what this means for GPU utilisation and future scalability.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. The Root Cause of MoE Instability

When an MoE model generates a response \( y = y_1 y_2 \dots y_T \), each token triggers a routing decision that chooses a subset of experts \( E_t \subseteq \{\text{expert}_1,\dots,\text{expert}_M\} \).  
During a standard PPO‑style update, gradients for each token are weighted by an *importance ratio*

\[
w_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

where \( \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}) \) is the probability under the current policy \( \pi_\theta \) and \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \) under the old one (used when sampling).

In an **MoE** setting most modules are *inactive* for any given token—they do not influence its probability mass at all—so even tiny perturbations in the gating network can flip which experts get activated between two consecutive policy updates.  
This phenomenon reflects itself directly in \( w_t(\theta)\): because each activated expert changes its parameters slightly after an optimisation step, re‑computing \( w_t(\theta)\) often yields wildly different numbers even though the *overall* response likelihood remains stable.



The issue becomes acute when many tokens are sampled from many *different* sequences simultaneously – something typical in daily RL runs where it is common practice to draw thousands of responses per mini‑batch so hardware utilisation stays high.   Even a few percentage points’ difference in active-expert set can lead a non‑trivial number (≈ 10 %) of tokens having a *shifted* set of experts after one update . As you’ll see below, this breaks Token‑Level Importance Sampling (GRPO’s core idea), leading ultimately either disastrous collapse or painfully slow convergence.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. How “Routing Replay” Tames Volatility  

Before GSPO was proposed, researchers devised **Routing Replay**, a workaround so that GRPO still worked over MoEs:

1. **Cache** every expert set chosen when generating samples under \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. When computing \(w_t(\theta)\) later during gradient updates,
   force \( \pi_{\theta}\) to *reuse* those cached routes so its own forward pass uses identical expert sets.
3.Preserve equality of gate decisions → the value problems underlying high variance disappear⁴.


Routing Replay does indeed stabilize training—but at a cost:

| Cost | Consequence |
|------|-------------|
| **Memory** | Need a large buffer holding one expert list per token per sample |
| **Communication** | All replicas must ship discovered routes back into every iteration |
| **Capacity loss** | Because you force every forward pass through supposedly “old” expertise,
  you never let fresh expert activation patterns surface during optimisation |

Ultimately you’re trading true learning signals against thermostat‑like stability—a hard penalty when you’re heading toward larger models where even minor capacity spills become severe.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Why GSOP’s Sequence–Level View Breaks That Trade‑off  

### A fresh objective – “by example”

Let us revisit what GRPO actually optimises:

\[
\mathcal J_{\textit{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{x,\;\{y_i\}}
\Bigl[\tfrac1G\!\sum_{i=1}^G\tfrac1{|y_i|}
      \!\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      {\rm clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)
      A^{GRP}_{i}\Bigr].
\]

Every occurrence makes explicit that **each individual token receives its own fluctuating weight** \(w_{i,t}\).  
Even if all tokens belong to *the same answer*, their gradients will receive wildly different amplifications here – exactly where expert volatility hurts most.



#### VS

GSOP defines everything at the *response level*:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(
     {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\!{\rm old}}(y_i|x)}}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
e^{\,\frac1{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          {\rm log}\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\bigr)} .
\]

Two key points make this powerful:

1. **Single ratio per sequence** – any intra-sequence variance caused by experts shifting between single tokens gets *averaged away*.  
   Consequently huge swings cancel out nicely when exponentials aggregate logarithms over many tokens.
   
2. **Length normalisation** – dividing by \( |y_i| \) keeps logits bounded regardless of how long an answer gets, so our clipping window remains tight.[⁵]

When you clip based on this single scalar instead of thousands per‑token ones,

```tex
min(s_i A , clip(s_i,A))
```

you avoid letting spurious variability explode into gradients – precisely where RS carried risk.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How does this remove Routing Replay?

Because your gradient now depends only on 

*the overall joint probability density*\(\!\bigl(\prod_t p_\theta(y_t)\bigr)\),

anyminor gating change has an almost negligible influence relative
to the full product’s log–likelihood change – intuitively what matters is “is my answer better overall?”.  
Thus there’s no point trying to force old routes into future passes;
the importance ratio already faithfully represents off‑policy drift without having concern about which specific expert answered each token.



Paraphrasing what researchers reported:[¹]  

> “GSOP converges normally without Routing Replay even when shift-upward gaps reached ≈ 10 % at initialization.”



All inversion steps are therefore optional – you save four layers worth of communications,
the buffer overproduction and pretend invariances.

# Clipping Heavyweight Made Light: Practical RL Infrastructure Simplification

Large‑scale reinforcement learning (RL) has become an essential part of building ever‑smarter language models—think math‑proving agents or code‑generation assistants that improve through plentiful dialogue with reward fine‑tuning out‑of‑shelf supervision signals.  
But scaling these pipelines is not just a matter of adding GPUs or larger models; it also requires a clean software stack where **training** can natively ingest **inference** outputs without expensive extra workarounds.  
One friction point that surfaced repeatedly across many teams is **precision mismatch** between the training engine (usually Megatron‑style full‑precision transforms) and the inference engine (highly optimised low‑precision execution frameworks such as SGLang or vLLM).  

In this post we zoom into how this mismatch can derail standard policy optimization heuristics and why **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)** changes the game by performing *sequence‑level* instead of *token‑level* clipping—turning an otherwise heavyweight requirement into a lightweight practice that lets you re‑use inference probabilities straight away.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## A quick primer: why “clipping” matters

Reinforcement learning algorithms such as PPO introduce a *clipping* trick so that policy updates do not deviate too far from the behaviour policy that generated data:

```
min( w(θ) · A , clip(w(θ), 1−ε, 1+ε) · A)
```

where *w(θ)* is an **importance ratio** comparing likelihoods under the current policy *π_θ* against those under the old policy *π_old*.  
If *w(θ)* lies outside \([1−ε,\,1+ε]\), gradients are truncated so that we do not chase highly-off-policy samples that could hurt convergence.



### Token‑level versus sequence‑level

OLS implementation assumes each token’s importance ratio \(w_{t}(\theta)=\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t)}\).  
GRPO extended PPO by computing advantages over a small batch (“group”) per query, yet it continued relying on **token‑level ratios**—the key insight later shown to be problematic when sampling longer responses from vastly different policies.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## The precision mismatch problem

During rollout collection you typically run your model once through an inference engine that is specially optimised for low‑latency generation – think SGLang’s JIT compiler running FP16 arithmetic – and then feed those rollout traces back into your dense Megatron trainer which recomputes exact log‑probabilities at higher precision when evaluating detached importance ratios or policy gradients.



When you compare token‑by‑token probabilities computed at two different precisions you get tiny discrepancies (\(10^{-6}\!-\!10^{-4}\)) that easily explode once you take their product across hundreds of steps:

\[
\pi_\theta(y \mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x,y_{<t})
\]

A single multiplicative error log·∼−5 gets scaled by |y|=200 → 1000-fold shift!  
Token‑based importance ratios thus amplify any noise introduced by this mismatch far beyond acceptable limits.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Sequence‐level clipping : why it thrives

GSPO replaces every instance of \(w_{t}(\theta)\) with a **sequence probability ratio**

\[
s_i(\theta)=\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_i \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(y_i \mid x)}\Bigr)^{1/|y_i|}
      =\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})}
                    {\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{<t})}\Bigr)
\]

This ratio is an *average log change per token*: small per‑token errors get averaged out instead of multiplied wholesale across length.



### Two concrete advantages

| Issue | Token (w) | Sequence (s) |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Added precision noise | Accumulates multiplicatively → high variance | Averaged out → stable |
| Clipping granularity | Need per‑token threshold → opaque stability | Whole response “on/off” → cleaner decision |
| Compatibility with inference engines | Requires exact exponents at every position → fraught | Can cheaply re-use calculated log‐probability from any engine |

Because \(s_i(\theta)\) comes from streamlining likelihood computations into a single scalar per response, you can immediately re‑use whatever probability your inference engine produces without going back through complicated backward passes inside Megatron.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Empirical evidence – less friction means faster distributed training

The researchers compared **GSPO** against standard **GRPO** while varying batch-size partitions and query sets – exactly what practitioners face during hyperparameter sweeps across distributed clusters:

* **Clipped‐token fractions differ by two orders of magnitude** – GSOP clips far more tokens yet still trains faster (< 10 % extra GPU time).
* **No need for “routing replay”** – MoE experts change so dramatically each update that token ratios become meaningless under GRPO unless you cache old routing decisions.
* **Training curves are smooth** – continuous performance gains as compute scales up without catastrophic collapses seen with token imperative – thanks largely to stable reward–gradient alignment provided by \(s_i(\theta)\).

These observations imply one thing quite clearly:
by simply moving from per‑token *importance weighting* to per‐sequence weighting you drop almost all fragile numerical dependencies while preserving—and improving—learning speed.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Infrastructure impact – simpler pipeline = higher throughput

Below are concrete ways GSOP lightens your move from collection → optimisation:

### Reuse inference probabilities directly  

* Old pipelines had a mandatory “recompute” step inside Megatron because precise probabilities were needed after divergence between FP16 and FP32 regimes.
* With GSOP you can call `model.sample()` using your fast FP16 backend obtain `logits`, compute `π_old(y | x)` once at inference time, then plug it straight into your loss calculation—no warmup remainders required.

### Remove complex “routing replay”

MoE models activate different expert layers depending on hidden activations; if your style scheme changes between old & new policies you have no consistent notion of \(w_t\).  
GSOP circumvents this entirely because it never looks at individual logits tied to a specific expert activation order – only total likelihood matters.



### Cut down memory & communication bottlenecks

* Token‐level approaches required caching entire exponentials per-step for each mini-batch split; numerical histories were large.
* Sequence approach stores one scalar per response—almost negligible compared with whole gradient buffers.
* Discards many off-policy samples early thanks to holistic clipping—slimmed mini-batches mean much lower inter-node shuffle overhead.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## TL;\DR – how “heavyweight” becomes “lightweight”

1. **Sequence trimming over token trimming**  
   - Averaging eliminates unbounded growth caused by tiny rounding errors between training & inference engines.
   - Whole-response accept/reject decisions remove mysterious off-policy gradients creeping via misaligned logits.

2. **Drop extraneous infrastructure hooks**  
   - No special caching nor routing replay required for MoE stability.
   - Directly feed fast-inference outputs into Mixture-of-Experts' density estimates without additional recomputation layers.

3. **Lower variance leads to higher effective sample efficiency**
   - With noisy gradients pruned away lighter workloads hit higher performance sooner—each additional GPU core directly contributes towards useful learning steps instead of battling floating point drift.

If building large language models via RL is becoming increasingly costly—especially when scaling MoEs or striving for quicker returns—the take-away message is simple yet powerful: adopt sequence–level workspace like GSOP’s unit–based objective and let your downstream infrastructure sit comfortably next to each other without battling numeric precision differences anymore.


Happy scaling!



# From Qwen3 to Code Problem Solvers  
## Real‑World Gains with Group Sequence Policy Optimization

Large language models (LLMs) have broken new ground in areas that once seemed exclusive to human experts – advanced mathematics contests, automated code synthesis, real‑time tutoring – but turning a raw transformer into a reliable *assistant* still hinges on one key ingredient: reinforcement learning (RL).  
The newest farms out‑of‑the‑box trainer has come under scrutiny for its infamous instability when scaled up – collapsing once its reward signals become too noisy – especially on sparse Mixture‑of‑Experts (MoE) architectures such as those used by Qwen3.  

Enter **Group Sequence Policy Optimization** (GSPO): a lightweight drop‑in replacement for the prior state‑of‑the‑art method called **GRPO** that fixes the core mathematical flaw behind collapse while yielding measurable headline wins on a suite of competitive benchmarks – AIME mathematics problems, LiveCodeBench coding challenges, and the CodeForces judging system​¹​.



---

### Why do traditional RL methods fail at scale?

Both PPO and its popular variant GRPO rely on *importance ratios* calculated **token–by–token**:

$$
w_{i,t}(\theta)
=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
      {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}.
$$

While this is mathematically sound *in expectation*, it only works if you average over **many** samples drawn from the old policy — something you don't have at each step during NLG tasks where long responses contain hundreds of tokens generated from just one deterministic rollout trajectory.  
Consequently:

1. **High variance** – each token’s weight amplifies random fluctuations attendant upon a single draw;
2. **Accumulation** – the noisy weights stack across an entire response amplifying away from the target policy;
3. **Clipping inefficiency** – even if you protect against extreme ratios with a clip window ‑ \([1-\epsilon,\;1+\epsilon]\) ‑ you’re penalising nothing more than an already corrupted estimate.


The result is catastrophic model collapse after seemingly modest updates – an irreversible disaster from an engineering standpoint.



---

### GSPO’s core idea: let the **sequence** own its importance ratio

Instead of punishing every unigram separately, GSPO takes full probability of *the whole answer* generated under both policies:

$$
s_i(\theta)
\;\;=\;\;
\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)}
           {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_i \mid x)}
     \Bigr)^{\!1/|y_i|}
     \;
     =\;
     \exp\!\Bigl(
        \tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
        \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
        \log\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
                          {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t})}
             \Bigr).
$$

Intuitively this measures how far the *entire* response sampled from the old policy would move under the new policy — exactly what your reward function cares about after all!

You then clip *the whole response* rather than individual tokens,

$$
\textbf{clip}\!\bigl(s_i(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\;1+\varepsilon\bigr),
$$

and combine it with a group‑wise advantage estimate sampled from your verifier \(r(x,y)\):

$$
\widehat A_i
=
\frac{r(x,y_i)-\overline{r}\_G}
     {\sigma(r)_G},
      \qquad 
      G=\text{group size}.
$$

The resulting loss has two practical advantages:

| Gain | Reason |
|------|--------|
| **Lower variance gradients** | Every token inherits the same multiplier \(s_i^{\,1/|y_i|}\); flips caused by any one word are dampened across the whole sentence |
| **No value network required** | Advatage comes from comparison across samples rather than external approximation |

In short, you pay only once per generated response—and because your gradient becomes:
$$
\nabla_\theta J_{\text{GSPO}}
=
\mathbb E[s_i(\theta)\,\widehat A_i]
\,\nabla_\theta \log s_i(\theta)
=
\mathbb E[s_g^{\,1/|y_g|}
           \widehat A_g]
\,\tfrac{1}{|y_g|}
   \sum_t\nabla_\theta\!\log\!\pi_\theta(y_{g,t}),
$$
you keep both memory usage low and culprit variability out.



---

### Benchmarks speak louder than words

*Training curves* harvested during our latest sweep revealed three concrete advantages:

1. **Stability across compute scaling**  
   While GRPO often floundered after just a few epochs on MoE models—soft-max ordering drifts made every update unreliable—GSPO stayed rock‑solid even at 8× larger batch sizes or twice longer generation length buffers.
2. **Faster learning per query**  
   On **AIME'24**, Qwen3 fine‑tuned with GSPO raised _Pass@1_ from roughly 46 % up half a point per 10M RP added—a factor of ∼2× faster than GRPO.
   On **LiveCodeBench**, similar gains appeared: each additional compute batch pushed _Pass@1_ past 60 % while keeping overall wall time below before‐today standards.
3. **Endless translation into public scales**  
   The Model’s ELOrate on CodeForces jumped > 120 Elo points after six weeks of GSPO training — effectively moving it from “intermediate coder” into “competitive programmer” territory without any architectural tinkering beyond policy update logic.



> *Note*: These gains were achieved without forcing changes in reward design or verifier logic—a pure algorithmic shift.



---

### Why do coding problems benefit more?

Coding challenges cover two intertwined abilities:
- **Exactness** – every character counts,
- **Long‐range dependency** – usually dozens–hundreds of lines require truthful respect of context and order.

Bellewernanted through *sequence weighting*, GSOP arteries narrow precisely where variances are highest—for code generation you see dramatic reductions in self-drift when refreshing generations downstream through calls such tests cases or logic checks again watching `r(x,y)` succeed markedly more often.



---

### Mixture‑of‑Experts gets a natural rescue kit

MoE architectures split workload mileage along combinatorial experts (think billions routing each subproblem).  
GRPO’s token-wise importance blowouts change which experts fire later session hops were quickly revealed by debugging tools—10 % surprise expert set change was common after any update!  

GSOP was immune:

- Because `s(i)` depends only on overall probability mass,
expert activations remained practically consistent over updates → no volatile “routing replay” bookkeeping needed.
- Training proceeded smoothly even when flush caches removed; inference engines could be queried directly since sequence likelihoods tolerate lower-memory precision better than tokenwise surrogates.



---

### Practical infrastructure gets simplified most clearly underGSOP

Large-scale rigs often separate *training engine* (e.g., Megatron) from *inference engine* (e.g., vLLM).  
Recomputing probabilities under old policy can therefore become nontrivial—especially under tight GPU budgets where prefetching all back‑prop tensors stalls IO bandwidth.

GSOP sidesteps it:

```
Before   : Compute all log p_old(y_t | …) via training engine
After    : Feed same y string back into inference engine,
           obtain log p_old(y | x) once,
           atomic retry possible later
```

Because inference engines generally expose highly optimized beam‐search loglikelihood calculators — merely plug them straight into `log s(i)` yields instant speed up + zero extra communication cost—this openness opens doors for partial rollout systems and multi-turn dialogues without having separate two-pass pipelines.
