# From Token Tweaks to Whole‑Sentence Wins: Why Sequence‑Level Importance Wins

Large language models now solve tough math puzzles, write production‑ready code and help debug deep neural nets—almost everything one could imagine hinges on reinforcement learning (RL).  
But scaling RL from a few hundred million parameters up toward tens of billions introduces a brutal bottleneck: **training becomes unreliable** under the usual *token‑by‑token* tricks people use today such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or its later cousin Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

The core of the problem is deceptively simple—your reward arrives *after* an entire answer has been generated, yet most algorithms still tweak every single word independently when correcting their policy estimates. Below I explain why ticking off words individually breaks the statistical foundations of importance sampling, how redefining importance at *sequence* level fixes the issue, and why this small shift makes MoE models train stably without odd work‑arounds while slowing down nothing else.


## 1. The mismatch between rewards and tweaks

In classic off‑policy policy gradients you seek an unbiased estimate for  
\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{tar}}}\!\left[f(z)\right]
\]
by drawing \(z\) from a behaviour policy \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\) and re‑weighting it:
\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \pi_{\text{beh}}}\!\left[\frac{\pi_{\text{tar}}(z)}{\pi_{\text{beh}}(z)}\,f(z)\right].
\]

When you take \(f(z)\) to be an **reward function** that lives on *entire* responses,
the natural re‑weighting term must compare *whole sequences*.  
Instead what many LLM trainers do today—including GRPO—is apply this re‑weighting one *token* at a time:
\[
w_{i,t}(\theta)\;\triangleq\;
   \frac{\pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)}
        {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}\!\bigl(y_{i,t}\mid x,y_{i,<t}\bigr)} .
\]
Each \(w_{i,t}\) operates on an almost random sample from the old policy’s next–token distribution,
so it does **not** correct any bias–theorem could rely upon—the definition assumes averaging over many draws from \(\pi_{\text{beh}}\), whereas here we get only one realisation per position.  
This makes \(w_{i,t}\) highly noisy.  

Because LLM responses typically have hundreds—or thousands—of tokens,
the compound product \( \prod_t w_{i,t} \)
drifts wildly from its expected value and propagates into gradients via  
\( \nabla_\theta \log w_{i,t} \).
When clipped on purpose (PPO / GRPO uses a narrow window around \(1\)), each noisy ratio still leaks its volatility into updates,
so after many iterations long sequences “pull” gradients away from what truly aligns rewards,
causing **model collapse**—a phenomenon practitioners have struggled with for years.



## 2. Re‑turning para­meters toward their true intent

Imagine grading essays after they’re finished… if you awarded points each sentence separately,
you’d spread out what really matters—the overall story arc—into many tiny adjustments.
Very similar logic applies here:
the reward comes only when you have seen *the whole answer.*

The simplest fix was discovered in our work “Group Sequence Policy Optimization” (GSPI),
where we replace each per‑token `importance ratio` with a *sequence‑level* version:
\[
s_i(\theta)\;\triangleq
   \Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i\,|\,x)}
              {\pi_{\theta_{\!old}}(y_i\,|\,x)}\Bigr)^{\! {1/|y_i|}}\!
   =
   \exp\tfrac{1}{|y_i|}
      {\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
         \log w_{i,t} } .
\]
The exponent \(1/|y_i|\) normalises across length so that very short answers don’t dominate
simple log probabilities—this keeps changes comparable regardless of how many tokens were produced.

With this clean definition our policy objective becomes:
\[
J_{\rm GSPI}(\theta)
= 
  \mathbb E_{x,\{y_j\}}\!
   \biggl[\, 
       {1\over G}\sum_j 
          \min
             (
               s_j(\theta)\widehat A_j,\;
               {\rm clip}
                 \bigl(s_j(\theta),\, 1-\varepsilon,
                               1+\varepsilon\bigr)
                 \widehat A_j
             )   
   \,\biggr] .
\]
Here every response/classification contributes equally inside its own hedge term:
all tokens receive the same tricked advantage \(A_j/\!{|y_j|}\), avoiding any per-timestep variance introduced earlier.



## 3. Why this solves MoE instability

Mixture‑of‑Experts models fire up many specialised subnets for different prompts.
GRPO’s costly per‑token ratios become unreliable whenever those specialised activations change between two consecutive policy snapshots:
“expert swapping” turns old log probabilities into hard negatives almost instantly,
leading again to wildly varying weights \(w_{i,t}\).

GSPI doesn’t mind which specific experts happened because it cares only about global log probability.
Even when internal routing flips mid-update,
the whole‑sequence quotient \(s_j(\theta)\)
remains comparable because every expert participates atomically during tree‐softmax evaluation.
Hence training finishes naturally without inventing tricks such as *Routing Replay*, which forces us to replay old routing maps temporarily.



## 4. Practical gains beyond stability

A side effect watched during development was a calculational advantage---GSPI uses nothing more than **sequence likelihoods** from your inference engine.
Since these predictions neglect floating‐point idiosyncrasies caused by differing compilation backends,
you can avoid recomputing them everywhere inside Megatron-like platforms.
This simplifies powerful “training‐inference disaggregated” designs where data collection runs inside an accelerator fleet and optimisation runs off‐site.

The curves look smooth through dozens—hundreds—of epochs;
no catastrophic trajectory breaks ever appeared,
despite clamping vastly more tokens than under GRPI’s narrow window—
a counterintuitive early warning accepted in hindsight:
more clipping merely means eliminating *noisy* gradients,
and migrating these away globally captures useful signal better.



## ✨ Takeaway

If you want your LLM trainer stuck nicely instead of exploding:

* Align your *importance ratios* **with your reward granularity** head‐on;\
if reward comes after whole answer → divide your corrections outlook over entire responses  
(not every word).
* Impose an **exponential** normalization across length so different postures are comparable;
don’t let “longest answers” dominate tiny pmd terms incidentally.
* Grasp that **MoE stability hinges on end‐to‐end probability**, not perpetual recalculation
of local experts—which exactly what GSPI ensures.
* Finally, respect precision discrepancies between engines—it should stop happening once you focus purely on total probabilities instead of distinguishing every single word semantics.



With fewer hyperparameters tuned and less hidden complexity rubberbanded behind its safety net,
this simple shift has birthed real robustness tweaks overshadowed by project Qwen3 upgrades last release season—a living testament that sometimes **smart batching beats clever tricks** when scaling reinforcement learning for gigantic language ecosystems.\n
