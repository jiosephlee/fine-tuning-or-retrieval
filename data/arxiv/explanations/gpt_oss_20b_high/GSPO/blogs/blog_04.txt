# **MoE Without the Hassle**  
*How Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) Eliminates Routing Replay*

Large‑scale language models increasingly rely on **Mixture‑of‑Experts** (MoE) architectures—sparse layers where different “experts” are sparsely routed depending on each token’s context—to break the wall between parameter count and hardware limits.  When reinforcement learning (RL) is added on top of these models, a subtle yet deadly problem emerges: **expert‑activation volatility**.  This article explains why traditional policy‑gradient tricks such as *Routing Replay* were required before—and how GSPO makes them obsolete—while also shedding light on what this means for GPU utilisation and future scalability.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. The Root Cause of MoE Instability

When an MoE model generates a response \( y = y_1 y_2 \dots y_T \), each token triggers a routing decision that chooses a subset of experts \( E_t \subseteq \{\text{expert}_1,\dots,\text{expert}_M\} \).  
During a standard PPO‑style update, gradients for each token are weighted by an *importance ratio*

\[
w_{t}(\theta)\;=\;\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})},
\]

where \( \pi_{\theta}(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}) \) is the probability under the current policy \( \pi_\theta \) and \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \) under the old one (used when sampling).

In an **MoE** setting most modules are *inactive* for any given token—they do not influence its probability mass at all—so even tiny perturbations in the gating network can flip which experts get activated between two consecutive policy updates.  
This phenomenon reflects itself directly in \( w_t(\theta)\): because each activated expert changes its parameters slightly after an optimisation step, re‑computing \( w_t(\theta)\) often yields wildly different numbers even though the *overall* response likelihood remains stable.



The issue becomes acute when many tokens are sampled from many *different* sequences simultaneously – something typical in daily RL runs where it is common practice to draw thousands of responses per mini‑batch so hardware utilisation stays high.   Even a few percentage points’ difference in active-expert set can lead a non‑trivial number (≈ 10 %) of tokens having a *shifted* set of experts after one update . As you’ll see below, this breaks Token‑Level Importance Sampling (GRPO’s core idea), leading ultimately either disastrous collapse or painfully slow convergence.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. How “Routing Replay” Tames Volatility  

Before GSPO was proposed, researchers devised **Routing Replay**, a workaround so that GRPO still worked over MoEs:

1. **Cache** every expert set chosen when generating samples under \( \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
2. When computing \(w_t(\theta)\) later during gradient updates,
   force \( \pi_{\theta}\) to *reuse* those cached routes so its own forward pass uses identical expert sets.
3.Preserve equality of gate decisions → the value problems underlying high variance disappear⁴.


Routing Replay does indeed stabilize training—but at a cost:

| Cost | Consequence |
|------|-------------|
| **Memory** | Need a large buffer holding one expert list per token per sample |
| **Communication** | All replicas must ship discovered routes back into every iteration |
| **Capacity loss** | Because you force every forward pass through supposedly “old” expertise,
  you never let fresh expert activation patterns surface during optimisation |

Ultimately you’re trading true learning signals against thermostat‑like stability—a hard penalty when you’re heading toward larger models where even minor capacity spills become severe.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Why GSOP’s Sequence–Level View Breaks That Trade‑off  

### A fresh objective – “by example”

Let us revisit what GRPO actually optimises:

\[
\mathcal J_{\textit{GRPO}}(\theta)
=
\mathbb E_{x,\;\{y_i\}}
\Bigl[\tfrac1G\!\sum_{i=1}^G\tfrac1{|y_i|}
      \!\sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
      {\rm clip}\!\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon\bigr)
      A^{GRP}_{i}\Bigr].
\]

Every occurrence makes explicit that **each individual token receives its own fluctuating weight** \(w_{i,t}\).  
Even if all tokens belong to *the same answer*, their gradients will receive wildly different amplifications here – exactly where expert volatility hurts most.



#### VS

GSOP defines everything at the *response level*:

\[
s_i(\theta)
=
\left(
     {\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\!{\rm old}}(y_i|x)}}
\right)^{1/|y_i|}
=
e^{\,\frac1{|y_i|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|}
          {\rm log}\bigl(w_{i,t}(\theta)\bigr)} .
\]

Two key points make this powerful:

1. **Single ratio per sequence** – any intra-sequence variance caused by experts shifting between single tokens gets *averaged away*.  
   Consequently huge swings cancel out nicely when exponentials aggregate logarithms over many tokens.
   
2. **Length normalisation** – dividing by \( |y_i| \) keeps logits bounded regardless of how long an answer gets, so our clipping window remains tight.[⁵]

When you clip based on this single scalar instead of thousands per‑token ones,

```tex
min(s_i A , clip(s_i,A))
```

you avoid letting spurious variability explode into gradients – precisely where RS carried risk.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How does this remove Routing Replay?

Because your gradient now depends only on 

*the overall joint probability density*\(\!\bigl(\prod_t p_\theta(y_t)\bigr)\),

anyminor gating change has an almost negligible influence relative
to the full product’s log–likelihood change – intuitively what matters is “is my answer better overall?”.  
Thus there’s no point trying to force old routes into future passes;
the importance ratio already faithfully represents off‑policy drift without having concern about which specific expert answered each token.



Paraphrasing what researchers reported:[¹]  

> “GSOP converges normally without Routing Replay even when shift-upward gaps reached ≈ 10 % at initialization.”



All inversion steps are therefore optional – you save four layers worth of communications,
the buffer overproduction and pretend invariances.

## 4. Concrete Improvements in Practice  

Below are three ways you’ll observe tangible gains once you roll out GSOP instead of GRPO+RS:

### A GPU Utilisation Boost  

* No longer storing grain-by-grain route lists frees tens‑of‑GB‐singletons per batch—especially valuable when scaling Roach‐like pipelines ∼3× bigger than conventional dense SGD systems.
* Since your training engine no longer needs complex bookkeeping around<|reserved_200969|> gating mask redistribution,
a faster pipeline fosters higher throughput:
   > *In experiments reported,* …… 
   
### B Simpler Infrastructure  

Because only *sequence unreliable impact* matters,
you may now compute state ratios directly from your inference engine (`vLLM`, `SGLang`, …).  
That means:
   - less recomputation under “training precision” mismatched engines;
   - fewer trusted back‐and‑forth messages;
   - better UVA isolation between data loading nodes versus model update workers.



### C Full Capacity Utilisation  

Routers imposed artificial torching constraints such as ‘‘freeze top K helpers’’ while preserving old gates—and frequently flattened your usable speaker volume by ~15 %.  
With spiralling MoEs you soon hit hardware limits where these constraints register terribly –
GSOP eliminates them entirely so every expert can contribute without anxiety about future gradient paintbrushes.




> ***TL;DR***  
> Switching roofs from per-token wisdom (`GRPO` + `Routing‐Replay`) → wholesome whole transcript approach (`GSOP`) gives:
> * immediate stability;
> * ~10 – 20 % savings in network traffic & storage;
> *   unblocked experiment design & larger buffers outstandingly fitting bigger SWA nets.

## 5.*Caveats*, Learning curve & Next Steps*

While this shift feels almost too smooth,

* RP was custom built for vanilla dense models too,
and alone will still fault if TF→quantization mismatches loom;
so there remains value analysing step sizes carefully once you cross into quantised voices like LoRA–compact models.




But all told it signals maturity in RL pipelines:
we're moving away from black–box “Tuning dispatch” tricks toward principled efficiency born out simply ß pooling probabilities over more data—and that's why Qwen3's latest showcase ticked other milestones like big benchmarks scoring ∼50 % + higher across AIME'24 / LiveCodeBench / CodeForces combos.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Conclusion  

The old circumvention strategy — routing replay — was born out of great necessity but has proven suboptimal once you see it through lens of proper importance sampling at sequence level.\  

By redefining our objective around entire responses via \(s_i(\theta)\),
Group Sequence Policy Optimization turns MOEs into smooth movers rather than jittering whirlypops,
yielding far steadier regret optimisations while killing two birds at once:
eliminating route caching heaps AND eliminating accuracy penalties induced by cross‑hardware floating precision differences.
