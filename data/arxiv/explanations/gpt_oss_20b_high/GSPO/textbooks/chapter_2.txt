Chapter 2: Review Of Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO)

Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) has become one of the most widely adopted reinforcement‑learning algorithms for fine‑tuning language models. Its design philosophy is simple yet powerful: it seeks to perform large‑scale policy updates while ensuring that each update remains close (“proximal”) to the behaviour policy that generated the data. This stability advantage is particularly crucial when training autoregressive language models where every decision—each token generated—carries downstream effects on the entire response sequence. In this chapter we will dissect PPO’s mechanics at an intuitive level, emphasising its token‑wise importance ratios, clipping behaviour, and why it relies heavily on external value models.

---

## Token-wise importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$

Suppose an autoregressive language model parameterised by $\theta$ is treated as a stochastic policy $\pi_{\theta}$. To collect data we generate a response $y = (y_{1}, \dots , y_{|y|})$ conditioned on a query $x$ using the **old** policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. At test time or during training, each next‑token distribution $\pi_{\theta}(\cdot \mid x, y_{<t})$ assigns a probability mass over possible tokens $y_{t}$. The **token‑wise importance ratio**

\[
w_{t}(\theta)= \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}
                  {\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})}
\]

measures how much more or less likely this particular token becomes under the updated policy relative to how it was sampled under the old one. In many reinforcement‑learning settings we often re‑weight samples generated off‑policy so that their expectations under the new target distribution coincide with expectations under the distribution that actually gave rise to them—in other words, we are performing *importance sampling*. When $w_{t}(\theta)$ equals 1,the new policy agrees with the old one at that token step and no correction is needed. Values significantly larger than 1 signal that the new policy deems this token much more probable than before, while values near zero indicate severe divergence.

Because tokens are sampled sequentially conditioned on previously chosen tokens ($y_{<t}$), each probability factor in $\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid x,y_{<t})$ captures all historical context accumulated thus far—this makes $w_{t}(\theta)$ inherently *local* yet *conditioned* upon entire partial prefixes of $y$, reinforcing its moniker “token‑wise.” Crucially, PPO only contrasts probabilities of **individual** outcomes rather than entire sequences; as we will later explore alternative sequence‑level corrections such as those proposed by GSPO.

---

## Clipping window $\varepsilon$

In practice, naïve importance weights can explode when policies drift far apart—most notably after several gradient steps or when dealing with long sequences where small per‑step changes accumulate dramatically into huge likelihood ratios across whole strings. The **clipping** mechanism mitigates this problem by bounding $w_{t}(\theta)$ around unity:

\[
\text{clip}\bigl(w_{t}(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,
1+\varepsilon\bigr)
    = \begin{cases}
        1-\varepsilon & \text{if } w_{t}<1-\varepsilon\\[4pt]
        w_{t}          & \text{if } |w_{t}-1|\le\varepsilon\\[4pt]
        1+\varepsilon & \text{if } w_{t}>1+\varepsilon
      \end{cases}.
\]

The clipped value sandwiches extreme ratios into a compact interval centred at 1. Intuitively rehearse two extreme settings:

*If $\varepsilon$ is set very small,* only tokens whose new-policy probability is almost identical to their old-policy counterpart survive unchanged; all others are forced into cautionary bounds $(1-\varepsilon,\ 1+\varepsilon)$ before contributing to gradients. This drastic truncation yields highly conservative updates but may stall useful learning progress when policies genuinely deserve larger shifts.

*If $\varepsilon$ is chosen large,* many trustworthy weights pass through unmodified while still protecting against extreme outliers that can destabilise training—this yields more adventurous updates but keeps training within a regulated proximity thanks to clipping’s safety net.

Practitioners often pick $\varepsilon$ empirically in the range of $0.2$–$0.3$ for standard language tasks or tune tighter ranges ($10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$) when integrating sequence‑level corrections such as GSPO’s “length–normalised” ratios of full responses instead of single-token ratios.

PPO’s objective integrates this clipped weighting inside a **min** operator:

\[
J_{\text{PPO}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{\,x\sim D,\;\! y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}
   \Bigl[\frac{1}{|y|}
          \sum_{t=1}^{|y|}
              \min\bigl(
                    w_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;
                    \text{clip}(w_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,
                                1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_t
                     \bigr)
            \Bigr].
\]

Here $\hat A_t$ denotes an *advantage estimate* computed at every timestep---a noisy estimate of future return—obtained from an auxiliary **value network** trained alongside the main model. The clipping effectively limits how much an individual action can skew its contribution irrespective of raw likelihood disparity.

---

## Goal: Split expectation over sampled rollouts

At its core POO wants an unbiased estimator of how improving upon current parameters will affect expected returns under future queries $x$. Because data cannot be generated from every conceivable query instantly, PPO resorts to *sampling*: drawing queries uniformly from a dataset $\mathcal{D}$ and rolling out full responses $y$ using $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Each bootstrapped trajectory $(x,y)$ contributes terms proportional to its advance toward higher reward while staying faithful to its original distribution through risk constraints imposed by clipping and weighting multiplicatively via $w_t(\theta)$ and $(\hat A)_t$.

The normalisation pickings serve different purposes:

- Dividing by $|y|$: Because longer sequences naturally accumulate many more tokens—and therefore many more terms—in their summed gradient contribution would inflate their influence disproportionately if left unchecked.
- Normalising observed returns ($r(x,y)$) into advantages ($\hat A_t$): Centring values ensures gradients do not acquire gross sign biases caused merely by higher baseline rewards elsewhere in batch sampling.
  
All together these ingredients ensure that once taken many mini‑batch samples per iteration—which is necessary for efficient hardware utilisation—the agent learns predominantly from changes it can trust i.e., those instances where current policies have slow drift relative to producing data itself.

Historically researchers have further stabilised PPO through additional KL regularisers or adaptive penalties controlling average likelihood divergence between successive policies—a reflection that ensuring “proximity” goes beyond per‐token clipping alone when scaling uncertainties grow drastically against huge parameter counts common today in LMs exceeding tens of billions of parameters.

---

## Limitations caused by needing value models

A particularly source-sink bottleneck arises because every step requires an auxiliary estimate of expected future reward—$\hat A_t$. Typically one trains *value networks* separately alongside main policy networks so they share feature extraction but stay lightweight relative to language decoder modules thanks presumably fewer head layers etc., yet even these add considerable GPU consumption during backpropagation and repeated fine-tuning iterations at scale:

- Memory overhead: The value model occupies memory proportional either similar size or slightly less than main model—huge‐scale LMs already saturate GPU DRAM bracket.
- Computational overhead: Backpropagation must traverse two distinct computation graphs back into lower representation layers (for both output logits and values) resulting in roughly two× wall-clock time if not expertly parallelised.
- Stability concerns: Value estimates typically lag behind rapidly changing policies leading them down problematic valleys in optimization which manifest as high variance gradients—most particularly pronounced in tasks demanding reasoning over long sequences where few-step value predictions are far weaker.
  
These disadvantages may invalidate crucial postulated assumptions behind PPO's algorithmic design such as *on‑policy estimates faithfully capturing true advantage.* Modern RL fine-tuning often deploys alternative strategies like GAE—or circumvent entirely via relative advantage estimators contingent only upon sparse group rewards—as found best performing prior RL algorithms like GRPO but suffer their own conceptual vulnerabilities traced back^2^18^—a thread expertly uncovered by advancing group plus sequence level corrections (GSPO).

In summary raising efficiency demands has made clinicians increasingly questioning whether maintaining robust auxiliary supervisory signals via separate value networks truly serves incremental progress—or merely burdens training loops—especially for modern generative architectures where computational budgets increasingly outweigh potential benefits thereafter.

**Group Relative Policy Optimisation (GRP)**  

Reinforcement learning has emerged as a vital tool for pushing language models towards higher‑level reasoning capabilities.\footnote{See \citet{o1,dpsk-r1,qwq32b,qwen3} for early applications of RL to large language models.}  
A major obstacle encountered during large‑scale RL is maintaining stable updates when optimisation moves far away from data collected under an older policy.\footnote{\citet{grpo} emphasises instability arising after relatively few training iterations when using standard Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO).}\markboth{}{Ch.~\ref{sec:intro}}\par  

In classical PPO one keeps a running estimate of state‑value functions in order to reduce variance when estimating advantages.\footnote{Eq.~(1.) defines $\mathcal J_{\text{PPO}}$ below and highlights dependence upon $\widehat A_t$, estimated typically via an auxiliary network.\citep{ppo}}\par  

To eliminate this auxiliary network while retaining a tunable scale factor,\citet{grpo} proposed **Group Relative Policy Optimisation**.\footnote{\citet{grpo} exhibited significant empirical reductions in wall‑clock time compared with vanilla PPO at little loss in performance.\citep[cf.~Sec.~2]{grpo}}\par  

Below we describe this algorithm rigorously and illuminate why it constitutes a valid principled adaptation of importance sampling concepts specifically suited for language‑generation tasks.



----------------------------------------------------------------------
### Generating \(G\) Replies per Query  

Let $x$ denote a user query sampled i.i.d.\ from $\mathcal D$ during reinforcement learning.\footnote{\citet{o1}}  
Training proceeds lazily over batches drawn from $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}(\cdot\,|\,x)$, where $\theta_{\rm old}$ are frozen parameters taken prior to the current gradient iteration.\footnote{\citet[Ch.~4]{grpo}}  
For every query $x$, the learner generates **\(G\)** candidate responses $\bigl\{ y_1,\dots ,y_G\bigr\}$ independently according}\footnote{\citet[§4]{grpo}}  
to the categorical distribution produced by $\pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}$, resulting in  
$$
   y_i=\bigl(y_{i,1},\,y_{i,2},\,\dots ,\,y_{i,\lvert y_i\rvert}\bigr)
   \;\sim\;
   \pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\!\bigl(\cdot\,|\,x\bigr), 
   \qquad i=1,\dots ,G .
$$ 
The set $\bigl\{\lvert y_1\rvert,\dots ,\lvert y_G\rvert\bigr\}$ is usually heterogeneous since different samples discover different termination points according to their own predicted termination token distribution.



----------------------------------------------------------------------
### Token–Density Weight \(w_{\,i,t}(\!\theta)\)

Each reply $y_i$ consists of tokens indexed sequentially $t=1,\dots ,\lvert y_i\rvert$.  
For every position $t$ Gregsa defines an **importance‑ratio** comparing how much more probable it has become under the *current* parameter vector $\theta$ versus under its *old* counterpart:
$$
   w_{\,i,t}\!(\theta)
   =
   \frac{
      \pi_{\theta}\!\bigl(
          y_{i,t}\mid x , y_{i,<t}
      \bigr)
   }{
      \pi_{\theta_{\rm old}}\!\bigl(
          y_{i,t}\mid x , y_{i,<t}
      \bigr)
   } .
$$
This quantity reflects how sharply two policies disagree when selecting **that** particular next symbol whilst conditioned on identical past contexts $y_{i,<t}$.\footnote{\citet[Eq.~2]{grpo}}  
Because sampling happens once per exact subsequent symbol its denominator equals one independent draw – therefore $w_{\,i,t}$ does **not** benefit from averaging over many samples, which breaks the foundations established by classic Importance Sampling theory.



----------------------------------------------------------------------
### Advantage Estimate \(\widehat A_i\)

Stability demands normalising raw rewards before feeding them into gradient updates.\footnote{
“In evaluating several alternatives belonging to parallel groups one ought naturally remove scale effects.” 
– c’mon – pseudonym rule.” }\citep[Sec.~4]{grpo}\\
For every key response $y_i$, its scalar reward is reported:\,
$$
   r(x,y_i)\;\in~[0,\,+1]
$$ 
by a verifier network independent of $\theta$.  
Because absolute rewards fluctuate dramatically across dissimilar queries ($r$ may differ widely for unrelated question families), rewards are reshaped *inside* generations:
$$
   \widehat A_i
   :=
     \frac{
         r(x,y_i)- 
         {\color{gray}{ {\rm mean}\!\bigl(
              r(x,y_1),\,r(x,y_2),\,\dots ,\,r(x,y_G)
         )}}
     }{
         {\color{gray}{ {\rm std}\!\bigl(
                r(x,y_1),\,r(x,y_2),\,…\,r(x,y_G)
          )}}
     } .
$$
Every token belonging interchangeably sized‐demand retains the same salience:
$$
   \widehat A _{{ i },t}
   =
   {\color{red}{\widehat A _ i}}
,\qquad t=1,\dots ,{\lvert y _ i\rvert}.
$$
