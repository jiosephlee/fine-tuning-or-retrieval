### "Group based advantage" understood
Question:
(Both Grpo and Gspo compute
$$A_i=rac{r(x,y_i)-{m mean}_j r(x,y_j)}{{m std}_j r(x,y_j)},$$
and≡ similarly grant every token in ${y}_i$ identical coefficient.)
How does normalising over several rivals minimise estimation noise
compared “naïve” ticketing where every action receives its own raw
reward?
Can you explain intuitively why subtracting mean then dividing std helps synchronous sample reuse?
Answer:
**Why GSPO/GRPO normalise over a “group’’ instead of attaching every token its raw reward**

In both algorithms a *query* $x$ is answered $G$ times ($y_1,\dots ,y_G$) by an old policy $\pi_{\theta_\mathrm{old}}$.  
The total reward $r(x,y_i)\in[0,\,1]$ measures how well the whole response satisfies the verifier.
The algorithms do **not** use this single scalar directly as an advantage for all tokens:
they instead compute  

$$
A_i=\frac{\,r(x,y_i)-\operatorname{mean}_j\,r(x,y_j)}
           {\operatorname{std}_j\,r(x,y_j)},
$$
and give every token in $y_i$ exactly this value $A_i$
$$
\nabla_\theta\,;
~~
\nabla_\theta
   \log
   \pi_\theta(y_t|x,\ldots).
$$

The quantity inside the brackets is nothing more than a *z–score* –
the centre‑shifting “mean subtraction’’ followed by “range‑shifting’’ division by the standard deviation.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### What happens if we skip this normalisation?

If every token were rewarded with its own (raw) return $r(x,y_i)$:

* **Different queries give different scales** – some questions have easily provable answers that always get close to one;
others yield very low scores near zero.
Without centring those values share no common reference,
so the gradients coming from a single hard problem can dominate,
while the rest are almost ignored.
  
* **The numerator does not cancel systematic offsets** –
the whole sequence may receive a slightly higher or lower score
because of trivial surface features (token bundle,
random bias,) independent of behaviours actually worth changing.
Un–centred signals therefore point repeatedly toward inappropriate directions.
  
* **Large magnitude differences amplify clipping artefacts** –
PPO‑style clipping operates on $w\,A$.  
When $A\simeq r$ varies wildly among batches,
the product $w\,A$
oscillates between huge values that are clipped frequently.
This produces many ineffective gradients—and even worse,
when paired with noisy token‑level importance ratios,
can drive catastrophic drift, exactly what is seen when GRPO collapses.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### How does “mean – std’’ reduce estimation noise?

1. **Relative comparison inside one snapshot**

   Subtracting $\operatorname{mean}_jr(x,y_j)$
   turns absolute performance into *difference against peers*.
   All five responses issued for a specific prompt are judged against
   one another,
   therefore any global bias particular to that prompt disappears.
   It is exactly what self‑critical training uses—“if I look better than my peers I should favour it”.
   Hence signals are centred around zero and carry only information about *which*
   current behavioural change confers an improvement relative‑to‑the‑group’.

2. **Scale invariance inside the snapshot**

   Dividing by the estimated standard deviation 
   $\operatorname{std}_jr(x,y_j)$ rescales those centred scores so their spread does not depend on how tight
   or wide class‐specific reward distributions happen to be.
   Consequently every grouped set contributes roughly comparable
   signal strength irrespective of question difficulty,
   response length or overall verifier tightness.
   In maths,

   $$
      \mathbb E[A_i^2]\approx 1 ,
   $$

   making Adam’s adaptive learning rate behave predictably and allowing us consistently tiny clipping margins ($ε\simeq10^{-4}$)
even when models grow extremely large—something impossible if raw scores were used.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Why does this help *synchronously* re‑use samples?

During large–scale LM replay you typically split one huge rollout batch into many mini‑batches so you can train continuously without holding all data simultaneously in memory.
If advantages were computed naively per token you would need
to recompute/re‑evaluate them every time another mini‑batch comes up –
each recomputation could give subtly different numerical results at lower precision settings 
(training engine = Megatron vs inference engine = SGLang).

By contrast

* The grouped z‑score depends only on 
  $\displaystyle \!\bigl\{\,r(x,y_1),…,r(x,y_G)\bigr\}$;
once it has been computed it stays fixed regardless how many mini‑batches share those samples.
* The subsequent clipping condition also depends only on sequence likelihood ratios,
which change negligibly under modest parameter perturbations;
hence successive gradient passes continue receiving 
the same effective weights without extra recomputation headaches.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Summary

Normalising over rivals removes two major sources of estimation noise:
a global offset specific to each prompt and an arbitrary scale tied
to task difficulty;
both jeopardise PPO’s stability when coupled with noisy token–wise importance ratios.
By applying centring plus rescaling inside every small local batch,
GRPO/GSPO produce bounded Z–scores whose magnitude stays near unity,
allow uniform clipping windows,
and make greedy samples reusable without changing their learned influence—
exactly what lets GSPO train millions–parameter MoE models stably while naïve ticketing would explode.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

This picture fits neatly into Section 4’s motivation:
importance ratios work correctly only at sequence level;
paired with ‑zscored intra‑group advantages gives us clean expectations,
therefore smooth downstream learning gradients even when sampling cheaply from an older policy—a crucial ingredient behind GSPO’s superior stability shown experimentally in Figures 2–4.*
