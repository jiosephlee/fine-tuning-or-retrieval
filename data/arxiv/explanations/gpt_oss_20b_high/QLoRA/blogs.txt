# Fine‑Tuning Huge Language Models on a Single GPU — That’s What **QLoRA** Does  

Large language models have become pinnacles of state–of–the–art language understanding and generation—but so far getting **anyone besides mega‑cloud playbooks** off the ground has been practically impossible.  
A single **LLaMA‑65 billion** parameter network would demand more than **780 GB** of VRAM during every forward–backward step if you were going to finite‐look up its greedily updated weights naively in BF16 form, forcing people who run IA research on rented clusters longer than a week onto toy mini‑models or gut hacks that hurt accuracy.



```mermaid

    B --> C[Full Precision]
```

In short *QLoRA throws away needless flexibility:* it freezes an aggressively *four‑bit concentrated* copy of your frozen huge network **and learns tiny rank adapters** instead of updating millions of parameters.

## Core ideas behind **QLoRA**

### Freeze all *uncritical* parameters
Your pre‐trained transformer has an enormous amount of redundancy—the expressive power sits mostly near its hidden states rather than its gigantic bottleneck matrices. By merely keeping every pre‑trained weight intact you eliminate *almost every source of stale gradients*, leaving virtually nothing you need wireingly propagate back.



### Learn little Dutch men’s hats over big Netherlands schematic buildings
Adding Low‑Rank Adapters (*LoRAs*) means augmenting any WRIGHT linear map  
\(f(x)=xW+\underbrace{s\,xL_{1}L_{2}}_{\small{\textbf{adapter}}}\).  
Those extra tensors (`L₁`, `L₂`) have tiny rank \(r\), usually \(64–128\), tiny enough that storing their activations alone costs < 30 MiB even when you scale all transformer layers up.



### Compress everything “in software” before spending cycles running it  
#### NormalFloat™ (NFᵏ)

Standard four–bit integer/bfloat has bad asymmetry around zero – you lose your most “common” values entirely instead shaping bins unevenly around rare extremes (outliers).  
Weights produced by popular initializers are statistically close to \(N(0,\sigma)\); NFᵏ gives us an **information theoretically optimal binned representation**, perfectly preserving mass around zero so padding stays clean while extreme angels stay nicely represented too.



```math
q_i=\frac12\!\left(
   Q_{N(0,\sigma)}({i}/{\,2^k+\,})+
   Q_{N(0,\sigma)}({i+1}/{\,2^k+\,})
 \right)
```

For \(k=4\) there are eight balanced equiprobable codes AND an exact zero slot—exactly what you need for convoluted transformer activations.



#### Double Quantization

Every four–bit block has its own scaling factor \(c_i\). Those were stored originally in FP32 → roughly **½ bit ≈¼ b‐per‐parameter overhead**!  
Below looks mysterious because it tackles *the constants themselves*. First you pack those scales into an extra layer itself using FP8 over blocks (=256 weight indices), then you compress those FP8 scalars once more into another vector via standard float symmetry over chunks (=64 × 256 slices). What remains is ≈ 0·127 unstored bits/parameter → about **3 GiB saved on the whole ‑65 billion network**.



```math
m_{\!F}=32/{64}=0½\;\text{bits/par};\\
m_{\!DQ}=8/{64}+32/(64⋅256)=0•127;\;\Delta≈0•373 .
```



#### Paged Optimizer States

Gradient checkpointing forces temporary allocation peaks hundreds‐of–megabytes higher than sensible offsets when dealing with fat hidden feedforward nets and long sequences (>512 tokens). CUDA Unify’d Memory silently swaps optimizer moments onto CPU RAM *only when needed*, making spikes invisible without incurring extra bandwidth bugs.

## Putting It All Together

```

for epoch....
    forward ← x @ deq(w,c_,c_dq)_⦁<!π> nice                 // BF16 matmul
```

# NormalFloat ( NF ↦   ⁴ᵃ ) : Compressing Gaussian Weight Distributions into Four Bits

In transformer pre‑training every weight tensor almost always looks like a noisy bell‑curve – normally distributed around zero – once you get past the layers’ learned biases and embeddings.  
If you simply throw these numbers through a naïve “map onto an 8‑bit integer grid”, you distort precisely the most useful values at the tails while wasting bits on outliers that rarely occur in practice.

**NormalFloat (NF)** rewrites that assumption:
* first squeeze every tensor block onto the tight interval \([-1,+1]\)
* then split this interval up so that exactly **equal numbers** of entries fall into every possible four‑bit pattern

Because this assignment line‑ups perfectly with a Gaussian density, NF breeds *information‑theoretically optimal* coding – it keeps essentially all useful variation while giving us an emergent eight‑fold reduction in memory cost compared with naïve FP16 or even fp32 formats.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## The problem with naïve low‑bit integer quantization

The usual approach takes a weight tensor \(\mathbf{W}\), splits it into small contiguous blocks, finds each block’s maximum magnitude \(m = \operatorname{absmax}(\mathbf{W})\) and rescales

\[
    \mathbf{W}^{\text{Int8}} 
      = \operatorname{round}\!\Bigl(\frac{127}{m}\,\mathbf{W}\Bigr)\qquad
    \bigl(127=-128,\dots,+127\bigr).
\]

During inference you restore binary values again by multiplying back with \(m\).

When \(\mathbf{W}\) follows a standard normal law \(N(0,\sigma^2)\), this linear mapping places most samples very close together near zero while few samples dominate the extremes – many bins stay empty and others become crowded — which defeats one’s hope of efficient compression.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## NormalFloat meets a Gaussian

Because LLM weights are approximately \(N(0,\sigma^2)\), we can exploit two facts:

| What we know | What we can do |
|--------------|----------------|
| Each **block** follows \(N(0,\sigma^2)\)        | Re‑scale *every* block so its variance matches the **fixed** “normal float” range \([-1,+1]\). |
| Quantiles are analytically available          | Compute equal‑probability bins directly from the standard‐normal cumulative function \(Q\). |

For a \(k\)-bit data type (\(k=4\) in our case) we first compute

\[
q_i   
   = \frac12
     \!\left[
        Q\!\Bigl(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\Bigr)
       +Q\!\Bigl(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\Bigr)
     \right],
   \quad i=0,\dots ,2^k-1,
\]

which gives us exactly one “midpoint’’ per bin such that each bin captures an equal share (\(\tfrac{1}{2^k}\)) of the normal distribution mass.  
`NFk` thus becomes an asymmetric lookup table that also contains **exact zero**, avoiding an error when representing blank embeddings.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## How NF⁴ fits inside QLoRA training

QLoRA keeps two separate notions of precision:

* **Storage** – each weight lives as four bits (`NF4`).
* **Computation** – at runtime we **dequantise** these bits back into BF16 before any matrix multiplication occurs.

The dequantisation routine works like a tiny two‑stage rocket engine until it reaches full precision:

```
HF→BF16 dequants:
   ↓              
HF→BF16 → QF -> BF16
```

More formally,
\[
   \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}
     =\operatorname{deq}
        \bigl(
          \operatorname{deq}(c_1^{\!\textbf{FP32}},\,c_2^{\,k\text{-bit}})
          ,\,\mathbf{W}^{\,k\text{-bit}}
        \bigr),
\]
where \(c_2^{\,k}\) are their own per‑block scales (**Double Quantisation**) and \(c_1^{\!\textbf{FP32}}\) lifts us back up to full float once during backward propagation – but only for *adapter* gradients, never for base weights.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why it matters – empirical evidence

The paper presents extensive experiments comparing several four‑bit schemes:

| Scheme            | Best historical TF / Perplexity | Relative gap vs FP32 |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|----------------------|
| Int / UQ            | ↑×Variation      | –10 %                |
| FP / UQ            | ↑×Variation      | –6 %                 |
| **NF ⁴**             | ≈FP32 performance | < ± 0.5 %              |

The upshot is that **NF⁴ sustained nearly identical inference quality while shrinking every `65B` model’s footprint from roughly *half a terabyte* down to under *50 GB* during training**—a real eight‑fold reduction without hurting downstream instruction‐following benchmarks (MMLU) or chat quality (Vicuna/Troika).

In short:
```
Full FP16 ╱ QLoRA │ → SF float       │ ↓ 48 GB ⬇︎
                │ → Rescale → Dequant│
NormalFloat  │ → Stored ↦ 4-bits
                │ + LoRA adapters   │
```


--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Take‐home points

* **Gaussianity ≈ entropy optimum** – By aligning bins with theoretical probability mass you keep essentially all meaningful signal.
* **Zero stays exact** – Architectural tweak guarantees non‑squared errors around padding or sparse padding indices.
* **Only a mild “double reshape” overhead** – The tiny rounding step used by *Double Quantisation* adds ~0.13 bits per parameter versus ~0½ bits otherwise—negligible compared with the overall compression benefit.
* **Training stays cheap** – Since only LoRA adapters learn gradients, you never update massive four‐bit tensors; all expensive SIMD work stays inside BF16 arithmetic where GPU cores shine.
* **Replicates F/F‐full finetune accuracy across scales up‑to `65B` models**, proving robustness beyond toy mini‑widgets exposed earlier in the literature.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Bottom line

If you’ve ever wrestled with why *inference*-only low‑bit tricks broke under training pressure, NormalFloat gives you an analytic route around brittleness while still mounting lean enough hardware budgets onto giant models—as demonstrated by its starring role behind open‐source chatbots that lock near ChatGPT performance (Guanaco series).

So next time you see those long tables filled with “Naïve Int8” rows, remember there’s one more row worth of research where “info‑theoretic equal-bin” stories meet powerful LLMs—four bits screaming almost full fidelity may just be your secret key for frontier scaling without blowing up consumable GPUs.

# Double Quantisation & Paged Optimisers – Shrinking Every Bit Without Sacrificing Speed  

When fine‑tuning a 65 B‑parameter language model you quickly hit an unseen bottleneck: **the per‑block scaling constants** that accompany every low‑precision weight tensor. Each constant is stored as a full‑precision float (FP32) even though the weight itself is only 4 bits wide, leaking roughly half a bit of unused space per parameter across billions of weights. QLoRA cuts this waste by half‑again—literally—and plugs another common memory hiccup triggered by gradient checkpointing using “paged optimisers.”  

Below we unpack how these two ideas fit together inside the QLoRA ecosystem and why they make training the largest open‑source models on a single GPU possible today.

---

## The Constant Overhead Problem  

Low‑bit quantisation splits *a weight tensor* into blocks of size \(B\).  
For each block it stores a **scale factor** \(c_i\) that tells us how many real‑values map into the low‐bit representation:

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{int}}_{i} = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{c_i^{\text{FP32}}}{2^{k}}\;\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{real}}_{i}\right),
\]

where \(k=4\) for NF4 (NormalFloat).  
In practice \(c_i^{\text{FP32}}\) is a full FP32 number (~4 bytes).  

With blocksize \(B=64\) this means:

* **Every 64 weights** carry an extra 4 bytes → **\(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per weight**  
* For a 65 B model this is ≈ 3 GB of scale overhead—well over the extra space you would otherwise want for model parameters or optimizer state!  

Why does this matter?  
Because base models already occupy > 5 GB in NF4, any added constant overhead kills headroom for adapters or gradient checkpoints—the bottleneck right before you hit GPU fragmentation or out‑of‑memory errors during training.

---

## Double Quantisation – Compressing the Constants  

Double Quantisation (DQ) tackles the problem head on: *re‑quantise the scale factors themselves.*

1. **First level** – original weights \(\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{norm}}\) → NF4 \(\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{NF4}}\) using blockwise scale constants \(c_2^{\text{FP32}}\).  
2. **Second level** – compress each \(c_2^{\text{FP32}}\) into an **8‑bit float** (\(c_2^{\text{FP8}}\)) using its own blocksize (e.g., 256).  
3 .The de‑quantised weight used during forward passes therefore becomes:

   \[
   \mathbf{W}^{\,\text{BF16}}
   = \operatorname{dequant}
     \bigl(\operatorname{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{FP8}}, 
            \mathbf{W}^{\,\text{NF4}}\bigr).
   \]

Because all *scale constants* now sit in FP8 instead of FP32, their average contribution per parameter shrinks drastically:

| From | To | Bits per value | Bits per weight |
|------|----|---------------|----------------|
| FP32 constant \(c_1(\!B=64)\) | FP8 constant \(c_2(\!B=256)\) | 8 | ≈ 0.13 |
| Combined | – | – | ≈ 0.13 |

In concrete terms:

* **Old**: ~\(0.5\) bits/per‑weight constant overhead  
* **After DQ**: ~\(0.127\) bits/per‑weight constant overhead   

So you save about **\(0.373\)" bits per weight**, which translates into ~3 GB saved for a 65 B model—exactly what gives us more room for LoRA adapters without hurting compute performance or accuracy (the paper reports identical perplexity / zero‑shot metrics after DQ).

---

## Paged Optimisers – Keep Your GPU In Tune  

Multi‑head…long sequence length? Gradient checkpointing starts cloning activations in off‑chip memory, producing surprising spikes in VRAM usage right before the optimiser step kicks in.



### What Happens Normally?  

* Forward + backward pass → activation gradients stored on device
* Optimiser updates (AdamW, LAMB…) act on parameter gradients & moving averages
* At certain minibatch sizes / sequence lengths those activations explode → your GPU’s VRAM runs out → OOM errors stuck at half way through an epoch

### What “paged optimisers” do instead  

QLoRA hooks into NVIDIA’s Unified Memory subsystem:

1. Allocate optimiser state tensors—e.g., Adam moment estimates—as **paged memory objects**.
2. When runtime detects insufficient VRAM during backpropagation or optimiser update,
   * items are automatically moved (“paged”) out to system RAM.
3. When they’re needed again (next optimiser step), they’re streamed back transparently.
  
The key benefit is that **OOM spikes are avoided without having to sacrifice batch size**, and the swap traffic is usually small compared with main parameter data.



### Practical Effect  

In experiments with a 65 B LLaMA trained via QLoRA:

* Batch size 16 on a single 48 GB GPU uses roughly equal time whether normal optimistic storage or paged version is enabled—nothing slows down overall throughput.
* The technique makes it possible to run longer sequence lengths than otherwise allowed by VRAM alone, which improves token utilisation during instruction fine‑tuning.
* No extra hyperparameter tuning required; it’s simply “on” once you enable `paged_optimizer=True` in QLoRA’s configuration.



---

## Takeaways for Practitioners

| Feature | What It Saves | Why It Matters |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| **Double Quantisation** | ~3 GB fewer clock cycles for scale constants | Lets you drop mass into LoRA adapters rather than paying an almost linear cost per additional adapter |
| **Paged Optimisers** | Automatic offloading of optimiser internals during spikes | Removes unintended OOM failures when experimenting with longer prompts or larger minibatches |
| Combined Impact | Low-memory fine‑tune up to 65 B parameters on one consumer GPU (~24 GB RTX 4090 / RTX 6000 Ti) while preserving full float‑16 accuracy | Opens large‐scale instruction tuning for teams lacking multi‑GPU clusters |

If you’re already saving on weights via NF4 or other hiddinorm-based schemes, adding these two layers makes your stack lean enough that both hardware savings and compute speeds come together gracefully.



---

## Final Thoughts

Quantising both *weights* and their *scales*, then letting hardware do its own paging when needed, gives us an almost “free” layer of compression without touching algorithmic quality:

* Recovered *full float‑16 performance* across benchmarks (GLUE, MMLU, Vicuna).
* Tiny computational penalty—modern GPUs still do multiply–accumulate operations in BF16.
* No extra code bugs: DQ is just another round of `torch.quantize` under-the-hood; paged optimisers are enabled through HuggingFace’s transformers `PagedOptimizer`.

With QLoRA you can now train state-of-the-art chatbots like Guanaco on standalone GPUs—an achievement once restricted to large TPU pods or multi-node GPU clusters.

> **Short story:** A handful of researchers leveraged *QLoRA*, an accelerator that turns a 65 billion‑parameter language model into a trainable “black box” right on a single petabyte‑scale commodity GPU.  
> They paired it with handpicked instruction datasets such as *OASST1*, producing the **Guanaco** family of chatbots that hit **≈ 99 %** of ChatGPT’s score on the Vicuna benchmark—under twelve hours of training—and beat every other open‑source competitor by more than twenty percentage points even at the tiny “7 billion” level.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why this matters  
Large language models (LLMs) have become remarkably capable once you just give them a few lines of *instruction-following* data—but bringing those gains into practice is still one giant wall older tech stacks keep standing up against hardware constraints.



| Legacy approach | Memory needed | Running cost |
|-----------------|---------------|--------------|
| Full‑precision finetune (LLaMA 65B) | **>780 GB** per GPU | Multi-GPU clusters + days |
| Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA only) | **≈40–50 GB** | Still needs ≥ 6 GPUs |
| **QLoRA** | **<48 GB** | One modern GPU can finish all work |

That last column says what drives *Guanaco*: no resorting to cloud clusters and no “state-of-the-art” bottleneck cost.


---------------------------------------------------------------------

## The recipe behind QLoRA  
At its core **QLoRA** blends two ideas:

1. **Low-Rank Adapters (`LoRA`)** – kept almost static so backpropagation never touches billions of parameters.
2. **Ultra-low precision (> 4‑bit) weights + smarter dequantisation** – split into three key tricks:

   * **NormalFloat (`NF4`)** – an information-theoretically optimal way to pack normally distributed weights into forty‐second gutsy less-than-four bits.
   * **Double Quantisation** – compresses *the constants used to reinscribe those constants*, pulling roughly 3 GB out of every sparse store.
   * **Paged Optimiser** – an NVIDIA sanity mechanism that lets optimiser states dance between RAM and VRAM without hiccups during long sequence checkpoints.

A quick illustration (ℓ¹ layer):

```
Y = X · deq(deq(c1 , c2 , W_NF4))          # Δ backprop keeps W frozen
    + X · L₁ · L₂                          # LoRA trainable low-rank trainheads
```

Only `L₁`/`L₂` get updated while all weight gradients happily flow through a reconstructed *BF16* copy.

# From Benchmarks To Elo Games – How We Really Rank Conversational Agents  

## Why Classic Metrics Get Confusing  
Paper‑benchmarks such as **MMLU**, **GLUE**, or even a raw crowd‑source *WinRate* tell us how many questions a model gets right in isolation.  
When dozens of recent language models arrive at very similar accuracy levels—e.g., several systems all achieve **\(97\,\%\)** on MMLU—the raw percentage becomes little more than noise that hides real differences in conversational behavior or safety knowledge.  
Moreover those measures assume a fixed task format while real dialogue scenarios change dramatically from one turn to another, making one-size‑fits-all scoring uncertain.

## Head‑to‑Head Tournaments  
To read “who really wins” researchers turned every prompt into a mini‑match between *two agents*.  
For a given question \(q\):

| Prompt | Agent A’s response | Agent B’s response |
|--------|-------------------|--------------------|
| “What is…?” | … | … |

Each operator could be any LLM—including Anthropic’s *AskSLTM*, Meta’s *Alpaca*, BigScience’s *Vicuna*, OpenAI’s *ChatGPT*, or any **QLoRA‑trained Guanaco variant**—and every match produced **a winner**, **a loser**, or sometimes a **draw** if neither answer clearly surpassed the other according to judgement criteria (see below).

Because every respondent sees exactly the same prompt sequence, these head‑to‑head games capture true interpersonal competitiveness rather than abstract accuracies spread across independent tasks.



### Creating Clean Matches  

The authors assembled two curated testbed collections:


| Dataset | #prompts | Nature |
|---------|----------|--------|
| **Vicuna‐Prompts** | <br>≈80 | Hand‑crafted questions covering multiple topics |
| **OASST1 Validation** | ≈953 turns | Real multi­turn dialogues collected via crowd‐source |

Every dialogue turn was paired against every other candidate response generated by different agents.



## Choosing the Judge  
Two sources were used simultaneously:


| Judge | Rationale |
|-------|-----------|
| **Human annotators** | Ground truth “best answer” perception backed by two assessors per prompt |
| **Automated GP​T‑4 scorer** | Cheap (>5× faster), yet surprisingly well aligned with humans (Kendall τ ≈ 0․55 when judged by system‐level comparison), providing scales comparable across rounds |

Because any scripted ranker is vulnerable to positional bias (“the first answer gets extra credit”), they asked JPT‑4 twice per prompt—the sequence was inverted the second time—and took the **mean score over both orders**, neutralising order effects.



### Rating Rules  
After all contexts were judged three possibilities emerged:


```
Winner → score = 1   (full point)
Draw    → score = ½   (half point)
Loser   → score = 0   (zero point)
```

These discrete outcomes fed directly into an **Elo–style tournament simulator** explained below.



## Turning Wins Into Rankings With Elo  

Chess experts use an algorithm called ELO to compare players based purely on match outcomes without assuming any central “ground truth”.  
It keeps an integer “rating” per participant while represents their skill level comparably under identical conditions.



### Algorithm Overview  

For each agent \(A\) start with rating \(R_A = 1000\).  
When it plays another agent \(B\):

1. Compute *expected success* against current ratings  

   \[
   E_A \;\gets\;\frac{1}{\,1+10^{(R_B-R_A)/400}\,},
   \]

   (*we’re implicitly using base 10 here—the exact base is irrelevant since we never change both sides.*

2. Observe its real outcome i.e., \(S_A∈\{0,\tfrac12 ,1\}\).

3.Update its rating  

   \[
   R_A \;\gets\;\;R_A + K\,(S_A-E_A),
   \]

   where \(K = 32\) gave good stability for all models.



The process was run five thousand times *with random pairing orders*, ensuring systematic biases such as which agent appeared first could never sway the overall outcome.



### Reading an Output Score  

Suppose *Agent X* ends up at 1400 while *Agent Y* sits at 1100 English refential baseball scores correspond roughly:


```
Probability X beats Y ≈ 68 %     (ΔR = 300 → P ≈ 68 %)
```

The smaller ΔR becomes, weaker confidence is attached—just like closeness observed among recent LLMs.

# Why This Matters ‑ Democratising AI Through Low‑Memory Fine‑Tuning  

Large language models (LLMs) are now routinely hit‑rate limited by *memory* rather than compute at all.  
A handful of companies have amassed clusters of multi‑TB GPUs in order simply **to run a single forward pass** when finetuning a 65 B parameter model – something that requires more than 780 GB of VRAM with native 16‑bit weights. For most researchers and small‑to‑mid sized companies this is an insurmountable barrier both financially *and* logistically because training data often includes sensitive user interactions that cannot leave local premises or even cloud environments connected to private networks.

Enter **QLoRA** (Quantised Low‑Rank Adapter tuning).  
It allows an entire *four‑bit*, fully calibrated portion of a gigantic pretrained network to be trained with almost no change in predictive accuracy compared with ordinary full‑precision finetuning – all while keeping the GPU memory budget below a single modern consumer bar (≈48 GB). In practice this means:

* A proprietary 65 B parameter model can be customised overnight on a single NVIDIA RTX 6000.
* A modestly sized proprietary aggregator can exact near‑ChatGPT quality outputs without ever paying multi‑million dollar cloud fees.
* Mobile phones can host and slightly refine their own conversation systems – an efficiency lift that moves us closer toward true *on-device* personalization.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## How QLoRA Wins the Memory Battle  

### 1️⃣ Downsampling “normally distributed” weights

Pretrained transformers nearly always store their weight tensors as zero‑centered Gaussian-distributed numbers (\(N(0,\sigma)\)).  
Traditional *blockwise* four‑bit integer (Int 4) or float（FP 4) schemes waste bins around outliers because they rely on coarse per‐block maximums:

```
X⁽Int⁴⁾ = round(127 / absmax(X) · X)
```

QLoRA replaces this with **NormalFloat (‘NF’)** – an information-theoretically optimal four‑bit format designed specifically for normally distributed inputs:

1. Estimate the \(2^k+1\) quantiles of \(N(0,1)\)
2. Recenter them so that zero has an exact representation  
3. Scale every weight tensor so its standard deviation matches that quantile set

The result is termed *Norally Float k* (`NFk`). Empirically `NF4` consistently beats both `Int4` and `FP4` across hundreds of model sizes – even outperforming their eight‑bit counterparts once adapted via LoRAs.



### 2️⃣ “Double” Quantisation cuts constant overhead  

Below each block’s weights lie two constant factors (`c₁`, `c₂`).  
With a block size of 64 per weight tensor those constants themselves occupy half a bit per parameter – disastrous when you multiply by tens of billions!

**Double Quantisation** compresses these constants again:

```
c₂⁽FP8⁾ ← quantise(c₂⁽FP32⁾)
```

(`c₁` remains FP32 because its magnitude distribution is far less problematic).  
On average this shrinks per‑parameter constant storage from 0·5 bits down to 0·127 bits – about **3 GB saved** on a 65 B model.



### 3️⃣ Paged Optimiser states keep gradient checkpointing sane  

When processing huge sequences you still rely on gradient checkpointing so you don't hold all activations simultaneously – however checkpointing birthing spikes often push you over your VRAM ceiling when your hidden states float across multi-chunk tensors you generated from `NF4`.

QLoRA sidesteps this by allocating optimizer states in **NVIDIA Unified Memory** (“paged” memory). Whenever hotspot spikes hit VRAM limits those parts are lazily swapped out onto host RAM and swapped back in only when required by iterative updates.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Low-Rank Adapters Let You Reach Back into Full Precision

Finetuning is cheap if **you only alter tiny subsets** of parameters while freezing everything else – known as *parameter efficient finetuning*.  
QLoRA uses **Low-Rank Adapters (LoRAs)** which are added after every transformer linear projection:

```
Y = X W + s · X L₁ L₂ ,   where W ≈ fixed ✔️
```

During backpropagation gradients flow through the frozen `W` into the newly introduced adapter matrices (`L₁`, `L₂`).  

Unlike earlier attempts where adapters were only fitted at key locations (e.g., queries & keys), QLoRA’s experimental sweep shows:

* **Adapters everywhere** (every linear layer!) are required to recover full‐precision behaviour at very large scales.
* The adapter parameter budget stays tiny (< 5 % of total network parameters), so even at **33 B/65 B scales** you never inflate your VRAM usage past what your main `NF4` weights consume.
