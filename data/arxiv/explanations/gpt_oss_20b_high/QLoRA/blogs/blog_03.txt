# Double Quantisation & Paged Optimisers – Shrinking Every Bit Without Sacrificing Speed  

When fine‑tuning a 65 B‑parameter language model you quickly hit an unseen bottleneck: **the per‑block scaling constants** that accompany every low‑precision weight tensor. Each constant is stored as a full‑precision float (FP32) even though the weight itself is only 4 bits wide, leaking roughly half a bit of unused space per parameter across billions of weights. QLoRA cuts this waste by half‑again—literally—and plugs another common memory hiccup triggered by gradient checkpointing using “paged optimisers.”  

Below we unpack how these two ideas fit together inside the QLoRA ecosystem and why they make training the largest open‑source models on a single GPU possible today.

---

## The Constant Overhead Problem  

Low‑bit quantisation splits *a weight tensor* into blocks of size \(B\).  
For each block it stores a **scale factor** \(c_i\) that tells us how many real‑values map into the low‐bit representation:

\[
\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{int}}_{i} = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\frac{c_i^{\text{FP32}}}{2^{k}}\;\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{real}}_{i}\right),
\]

where \(k=4\) for NF4 (NormalFloat).  
In practice \(c_i^{\text{FP32}}\) is a full FP32 number (~4 bytes).  

With blocksize \(B=64\) this means:

* **Every 64 weights** carry an extra 4 bytes → **\(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per weight**  
* For a 65 B model this is ≈ 3 GB of scale overhead—well over the extra space you would otherwise want for model parameters or optimizer state!  

Why does this matter?  
Because base models already occupy > 5 GB in NF4, any added constant overhead kills headroom for adapters or gradient checkpoints—the bottleneck right before you hit GPU fragmentation or out‑of‑memory errors during training.

---

## Double Quantisation – Compressing the Constants  

Double Quantisation (DQ) tackles the problem head on: *re‑quantise the scale factors themselves.*

1. **First level** – original weights \(\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{norm}}\) → NF4 \(\mathbf{W}^{\,\text{NF4}}\) using blockwise scale constants \(c_2^{\text{FP32}}\).  
2. **Second level** – compress each \(c_2^{\text{FP32}}\) into an **8‑bit float** (\(c_2^{\text{FP8}}\)) using its own blocksize (e.g., 256).  
3 .The de‑quantised weight used during forward passes therefore becomes:

   \[
   \mathbf{W}^{\,\text{BF16}}
   = \operatorname{dequant}
     \bigl(\operatorname{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{FP8}}, 
            \mathbf{W}^{\,\text{NF4}}\bigr).
   \]

Because all *scale constants* now sit in FP8 instead of FP32, their average contribution per parameter shrinks drastically:

| From | To | Bits per value | Bits per weight |
|------|----|---------------|----------------|
| FP32 constant \(c_1(\!B=64)\) | FP8 constant \(c_2(\!B=256)\) | 8 | ≈ 0.13 |
| Combined | – | – | ≈ 0.13 |

In concrete terms:

* **Old**: ~\(0.5\) bits/per‑weight constant overhead  
* **After DQ**: ~\(0.127\) bits/per‑weight constant overhead   

So you save about **\(0.373\)" bits per weight**, which translates into ~3 GB saved for a 65 B model—exactly what gives us more room for LoRA adapters without hurting compute performance or accuracy (the paper reports identical perplexity / zero‑shot metrics after DQ).

## Paged Optimisers – Keep Your GPU In Tune  

Multi‑head…long sequence length? Gradient checkpointing starts cloning activations in off‑chip memory, producing surprising spikes in VRAM usage right before the optimiser step kicks in.



### What Happens Normally?  

* Forward + backward pass → activation gradients stored on device
* Optimiser updates (AdamW, LAMB…) act on parameter gradients & moving averages
* At certain minibatch sizes / sequence lengths those activations explode → your GPU’s VRAM runs out → OOM errors stuck at half way through an epoch

### What “paged optimisers” do instead  

QLoRA hooks into NVIDIA’s Unified Memory subsystem:

1. Allocate optimiser state tensors—e.g., Adam moment estimates—as **paged memory objects**.
2. When runtime detects insufficient VRAM during backpropagation or optimiser update,
   * items are automatically moved (“paged”) out to system RAM.
3. When they’re needed again (next optimiser step), they’re streamed back transparently.
  
The key benefit is that **OOM spikes are avoided without having to sacrifice batch size**, and the swap traffic is usually small compared with main parameter data.



### Practical Effect  

In experiments with a 65 B LLaMA trained via QLoRA:

* Batch size 16 on a single 48 GB GPU uses roughly equal time whether normal optimistic storage or paged version is enabled—nothing slows down overall throughput.
* The technique makes it possible to run longer sequence lengths than otherwise allowed by VRAM alone, which improves token utilisation during instruction fine‑tuning.
* No extra hyperparameter tuning required; it’s simply “on” once you enable `paged_optimizer=True` in QLoRA’s configuration.

## Takeaways for Practitioners

| Feature | What It Saves | Why It Matters |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| **Double Quantisation** | ~3 GB fewer clock cycles for scale constants | Lets you drop mass into LoRA adapters rather than paying an almost linear cost per additional adapter |
| **Paged Optimisers** | Automatic offloading of optimiser internals during spikes | Removes unintended OOM failures when experimenting with longer prompts or larger minibatches |
| Combined Impact | Low-memory fine‑tune up to 65 B parameters on one consumer GPU (~24 GB RTX 4090 / RTX 6000 Ti) while preserving full float‑16 accuracy | Opens large‐scale instruction tuning for teams lacking multi‑GPU clusters |

If you’re already saving on weights via NF4 or other hiddinorm-based schemes, adding these two layers makes your stack lean enough that both hardware savings and compute speeds come together gracefully.



---

## Final Thoughts

Quantising both *weights* and their *scales*, then letting hardware do its own paging when needed, gives us an almost “free” layer of compression without touching algorithmic quality:

* Recovered *full float‑16 performance* across benchmarks (GLUE, MMLU, Vicuna).
* Tiny computational penalty—modern GPUs still do multiply–accumulate operations in BF16.
* No extra code bugs: DQ is just another round of `torch.quantize` under-the-hood; paged optimisers are enabled through HuggingFace’s transformers `PagedOptimizer`.

With QLoRA you can now train state-of-the-art chatbots like Guanaco on standalone GPUs—an achievement once restricted to large TPU pods or multi-node GPU clusters.
