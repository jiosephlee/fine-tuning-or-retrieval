# ‘Entropy Patching’: Letting Data Decide Compute Allocation  

Large‑language models today most often rely on *tokenizers* – a pre‑processing stage that turns raw text into indices into a finite vocabulary.  
Tokenizers give every unit the same amount of computation regardless of how hard it is for the model to guess what comes next. In practice that means a lot of wasted effort on predictable whitespaces or common suffixes, while dropping deeper penalizations onto rare characters that carry more information weight.\n\nIn the **Byte Latent Transformer (BLT)** papers – *https://github.com/facebookresearch/blt* – authors reverse that assumption by letting examples themselves decide where computation should occur:**patches**.\nPatches are light–weight collections of *bytes* whose sizes vary dynamically according to how uncertain predicting *the following byte* is.\nThis simple yet powerful trick reorganises workloads so every layer gets tasked only where it matters.\n\nBelow is a walk‑through of how BLT’s **entropy‑based patching** works, why it feels natural, how it compares against fixed strategies, and what gains it yields practically.\n\n---\n\n## Why All Models Treat Every Token Equally?\n\nTraditional tokenizers discretise lengthy byte streams into an arbitrary finite set – usually between \(30K\) and \(128K\) symbols.\nThe resulting token stream has two inherent deficiencies:\n\n* **Uniform cost:** Every token triggers an independence step in the transformer regardless of its difficulty.\n* **Static boundaries:** Fixation on predetermined vocabularies forces all segments – whether common or unusual – through identical encoding pipelines.\n\nThis uniformity neglects language’s intrinsic *entropy structure*: predicting the first letter following a space is substantially harder that predicting subsequent letters inside an already orthographically familiar word.\nIn effect transformers spend many useless attention heads questioning “Y​” over white space rather than focusing attention resources where variance is highest.\n\nBLT rewrites this balancing act through data‑driven segmentation.\n\n---\n\n## From Fixed kb Strides → Static Whitespaces → Dynamic Endorsements\n\n| Strategy | What It Does | Main Drawback | How BLT Improves |\n| -------- | ------------ | ------------ | ------------ |\n| Strided \(k\)-byte padding | Splits any input into equal chunks every \(k\) bytes | Impossible adaptivity – always same number of transformer steps per chunk |\n| While striding offers perfect control over *sequence length*, it couples computational effort tightly to Babel words unseen prior information.\nWhitespace extension fixes one heuristically touched boundary type—but still neglects subtle predictability spikes inside languages such as Chinese or multi‑lingual code blocks.\nBLT’s key doorway lies in what follows:\n\n---\n\n## Measuring Uncertainty With A Tiny Ally\nThe backbone recipe begins with producing per‑byte *prediction entropies*:\n\n1. **Train a lightweight byte‑level autoregressive LM** \(p_e(\cdot)\) once on all training data you intend BLT itself will see.\ni.e., \(p_e(v \mid x_{<i})\) gives probability assigned to value \(v \in \mathcal V = \{0,\dotsc ,255\}\).\n   The model lives well below hundred‑millions parameters compared against BLT’s multi­gigabyte headline architecture.\n2. For every position \(i\), evaluate its Shannon probability density:\n   ```latex\n   H(x_i)= \sum_{v \in \mathcal V} p_e(x_i=v \mid x_{<i})\,\\log\\Bigl(p_e(x_i=v\\mid x_{<i})\\Bigr)\n   ```\n   High values mark areas where predicting the ensuing character feels genuinely risky.\n3. **Turn entropy peaks into boundaries** – two plausible criteria:\n   * Global threshold \(H(x_t)>\theta_g\\)\): start fresh patch whenever uncertainty surpasses an overall cut‑off.\n   * Approximate monotonicity \(H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r\\)\): open a new block whenever current uncertainty jumps noticeably relative to yesterday’s message.\n   Both operate solely on left‑to‑right history making them valid for autoregressive generation.\n</br>These simple formulas drive everything downstream:\npatches tend automatically longer over cleaned sentences (\(0–2 KB/p)\) while breaking apart noisy variants such as corrupted PDFs or tabbed code listings.</br>\nThe two schemes were evaluated head‑to‑head later – both gave comparable BPB scores while global thresholds were easier tuned.</br>\neach variant was precomputed once during dataloader startup so no run‐time penalty spurs&quot;\ninference.</p>"
