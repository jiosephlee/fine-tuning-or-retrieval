# Breaking Free from Tokens

Large language models (LLMs) have become the default way we talk about “intelligent” text generation – hence their reliance on large vocabularies of sub‑words or words has seemed inevitable for years. The new Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) shows that it is possible – even preferable – to drop that rigid vocabulary entirely and let a model learn directly from raw bytes while remaining competitive with leading token‑based systems such as Llama 3.

---

## What’s the problem with tokenization?

| Issue | Why it hurts | Impact in modern LLMs |
|-------|--------------|----------------------|
| **Fixed vocabulary** | The set of tokens is chosen *before* training; it cannot accommodate out‑of‑vocabulary words or new characters that appear during inference. | Most rare or domain‑specific words are split into awkward sub‑styles or left out entirely. |
| **Compression bias** | Tokenizers learn *heuristics* that group bytes according to frequency rather than prediction difficulty (e.g., they treat whitespace as a rare boundary). | Predicting first characters in new words still consumes one full transformer step even though most of a word’s letters may be easy guesses afterward. |
| **Noise brittleness** | Small perturbations (typos, case changes) can flash an input through several different token sequences. | Token‑level models often stumble at noisy prompts or mildly garbled text where raw‐byte models shine. |
| **Harder long‑tail generalisation** | Because tokens are fixed symbols without direct byte awareness, the model needs extra capacity to infer them from context – leading to wasted compute on straightforward patterns like repeated code snippets or familiar names. | Tokenised models must pay for each token even if many bytes are statistically predictable. |

Simply put: every token comes at a fixed cost in a transformer step regardless of how “surprising” its next character actually is.

---

## Enter BLT – a tokenizer‑free, byte‑aware Transformer

BLT rethinks the building block used by all modern transformer–based LLMs:

1. **Byte → patch**: A *patch* is a contiguous segment of raw bytes whose length is not predetermined by a fixed recipe.
2. **Patch representation**: Each patch is transformed into a dense vector using a *local encoder*.
3. **Global latent transformer** processes the sequence of patch vectors autoregressively.
4. **Decoder** turns the final patch representation back into individual bytes for generation.
5. **Cross‑attention layers** inside encoder/decoder glue together per‑byte context with its owning patch boundary – similar in spirit to Perceiver cross‑attention but adapted for varying patch sizes.

In short: TB between “bytes” (the lowest granularity) *and “patches”* (effective computational units), arranged dynamically based on context instead of fixed slots like tokens.

---

## How are patches chosen?

### Discarded ideas
- **Strided patches** (e.g., every 4⁄6 bytes): simple but wasteful because predictions inside highly predictable areas still incur one transformer step.
- **Whitespace patching** (pseudocode borders after every non‑space byte): better because notes words as boundaries but cannot adjust length per context nor adapt gracefully across languages that lack spaces.
- **BPE/tokenizers**: still have static vocab → expose its usual weaknesses above.

### Entropy‑based dynamic patches

BLT introduces an *entropy* signal coming from a lightweight byte‑level language model (`HRM`) trained on the same data:

\[
H(x_i)=\sum_{v\in \mathcal{V}} p_{\text{e}}\!\bigl(x_i=v \mid x_{<i}\bigr)\;
                \log p_{\text{e}}\!\bigl(x_i=v \mid x_{<i}\bigr)
\]

A high value means the next byte is hard to predict; low values indicate many predictions agree (data is overly repetitive).

Two simple criteria mark new boundaries:

- **Global threshold:** \(H(x_t)>\theta_g\).
- **Monotonicity jump:** \(H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r\).

Both work well; we usually pick the monotonicity rule because it naturally adapts when many identical blocks repeat one after another (common in code or caption datasets). We run this processor during data loading – no extra inference cost – so all batches contain comparable amounts of byte data irrespective of patch size choices.

---

## Architectural highlights

| Component | Idea | Key Detail |
|-----------|------|------------|
| *Local Encoder* | Light Wixture that folds raw bytes into patches quickly | <span style="color:#006400">Cross‑attention after each shallow transformer layer pulls together all bytes belonging to one patch.</span> |
| *Latent Global Transformer* | Main heavy hitter handling high‑level dependencies between patches | <span style="color:#006400">Block‐causal mask = remember only current and past patches within this document.</span> |
| *Local Decoder* | Turns patch‐output back into raw bytes during generation | <span style="color:#006400">Decoder’s cross‐attention inverses encoding logic – keys/values come from patch reps.</span> |

Some specifics you’ll notice if you care about numbers:

- Two local layers are adequate for encoding while nine for decoding provide enough unwind capacity downstream.
- Hash n‑gram embeddings (inline n-grams hashed into an embedding table) help bring light feature expressivity into each byte without exploding parameters.
- When measured in floating point operations (*flops*) per byte, smaller patches produce more steps ⇒ more overall work in both training and inference; larger patches spare that work but require bigger global weights so we can simply scale those instead of adding more layers everywhere else.

---

## Scaling evidence

### Training costs ↔ performance

On training data made of 2 trillion cleaned tokens piped through BLT’s entropy patcher (or its 1 trillion–token variant), we trained:

- 400 M
- 1 B
- 2 B
- 4 B
- 8 B parameters

and plotted *training flops* versus *bits-per-byte (BPB)* – a tokenizer‐agnostic analogue of perplexity. Results demonstrate:

1. All sizes neared or outright surpassed their BPE counterparts trained under identical compute budgets.
2. When measuring BPB over an equal amount of input bytes per batch, BLT’s curves were consistently better—or at least competitive—especially when using entropy patches instead of static ones.
3. Performance gains became more pronounced at larger scales because large global transformers can now be run fewer times thanks to longer patches—this frees up compute for deeper modeling without shifting batch size or context length drastically.

### Inference efficiency

Because each latent transform step now processes an average of six or eight random bytes instead of thirty–plus on tokenised BPE inputs:

- Blt models consistently saved between 30 %–50 % flops per generated byte compared with an equivalent Llama 3 sized model.
- We also ran *fixed–inference flop* experiments, keeping total flops per generated batch constant while repeatedly increasing both model size and average patch size simultaneously—BLT managed this better than pure tokenizer-based architectures because larger globals compensate for fewer steps naturally.

---

## Robustness feats

BLT’s raw access to characters unlocks several benefits unseen in BPE systems:

1. **Noise resistance:** On HellaSwag under five different character-noise regimes (case flips, deletions, repeats), BLT consistently outperformed its BPE counterpart by roughly +8 points average—quite remarkable given it was trained on less data overall.
2. **Granular text understanding:** On the CUTE benchmark covering spelling manipulation and orthographic similarity tests—a domain notorious for choking BPEs—BLT scored >25 points higher across three subcategories including perfect (~99%) scores on spelling fluency tasks.
3. **Phonology & low-resource MT:** On G2P(Google's phoneme transcriptions) five-shot tests and FLORES‑101 translations involving twenty-one low-resource languages across six families: BLT beat tokenised Llama 3 by two BLEU points translating into English and most low-resourced pairs benefited —-showcasing stronger long-tail capacity when dealing with diverse character sets.
4. **“Byteifying” pretrained giants:** We also explored initializing BLT’s latent transformer directly with weights from Llama 3 (1 trillion-token); freezing them yields substantially higher downstream performance than training from scratch—even against larger BLTs produced on raw data—without drastically extra training steps! That suggests mutual benefits between conventional fraught pretraining pipelines and the byte-level paradigm itself.

## Why does this matter?

<ul>

<li><strong>Compute reallocation</strong> – longer dynamic patches mean each tick tells far more information while consuming similar hardware resources.</li>

<li><strong>One-dimensional scaling burst</strong> – you can grow model quasi-linearly AND enlarge your functional units (patches) while holding total inference cost steady → perfectly suited for platforms that limit GPU memory per request.</li>

<li><strong>Robustness advantage spans brittle edge cases</strong> – vet your system with random casing or typographical errors without sacrificing performance tropes tokenised models stumble upon.</li>

<li><strong>No harder inductive assumption</strong> – your vocab need not drive your architecture; instead you sculpt compute allocation directly based on statistical surprise.</li>

</ul>

In short perhaps,”the sheer lot” invites us back toward pure textual primitives – raw bytes – without paying cloudy penalties associated with mutable vocabularies or scrambling artifacts inherent in learn-rich subword schemes.

---

## Limitations & outlook

The authors openly note potential gaps awaiting future work:

- The current scaling studies follow logic derived from BPE-based optimality studies—new laws dedicated specifically to shiftable-byte processing might open further efficiency heights.
  
- The present focus rests almost entirely on head-up linear `flash` algebraic attention implementations (“FlexAttention”). A full wall-clock optimisation equivalent—backed by how transformers typically handle long sequences—is still under investigation.
  
- The entropy checker used here was hand-crafted offline rather than learned end-to-end inside the same objective loop—a fully differentiable version could co-adapt beyond static heuristics.
  
Finally think big: If increasingly benign pixels yield profound insights about downstream task robustness—that inspires augmentations such as learning compositional encoders over hex-wide contexts rather than just linear shifts—and potentially scaling billions—or trillions—of parameters at fixed budgets becomes well reality!

---

## Bottom line

Byte Latent Transformers show that we can ditch hardcoded vocabularies yet keep pace with—or even outstrip—the dominant token-based stars like Llama 3 while saving up-to 50 % fewer floating point operations at inference time! Thanks largely to entropy-driven adaptive batching ("patching") and clever hybrid modelling between low-level encoders/decoders plus a heavy-weight global latent transformer—any foundation built upon raw bytes now feels more viable than ever before.

# Patch‑Powered Prediction

Large‑scale language models today grow faster **thanks** to a fixed vocabulary (BPE / WordPiece) that turns raw text into tokens before feeding it into a Transformer.  
That trick solves one problem—sequence length—but creates another one:  
the same amount of self‑attention work is paid even when the input pieces are trivial (frequent words, repeated code patterns).  
The _Byte Latent Transformer_ (BL T) reverses this convention by letting **bytes themselves decide how much compute they deserve** through an adaptive segmentation called **entropy packing** (or “blob” packing, since each segment starts small and can grow quite large).

---

## From Bytes Back Into Pieces

### What went wrong with hard‑coded tokens?
A BPE tokenizer groups contiguous “bytes” into a fixed dictionary entry regardless of how predictable the resulting symbol is.
Two bad things happen:

| Problem | Consequence |
|---------|-------------|
| Every token costs a full stack‑trace | Low‑entropy parts get wasted memory |
| Tokens embed categorical information only | No explicit link between raw characters and learned weights |

### Entropy packing solves this

If we know how surprised the next byte is given what precedes it, we can decide “stop” precisely when uncertainty drops below a level worth paying attention for again.



For every position \(i\) in a corpus \(x_1,\dots,x_n\), an auxiliary tiny byte‑level language model predicts  
\(p_e(v\, |\, x_{<i})\), the probability that the upcoming byte equals \(v\).
From that probability distribution we compute its Shannon entropy

\[
    H(x_i)\;=\;
    -\,\!\!\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}
      p_e(v\,|\,x_{<i})\,\log_2 p_e(v\,|\,x_{<i}) .
\]

Large values mean “I don’t know what comes next”; small values signal redundancy (e.g., a space followed by “c”).
Two simple rules turn these scores into boundaries:

| Rule | Condition | Effect |
|------|-----------|--------|
| **Global** | \(H(x_t)>\theta_g\) | Split whenever uncertainty exceeds an absolute threshold |
| **Monotonic** | \(H(x_t)-H(x_{t-1})>\theta_r\) | Split whenever uncertainty suddenly jumps compared to its immediate predecessor |

By choosing \(\theta_g,\;\theta_r\) proportionally to the desired average clause length,
the procedure produces highly compact packets that compress richly informative stretches while keeping repetitive regions wide—exactly the division most useful during language modelling training.



---

## A Two‑Layer Pipeline Between Bytes & Patches

Once our binary stream is subdivided into variable‐length blobs,
the Belt–Lee architecture realises three interacting components:

| Step | What happens | Purpose |
|------|--------------|---------|
| **Local Encoder (\(\mathcal E\))** | A lightweight Transformer takes every raw byte’s embedding (plus supplemental “hash‑ngram” cues) and feeds it through alternating Transformer + cross‑attention layers until each package emits one dense latent vector \(p_j∈ℝ^{h_{\mathcal G}}\). | Condenses many bytes into one efficient chunk without losing positional flexibility |
| **Latent Global Transformer (\(\mathcal G\))** | An autoregressive big model processes the sequence \(p_1,\dots,p_m\), generating predictions \(o_j∈ℝ^{h_{\mathcal G}}\). Its causal mask spans whole documents rather than tiny blocks, leaving inference cost dominated mostly by \(m≪n_{\text{bytes}}\). | Learns high‑level structure across long horizons |
| **Local Decoder (\(\mathcal D\))** | A mirror­ing shallow stack turns each predicted latent vector back into individual-bytes predictions through cross‑attention followed by standard byte logits (\(256→256)\). | Leaves downstream tasks unchanged – you still read out ordinary ASCII |

Because cross‑attention focus per target latent vector lies only on its own bytes,
computational budgets never explode even if chunks grow twentyfold—but there’s no statically imposed limit.



---

## Why Variable Logarithm Surfaces Outperform Static Striding

Earlier efforts tried plain strided packing (“every \(k\) bytes”) or whitespace splitting (“new‐word borders”).  
Both suffer at two levels:
* **Unnecessary work** on predictable sequences.
* **Fixed granularity** which has no graceful way to encode very long bursts or very short delimiters simultaneously.

Entropy consequently delivers two main benefits captured cleanly in experimental curves:

1. **Compute‑to‑accuracy Pareto frontier**  
   For an identical FLOPs budget every extra parameter purchased translates into lower Bits‑Per‑Byte—the trusty metric when comparing byte‐with–token models—ready comparable to state‑of‑the art bpe systems such as Llama 3 up through eight billion parameters.
2. **Inference economies**  
   On an inference budget-matched comparison,
 a fourfold larger average chunk forces fewer calls to the giant global Transformer while keeping total hidden state capacity unchanged;
 this translates into roughly half the computational effort at test time without hurting accuracy.

# Cutting Inference Costs by Half  

Large language models today deliver their power through tokenization—a pre‑processing step that groups raw bytes into a fixed set of sub‑words before feeding them into a transformer. While tokenization gives an efficient representation, it also fixes how many computation steps the model has to walk through regardless of how difficult a prediction actually is.  
The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) flips this paradigm on its head by letting the model decide **how many bytes belong together** while it learns itself. A result worth pausing on is that BLT matches token‑based models such as Llama 3 **isolate** their performance while trimming *inference* floating point operations (FLOPs) by close to **fifty percent**.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why Tokenization Isn’t Optimal  

A standard tokenizer partitions every sequence into tokens of roughly equal length – around three‑to‑four bytes in popular models – and then runs the large transformer once per token.  
The great thing about this approach is simplicity: joint train‑time losses are great and wall‑clock speed is consistent across inputs.  
The downside?  
* Every token triggers an expensive “global” attention pass even if the next byte is trivial to predict (say another space character).  
* Switching vocabularies forces a shift between higher average token length ➜ fewer attention calls **and** larger output projection matrices – two trade‑offs that aren’t always beneficial.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## BLT’s Core Idea – Patches That Scale With Complexity  

#### Dynamic Patching via Entropy  

BLT runs a light‑weight *entropy model* that looks ahead one byte into the stream during preprocessing and computes:
```
H( xᵢ ) = Σᵥ∈𝓥 pₑ( xᵢ = v | x_<<ᵢ ) log pₑ( xᵢ = v | x_<<ᵢ )
```
If `H` jumps past a predetermined threshold `θ`, BLT closes the current byte group (a **patch**) and starts a new one:
```
H( xₜ ) > θ_g        or  H( xₜ ) – H( xⱼ–₁ ) > θ_r .
```
Two things happen:

1. **Predictable stretches**—the occurrence pattern of code indentation, repetitive sentences, copyright blocks—yield low entropy → longer patches.
2. **Unpredictable spikes**—the start of a new name, code comments, opening parenthesis—show high entropy → shorter patches.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### From Bytes → Patches → Byte Reconstruction  

| Component | Role | Why It Matters |
|-----------|------|----------------|
| **Local Encoder** | Compresses raw bytes into dense *patch* vectors | Cheap – only intercepts once per byte |
| **Global Latent Transformer** | Processes patches autoregressively | Dominates FLOPs ≈ *All heavy lifting* |
| **Local Decoder** | Re‑creates raw bytes at generation time | Lightly weighted |

Because each global transformer step feeds labels for an entire lightweight chunk rather than one byte, you get:
> **Fewer global passes → dramatically lower overall FLOPs at speed/latency K̇**

With BLT’s dynamic blocks the *average number* of waveform indices seen by the big transformer cuts roughly in inverse proportion with mean patch size (`m`).  
When `m` grows from ~4 bytes (typical tokenizer tokens) toward ~8 bytes through entropy grouping, each forward pass tackles **half** the number of “steps”.

## Where Does the Extra Power Come From?  

Three aspects synergistically shift compute allocation:

1. **Dynamic computational load:** Predictable regions consume one big shot instead of many tiny ones.
2. **Constrained overhead:** Local encoder/decoder layers stay tiny (~≤3 layers), so hop count stays constant regardless of `m`.
3. **No vocabulary ceiling:** Because bits are grouped “on the fly,” you’re never capped by embedding table expansion costs.


With all heavy operations shifted off critical hot paths you get downtown throughput gains unmatched by any static chunk approach — yet keep external prompt handling symmetry unchanged.

# Resilient Models

Large‑scale language architectures are usually *tuned* around a fixed sub‑word vocabulary—the high‑frequency patterns that give them an “inductive bias.”  
That bias is costly when reality diverges from the ratio of words that fit neatly into those buckets—mistyped text, brand‑new acronyms, code mixed with prose, many unseen foreign scripts—any perturbation that changes a single byte will often hide it behind an unknown gloss or completely break the eager splitter that raised it past the end‑of‑vocabulary cutoff.  

Enter **Byte Latent Transformer** (*BLT*): an architecture that never steps outside plain byte streams during both training and inference while still delivering headline‑grade perplexity figures at scale.
Its chief claim isn’t raw speed or grunt power — it’s **robustness** measured under noisy input conditions and at fine orthographic resolutions where token‑centric models stumble quite often.


---

## What makes *BLT* resilient?

### No vocab‑level assumptions
*Tokens* come from a static list built once before any data arrive.
If an incoming character string spills outside that list (`1234$%^`), most modern models spit out an “unknown” symbol or chop the string into awkward sub‑tokens that break continuities later in decoding.\_

*Bl*t!* works off every input byte directly:
– Every character is seen exactly once in its native encoding form.
– There is no “missed” symbol picture—if the very first byte is unseen it simply encourages higher uncertainty rather than catastrophic failure.\_

### Dynamic patching based on entropy
Rather than hashing input uniformly (`k` bytes per patch) or slapping termination marks at whitespace boundaries,
BLT learns an *entropy probe*—a tiny pause‑model that predicts how surprising each next byte will be given everything seen so far.\_
When this entropy spikes it signals an opportunity for ``gliding'' further attention into novelty;
outside these spikes patches can grow longer because they are predictable chunks.\_
This way computation keeps awake only where needed – precisely what makes the model stingling through garbled text without burning extra flops.

## Building *BLT* Right From Tokenized Roots

Rather than learning *from scratch*, fine tuning upgraded full‐scale pretrained engines via a simple freeze/gradient rule gave “many times less” computational load and yet higher downstream scores than larger vanilla flare training runs.^[The paper showed this distilled variant bested both vanilla BLT and dense-BPE designs after matching flop budgets.]

This confirms two things:
1️⃣ Byte representations indeed offer hidden modularity—they sit inside larger transformer mass without destroying learned structure.\_
2️⃣ Pretrained global self‐attention biases carry well into an almost identical architecture even though look key/value comes via local patches—an important path toward future zero-cost adaptation scenarios.

## Takeaways for Practitioners  

* Tokenization is a **constraint**, not just convenience—in noisy production—every missed byte yields catastrophic misinterpretation.\_  
* By working on raw bytes while distributing attention via dynamic patches you get—but you do not sacrifice—state‐of‐the‐art scaling curves (\~Llama‑3 parity) yet slash inference load up to half compared against static chunking strategies (\~Megabyte’s chunker). \_  
* For emerging multilingual pipelines dealing with many non-Latin scripts or interactive translation bots visiting dialectic edges,
the combination of simple entropy boundary detectors and hash n‑gram tricks keeps predictive quality strengthened without brute‑force resizing vocabulary catalogs.\_  
* Not least—and perhaps most tangible—are reductions in drop‑rate since misshaped prompts hurt subword models far more aggressively than direct‑byte analogues.


**Bottom line:** If fault tolerance under conversational quirks is your priority, go bytewise—and let entropy decide where your attention lives.”

**From Fifty-Fold Data To Eight‑Billion Parameters**  

---

Large language models today still depend on a pre‑trained vocabulary that maps sequences of bytes into sub‑word symbols  
(**tokens**).  
Tokenisation wastes useful information and forces every symbol through an expensive output projection layer whose size grows linearly with vocabulary cardinality.  
When you scale a model you are therefore forced onto an inflexible trade‑off­–larger vocab ≈ fewer steps ≈ larger softmax tables  
and vice versa—and it is difficult to permute one without paying another penalty.




---

## What is **BLT**?

> **Byte Latent Transformer** – an end‑to‑end architecture that learns directly from raw bytes without a fixed tokenizer.



> **Key idea:** *group consecutive bytes into “patches” whose size adapts dynamically based on how hard the next byte is predicted*.  
> • Random stretches of predictable whitespace become long patches → very few autoregressive steps are needed  
> • Suffixes after an uncommon character become short patches → more expensive computation is devoted exactly where uncertainty spikes.



Formally let  

```
f_p : {bytes } → {patches}
```

assign each byte either “continue” or “start new”.  
At run time *BLT* invokes its core autoregressive transformer only once per patch rather than once per individual byte.



---

## Architecture Overview  

```
local encoder   →    latent transformer   →    local decoder
      ∆                            ∆                      ∆
bytes → [patch reps] → [patch reps] → [bytes]
```

* **Local Encoder** – lightweight transformer plus cross‑attention that pools together all byte embeddings belonging to one found‐patch  
* **Global Latent Transformer** – much larger stack operating only over patch embeddings (`h_G` features); receives a *block causal* mask so every step can attend back up until current document position  
* **Local Decoder** – inverse mapping from patched representations back into raw bytes (useful during decoding / generation)

Cross‑attention layers provide an efficient bridge without maintaining a huge shared embedding table.



---

## Why Patch Sizes Matter  

Tokenisation fixes packet length implicitly through its vocabulary (`~4–5` bytes average).  
Longer patches translate directly into *fewer* global transformer steps work­horse cost reduces proportionally,

| Patch length | # global steps | Leakage into local modules |
|--------------|----------------|---------------------------|
| <4 bytes     | many            | ∼80 % FLOPs                |
| Ø 6–8 bytes   | fewer           | local users dominated       |

Because *local* modules are shallow (`l_E ≪ l_G`) dominating FLOPs stay fairly constant even when `h_G` grows larger,
BLT can concurrently let **patch length increase** *and* **model depth expand**, both staying within the same inference FLIP budget.

## Experimental Design  

| Component | Done |
|-----------|------|
| Training set | Two corpora:<br>`LLaMA‑2 mix` (~2 trn tokens ↔ ~0.5 trn bytes)<br>`BLT‑1T mix` (~1 trn tokens ↔ ~4 trn bytes) |
| Flop control | Every experiment matched floating‑point operations (FLOPs). LFLOPs counted across all components plus cross‑attention using Chinchilla equations |
| Metric | **Bits‐Per‐Byte (BPB)** replacing perplexity since it aligns across tokenised & raw byte formulations |

The *entropy model*, a small byte‐level transformer (~100 M params), predicts next‑byte entropy `{H(x_i)}` from which thresholds `θ_g`, `θ_r` carve patches dynamically.



---

## Compute‑Optimal Scaling Curve  

Standard Language Models tune their “compute–optimal” ratio `(parameters × steps)` via data released by LLaMA 3.
BLT’s construction allows us to apply the very same ratio:

1️⃣ Fixed architecture parities – each BPE sized global transformer exactly matches one BLT latent one (`l_G`, `h_G`).  
2️⃣ Train every pair *PFLOP* matched (**parameter equals dense memory**) on identical data mixes (`LLaMA 2`).  

At **400M**, **1B**, **2B**, **4B**, **8B** parameters — all runs used *sequentially matched FLOP budgets*.  
Having fixed weight counts strengthens comparability since nothing else changes except sequence length adaptation via patches.



**Result**

> From every curve plot the slope is almost identical – BPB decreases roughly linearly with increasing FLOPs irrespective of tokenisation being present or absent.
>
> For all three centres (`400M`, `2B`, `8B`) both families literally fall upon each other within ±0.002 bits/byte uncertainty once we hit ~30 TFLOPs / layer width.
>
> Therefore, *a pure byte model can walk straight into the “state‑of–the art” terrain set historically by tokeniser driven models.*

## Inference Efficiency  

When decoding we pay cost mostly equalised across two components:

    ⇑ Global Tensor Ops / #patches          ⇓ locally cheap But many steps only when short patches appear


Because long patches halve global operations when data happens largely predictable,
executing an eight‑billion param BLT at request time consumes up-to **50 % fewer FLOPs** compared with an equivalent sized tokeniser based LLaMA 3 at identical task / speed goals.

## Going Further – Fixed‐Inference Budget Experiments 

To expose a new lever exclusive to “patch” methods we held overall TFLOP budget fixed during pretraining yet let both *model depth* AND *average patch length* grow simultaneously (`ps=6`, `ps=8`).  

Contrast with tokenised baselines who have virtually no room here – their effective batch length cannot rebound because vocabulary remains fixed.


🎯 **Key Observation**

| Patch Size | Comparable TFLOP Budget | Peak Model Size Achieved |
|------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| BPE (~4)   | Target                 | ~7 B                     |
| ps=6       | Target                 | ~9 B                     |
| ps=8       | Target                 | >12 B                     |

While small‐scale runs favour BPE initially, after crossing roughly twice σ\(≈2×\)kstep counts any stride traversal passes through “pitch shift” point where **BLT overtakes** before peaking beyond compensating plug‐capacity margin.



> The stabilizing effect comes mainly because longer patches compress attractive portions of input most cheaply while still feeding enough timestep signal so hidden representations grow richer.


Results confirm excellent scalability behaviour anticipated by theory derived from Section §Defense Points:


* “Pointwise autoregression” performed dozens× lighter investments in feed forward calculations relative 
to coding variant baselines (“megaByte” static stride).

* Cross logic keeps locality sharp thanks “perceiver style” pooling masks.

## Why These Numbers Are Interesting

* ***Tokenisation’s rigidity*** forces us either pack many characters into huge vocab slots (+ expensive projection layer)
or break paragraphs leaking memory overheads (+ more space comput).  
Throughout the entire chain it limits opportunity cost × memory footprint surface area.
* ***Dynamic fewbits*** empower internal stochastic estimation guiding how much attention budget each part of sequence receives,
catering evidence currying conclusion near Beethoven turned plain prose versus forest fire zone.
* On top level practical significance:
    * Faster decoding ⟶ lower latency LLM APIs
    * Potential adaptiveness => cheaper end user pipelining at scale
    * Raw byte modelling proves superior generalisation toward rare / low‐resource scripts & noisy data.

## Quick Takeaways  

- Byte Latent Transformer learns entirely end-to-end directly from raw bytes.
- Dynamic entropy-driven patches reduce sequence length by orders-of-magnitude while preserving contextual density.
- Under strictly FLOP controlled experiments:
    + Sharp µ-dash curves overlap those produced by top tagged BPE baselines,
    + Request time cost drops ≈50%, independent of model size.
- By leveraging increasing average patent lengths simultaneously throttling global steps → available budget gets reallocated largely towards stacking deeper auto-regressive layers.
- Newly accessible dimension yields continuous improvement beyond classical pretrained paradigms, opening door toward future next-gen corpora sizes coloured very richly exceptional linguistic structure.
