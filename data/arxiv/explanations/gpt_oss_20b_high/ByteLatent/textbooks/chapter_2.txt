Language‑modeling research has long hinged upon a single objective function—cross‑entropy—paired with two principal performance metrics concerning model quality and computational resource usage: perplexity for token‑based systems and bits‑per‑byte (BPB) for byte‑level systems, together with a flop counting regime that measures real‑time computational effort during both pre‑training and inference.\n\n# CrossEntropy Loss on Sequences\nLearning an autoregressive language model amounts to solving an optimization problem where the model predicts each element of an input sequence conditioned on its entire past history. The most common objective function used throughout this work is Sequential Cross‑Entropy (CE). Concretely, CE aggregates over every position \(i\) inside a finite sequence \(\pmb{x}=\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\) and penalizes divergent probability estimates \(p_{\theta}(x_i|\pmb{x}_{<i})\). Formally one writes\n\n$$\\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x}) = \\sum_{i=1}^{n}\\!\\bigl(-\\log p_{\\theta}(x_i\\mid\\pmb{x}_{<i})\\bigr),$$\n\nwhere \(p_{\\theta}\) denotes the distribution parametrized by model weights \(\\theta\). Because every prediction step consumes one operation during forward passes—and later incurs back‑propagation overhead—the CE computation directly impacts both model convergence speed and total floating‑point operations performed while training.\n\nUnlike generic classification losses, CE has an informative interpretation:\n\n* It measures expected negative log probability per token or byte—exactly what we wish a language model to learn.\n* Minimisation pushes predictions toward higher likelihood over true next symbols.\n* Regularising gradients as necessary keeps fit between overspecialized early training stages and generalisation later.\n\nTo facilitate comparisons between diverse vocabulary sizes—including naive vocabularies of single bytes versus sophisticated learned tokens—the CE sum appears verbatim inside derived assessment metrics such as BPB or perplexity calculations described below.\n\n# Perplexity versus Bits‑Per‑Byte (BPB)\nTraditionally evaluated speech —especially within subword‑tokenised transformers—perplexity has been regarded as a natural measure: it interprets CE normalisation through base‐\(e\) exponentiation over tokens:\n\n$$e^{\\frac{\\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{t})}{n_{tokens}}},$$\nand expresses how many equally likely candidates an idealised system would consider when predicting each token.\n\nIn byte‑level settings however, token counts are ill‑defined – applied vocabularies are fixed yet useless for raw bytes, whose symbolic alphabet size is intrinsically twenty‐six and varies according to concatenated contexts. To remedy this mismatch, **bits‑per‑byte** replaces token normalisation by direct byte normalisation. BPB discards exponential steps entirely and instead normalises CE by both nybble length and constant \(\ln(2)\), yielding:\n\n$$\\text{BPB}(x)= \\frac{\\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\\pmb{x})}{\\ln(2) \\cdot n_{bytes}},$$\nand reflects average uncertainty expressed most naturally in standard units of information theory—bits per output symbol.\n\nBPB enjoys several conceptual advantages inheriting organised prediction credit across patches:\n\n* Because underlying symbols are uniformly sized digits (\(256^{\,}/256)\), BPB retains comparability irrespective of vocabulary engineering choices.\n* Bytes carry orthographic properties lost when inflectional words become hard token commitments.\n* Computing outer logs transforms variability between long context padded versus minimal length into linear scales appropriate for evaluating modelling fidelity at deep network depths—a property particularly useful when performing extensive scaling studies such as those reported at up to eight billion parameters.\n\nRecent experiments demonstrate these metrics’ sensitivity differs markedly across approaches—canonical challenges emerge when character noise interferes with learned integers while tokenised systems display brittleness relative noise conditions, prompting robust worthiness tests highlighted later.\n\n# FLOP Calculation Basics\nThe authors adopt all preexisting methodology demonstrated Kuhndrick et al.’s Chinchilla framework—printing detailed duplication coefficients such as feed‐forward cost being proportional simply via multiply-adds collapsed into net operations-descriptive tensors—and outsource vectorisation efforts into Flash Attention or Flex Attention when nonstandard scopes appear.\n\nA BLT architecture comprises three core modules:\(l_{E}\)<\(l_{\mathcal G}\),\(l_D\<l_G,\) alongside multiple multi‑head attention computations at local/latent scales embodied through~solid practical solutions such as `SwiGLU`.
