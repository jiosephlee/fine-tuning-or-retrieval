# Behind the Magic of Quantization in BitNet b1.58  

Large language models (LLMs) have made it possible for machines to read, write and interpret text with striking accuracy.  Yet their massive sizes—often dozens of billions of parameters—have turned them into energy‑hungry slow machines that are difficult to deploy on consumer GPUs or edge devices.  One of the simplest ways to tame a model’s footprint is **quantization** – forcing the many real‑valued weights into a compact discrete set.

In BitNet b<sub>1.58</sub>, a recent variant of the BitNet family, a single clever trick called **absmean quantization** pushes every weight into three possible values \(\{-1,\;0,\;+1\}\).  The “zero” option turns the model into a dynamic filter that can turn off features on demand while keeping a tiny memory and power bill.



## What is Absmean Quantization?

At its core, absmean quantization is nothing more than “scale‑then‑round” – but with a scaling factor that respects the overall *shape* of each weight matrix:

\[
\widetilde{W}
    = \operatorname{RoundClip}
      \!\left(
        \frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon},\, -1,\,+1
      \right),
\]

where  

* \(W\) is an \(n\times m\) weight matrix,
* \(\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j} |W_{ij}|\) is the *average absolute value*,
* \(\epsilon\) is a tiny stabilizer (preventing division‑by‑zero),
* \(\operatorname{RoundClip}(x,a,b) = \max(a,\min(b,\;\operatorname{round}(x)))\).

In plain English:

| Step | Action | Why it matters |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **Scale** | Divide every entry by its average absolute value \(\,\gamma\). | This normalizes all weights so they lie roughly in the range \([-1,+1]\), making them easy to shrink discretely without crushing important patterns. |
| **Round** | Apply integer rounding, then clamp between \(-1\) and \(+1\). | The final result is one of three values: laser‑sharp +1, clean cycle‑breaking 0, or committing negative –1. |

The key insight is that by scaling with \(γ\) first, each layer respects its own distribution while keeping everything controlled at *one bit* plus an explicit “don’t care” slot (the zero). That extra slot transforms how the network computes.



## Why Include Zero? Dynamic Feature Filtering

If you think about matrix multiplication:
\[
Y = X W,
\]
each element in \(W\) either contributes positively (\(+1\)), negatively (\(-1\)), or not at all (\(0\)).  
Because there’s no real multiplication—everything collapses to integer additions—any entry set to \(0\) simply vanishes from the sum:

* **Zero means “skip.”**  
With modern GPUs and CPUs almost all time is spent adding or multiplying values from large tensors; dropping an entry bypasses both operations entirely.

When training proceeds under this scheme:

* The network learns which connections are *essential* and which are *redundant*.  
Current training algorithms drive many weights toward zero naturally through stochastic gradient descent and regularisation terms (like L₁ penalty).  
Thus each layer ends up acting like an **adaptive filter** – it selectively passes through signals from certain positions while blocking others.



## Put Together With Symmetric Activation Quantization

Quantizing weights is only one side of the story – activations matter too because they’re fed back into subsequent layers.  
BitNet b<sub>1.58</sub> keeps activations **8‑bit** but scales them symmetrically around zero instead of shifting them into a positive range:

```
Active value → scale → clip → map back → [-Q_b , Q_b] per token
```

Why “per token” rather than globally?  
Because token outputs can vary wildly (some words spark huge neural spikes), per‑token scaling keeps each token’s dynamic range tight while avoiding artificial offsets (“zero‑point”) that would otherwise introduce extra error.



## The Bottom Line – Simplicity Meets Performance

By handing every part of the model two choices—use or ignore—plus two polarized actions (+/- rating), BitNet b<sub>1.58</sub> collapses storage from FP16 (16 bits per parameter plus an extra bit for sign) down to just **≈ X bits** per parameter:

```text
FP16  -> 16 bits
BitNet b = 2                         -> ~2 bits for non-zero entries
BitNet b = 2 + optional zeros         -> effectively ~1.58 bits per parameter
```

The empirical proof comes from matchups against state‑of‑the‑art full‑precision models (like LLaMA). When trained from scratch on roughly 100 B tokens:

| Model Size | Perplexity Match vs FP16 |
|------------|--------------------------|
| **3B**     | Matched               |
| **3.9B**   | Outperformed FP16     |

And beyond perplexity grows further benefits:

* GPU memory consumption drops by > 70 %,
* Inference latency goes down by ≈ 2–4× depending on size,
* Energy consumed during matrix multiplications shrinks by ~70× after accounting for cheaper integer ops versus floating point ones.



## In Short

The absmean trick does something deceptively straightforward:

- Scale weights so their average absolute value becomes unity.
- Round those scaled values onto three clean discrete points.
- Keep odd zeros alive as gates that silence unneeded features automatically.

This small redefinition yields > 3‑bit saving while preserving—and even enhancing—LLM quality across tasks like ARC, Winogrande and Q/A benchmarks.


### Takeaway

Beyond nailing energy & speed gains, letting each weight decide *whether it should fire at all* turns large models into lightweight filters that retain their depth without drowning computation overheads—a clear recipe for bringing LLMs onto GPUs, CPUs and even mobile corners alike.”
