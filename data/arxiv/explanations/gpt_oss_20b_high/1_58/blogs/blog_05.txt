\title{Blogs about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

# Powering AI With Less Energy—BitNet’s Arithmetic Saves Up To Seventy‑Times

Large language models (LLMs) have become the de‑facto backbone for virtually every text‑centric AI service today. While their performance keeps improving with each new release, their compute and power footprints also balloon at an alarming rate—even a single inference can cost significant electricity, carbon and hardware resources.  
The recent work on **BitNet b1.58** shows that it is possible to maintain competitive quality while collapsing that cost envelope by almost an order of magnitude through a very simple change in the math inside the Transformer blocks.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why is inference usually expensive?

A Transformer inference step is dominated by a cascade of linear layers (`nn.Linear`).  
Mathematically each linear layer maps an input vector **x** ∈ ℝ^n to an output **y** = W x + b where W ∈ ℝ^{m×n}.  
Each matrix‑vector multiplication involves *n* multiply‑add operations – every multiply is usually performed in floating point (FP32 or FP16).  
On modern GPUs or CPUs the “multiply‑add” operation consumes more power than an addition and occupies far more silicon area.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## What BitNet does differently

| Conventional LLM | BitNet b1.58 |
|------------------|-------------|
| Weights are FP16 / BF16 | Weights are *ternary*: \{-1, 0, +1\} (≈ 1.58 bits each) |
| Activations often FP16 or higher | Activations become fixed‑point INT8 |
| Uses `nn.Linear` written in generic C++/CUDA | Uses a custom `BitLinear` that never multiplies |
| Has full precision biases everywhere | Biases removed entirely – they add little ‑M |

Because every weight is only \{-1, 0, +1\}, multiplying an activation by a weight collapses into *either drop it (×0), keep it (×+1), or invert it (×−1).*  
All that remains are integer additions or subtractions – simple bit‑shift operations on cache‑friendly integers.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## The quantisation recipe

To keep the ternary set without sacrificing expressiveness the authors use what they call **absmean quantisation**:

```text
γ   = mean(|W_ij|)
Ŵ_ij = round_clip( W_ij / (γ + ε), -1 , +1 )
```

`γ` rescales the column so that most values fall inside \{-0.5…+0.5\} before rounding.
With this small scaling factor the distribution of weights stays centred at zero,
which preserves the signal flow necessary for a high capacity network.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Energy savings in practice

> **Figure 3 (conceptual)** in the paper shows a breakdown of the arithmetic power draw per operation against a typical 7 nm GPU chip model.
> For every dot product inside a transformer block:
> 
> * An FP16 network spends roughly \(P_{\text{FM}}\) joules per FMA step.
> * A BitNet b1.58 network spends roughly \(P_{\text{INT}}\) joules per INT addition,
>   which is about \(\frac{P_{\text{FM}}}{71}\).

That turns into almost **70× reduction** in arithmetic‑energy consumption *just* for the matrix multiplications that dominate inference time.
Because most transformer workload now lives in those linear layers,
the overall battery life or datacenter CO₂ output per token improves by roughly *one order of magnitude*.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Performance parity comes at no loss

The authors trained **BitNet b1.58** from scratch on 100 B tokens using a RoPE‑enabled,
SwiGLU architecture identical to open‑source LLaMA models.
With identical token budgets:

| Size | Metric | FP16 baseline | BitNet b1.58 |
|------|--------|---------------|--------------|
| 3B   | Perplexity | ~25 | ~25 – matches fully|
| 3B   | Zero‑shot accuracy (average over ARC·Easy / ARC·Challenge / Hellaswag / …) | – | – |
| 3․9B | Latency per token | – | **2× faster** |
| 3․9B | GPU memory | – | **~3× smaller** |

And as size grows further (7B → 70B), speedups rise from ≈2× to >4× while memory demands fall proportionally.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Scaling law unlocked by “cheap” arithmetic

Because each linear layer costs almost nothing,
the effective *cost* of adding more layers shrinks dramatically:
* 13BBitNet ≈ performance‑efficient version of a 3B FP16 model
* 30BBitNet ≈ efficient counterpart to a 7B FP16 model
* …and so on up to seventy billion parameters with only ≈4× latency overhead compared to its FP16 sibling.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why this matters beyond counting FLOPs

* **Hardware:** The low precision means specialized low-power ASICs can be written that target INT8 addition kernels rather than heavy SPU pipelining required by FP32/FP16.
* **Enterprise:** Massive saving in data‑center wattage translates directly into cost reductions; even small bursts like generating document summaries become cheaper.
* **Mobile & Edge:** A single mobile SoC could run a multi‑billion parameter LLM locally – previously impossible with standard floating point thanks purely to reduced memory bandwidth.
* **MoE & Long Sequences:** Compressing the KV cache from 16‑bit to 8‑bit doubles usable context length without extra compute budgets – another “energy” win.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Takeaway

BitNet b1.58 demonstrates that the key bottleneck in LLM inference isn’t just raw FLOPs—it’s *floating point math itself*.  
By restricting weights to \{-1,0,+1\} and using fixed‑point activations,
every matrix multiply collapses into fast integer additions that die off quickly in power budgets.
The result? Near‐seventy‑fold reductions in arithmetic energy while preserving full task performance—and all this without requiring exotic training techniques or hand‐tuned compression hacks.



When deploying next-generation language models,
thinking about how you *compute* inside your networks becomes as important as improving what you learn from data.
