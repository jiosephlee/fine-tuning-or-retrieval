\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### "How does fastText help retrieve math web pages during DeepSeekMath corpus construction?"
Question:
"I read that the authors train a fastText classifier on OpenWebMath data and then use it to recall more math-related pages from Common Crawl.

I'm unclear what exactly fastText outputs here – does it rank all crawled URLs based on similarity scores?
Does it filter down a huge crawl set down to merely those whose cosine similarity exceeds some threshold?
And how do those scores translate into picking *the top X %* of pages?

The whole promise relies on this classifier being accurate yet easy enough for millions of queries – I'd love an intuitive explanation of that pipeline."
Answer:
In DeepSeekMath the “fastText‐based classifier” is used only as a lightweight, one-pass filter that turns an enormous Common-Crawl dump into a tractable collection of mathematically-relevant sites.  
The whole idea is very much the same as what most web scrapers do when they want a domain-specific corpus:  
* make a *seed* set that is known to contain target content,  
* learn a tiny discriminative model on this seed,
* apply it to every document in the raw crawl,
* keep only those documents whose predicted score looks convincing enough for downstream human curation.



### Training the model

From the paper’s own description

> “Using this corpus we train a fastText model … selecting **500 000** data points from OpenWebMath as positive examples … together with **500 000** other web pages from Common Crawl as negatives… vector dimension 256 … word n‐gram up to 3 … number of epochs 3” \citep{DeepSeekMath}..

Thus the final binary classifier is simply
$$
s(\mathbf{x}) \;\triangleq\;\mathbf{w}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{x} + b ,
$$
where $\mathbf{x}$ is the hashed representation produced by fastText’s sub-word look-ups  
(“ngrams up to length 3”), $\mathbf{w}$ is learned across positives and negatives,
and $s(\mathbf{x})$ can be interpreted either as a signed log-likelihood ratio or,
after applying $\sigma$, as $P(\text{math}\mid \mathbf{x})$.



### How it is used on Common Crawl

1. **Deduplication first** – Raw CC contains roughly \(100\,000\,000\,000\) URLs; before filtering it is collapsed via URL-based and near-duplicate removal down to about \(40\,000\,000\,000\nobreakspace{}HTML\nobreakspace{}pages\rule[--]{\.}{5pt}{<cite>{DeepSeekMath}}</cite>}.  
   Fast-text inference is inexpensive at this scale because its core operation is just several dot products between pre-hashed ngrams and weight vectors—no attention graph needs constructing.
2. **Scoring** – Every remaining page (after stripping tags) is fed into our trained fast-text model once, yielding $\textit{s}_{\,{\rm page}}$.  
   The authors explicitly say “We rank the collected pages according to their scores predicted by the fast-text model…”, meaning that higher real-valued logits correspond directly to higher confidence in being a mathematics site.
3. **Top-rank filtering** – Pages whose scores fall into the upper tail are preserved while others are discarded.  
   The percentage retained isn’t hard coded in one single number: instead it’s chosen empirically based on how many input tokens are needed for pre-training runs (the authors experiment with \$40,\,$80,\,$120,\,$160\billion\$ token budgets).   
   Concretely, after sorting by $\textit{s}_{\,{\rm page}}$ you continue taking documents until you hit your desired token horizon—for instance keeping enough files so their cumulative content equals roughly \$40\billion\$ tokens when first tested.
///
/// This sorting mechanism therefore realizes “the top X %” implicitly—the exact percentile depends on how briefly you want your trial corpus.
///
4. **Iterative refinement** – After processing one tranche of CC you discover many math pages were missed because the initial seed was shallow (e.g., none from MathOverflow).   
   You therefore add new positives extracted by manual inspection of domains where over ten percent of sites were already found (“math-related domains”).   
   With these enriched positives you re-train another thin fast-text model and repeat step&nbsp;2–3, which gradually increases recall until successive passes return \>\!\,98\,% duplication rate (“nearly all … had already been collected” in iteration&nbsp;4).



### Why fastText works well here

| Criterion | What FastTable offers | Practical consequence |
|-----------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| Speed      | Vectorized hashing + dot-product:\($O(\{\# {\rm ngrams}\})$\per page | Millions of queries finished within minutes on commodity hardware |
| Accuracy baseline | Logistic regression trained on clear positives/negatives gives AUC ≈ .9‒1 : very clean class boundary for pure math sites | List has very few false positives so humans focus quickly |
| Scalability | No GPU memory blow-up – entire vocabulary fits into RAM (\rule[-5pt]{5pt}{5pt}\nobreakspace{}256 D×{\nobreakspace{}# unique-ngrames}) | Scoring all \$10^{10}\$ URLs without distributed training |

These properties mean even though Common Crawl enormous, fast-text turns an almost impossible search problem into “rank/keep” operations both honing relevance and faithfully controlling volume.



### Summary

In short:
1\. Train a tiny logistic regression (fast-text) on “math’’ versus “non-math’’ webpages drawn from OpenWebMath versus random junk webpages.\n\
2\. Score every de-duplicated Common-Crawl page once → real-valued score indicating probability of containing math.\n\
3\. Select only high-scoring pages—by imposing an explicit cutoff or stopping once enough tokens met budget—for huge volume reduction (~4× faster than naive crawling).\n\
4\. Repeat adding newly discovered presences so later rounds recover even more missed sites.



Hence “fast-text outputs’’ are per-page confidence scores (larger → more likely medical); these scores are sorted or capped so only most promising fraction survives; inference cost per URL trivial → millions queries easy even at production scale—exactly what DeepSeekMath needs harvesting its \$120\-billion-token maths corpus from CC.\n

### "How do you avoid benchmark leakage when building the DeepSeekMath Corpus?"
Question:
"The paper mentions removing any segment containing a ten‑gram identical substring from benchmarks such as GSM8K or MATH.

I don't understand why exactly this 10‑gram rule was chosen.
Does it guarantee that more concise problems won't slip through?
What happens when problems are shorter than ten words?

Also how is de‑duplication performed beyond exact matching on these substrings?”
Answer:
## De‑contamination in the DeepSeekMath Corpus

### Why an “exact‑10‑Gram” filter?

The authors’ primary aim in constructing the corpus is to **prevent models from seeing their evaluation data during pre‑training**—otherwise any gains could be unrealistically attributed to memorised answers instead of genuine reasoning ability.  
They follow the same approach used in the earlier *DeepSeek‑Coder* work: scan every chunk of text that will be fed into training and drop it if it contains an *exact* n‑gram that also appears in one of the public math benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH, CMATH, AGIEval).

The choice of *ten* consecutive tokens strikes a practical compromise:

| Factor | Reasoning |
|--------|-----------|
| **Length** | A run of ten words (or tokenised tokens) is long enough that it is unlikely to arise by chance in two unrelated documents, yet short enough that a reasonably efficient linear scan can be performed over tens of billions of tokens. |
| **Coverage** | Most problem statements or solutions in these benchmarks are self‑contained passages of several sentences; a true copy will almost always include at least one contiguous block of ten words identical to what’s on the benchmark page. |
| **Computational cost** | Checking every 10‑gram against all benchmark substrings requires only simple hash­based lookups, whereas longer thresholds would increase memory pressure without proportionally boosting safety, and shorter thresholds would cause many false positives and an explosion in discarded data. |

The rule does **not guarantee** that every contaminating excerpt is removed – it only guarantees that an overlapping phrase as long as ten tokens will trigger a drop. Short fragments can slip through if no contiguous block meets or exceeds that length, but those are usually too tiny to provide nontrivial reasoning help (e.g., a single word like “quadratic”) and are treated separately (see below).

### Dealing with benchmark snippets shorter than ten tokens

For benchmark entries whose original text is shorter than ten words (the bulk of which are < 12 tokens), the paper specifies an auxiliary rule:

> *If a segment contains at least *a* 3­‐gram string that matches exactly one from the evaluation set, drop it.*

This catches short problems or solution fragments that would otherwise escape the ten­–gram filter because they never exceed nine tokens but still pose a high contamination risk.

Hence both rules together cover almost all plausible overlaps: long blocks get filtered by 10­–grams; very short zones by 3­–grams.

### Other deduplication techniques applied before benchmarking checks

The paper outlines several preprocessing steps aimed at reducing duplication **before** any benchmark­‑based filtering:

1. **URL–based deduplication** – pages sharing the same base URL are collapsed into one representation; duplicate HTML files are discarded early on.
2. **Near–deduplication** – a lightweight shingling/hash technique discards nearly identical pages (e.g., mirrors or comment‑heavy clones). This reduces redundancy while preserving diverse mathematical content.
3. **FastText classifier ranking** – after collecting all candidate math pages with the trained FastText model, pages are sorted by their predicted score; only top­‐ranked chunks are kept so that low­quality or non​-​math passages never reach downstream stages.
4. **Benchmark contamination check** – once token counts have been cleaned analytically via the two n­–gram filters above, pages containing matching substrings are thrown out entirely from the training corpus.

These stages work hierarchically: first strike out obvious duplicates globally (URLs), then prune near‒duplicates locally (shingles), then rate content quality by NLP model scoring—and finally perform fine granularity leakage checks against known benchmarks.



---

### Summary

- The ten­–gram rule trades off coverage against practicality  
  (it protects most copied material while keeping false positives manageable).
- The supplementary three­–gram rule handles very short
  benchmark entries (< ten tokens).
- Together they form an efficient contaminant filter suitable
  for gigantic corpora collected automatically online.
- Other utilities (`URL dedupe`, sharding,
  FastText quality ranking) form layers upstream,
  ensuring plausibly clean inputs throughout ever larger datasets.



When assembling your own corpus you can reliably reproduce this layered pipeline  
(URL & shingle deduplication → FastText filtering → exact n­–gram enforcement)
and guarantee freedom from blatant leaks while retaining most valuable
mathematical content.

### "What specific benefits does code‑training provide for mathematical reasoning without tools?"
Question:
"Experimental results show two‑stage training (code → Math) improves solvability even when tools aren’t allowed.

I'm trying to pin down *why* the presence of programming tokens boosts pure symbolic reasoning.
Could it be teaching precise variable handling or structural habits?
Or perhaps it's due to longer sequences mimicking proofs?”
Answer:
**Short answer**  
Pre‑training a small language model first on a large collection of programming‑language tokens gives it an *inductive bias* that mimics formal algebraic manipulation—precise symbol handling, disciplined control flow and systematic structuring—which carries over into pure mathematical reasoning (even when external solvers are disallowed).  

---

#### Evidence from DeepSeekMath

The authors carried out a controlled study comparing three regimes  

1. **Code → Math** : 400 B code‑tokens followed by 150 B math‑tokens  
2. **General → Math** : 400 B generic text followed by 150 B math‑tokens  
3. **Mixed** : 400 B combined‑code‑and‑math tokens trained together  

On every benchmark where *no* external tool was permitted—GSM8K (chain‑of‑thought), MATH (chain‑of‑thought), MATH (program‑of‑thought)—the **Code → Math** link produced higher accuracies than its General counterpart (see tables showing gains ranging from several percentage points up to ~10 ppt gains depending on task).  
When ancillary test items required executing Python programs (“tool use”), the same pattern held true—the isolated “code” phase supplied a very strong engine for stepwise computation before any problem–specific mathematics was introduced.

Importantly, when recipes were merged into a single train layer ("Mixed"), solvability *with* external tooling remained comparable while **accuracy without tooling dropped slightly**, suggesting an interference effect attributable either to limited representational capacity or catastrophic forgetting when both signal types compete during weight updates—a problem alleviated when stages are separated chronologically (“Curriculum Learning” style).

---

#### Why does this happen?

| Aspect                            | How coding data shapes it | Consequence for pure mathematics |
|-----------------------------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Discovering symbols consistently** | Most self‑contained symbols (`x`, `y`, `z`, `$\\sum$`, `$\\pi$`) appear literally inside syntactically correct statements (`int x=5`, `for(i=0;i<n;i++)`) | The network learns an internal alignment between token identity and semantic role — crucial when it later has no literal definition visible |
| **Structured decomposition** | Language models repeatedly see nested blocks (`if`, `{…}`), loops (`while`, `for`), function definitions — each defining scopes/attributes systematically | These patterns mirror proof skeletons ("step", “sub–step”) encountered during chain–of–thought generation, facilitating implicit parsing of multi–step logic |
| **Deterministic arithmetics** | Operators (`+`, `*`, `/`) always act on typed operands (`int`, `float`) and produce predictable payloads (`return int(a+b);`) | Repeated exposure paints an inner mental map encoding basic algebraic identities (e.g., distributive/associative law), allowing quick symbol elimination during derivations |
| **Large scale repetition** | Millions of snippets share identical idioms ("if … else …", “while …”, “calculate sum”) | Reinforcement through sheer breadth reduces noise in embedding space—reduces spurious correlations common in natural language datasets |

Together these signals create what can be thought of as an *internal compiler* memory:
the raw transformer weights develop a lightweight latent representation akin to type–checking tables—we are told implicitly rather than explicitly—but this enables successful symbolic reductions even when no outside script engine runs them at inference time.

---

#### Connection to broader theory

The phenomena studied here parallels curriculum learning ideas explored in instruction tuning literature:
introducing structured intermediates helps downstream tasks that demand long chains of logic (Mathematics → External evaluation modules).
Moreover, RL methods such as GRPO further magnify gains introduced during “code first” warm start because reinforcement signals leverage precisely those learned inductive biases—as evidenced by >4\,ppt bumps following GRPO fine–tuning compared with vanilla SFT models—even though RL only samples questions generated by the existing policy itself.



---

##### Bottom line

Code-training injects into the transformer an architectural awareness commonly associated with formal proof systems:
variables get named deterministically,
operations obey strict combinatorial rules,
and hierarchical control structures look very much like deductive steps.
When later exposed to math corpora, these old skills serve as scaffolding allowing English prompts alone—and sometimes even within purely symbolic token regimes—to provoke faithful stepwise derivations demanded by benchmarks such as MATH or GSM8K.
That explains why “Code → Math” consistently outperforms starting from plain linguistic pre-training even if the model isn’t allowed any external solver during inference。
