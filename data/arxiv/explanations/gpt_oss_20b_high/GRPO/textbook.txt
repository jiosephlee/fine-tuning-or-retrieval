### Historical Progress and Benchmarks  

Mathematical reasoning has evolved alongside the growth of large language models (LLMs). Early generative systems were remarkable at producing fluent text but struggled utterly when asked to follow multiple logical steps or evaluate numeric expressions accurately — a gap that became evident when evaluating synthetic challenges such as integer arithmetic or basic algebraic transformation questions presented in datasets released around the mid‑2010s.\footnote{Typical examples include automatically generating solutions for questions that only demand elementary counting or simple equation manipulation}.  
The advent of transformer architectures revived this area considerably because self‑attention provides a systematic architectural dialectist that can capture long‑range dependencies between tokens ― a feature indispensable for sustained arithmetic deductions.\footnote{Transformer models tend toward latent structured representations that encode algebraic relationships implicit in mathematical problem statements}.

* **GSM‑8K**\citep{gsm8k}. This resource contains eight thousand randomised grade–school style question–answer pairs featuring multi‑step calculations which require precise synthetic iconography plus an accurate short handwritten-type trace calling upon arithmetic rules without external support.

# Building a Large-Scale Math Corpus  

Mathematical reasoning remains one of the most demanding tasks for language models because it requires both deep symbolic understanding and the ability to manipulate multi‑step proofs or calculations. A key enabler for recent breakthroughs is **high‑quality mathematical pre‑training data** that captures the breadth and depth of mathematical content available on the Internet. This chapter explains in detail how the *DeepSeekMath* team constructed their flagship corpus—120 billion tokens mined from Common Crawl—by iteratively augmenting an initial seed set until coverage stabilized at multiple times greater than prior collections such as OpenWebMath and Minerva’s math subset. The construction pipeline is modular yet tightly coupled: it begins with carefully labeled seed examples that train a lightweight classifier, proceeds through URL deduplication and near‑duplicate filtering, expands via an enrichment loop across domains, and finally rigorously decontaminates any overlap with evaluation benchmarks. Each component is elucidated below so that you can reproduce or adapt it for other mission‑critical domains.

---

## Seed Corpus Choice → Positive/Negative Labeling  

The first step is to supply **positive** mathematical examples that represent the signal we wish the model eventually uncovers across Common Crawl’s massive web archive. The authors selected **OpenWebMath**, an existing curated collection of high‑quality webpages containing interactive mathematics (e.g., discussion threads on mathematics forums and educational sites), as this seed set because it balances lexical variety and content depth while being small enough (≈ a few million tokens) to be amenable for quick experimentation.  

To provide contrast for classification training, an equal‑sized set of **negative** samples must be chosen from random webpages that are presumed not to contain targeted mathematical text. From our own scraped Common Crawl subset (40 billion HTML pages after initial cleanup), we uniformly draw 500 000 pages that exhibit no obvious mathematics marker (e.g., absence of equations or mathematical symbols). Each page becomes one negative example—thereby constructing a balanced “math vs non‑math” training set of one million examples (500 k positives + 500 k negatives). This explicit labeling strategy equips the fastText model to learn discriminative lexical patterns associated with mathematics while suppressing generic web noise.

---

## FastText Feature Setting (#dim=256…)  

With labeled data prepared, we instantiate a **fastText binary classification** model because its simplicity and speed allow rapid iteration over the huge Collective Crawl (Common Crawl) distribution. The key hyperparameters are:

| Parameter | Value | Rationale |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| `dim` (word vector dimension) | 256 | Sufficient capacity for semantic encoding while remaining lightweight for large‑scale training |
| `lr` (learning rate) | 0.1 | Prevents premature convergence given short epochs |
| `epoch` | 3 | Balances accuracy against wall‑clock cost |
| `minn`, `maxn` (n‑gram limits) | 3 | Captures short morphological cues prevalent among LaTeX‑style tokens |
| `minCount` | 3 | Excludes extremely rare words that introduce noise |

The fastText representation learns both whole words and character n‑grams; thus it effectively identifies obscure symbols like “∑”, “∫”, or function names such as “omega(n)” in contexts where they appear alongside natural language discourse—exactly what distinguishes a math page from ordinary text. After training on our balanced set, we apply the resulting classifier to every page still present after preliminary URL deduplication (see next section); each page receives a probability score reflecting its likelihood of being mathematically relevant.

---

## URL Deduplication & Near-Dedup Removal  

Common Crawl is characterized by massive duplication—multiple URLs often point to identical HTML content or only small variations thereof (e.g., tracking parameters). Processing every document is neither computationally feasible nor necessary; moreover duplicate documents can skew classifier thresholds and waste storage space during fine‑grained filtering. Thus an initial **deduplication step** is performed:

1. **URL-based deduplication** removes completely identical URLs across shards.
2. **Near-deduplication** uses canonical hashing techniques (hashing document fingerprints via MinHash or similar similarity hashing), collapsing near-identical documents into clusters such that only one representative remains per group if their hamming distance falls below a chosen threshold.
   
Post‑deduplication yields about **40 billion** unique HTML documents—a manageable input volume for fastText scoring while still offering deep coverage across domains.

After scoring these URLs by their fastText probability \(p_{\text{math}}(u)\), we retain only those surpassing top percentiles until we reach desired token limits derived empirically from pre‑training experiments (see §4). This greedy selection ensures high precision while allowing us later to recover recall via enrichment loops.

---

## Iterative Enrichment Loop Section Expansion Processing  

From the first pass alone, many mathematically interesting pages remain invisible because the initial training set targeted only OpenWebMath content—a narrow slice of all possible math narratives on the web. To widen coverage, DeepSeekMath employs an **iterative enrichment pipeline**:

1. **Domain grouping**: All remaining HTML files are grouped by *base domain* (\(d = \{ \text{example.com} \}\)) where all URLs share this root.
2. **Recall coverage metric** \(C(d)\): For each domain \(d\), compute what fraction \(\frac{N_{\text{collected}}(d)}{N_{\text{total}}(d)}\) were already captured in previous rounds.
3. **Domain classification rule**: If \(C(d) > 0.10\) (i.e., more than 10 % of its pages were obtained earlier), flag \(d\) as *math-related*. Domains satisfying this condition garner special attention because they already demonstrate latent signals.
4. **Human annotation cue**: Within these flagged domains we identify significant sub‐paths where mathematics legitimately appears—e.g., `/questions` on mathoverflow.net or `/learn/math` on educational websites—and manually curate their URLs.
5. **Seed augmentation**: Any webpage reachable through these curated links but absent from prior iterations is appended back into the positive set \(\mathcal{P}\).
6. **Re‐train fastText** on updated \((\mathcal{P},\mathcal{N})\) using identical hyperparameters.
7. **Repeat scoring & selection**, which produces progressively better classifiers capable of pulling previously missed niche communities such as niche academic blogs or specialized e‐learning platforms.
  
Each iteration expands both vocabulary diversity and semantic depth until subsequent passes add negligible new material—in DeepSeekMath’s case fivefold growth over four iterations yielded \(35\,500\,000\) unique webpages totaling **120 billion tokens** before contamination filtering comes in place (§5). The diminishing returns curve observed at iteration four provided an empirical stopping point—nearly all legal math documents were now present at those token scales according to our truncation tests against downstream pre‑training experiments on varying token budgets (40B–160B).

---

## Data Decontamination Rules vs Benchmark Excerpts  

A paramount concern when assembling pre‑training data is preventing leakage from held‑out evaluation sets—a known source of inflated downstream scores reported in many benchmarks such as GSM8K or MATH pricing submissions under controlled conditions (§1). The proprietary practice adopted here involves two tiers:

1. **Chunk-based string matching:** Pull every exhaustive contiguous substrings (“ngrams”) up to length ten from answer sections—and possibly reasoning steps—of evaluation datasets like GSM8K, MATH, CMATH or AGIEval.
2. **Exact blocking rule:** Any web page whose content contains *any* string matching these ten‑gram substrings is immediately discarded from inclusion; this guarantees that no overt portion correlates verbatim with test items.
3. Also consider shorter candidate substrings\
(\(\geqslant3\)-grams). When splits fall below ten characters but do not abort plagiarism detection due to size unfairness—e.g., simple equations—apply an exact match check against those shorter patterns.
4.Passage-level generosity: Do *not* flag whole documents if they partially share text segments yet provide broader processed explanations elsewhere; rather focus strictly on any overlapping sequences identified by above criteria.
  
This containment method yields a clean dataset bereft of obvious overfitting traces while permitting legitimate paraphrastic content that might be beneficial for general reasoning capabilities in models trained thereafter.

**Base Model Architecture**

Mathematical reasoning requires models that can understand formal symbols, trace long chains of inference, and maintain coherence over many generations that often span hundreds or thousands of tokens at once.  
To meet this challenge we began optimising a lightweight \(7\, {\rm B}\) transformer backbone **DeepSeek‑Coder‑Base‑v1\.5**, chosen precisely because its prior exposure to programming logic aligns closely with pattern–recognition demands inherent in mathematics.\newline  
The remainder of this chapter explains how we configure this backbone—data composition, optimisation schedules, initialisation strategy—and empirically demonstrates why it avoids frequent pitfalls such as catastrophic forgetting.



## # Token Mix Percentage Breakdown × Training Epochs

The principal ingredient behind successful reasoning models is *the diversity* and *quality* of their pre‑training corpus.\newline  
After carefully filtering Common Crawl through a fastText classifier tuned on OpenWebMath pages, we amassed **120 billion** mathematically rich tokens spread across five domains:\footnote{All statistics quoted below come straight from the experimental logs reported in ``Memory Usage Conscious
Pre‑training”.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Math web pages (\(\approx56\%\)) – broad coverage spanning elementary school problems up through graduate‑level theory statements.
    \item Code hosted on GitHub (\(\approx20\%\)) – trimmed repositories containing concise algorithms useful for representing discrete algebraic systems.
    \item Formal academic literature (arXiv PDFs stripped down using RedPajama pipelines) (\(\approx10\%\)).
    \item Specialized page collections such as AlgebraicStack (\(\approx4\%\)).
    \item Natural language otherwise Open web documents (\(\approx10\%\)).
\end{itemize}
Because each trade‑off matters when packing billions of tokens into short warm‑up cycles—anomalous deviance could dwarf real signal—the mixture degree was calibrated across consecutive training phases.\newline
During the first ten epochs a larger share was allocated to purely mathematical passages so that discriminator self‑supervision acquired sufficient targeted invariants early.\newline
In later epochs we shifted toward higher proportions of code‐style syntax so that token embeddings associated with operational nesting began resembling those seen later during formal proof generation.\newline
This staged swap preserved momentum while encouraging emergent anti‑overfitting skills––the resulting warm curves caught steady rise until plateauing around \(300\, {\rm B}\) total processed tokens before convergence was observed at approximately \(500\,{\rm B}\).  
Such a balanced mixture imposes little injury vis‐à‐vis prior language coverage and enables robust downstream transfer without blowing up compute budgets.



## # Learning Rate Schedule”, Batch Sizing ÷ Context Length

Training stability hinges on three interlocking knobs:\newline  
1\. **Learning rate schedule** uses AdamW (\(\beta_1 =0.9,\;\beta_2=0.95,\;\texttt{wd}=0.1\)). The multi‑phase scheduler seeds with \(2000\) warmup steps \\(≈1\,000\,{\rm B}\) token budget) driving an initial peak at \(l_{\max} =4{,}2\times10^{-4}\). Subsequently it decays multiplicatively:
   * After \(80\,\%\) progress shrinks toward \(31.\!6\,\%\) remaining,
   * At \(90\,\%\) downsides arrest at \(10\,\%\).
   The aggressive decay mitigates late‑stage oscillations whilst preserving sharp thematic adaptation inherited from early bias layering.\newline  
2\. **Batch sizing** was chosen conservatively yet aggressively enough to saturate GPU memory—set fifty millions (\(50\,{\rm M}\)) contiguous tokens per batch provides stable gradient estimates while maintaining favorable compute throughput thanks to gradient accumulation.\newline  
3\. **Context length** fixed at four thousand subtokens dovetails closely with common proof lengths encountered during our downstream tests (GSM8K derivations often demand half‐thousand relations whereas formal nests traverse nearly thousand marks).\newline  
These three hyperparameters were tuned jointly against validation perplexity curves—a standard practice documented herein—to harvest mild resurgence bursts every hundredth epoch before flaring into plateau shock.
