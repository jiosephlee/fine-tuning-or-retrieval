\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Mining Math from the Web

## The problem we’re trying to solve

Mathematical reasoning is one of the hardest tasks for language models. It demands precise symbols, structured logic, and often an ability to compute exactly. The standard way to give a model this kind of knowledge is to pre‑train it on a massive amount of math text – textbook chapters, arXiv papers, Q&A sites – and then fine‑tune it on carefully crafted instruction data. In practice most small‑to‑medium models still lag far behind giants like GPT‑4 or Gemini because they *lack* high‑quality math training data.

DeepSeekMath tackles this bottleneck by building its own gigantic mathematics corpus *from scratch* out of publicly available web data. The result is 120 billion tokens of math‑rich text sourced entirely from Common Crawl; no costly proprietary corpora required.

## Why Common Crawl?

Common Crawl is an open dataset that contains petabytes of web pages collected over many years. It’s not organised as “math papers” or “math forums”, but it does hold millions of pages that talk about formulas, proofs, and numerical problems in dozens of languages.

The question was how to sift through that noise and keep only the pages that are worth learning from. That led to a clean‐pipeline design:

1. **A good seed** – start with OpenWebMath (a small but curated set of math webpages).  
2. **A fast classifier** – train a fastText model on those pages vs random junk from Common Crawl.
3. **Iterative refinement** – repeatedly use the classifier on more crawled data, augment it with new positive examples found by hand‑checking domains (e.g., MathOverflow), re‑train the classifier and grow the collection.
4. **Deduplication & decontamination** – remove near‑duplicates before training; filter out any text that overlaps with official benchmark evaluation sets so the model doesn’t simply memorize answers.

With four iterations they hit 35 million webpages amounting to **120 billion tokens**—about nine times larger than OpenWebMath alone and almost seven times larger than Minerva’s own web‑based maths corpus.

## Cleaning up *benchmark contamination*

Even if you can pull 10^11 tokens of math text, you have to be careful: some crawl files may contain entire test problems verbatim (e.g., GSM8K or MATH questions). The authors ran an exact overlap check against all known benchmark passages:

- Any page containing a 10‑gram exact match with a benchmark snippet was removed.
- Shorter passages (≤ 10 grams) were filtered if they matched at least three grams against any benchmark text.

That guarantees downstream evaluations truly reflect reasoning skills rather than memorised solutions.  

## Validating that the data matters

To confirm that this curated web dump is better than existing math corpora they done head‑to‑head fine‑tuning experiments on a tiny 1.3 B parameter model:

| Corpus | Size | Processor |
|--------|------|----------|
| DeepSeekMath Corpus | ~89 B tokens | FastText + domain filtering |
| Proof-Pile‑2 | 48 B tokens (≈80% arXiv) | Standard multi‑source mix |
| OpenWebMath | 13•6 B tokens | Basic Clean/Hash filtering |

The DeepSeekMath pre‑trained model scored higher across eight different quantitative reasoning benchmarks than its counterparts trained on Proof-Pile or OpenWebMath—even after only one epoch over Proof-Pile—demonstrating higher average quality per token.

Beyond numbers, their tables showed consistent gains in Chinese benchmarks too: because DeepSeekMath’s data contains many multilingual pages (English & Chinese dominate), models improved Not just in English but also in K–12 Chinese problem datasets like CMATH or Gaokao-MathQA—which were found not to benefit much from existing English‑centric corpora.


## Feed it into the chain

Once pre‑training finished (the final *Base* model was trained for ~500 B tokens on 56% DeepSeekMath + other curated sources), they did two more steps:

1. **Instruction tuning** – ~800K questions paired with chain‐of‐thought or program‐of‐thought solutions (English & Chinese).
2. **Reinforcement learning (GRPO)** – a lightweight variant of PPO that replaces its learned value network with group‑relative baseline estimates; this cuts memory usage dramatically while still nudging the policy toward higher rewards given by a headless reward model trained on output comparisons.

With these layers stacked, DeepSeekMath reaches **51.7 % accuracy** on competition‑level MATH—higher than all other open‑source 7 B models—and even exceeds some closed models once self‑consistency is applied.


## Takeaways for researchers

- *Public web data can replace expensive proprietary corpora.*  
If you’re working at scale but don’t have GNOME license holdings or enterprise licenses for massive academic datasets, go see how well fastText can recover clean mathematics content from Common Crawl.
  
- *An iterative feedback loop matters.*  
Instead of a one–shot scrape followed by random pruning, iteratively re–train your selector while injecting hand–verified positives gives you exponential growth without compromising quality.
  
- *Respect evaluation sets.*  
Guarantee your training data does not leak even partial snippets; otherwise benchmarking results are meaningless.
  
- *Treat multilinguality as an asset.*  
When your target applications include non‑English contests—like Chinese Gaokao problems—a diverse language mix can provide an unexpected lift.


### Bottom line

DeepSeekMath shows that carefully mined web pages can construct an economic yet superb mathematical foundation for LLMs—a great headline if you’re hoping for open research alternative no matter how big the token budget vertises or gets weighted by domain specialization.
