### What is the purpose of the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) in Direct Preference Optimization?
Question:
The DPO formulation uses a “reference policy” \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\), which I understand is usually first fine-tuned on high‑quality data (SFT). In practice, why do we need this separate reference distribution when training \(\pi_{\theta}\)? What would happen if we omitted it or set \(\pi_{\text{ref}}
     = \pi_{\theta}\)? How does it relate to the KL‑divergence term that appears in Eq.…?
Answer:
The **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$ is an essential ingredient both for traditional RLHF and for Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).  
Its primary role is threefold:

1\. **Anchoring the optimisation**  
2\. **Normalising the implicit reward**  
3\. **Preventing mode collapse**

---

### Anchoring the optimisation

In vanilla RLHF one maximises  

$$
\max_{\theta}\;
\E_{x,y\sim \pi_\theta}\!\bigl[r(x,y)\bigr]
-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta\,||\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr] ,
$$  

where $r(x,\cdot)$ is a learned reward model and $D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\,.\,,]$ penalises divergence from a *reference* distribution.  
The reference policy is usually an SFT model $\pi^\texttt{sft}$; it represents behaviour “good enough” for the task while staying close enough so that $r(x,\cdot)$ remains reliable.  
If this penalty were removed ($\beta=0$) or replaced by a different, unstable prior, a language model could drift arbitrarily far from the data seen when estimating $r(x,\cdot)$,
leading either to nonsensical generations or degenerate mode–collapse
(single‑high‑reward completions).

### Why setting \(p_{\mathrm{ref}}\!=p_\theta\) kills learning

If one were careless and chose \(p_{\mathrm{ref}=p_\theta}\),
then every local gain vanished:
\(r_\theta(x,y)=0,\;\forall y.\)
Consequentlyall terms inside the logistic function become zero,
and $\underline L_{\mathsf{DPO}}$ reduces simply to a constant value independentof parameters —no gradients flow.

### Bottom line

The reference policy supplies two crucial functions :

* A baseline against which likelihood ratios are measured,
keeping optimisation grounded in well‑behaved language‐model statistics;

* A regulariser equivalent (via formula (*))
to an explicit Kullback–Leibler penalty,
constraining behaviour away from unsuitable regions yet permitting rapid progress toward preferred outputs.

Without it optimisation either stalls ($p_{\mathrm{ref}=p_\theta}$),
or collapses wildly – exactly what observations reported in Section 3 exhibit when either omitting it or discarding its weight.
Setting up an appropriate proposal SFT model beforehand therefore guarantees both stability and fidelity when translating raw human preferences into practical language models through Direct Preference Optimization.'
