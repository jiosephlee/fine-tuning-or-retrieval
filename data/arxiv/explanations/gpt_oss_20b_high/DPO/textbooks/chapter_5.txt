\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

Chapter 5: 3 | Preference Modelling Fundamentals (Bradley‑Terry & Plackett–Luce)

When fine‑tuning large language models with human feedback, it is far easier for annotators to say *“response A is better than response B”* than to give an absolute score to each response independently.  Relative judgments eliminate many of the inconsistencies that arise when different people try to put different pieces of text onto a single numeric scale.  The research community therefore favours *probabilistic choice models* that translate these comparative annotations into a learnable objective for a neural network reward or policy.  Two of the most prominent examples are the Bradley–Terry model and its generalisation, Plackett–Luce.  This chapter develops these probabilistic models from first principles, derives the corresponding maximum‑likelihood objective and explains practical tricks such as reward normalisation and odds‑ratio gradients that are essential for robust training of language‑model fine‑tuning pipelines such as RLHF and its RL‑free successor Direct Preference Optimization.

---

## Bradley–Terry softmax pairwise probability formula

Let a *prompt* \(x\) be presented to a language model and suppose it generates two candidate completions \(y_{1}\) and \(y_{2}\).  Human annotators observe these pairs and preferentially label one over the other: we denote this as \(y_{w}\succ y_{\ell}\mid x\), where \(y_{w}\) is the *winning* completion and \(y_{\ell}\) is the *losing* completion for prompt \(x\).  To predict such preferences from data, Bradley and Terry (1952) proposed modelling a latent real‑valued *reward* function \(r^{*}(x,y)\) attached to each (prompt, completion) pair and defining a probability of one completion winning over another via a logistic link:

\[
p^{*}\!\left(y_{1}\succ y_{2}\mid x\right)
   = \frac{\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)}
          {\exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{1})\bigr)+
           \exp\!\bigl(r^{*}(x,y_{2})\bigr)}.
\tag{3.1}
\]

The likelihood above is sometimes called a *softmax pairwise probability*: if both completions have identical rewards then each side wins with probability \(1/2\); if one exceeds the other by \(\Delta r\), then the winning probability is \(\sigma(\Delta r)\), where \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+\exp(-z))\) denotes the logistic function.

Intuitively, Bradley–Terry assumes that each completion’s latent “quality” contributes an additive exponential term inside a normalising sum.  The ratio of these exponentials captures how much better one answer expresses what humans value compared with another; because human fatigue or personal bias tends to shift all scores up or down by a constant amount, adding any fixed function \(f(x)\) to every reward leaves all pairwise probabilities unchanged—a point discussed comprehensively in reformulation reviews of choice models.

## Maximum likelihood estimation gives an easy negative log loss

Having specified a stochastic preference model, learning proceeds by fitting neural parameters \(\phi\) that instantiate the reward function \(\displaystyle r_{\phi}(x,y)\).  Consider a dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{\ell}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}\) of human preferences sampled according to \(p^{*}\).  For any particular preference triple \((x,y_{w},y_{\ell})\), plugging \(r_{\phi}\) into Equation (3.1) yields

\[
p_{\phi}(y_{w}\succ y_{\ell}\mid x)
     = \sigma\!\bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{\ell})\bigr).
\]

The dataset defines an empirical distribution over such comparisons; under standard maximum‑likelihood principles we maximise
the joint likelihood product over all examples or equivalently minimise its negative logarithm:

\[
\mathcal{L}_{R}(\phi;\mathcal{D})
   = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{\ell})\sim \mathcal{D}}
       \Bigl[\,\log\, 
              \sigma
              \bigl(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{\ell})\,\bigr)
           \Bigr].
   \tag{3.2}
\]

Equation (3.2) takes exactly the identical form as binary cross entropy between two labels (“win” vs “lose”) where the logits are differences of rewards.  Consequently training may be carried out with any standard automatic‐differentiation framework: compute per‑example gradients via backpropagation through both completions’ scores and update \(\phi\) with SGD or Adam optimisers.

Because this objective only depends on *differences* of rewards,
the optimisation problem suffers from an underdetermined transformation: adding a prompt‑specific offset \(f(x)\) shifts every reward while leaving loss values unchanged (see next section).  A convenient signal to disambiguate solutions emerges by imposing an extra regularisation constraint on mean rewards per prompt—this yields more stable training dynamics—which we discuss next.

## Score normalisation safeguards against global shifts

Suppose two functions \(r(x,y)\) and \(r(x,y)+f(x)\) produce identical preference probabilities for every query because shifting all scores by
\(f(x)\) scales every exponent by a constant factor regardless of which completion wins.
In practice this degeneracy leads neural networks across prompts to drift arbitrarily high or low depending on parametrisation or optimiser scheduling,
thereby inflating variance in sampled scores during later policy optimisation stages.
To keep reward estimates comparable across prompts,
researchers normalise them so that their expectation over completions drawn from whatever policy supplies them equals zero:

\[
     \mathbb{E}_{y\bigr\rangle x}
          \bigl[r_{\phi}(x , y )\,|\, x\bigr] = 0
          \quad
     \text{for all } x.
   \tag{3.3}
\]

In practice this is achieved by constructing an auxiliary linear projector atop the language‑model backbone – effectively feeding back into architecture layers downstream – whose output circulates through an activation chosen so that its average value vanishes at convergence.
After training completes this ensures
the learned reward surface has well‐defined absolute levels up to global additive constants which no longer cause instability during subsequent reinforcement handling or direct optimisation.
When used in later stages like PPO in RLHF, normalising rewards also improves sample efficiency because it keeps estimated returns within comfortable dynamic ranges for variance‐reduction baselines.

## Practical odds ratio gradient derivations

To understand how updates move model parameters in practice,
it helps view Equation (3.2)’s gradient.
Let us denote
\(z=\displaystyle r_{\phi}(x , y_{w})- r_{\phi}(x , y_{\ell}) =: s_{w}-s_{\ell}\).
By chain rule,

\[
\nabla_{\phi}\,\log\sigma(z)
     = (1-\sigma(z))\,z'\quad
     =\,(-\,{\sigma(-z)} )\, z'.
   \tag{3.4}
\]

Here \(z'\!=\,0:\partial s_w/\partial \phi-\partial s_\ell/\partial \phi.\)
Because \(1-\sigma(z)=\sigma(-z)\),
the sign of this gradient flips precisely when
the current estimate places more probability mass on affectingly disfavoured completions:
if human prefers \(y_w\), then at convergence \(s_w > s_\ell,\;\sigma(-z)<0.5,\),
hence weights shrink; otherwise (\(s_w<s_\ell,\;\sigma(-z)>0.5/\))
the parameter update aggressively increases `score difference'.
In many empirical implementations this expression reduces to an *odds ratio style update*: 

```
update ← −α · σ(s_ℓ−s_w)
           · ∇_φ log π_θ(y_w|x)
           + α · σ(s_ℓ−s_w)
           · ∇_φ log π_θ(y_ℓ|x ),
```

where α is a learning rate.
Because gradients contain factor σ(sℓ−sw),
they automatically downweight examples already predicted correctly; conversely,
mispredicted pairs dominate learning progress – exactly what one desires in preference optimisation.

The form above mirrors more elaborate policy gradients such as those used in PPO;
however there it typically appears alongside clipped surrogate terms or KL penalties.
In contrast, Direct Preference Optimisation simply embeds them implicitly by treating πθ as both policy **and** reward parametric function,
yielding computationally leaner training without sampling loops while inheriting identical theoretical guarantees re optimality under Kahaye’s constrained maximisation formulation.

### Extensions toward Plackett–Luce

While Bradley–Terry suffices when also annotators supply only binary pairwise comparisons,
many settings naturally produce multiway rankings (“completion A satisfies more criteria than B makes C”).
Plackett–Luce generalises Bradley–Terry by inserting all generated completions into one ranking list:
given set {\(y_1,...,y_k\)} conditional on prompt \(x,\)

```
P(rank=y_i first | x)= exp(r*(x,y_i))
                      / sum_j exp(r*(x,y_j)).
```

Subsequent picks reduce remaining set size until full ranking has formed.
This equivalently studies successive favourite draws without imposing independence assumptions on lower-ranked outcomes.
For learning purposes it yields one binary cross‑entropy loss for every adjacent ordering pair or alternatively one weaker log‑loss over full permutations,
which reduces sample complexity at cost of needing extra pairs per prompt.
Despite algorithmic differences,
the key property – replacement invariance under addition of functions f(x) – continues to hold in Plackett–Luce;
hence normalising rewards per prompt remains equally important,
// but now must be respected across potentially many items per ranking event.

### Takeaway

The power of Bradley–Terry and Plackett–Luce lies not only in their elegant mathematical formulation but also 
in their practical grounding:
they give rise directly to log‑loss objectives familiar from standard neural network optimisation,
they reflect real human engagement (preferring “better” than “worse” rather than rating “something”),  
and they can be converted into weightings controlling how strongly misordered pairs influence learning rates—all making these models indispensable pillars for modern preference-based fine‑tuning pipelines such as RLHF’s committee reward fitting stage or Direct Preference Optimisation’s single-stage policy learning paradigm.’​

4 | Reinforcement Learning With KL Constraint In Language Generation  

When a language model has been trained only on raw text it typically reproduces patterns it has seen many times during pre‑training without heed to why those patterns were useful or harmful.  
Reinforcement‑learning‑from‑human‑feedback (RLHF) tackles this mismatch by asking people which **two** completions are preferable, learning a scoring function \(r^{*}(x,y)\), and then adjusting the model so its outputs receive higher scores while staying close enough to its original behaviour so that it continues to perform well on unrelated follow‑up tasks such as question answering or summarization[^1].  
A practical way to formalise “close enough” is via a Kullback–Leibler divergence penalty against a **reference policy** \(\pi_{\text {ref}}\), usually an instruction‑tuned version \(SFT\) of the base LM [12].  
This leads naturally to an objective that jointly maximises expected reward while limiting deviation from the starting distribution—

**5 | Core Idea – Direct Preference Optimization**

Large language models (LMs) trained purely on text data acquire wide‑range knowledge and some reasoning skills, yet steering their behavior toward human‑desired outputs remains difficult once training has finished. Conventional Alignment methods package this task into a reinforcement‑learning (RL) problem: first fit a *reward model* \(r_\phi(x,y)\) from pairs of human‑judged generations,
then apply an off‑policy actor–critic algorithm (often PPO) to raise the expected reward while staying close—measured through Kullback–Leibler divergence—to the original pre‑trained policy \(\pi_{\mathrm ref}\).  
Despite its empirical success, this pipeline incurs multiple training stages,
sampling cycles over stochastically generated completions and suffers from high variance gradients and delicate hyper‑parameter tuning.

The idea behind **Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO) is conceptually clean:
eliminate the separate “reward→policy” loop entirely and train the LM *directly* from preference data.
DPO achieves this by reexpressing an arbitrary reward function purely in terms of density ratios between the target policy \(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\) and some reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm ref}(\cdot|x)\).
With this change of variables every element of policy optimisation appears already embedded into a simple binary‑cross‑entropy objective.
All elements—density ratio weighting rule, canceling normalisation constants, and an implicit reward definition—can be traced back explicitly from the Bradley–Terry ranking model introduced during preference learning.
The resulting algorithm trains policies efficiently without sampling loops or extra neural networks beyond the LM itself,
yet preserves exactly the theoretical properties granted by standard RLHF formulations.

### Rough recap & practical takeaways

The core insight has three parts that fit together perfectly:

1. **Density‐ratio parameterisation** turns any scalar reward into a surprisal term calculated directly between two policies—no separate neural network needed beyond existing LM weights.
2. When fed into Bradley–Terry preference likelihoods—the dominant statistical model behind most recorded human score tables—the joint normalising factor disappears cleanly.
3. What remains is exactly a *binary cross‑entropy loss over predicted relative rankings*, whose gradients rescale changes depending how much currently misordered preference pairs exist—highly interpretable because it guards against drastic changes when produced ranking conflicts are minor (<0°).

Because everything collapses back into standard supervised training machinery—
no sampling loops, no value functions, no shifting baselines—many researchers who know nothing about modern reinforcement learning frameworks now can start imposing complex human values upon large models at experimental speed very quickly – freeing time normally occupied swallowing comments behind intricate optimisers.

**Chapter 6 ‑ Implementation Pipeline & Practical Choices**

Large‑scale language models are inherently inexpensive when their parameters are trained with vanilla supervised objectives because all gradients flow through a single transformer encoder‑decoder stack while the input‑to‑output mapping is fixed during generation. Direct Preference Optimization follows that same philosophy – it replaces the multi‑phase reinforcement learning culprit by a single computation graph that consumes the human‑“win/lose” annotations already collected during data preparation. Below we detail how one implements this pipeline efficiently out of the box, how you choose an appropriate reference model \( \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), how hyper‑parameters such as \(\beta\) govern exploration versus adherence to the reference policy, and finally why this method is considerably cheaper on GPUs than prevailing PPO‑based RLHF methods.
