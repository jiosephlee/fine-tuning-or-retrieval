Foundational Knowledge  

The last decade has witnessed an unprecedented growth in natural‑language research, driven mainly by highly parameterised generative models known as large‑scale pre‑trained language models (LMs).  Trained exclusively in an unsupervised manner on ever larger corpora, these transformer‑based LMs acquire remarkable breadths of world knowledge and reasoning skills, often displaying performance that rivals or exceeds human abilities on a wide array of benchmark tasks~\citep{chowdhery2022palm,brown2020language,touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}.  While their construction offers enormous promise—allowing systems to adapt quickly through prompting or few‑shot learning—it simultaneously brings latent gaps that arise when such models are harnessed for real‑world applications where safety, correctness, and controllability are paramount.\n\n# Large-Scale Self-Supervised LM Capabilities  

Large‑scale LMs learn by predicting tokens within massive datasets composed largely of unconstrained human text.\nThis pure data‑driven objective equips them with useful linguistic structure, factual grounding, and disparate reasoning procedures~\citep{brown2020language,chowdhery2022palm,touvron2023llama}.  Their transferred knowledge lets them generalise zero‑shot or few‑shot across novel instructions without further supervised signal~\citep{radford2019language,gpt3,megatron}.  However, these strengths emerge only when paired with extensive compute and careful curriculum design during pre‑training—many smaller architectures fail to surface comparable skill sets.\n\nAn essential point raised early in recent literature concerns how an unconstrained objective inevitably embeds “everything it sees.”  Because no external supervision enforces selectivity over desirable behaviours versus undesirable ones, an LLM learns *all* patterns observed in its training data—including brittle or socially problematic behaviours.\n\n# Challenges Posed by Diverse Real‑World Objectives Captured During Pre‑Training  

The core problem lies not merely in scale but in diversity: any projective curation strategy leaves the model exposed to myriad human intents—some beneficial, some harmful.\nConsider coding assistants: while recognising common mistakes aids debugging later stages, one might wish generation itself favor high‑quality code over merely imitating imperfect patterns that dominate training corpora.\nSimilarly a model ought to understand widely held misconceptions among half the population yet should *not* repeat those misconceptions unchecked whenever queried.\nThese dilemmas underline that pre‑training captures *every* communicative goal appropriately weighted only by frequency—a property ill-suited for specialised or safety-critical settings.\n\nMeanwhile researchers have noted that existing open datasets often contain heterogeneous annotations—question answering pairs enriched with subjective flair or news articles laced with defamation flags—all contributing conflicting signals when fed blindly into an optimisation loop~\citep{ouyang2022training}.  Consequently unfiltered downstream outputs risk amplifying undesired biases hidden deep within raw corpora.\n\n# Why Steering Behaviour Toward Desired Intent Matters  

If these artificial intelligences were simply rendered more fluent via data augmentation alone, we would still face crucial gaps:\n1) *Safety*: The sole assurance that a motivated user will not stumble upon dangerous content cannot rely exclusively on smoothed language models.\n2) *Correctness*: Many generated facts depend on noisy correlational patterns rather than genuine inference pathways.\n3) *Controllability*: Users often demand rephasing prompts (e.g., “explain this concept kindly”) which necessitates aligning outputs more directly than autonomously recalling raw preference distributions.\nThus training pipelines increasingly add a dedicated **steerability** phase—sometimes called “instruction‑tuning”—to imbue specific behaviours even when raw pre‑training does not yet encapsulate them fully.\n\nAligning behaviour reduces risks similar to bias disaggregation measured post hoc—in small scale studies it has encompassed improvements in fairness metrics such as demographic parity—for critical applications commercial organisations fear silently ride along already divergent preferences embedded within very wide pre‑trained representations~\citep{ouyang2018lexia}.\n\n# Role Played by Supervised Fine-Tuning Before Reinforcement  

The most widespread modern approach separates alignment into two distinct trainsets:\n1\. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**—LMs initially refined using curated examples labeled with acceptable worst-case answers (\pi^{\text{SFT}}). This step provides two benefits:\na) it introduces task-specific language refinements previously absent from pure generative objectives;\nb) it offers an immediate policy baseline (\pi_\text{ref}) which acts both as constraint and as initial policy for subsequent reinforcement steps.\nThis idea was popularised during early RLHF pipelines—for instance partnership experiments required many iterations of assistant-with-human-workflows already producing plausible dialogue ~\citet{kogel2008}\footnote{This example serves merely illustrative purposes and was included due to explicit usage within pretrained literature}. \newcounter{substem}\usecounter{substem}.\n\n2\. **Reinforcement Learning From Human Feedback** builds directly upon that baseline. After obtaining prompt completion pairs (\pi_\text{ref}) one obtains comparative ballots between competing completions \(y_1,y_2\) labelled \(y_w \succ y_l\mid x\). These samples are fed into a parametric reward network \(r_{\phi}\), conditioned on prefix \(x\), thereby building an estimate \(r^*\) follows Bradley–Terry odds~(\ref{eq:bradley-terry}). Then environmental optimisation maximises expected reward while penalising divergence from \(\pi^{\text{SFT}}\) through a KL regulariser defined mathematically as:

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}\mathbb E_{x,y}\!\left[r_\phi(x,y)\;-\,\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\bigl[\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm {ref}}(\cdot|x)\bigr]\right].
\]

In practice such optimisation requires sampling from the policy at many steps and runs various actor–critic tricks e.g., PPO updates~\citet{sutton2018reinforcement}---a considerably more involved pipeline than pure supervised fine-tuning alone.\nIn contrast primary research now demonstrates a simpler route where one can implicitly optimise both components together—including finding an implicit optimal policy—using only cross entropy over preference pairs (the Direct Preference Optimisation framework detailed later).\n\n--- 

Collectively these four subtopics delineate how continued advances have moved beyond unfettered unsupervised scaling towards structured alignment procedures capturing both task specificity ("supervising") and preference signals through subsequent readjustment ("reinforcement"). They represent foundational knowledge required for deeper investigation into novel methods such as DPO presented across subsequent chapters.

# Data collection pipelines for preference labels  
In reinforcement‑learning–from‑human‑feedback approaches, every query supplied to a language model \(x\) generates one or more candidate completions \(y\). Instead of asking annotators to provide an absolute score set forth they simply indicate which completion among a pair they prefer:
\(y_{w}\succ y_{l}\mid x\), where \(y_{w}\) is labeled “better” and \(y_{l}\) “worse”.  
These paired annotations form a reference‑free corpus
\[
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y_{w}^{(i)},y_{l}^{(i)})\,:\,i=1,\dots,N\}
.\]
The pairwise format has two practical advantages that drive its adoption within large‑scale language‑model alignment pipelines.  
First, rewards trained purely on comparison data instantaneously capture trade–offs between competing objectives inherent in real‑world prompts—such as adhering both to factual correctness and conversational tone—without presupposing an arbitrary scalar target value pertinent for every task at hand.  
Second, generating pairs is algorithmically straightforward once a *reference* source policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) exists. The usual practice is simply sampling \(y^{(i)}_{1},y^{(i)}_{2}\sim\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\,|\,x^{(i)})\), feeding these abstractly constructed completions directly into crowdsourcing platforms, and letting annotators decide through side‑by‑side scrutiny which version best satisfies their intent.\footnote{Here \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) often originates either from an initial supervised–fine‑tuned model \(\pi^{\,\text{SFT}}\) or directly from a large pretrained autoencoder whose sampling statistics match production deployment conditions.*}

# Relative versus absolute feedback paradigms  
Human evaluators typically deliver two main styles of guidance when steering language models: *absolute grading* and *relative comparison*.  

Absolute approaches request annotators assign an individual utility value—such as “score this completion 0–5”—to every utterance independently:
\(u(y\,|\,x)\in[0,\infty)\).
Consequently all subsequent model updates hinge upon employing these scores directly—as targets in regression objectives—or plugging them into policy‑gradient estimators where variance grows dramatically if annotator‐specific scales differ wildly.\footnote{Annotator idiosyncrasies span number‑line slip surfaces whenever aggregated.*}  

In contrast, *relative* paradigms constrain annotators only to judge which member among two alternatives appears superior.
This comparison operation collapses everything beyond binary choice into an equivalence class determined solely by who wins.
