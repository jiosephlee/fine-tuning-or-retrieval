\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

# DPO vs PPO—Why Simple Is Stronger

Language models trained on petabytes of internet text are surprisingly good at many tasks **without** any instruction‑following signal. Yet the same models can produce hallucinations or biased replies when their outputs clash with what humans actually want.  
The industry standard is *Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback* (RLHF): collect human choices over two model responses, learn a *reward* model from these comparisons, then fine‑tune the large language model so that its behavior maximizes this reward while staying close to its starting policy (the so‑called **proximity constraint**).

In practice RLHF is a moving target: it needs an extra neural net for the reward model, it worries about off‑policy samples during rollout, it requires a careful tuning of KL‑penalties or clip ratios, and it spends gigaflop‑hours just re‑sampling from the policy each epoch of training.  

**Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO) is a very different approach that removes the whole RL loop while preserving ‑or even improving ‑ performance on a set of hard practical tasks: controlled sentiment generation, summarization (TL;DR) and single‑turn dialogue (Anthropic’s Helpful & Harmless data).  
Below we walk through why DPO’s simplicity translates into real gains on these benchmarks.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. From “reward” back to “policy”

### A reminder of the usual RL objective

RLHF trains a reference policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}}\) (typically the SFT model) towards an optimal policy \( \pi_{\theta}\) by solving

\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}
\mathbb E_{x,y\sim \pi_\theta}\!\Bigl[
\,r_\phi(x,y)\,-\,\beta\!\bigl(
 \log \pi_\theta(y|x)-\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)
\bigr)\Bigr].
\tag{1}
\]

Here  

* \(r_\phi(x,y)\) is the learned reward function;
* \(\beta>0\) controls how far we allow \(\pi_\theta\) to drift from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

Solving Eq (1) by standard policy gradients requires **sampling** from \(\pi_\theta\) during every update – expensive since each sequence generation can cost about \(10^6\!+\) tokens.



### The closed‑form optimal policy

Equation (1) admits a neat analytic optimum:

\[
\boxed{
\pi_r(y|x)
=
\frac{1}{Z(x)}\, 
\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\;
e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}
}
\tag{2}
\]
where \(Z(x)=\sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\).

If we knew \(r^*(x,y)\), plugging it into Eq (2) would give us the *considered best* policy without any learning loop.



### Rewrite the reward as a log‑ratio

Take logarithms of Eq (2):

\[
r(x,y)
=\beta\, \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}
+\beta\, \log Z(x).
\tag{3}
\]

The second term depends only on \(x\); it does not influence which completion humans would prefer between two candidates.


### From preference data to a cross‑entropy loss

Suppose humans ranked two completions \(y_1 \succ y_2 | x\).  
Under the Bradley–Terry model their probability depends only on the *difference* \(r(x,y_1)-r(x,y_2)\).  
Substituting Eq (3) into that difference eliminates the partition function:

\[
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x)
=
\sigma
   \!\Bigl(
       \beta\, 
       \log
         \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)}
      -
       \beta\, 
       \log
         \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)}
   \Bigr).
   \tag{4}
\]

Thus **the human preference likelihood depends only on two ratios** involving the *optimal* policy \( \pi^*\).

Finally we replace \(p^*\) by our parameterized policy \( \pi_\theta\):

> $\displaystyle
>   \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\,\pi_{\text{ref}})
>   =-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in \mathcal D}\!
>     \bigl[
>       \log\sigma
>          (
>            \beta\, 
>             {\log}\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}
>            -
>            {\log}\!\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}
>          )
>     \bigr].
>     $

This is just binary cross‑entropy applied **to log‑ratios** rather than raw logits.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2. What does this loss actually do?

Take its gradient:

```
∇θ ℒ_DPO
= -β·E_{prefs}
     σ(r̂θ(y_l)-r̂θ(y_w))
       · [ ∇θ log πθ(y_w)
           - ∇θ log πθ(y_l) ] .
```

* The term `σ(r̂θ(y_l)-r̂θ(y_w))` acts as an *importance weight*: if our implicit reward `r̂θ` already ranks `y_w` above `y_l` well (the difference is large and negative), its weight shrinks toward zero—no corrective steps are taken.
* The remaining part is simply “make `πθ` more likely for `y_w`, less likely for `y_l`”.

So each update nudges “good” sentences up and “bad” ones down **proportionally** to how badly our current implicit reward mis‑orders them.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3. Why does no sampling help?

Because all parts of Eq (4)—the two ratios—are computed **once per example** using *deterministic* log‐probabilities from forward passes through both policies (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) fixed from SFT).  
No rollout samples are generated during training, no entropy terms have to be estimated from noisy trajectories, and there is no separate experience buffer.

In contrast:

* PPO must sample N≈20–30 full sequences per prompt so that its clipped objective can see enough stochasticity,
* REINFORCE variants need huge numbers of sampled trajectories,
* Even when a learned reward model exists, every forward pass must still sample tokens so that `rφ(x,y)` can be evaluated online.

## 4. Experimental evidence across three domains

The authors tested DPO against standard approaches—including PPO with various rewards—on four tasks:

| Task | Dataset | Baselines Compared |
|------|---------|--------------------|
| Controlled sentiment | IMDB reviews | Zero‑shot GPT‐J; SFT ; Preferred‑FT ; Unlikelihood ; PPO ; Best–of–N |
| Summarization | Reddit TL;DR | Same + GAN? |
| Dialogue | Anthropic HH (Helpful & Harmless) | Same + State‑of‐the‐Art PPORLHF |

For every task they ran thousands of hyper‑parameter sweeps: β∈[0.05,5] versus target KL∈[3,…12] for PPO and other knobs like temperature (T∈[0…1]) at inference time.



### 4.1 Sentiment control – comparing frontier shapes

The payoff here was easy to quantify because they had access to an oracle sentiment classifier ⇒ true reward function `R_sentiment`.

Plotting **true reward** → **KL divergence** produced a frontier:

* The *PPO frontier* hugged low KL but plateaued early – higher rewards demanded huge KL penalties.
* *DPO*, however—same objective behind-the-scenes—reached far higher rewards while staying close to reference distribution:
  
> *“DPO produces by far the most efficient frontier … highest reward while still achieving low KL.”*

This shows that without any additional variance reduction tricks or meticulously tuned clipping ratios you get superior trade‑offs.



### 4.2 Summarization – TL;DR benchmark

Evaluation used **win rates** against ground truth reference summaries measured by GPT‑4 after confirming strong correlation with real annotators.

Resulting curves:

| Temperature | PSL (SFR?) | Prophet |
|

Actually table omitted here due actual relative numbers but key points were:

* At T=0 (greedy decoding), **DPO** achieved ~61 % win rate versus ~57 % for *PPO*, even when PPO had access to ground‑truth rewards (“PPO‑GT”).
* Even though Best–of–N picked using an expert reward gave ~62 % win rate overall, **DPO matched / surpassed its maximum win rate**, meaning you did not need expensive multi‑sample rescoring at inference time.
* Importantly – ‖temperature sensitivity:* While PPO’s performance collapsed sharply at higher temperatures (>0), leaving you stuck at baseline quality unless you tuned every doc’s T separately, **DPO remained stable across T∈[0…1]**, giving reliable quality under any deterministic decoding strategy.



### 4.3 Dialogue – Anthropic HH dataset

Unlike summarization a *SFT checkpoint was unavailable*: they started from Pythia–2.8B pretrained on OpenAI standards then fine–tuned on “preferred completions” (**preferred‑FT**) solely as supervised learning over winning responses – this gives a decent but sub‑optimal baseline.

Comparisons:

* **Preferred-FT alone** barely beat vanilla Pythia –
  ⇒ starting point defines ceiling.
* **Best–of–128**, sampling many completions from preferred-FT then picking one scored by an oracle reward—a heavy computation expense but strong baseline achieving ~63 % win rate under GPT‐4 evaluation.
* **PPO**, trained online using its own learned reward, reached about ~58 % win rate under optimal temperature (~0). Even reusing refit rewards didn’t reach Best–of–128 levels while costing similar compute per epoch as training itself.
* **DPO**, trained with β≈0.5 without any extra tuning or sampled rollouts fixed all parameters once:
  ≈55−58 % win rate depending on temperature,
  matching or outperforming best-of-128 despite being <10× cheaper per epoch,
  and importantly being considerably faster overall because there was no need for repeated sequence sampling during training.

## 5­ Compute savings & ease

When you remove all sampling:

| Stage                | Conventional cost      | With DPO |
|----------------------|------------------------|----------|
| Reward modelling    | Train RM ⟶ separate net | Happens internally via swept logits |
| Policy gradient     | Sample N≤30 seqs/step @ O(tokens × N × batchsize) | One forward pass per example |
| Hyper­parameters    | Tweaks ε_tuning / KL control per environment | Single β knob |
| Training wall‑time  | Hours → days           | Minutes → hours |

In practice all experiments ran within an hour on A6000 GPUs whereas analogous PPO runs required weeks at scale.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 6 ­ Why does this simplicity matter?

A few intertwined reasons make “dumb” simple optimisation winners here:

1. **Closed form transfer helmet:** By reparametrising rewards in terms of policies you never step outside deterministic logit space—you just feed ordinary cross‐entropy losses into familiar backprop chains.
2. **Self‐aversion weighting:** The importance weight automatically dampens updates where your current implicit modeling already has correct ordering—an elegant way that replaces many engineering tricks such as advantage normalisation or value baselines often needed in actor–critic algorithms.
3⁀ Less noisy gradients → more stable convergence even when β stays fixed across corpora units—a knob normally fine­-tuned finely for each new task when using PPO.


--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 7 ­ Takeaways & future directions

This work shows that simple is often strongly preferable when aligning language models via humans ratings:

* **Same objective → fewer degrees of freedom → strongly lower variance training**
    * No rollouts → no extra compute → less risk of drifting into mode collapse
    * Directly optimises human preference probabilities via cross entropy
    * Requires fewer manual hyper­parameter sweeps—even one away-from-zero β often works universally
* Empirical gains across multiple downstream tasks:
    * Controlled contentful generation (sentiment)
    * Fact accurate summarisation
    * Conversational generality

The authors note open questions remain:
 • How do DPO policies generalise out-of-distribution compared with explicit rewards?
 • Can self‑labeling from an already trained DPO help expand unlabeled prompt sets?
 • Do larger generative models amplify any subtle difference between perfect & poorly regularised optimisation?

But even before scaling further you already have an RTL path toward fine-tuning large LMs purely from binary choice datasets—no value network needed, no bandit buffering needed—and do it with roughly one fifth the compute cost typical of RLHF while keeping accuracy competitive or better.

### Quick Glimpse at User Code

Here’s what training a toy GPT has in mind if you choose DPO instead of PPO:

```python
# PyTorch pseudo code fragment — very lightweight
for step in range(num_steps):
    # assume pref_dataset provides tuples (prompt,x,w,l)
    probs_ref = ref_model.logits(prompt,x,w,l).exp()   # >-> reference pmf
    probs_hat = tgt_model.logits(prompt,x,w,l).exp()   # >-> current pmf
    
    # Prepare log-ratio terms
    wa = torch.log(probs_hat[..., w] / probs_ref[..., w])
    la = torch.log(probs_hat[..., l] / probs_ref[..., l])
    
    diff = beta*(la - wa)
    loss   = -torch.mean(F.logsigmoid(-diff))   # binary cross entropy
    
    optimizer.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
```

No sampling inside loops — just one forward pass per example!
