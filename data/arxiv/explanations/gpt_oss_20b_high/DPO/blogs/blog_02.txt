# Direct Preference Optimization Made Simple

Large‑scale language models are astonishingly good at recalling facts and following prompts,
but getting them *exactly* right for every task often requires an extra round of fine‑tuning.
The most successful industry recipe has been **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback** (RLHF).
RLHF first trains a *reward model* on pairs of completions annotated by people,
then runs an expensive reinforcement‑learning loop on top of an already‑fine‑tuned language model
to maximize that learned reward while keeping it close enough to its pre‑training distribution.
The result is powerful, yet each stage adds complexity, hyper‑parameters and run‑time cost:
training separate networks, sampling thousands of outputs per prompt,
and careful tuning of trust‑region widths or clipping parameters,
all while keeping an eye out for mode collapse or runaway generation quality loss.



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
## What Direct Preference Optimization Changes

The crux behind Direct Preference Optimization (**DPO**) is a clean algebraic trick:
the *optimal* policy for a KL‑constrained reward maximisation problem can be written right
hand side in terms of itself.
Once you know how the policy would look *if* you had perfect knowledge
of every possible clue from humans,
you can reverse‑engineer the loss you need to train directly *this* policy—no separate reward network,
no sampler loop.



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
### The classic RLHF recipe

1. **SFT** – fine‑tune a huge LM on hand‑crafted next‑token data  
   \(p_{\text {SFT}}(y | x)\).

2. **Reward modelling** – generate two completions \(y_1,y_2\sim p_{\text {SFT}}\),
   let humans choose which one looks better \(\, y_1 \succ y_2 | x\,.\)
   Fit a neural network \(r_\phi(x,y)\) under the Bradley–Terry likelihood  

   \[
   p^*(y_1\succ y_2 | x) = 
   {\displaystyle 
      {\exp(r^*(x,y_1))}\over {\exp(r^*(x,y_1))+\exp(r^*(x,y_2))}} .
   \]

3. **RL optimization** – maximize  

   \[
   {\mathbb E}_{p_{\rm ref}}\!\bigl[
         r_\phi(x,y)-\beta\,D_{\rm KL}\bigl[p_\theta(\cdot | x)\,\bigl|\bigl|\,
         p_{\rm ref}(\cdot | x)\bigr]\bigr].
   \]

   Here \(p_{\rm ref}\) is usually the SFT policy,
   and \(p_\theta\) starts near it and gradually shifts toward higher rewarded texts.



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
### Closed‑form optima bend everything back around

If you solve this constrained optimisation analytically
(one step calculus w/o sampling!) you obtain

> **Optimal Policy:**  
> 
> \(p_{r}(y | x)=
>    {1\over Z(x)}
>    p_{\rm ref}(y | x)\;
>    e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}\),

> where \(Z(x)=\sum_{y}p_{\rm ref}(y | x)e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta}\).

Take logs,

> \(r(x,y)=
>    \beta\,\log {p_{r}(y | x)\over p_{\rm ref}(y | x)}
>    + \beta\,\log Z(x).\)

The second term depends only on \(x,\!Z(\cdot)\);
when inserted in the Bradley–Terry probability it drops out!
Consequently the *human preference distribution depends solely on the optimal policy*

```
Pr*(that y₁ beats y₂ | x) =
      σ(
           β · log [ p*(y₂ | x)/p_ref(y₂ | x)]
           ‑
           β · log [ p*(y₁ | x)/p_ref(y₁ | x)]
        )
```

with σ being the logistic function.



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
#### Turning it into a simple loss

We replace “the unknown optimal policy” by our neural LMs’ θ‐parameters:
\(p_\theta(y|x)\) now plays both IP roles—policy *and* implicit reward.
Minimise across comparison triplets \( (x , y_w , y_l)\)

```
L_DPO(θ)
= − E_{triplets} [
        log σ(
            β · log(pθ(y_w|x)/pref_ref(y_w|x))
            −β · log(pθ(y_l|x)/pref_ref(y_l|x))
          )
      ] .
```

In words:

* For every human–chosen “win/lose” pair  
  increase the likelihood mass on `win` relative to `lose`;  
* The magnitude adjustment comes from a weight proportional  
  (`β`) ideas measure how badly our current model orders those two samples—  
 calls forward enough rallies that it fixes boundary cases where naive unlikelihood collapses.


This is nothing more than a **binary cross‑entropy** loss,
except each sample carries its own weight fixed online during training.


-------------------------------------------------------------------  
### Why does it work?

The cost contains two subtle yet crucial components:

* **KL regularisation** derives automatically from contrastive learning ‑ your moves are penalised if you drift far from `p_ref`.  
* **Dynamic importance weighting** prevents extreme “governed rewards”: 
if our current implicit reward already puts `win` ahead properly,
the term σ(...) will clamp close to zero and stop further moves—
this mitigates early mode collapse observed in plain unlikelihood tricks.


The method aggressively mirrors what PPO optimisation does *in closed form*: optimize expected rewards under a soft divergence penalty uploaded en masse over all tokens simultaneously–no sampling needed.



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
### Theoretical guarantees?

Two observations protect validity:

1️⃣ **Reward invariance:** Every truly different human preference signal corresponds either
to adding just a constant per prefix \(f(x)\) or stacking equivalent rewards  
that share identical pairwise ordering.
Because any two such rewards induce identical equations above,
our logs-ratio representation preserves all their “meanings”.

2️⃣ **Completeness theorem:** For any class-equivalent reward function
there exists some policy π such that  

\( r(x,y)=β·ln[ π(y|x)/π_{ref}(y|x)] + const , ∃ π . \)

Thus DPO never loses expressiveness—
it merely reexpresses existing solutions with fewer moving parts.

## Practical Checklist for Running DPO  

```markdown
• Build your human precedent set:
    • If you have an SFT model conditioned on prompts → generate <prompt,x>, two candidates ← sample once;
    • Ask annotators which completion is preferred → get triples {(prompts,x),win,lost} ;
• Pick a reference distribution `p_ref`.
    • Often just set it equal to your SFT LM.
    • If none exists train `p_ref` simply through supervised learning on all *winning*
      completions (`σ=+∞` case).
• Tune one hyperparam:
    • β controls how hard you allow deviation from `p_ref`.
      Typical range 0–5 works across tasks;
      less than ~0 causes under-exploration,
      bigger values risk collapse.
• Optimise θ by stochastic gradient descent using:
      L_DPO = −E[log σ(
           β·log(pθ(win)/pref_ref(win))−β·log(pθ(lost)/pref_ref(lost))
      )]
• Output probabilities change automatically with temperature at test time;
    there’s no separate rescaling phase.
```

Because every term already sits inside an expectation over real human labels,
you never handle noisy rewards directly.
All uncertainty gets smoothed out during optimisation—neither sampling errors nor value-function baselines are needed.



-------------------------------------------------------------------  
## Why This Matters

* **Simplicity** – single backward pass per comparison pair;
no auxiliary networks, no imaginary trajectories,...

* **Stability** – internal KL penalty guarantees generators stay close enough so replay queues stay realistic;

* **Speed** – off‑the‐shelf PyTorch/accelerator ops suffice;
training times match supervised fine‑tuning orders cheaper;

* **Practical performance** – diverse tasks see comparable or superior *win rates*
Compared against traditionally heavier PPO pipelines with learned rewards.
