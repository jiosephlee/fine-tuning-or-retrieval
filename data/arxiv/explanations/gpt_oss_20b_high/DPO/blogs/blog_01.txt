# Unpacking RLHF  
## Why Fine‑Tuning Large Language Models With Human Feedback Is Hard  

Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT‑3, PaLM‑2 or LLaMA have become a de‑facto backbone for modern AI applications. They are trained “by themselves” on terabytes of internet text – a process known as **unsupervised language modelling** – simply by predicting the next token in every sentence they read:
\[
p_{\theta}(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x)
\]
with \(y\) denoting a completion and \(x\) a prefix (or “prompt”).  
During those billions of updates the model learns world knowledge, math tricks and even rudimentary reasoning skills because these patterns exist everywhere in its data set.  

However, since it has never been told **what behaviours are good or bad**, an LLM can repeat misinformation, converse aggressively or even generate dangerous instructions simply because they appear somewhere in its corpus.  
This disconnect forces developers towards a second optimisation hook called **Reinforcement Learning From Human Feedback (RLHF)** – a pipeline that tries to bend an otherwise wild system into something useful while staying safe.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 1. What Does “Aligning” an LLM Mean?

When a user sees an answer produced by a well‑trained model it probably shouldn’t feel random noise either:
the user expects **helpful** answers,
**safe** explanations,
and responses that match their intent 
rather than just regurgitating prompts found online.
To enforce this behaviour we must expose the model roughly three time‑zones:

| Time‑zone | What happens | Why it matters |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| **0° — Pre‑training** | Purely self–supervised next‑token regression | Gives abundance of factual/statistical knowledge |
| **90° — Instruction Finetune** | Supervision on task demonstrations (`input → desired output`) | Shapes basic behaviour patterns |
| **180° — Reward+RL Stage** | Current LLM produces two completions → humans decide which one looks better → convert this preference into a mathematical signal → back‑propagate through parameters | Tweaks low‑probability answer space toward what humans actually prefer |

The second slot already adds extra labour – crowdsourcing demonstrations or chat logs.
The third slot is where things explode.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### 2. “Step 3” Laughingly Gets Very Complicated  

Below is a bare bones copy‑paste of the usual three‑step procedure that most papers follow:

| Step | Goal | Why it’s technically tricky |
|------|------|-----------------------------|
| **SFT** (`π^{SFT}`) | Teach language generation basics through ordinary supervised learning | Needs millions of labelled exchanges |
| **Reward Modelling** (`r_\phi`) | Estimate unseen real–world score `r*(x,y)` from human pairwise likes `y_w ≻ y_l` | Uses statistical ranking (`Bradley–Terry`)—the estimator competes against noisy sparse labels |
| **RL Fine–tuning** (`π_θ`) | Make `π_θ` favour high rewards while staying close to `π^{SFT}` | Involves hard discrete gradients + heavy sampling |

Let’s dive deeper into where exactly every corner hits.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

#### a) Gather Human Preferences Over Relative Quality  

For every prompt `x` we let `π^{SFT}` generate two continuations `y₁`, `y₂`.  
A crowdworker votes which one looks better;
the outcome becomes `(x , y_w , y_l)` defining “weak preferences”.  
These labels are expressive yet economical—workers only choose one side rather than give absolute scores.



#### b) Train a Reward Model With Bradley–Terry  

A continuous score network $r_\phi(x,y)$ receives `(prompt + completion)` pairs,
and we force it into agreeing with human votes:
\[
p^\ast(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)\;\stackrel{!}{=}\;
\frac{\exp(r_\phi(x,y_1))}
     {\exp(r_\phi(x,y_1))+\exp(r_\phi(x,y_2))}.
\]
Optimising this likelihood turns into minimizing
a standard binary cross entropy:
\[
\mathcal{L}_R(\phi)
= - \mathbb{E}_{(x,w,l)}
      \bigl[\log\sigma(
          r_\phi(x,w)-r_\phi(x,l))\bigr].
\tag{★}
\]
Even after this seemingly simple loss,
the learned $r_\phi$ frequently oscillates,
has skewed norm ranges,
and remains highly biased toward regions heavily sampled during rollout.



#### c) Turn Rewards Into Actions With Reinforcement Learning  

Once $r_\phi$ claims similarity with hidden human taste,
the actual optimisation problem reads:
\[
\max_{\pi_\theta}
      \;\mathbb{E}_{x}\Bigl[
        \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\,|\,x)} 
            r_{\phi}(x,y)
          \Bigr]
      \;-\;\beta\, 
        \mathcal{D}_{KL}\!\bigl(
            \pi_\theta(\,\cdot\,|\,x)\,\big|\big|\,
            \pi^{SFT}(\,\cdot\,|\,x)\bigr).
      \tag{☆}
\]
The first term pushes diverse useful replies;
the second keeps us tethered near our reliable base model.
But two facts conspire against us:

* **Non-differentiability:** Generating token sequences is basically drawing samples.
  Gradient estimates come via *policy gradients* such as REINFORCE.
  Their variance explodes because each sequence outcome dominates numerically distinct tokens.
* **Sample-Prolific Cost:** Every gradient update needs fresh rollouts from $\pi_{\theta}$—
  thousands of full sentences per batch,
  multiplied across several update epochs,
  pushing GPU hours far beyond what was feasible during early LLM research.

### Bow On                        ‑―•  
“### What Makes This Pipeline Tricky?”

Even building a single agent does double work compared with ordinary supervised learning:

```text
   Pretrain → SFT → RewardModel → Reversible 🎭 →
      PolicyGradient ← heavily sampled ← loss shot noise ↑ #
```

Some headlines underlining these obstacles emerge repeatedly from experiments:

1. **Hyperparameter Housekeeping**
   
   • $\beta$ controls how conservative $\pi_{\theta}$ may move.
     
   • Clip ratio $\epsilon$, value network coefficients—all have dramatic effect on bias/variance balance.
     
   • Nothing straightforward tells us what range works before iterations have already ruined earlier checkpoints.


2. **Reward Estimation Noise**
   
   • Human disagreement forms huge random noise which propagates unfiltered through $r_{\phi}$.
     
   • If $r_{\phi}$ overshoots tangentially low-quality replies get inflated scores—RL then drives $\pi_{\theta}$ into degenerate regimes.


3. **Computational Explosion**

   • Token generation costs scales linearly with hidden dimensionality—larger models mean almost linear scaling surged together with memory footprints.
     
   • Even state‐of‐the‐art VRAM tech needs dozens MB per token when batching deep sequence rollouts.
     
   • In practice yes’ great results came after thousands ✰ hours per experiment paraphrasing small text snippets.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Take Home Point  

The unexpected order-of-magnitude jump between building an unsupervised LM 🎒↦ practical command-and-control AI 🎯 demands collected human judgments + inference tricks alongside re-parametrising discrete probability landscapes —
isn't just tedious engineering—it’s fundamental algorithmic gymnastics that seriously limits how many folks can experiment safely.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### A Glimmer Of Fresh Air : Direct Preference Optimization 

A recent line of research sidesteps those hand‑crafted dual­loops entirely—
by proving mathematically that some specific choice of “reward parametrisation” lets us solve equation (**☆**) immediately without any discrete sampling at all.\*
Instead you simply push an LM’s own probabilities through an easily computed log‐ratio,
then train it under exactly the same convenient binary cross‑entropy loss beloved by brand‐new trainers!!

Key ingredient:

```
Natural Parameter ↔ πθ ≈ exp(log πθ − log πref)/Z[x]       (* prior†)
p*(preferences ｜πθ ) ∝ σ(β[log(πθ/πref)(y_w)-log(πθ/πref)(y_l)] )
```

This amounts to treating every transformer parameter *both* as language generator AND implicit reward scorer—no separate network grows any weight bars!  
And because no further samples are required during fine tuning,
CUDA hours dip dramatically while keeping win rates comparable—or occasionally superior—to classic PPO baselines across sentiment control, summarisation & single‑turn dialogues.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
##### A Bullet List Summarising GADGETS OF THE STELLAR BUT HARD PIPELINE  

* unsupervised LM pre‑train → endless noise capture  
* explainable Finetune (instruction matching) → near‐generic capability   
* worker‐labelled pairwise preferences → cheap yet rough score signal   
* Bradley–Terry logistic loss → rewards mired with bias & scale issues   
* KL-constrained RP formula ⇒ requires iterative optimisation over huge discrete sequences  
* Policy gradients ⇒ gigantic variance hurdles & heavy GPU cycles  
* Hyperparameters ($β$, clip ratios…) act like levers unseen until several epochs pass  

Through all of this researchers repeatedly discover similar trade–offs:
wishful win rates ↔ CPU dust bowls ↔ chasing stable baselines again–again.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

**Bottom line:**  
Human preferences add amazing familial nuance ambitionally buried beneath layers already heavy enough—why did aligning get so hard? Because it forces us deeper down quantized probability trenches where gradients vanish unless carefully reweighted—and all while watering down imperceptible false signals just before storing them back into awe‑some base models. But knowing why matters—when your goal shifts towards “easy buildable alignment”, you now spot precisely where simplifications like Direct Preference Optimisation fit your workflow without chasing unseen knobs all day long.
