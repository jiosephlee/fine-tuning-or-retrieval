### What Is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) At Its Core?
Question:
I understand that traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback trains an independent reward model followed by policy optimisation with an RL algorithm such as PPO. In contrast the authors claim that DPO optimises a language‑model policy directly using only a binary cross‑entropy loss over human preference pairs. Can someone explain intuitively why this works – why isn’t an explicit reward worth learning needed?
Answer:
**Short answer**  
DPO replaces the two‑step “learn a reward → then act” cycle with one analytic mapping from any *reward* that explains the observed preferences to its *optimal* policy. Once we know such a closed‑form mapping, we can replace “reward‑model training” with a single binary‑cross‑entropy loss that directly pushes a language‑model’s probabilities so that the preferred token sequences become more likely than the dis‑preferred ones, while keeping the model close to its reference distribution through an implicit KL term. Because this mapping already accounts for the KL constraint, we never need an independent reward network or any reinforcement–learning sampling loop.



---------------------------------------------------------------------



### 1. What does RLHF actually do?



In standard reinforcement‑learning–from‑human‐feedback (RLHF)

1. **Reward model** \(r_{\phi}(x,y)\) is fitted from comparison data  
   $
      p^*(y_1\succ y_2 | x)=
      \sigma(r_{\phi}(x,y_1)-r_{\phi}(x,y_2))
   $
   where $\sigma$ is the logistic function (Bradley–Terry).

2. **Policy** $\displaystyle {\rm PI}_{{}_{θ}}\!(·|x)$ starts at an SFT model ${}^{𝜋}_{𝑅𝑒𝑓}$.
   It is fine‑tuned with a *constrained* objective
   $
      {\rm max}_{π_{θ}}
            {\mathbb E}_{y∼π_{θ}}\!
            [
               r_{𝜙}(𝑥,𝑦)
               -
               β~ {\rm KL}[π_{θ}\Vert π_{𝑅𝑒𝑓}]
            ] .
   $
   Because decoding is discrete, this is usually optimized by PPO or REINFORCE.

#### Why ML philosophy makes classic actor–critic fragile


1\. **High‑variance return estimates**  

Reinforcement learning samples discrete completions from $\displaystyle (\boldsymbol y|\mathbf x)$;
estimating $r_\phi(x,\mathbf y)$ after every sample gives huge Monte Carlo noise.
Actor–critic methods latch onto this noise,
constantly mis­profiling advantages—especially when coupled with large language models whose output space grows exponentially with prompt length.
When the stance flips suddenly,
PPO’s clipping step might either do nothing (gradient vanished) or take an overly greedy step toward a single high‑reward regime (*mode collapse*).
