\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

Chapter 4: Limitations of Direct Orthonormal Updates  

The Orthogonal Finetuning framework was introduced as an elegant way to modify pretrained weights while rigorously preserving key geometric properties: namely, pairwise angular relations among neurons inside each layer remain invariant once an orthogonal transformation \( \boldsymbol R \in \mathbb R^{\,d\times d}\) multiplies the frozen weight matrix \(W^{0}\). Although conceptually appealing, simply learning an arbitrary dense orthogonal matrix turns out prohibitively expensive when the hidden dimension \(d\) assumes realistic magnitudes found in today’s foundation models.\n\n## Full dense orthonormal matrix cost ≈ \(d^{2}\)\nOrthogonal matrices form a curved manifold embedded in Euclidean space whose intrinsic dimensionality equals one half the square minus lower‐order terms—the simplest way to see this is through Cayley’s parametrisation. Writing
\[
   R=\bigl(I+Q\bigr)\bigl(I-Q\bigr)^{-1},
\]
with skew–symmetric \(Q=-Q^{\top}\), shows that every independent entry in \(Q\) becomes part of the optimisation target while maintaining strict orthogonality during training. A skew–symmetric \(d\times d\) matrix carries precisely
\( \tfrac12\,d(d{-}1)\)
free real‐valued degrees of freedom—two entries per off–diagonal couple are tied together by symmetry leaving one arbitrary value per pair.\n\nFrom an implementation standpoint this means every linear layer must store roughly half a million independent scalars when its hidden size reaches one thousand (since \(\tfrac12\,1000(999)\approx5\times10^{5}\)). Deep foundations grow beyond these sizes very quickly—in systems such as GPT‑3 whose parameter count attains rival terabyte scales—even when only a thin slice corresponding to one transformation needs temporary storage during optimisation.” This quadratic blow‐up creates three major problems:\n\n* **Memory pressure** – Storing megabytes or gigabytes worth of coefficients for each layer multiplies cache misses and causes frequent off‑chip traffic.\n* **Learning instability** – Optimising millions of tightly coupled variables without significant regularisation magnifies sensitivity towards learning rates.\n* **Cost over honorific parallelism** – GPUs rely on batched integerial multiplications whose throughput drops swiftly when custom tensor shapes become irregular.\n\nBecause every element of \(R\) influences *every* input dimension through left multiplication—no separating structure limits routing—directly inheriting all those free variables implies an inability to concisely encode proximal geometric constraints while still having enough expressive weight directions.\n\n## Block‑diagonal scheme squeezes parameters →≈ \(b\,d\)\nTo tame this blow‑up OFT introduced a sparsity pattern preserving only intra‑group rotations:\[
   R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},\,R_{2},\,\dots ,\,R_{r}),
   \qquad
   R_{i}\in {\mathbb R}^{\,b\times b}, 
   \quad br=d.
\]
Each small orthogonal block seeks its own local rotation – distinct plans suffice when models contain cheap repetitions—but now their collective parameter budget collapses dramatically:\footnote{\( \tfrac12\,r\,b(b{-}1)=
      \tfrac12\,(\!{\textstyle\b}/(\!{\textstyle\b}))\,(\!{\textstyle\b}-{\textstyle\b})\,d
    =O(\![\!{\textstyle\b}]\,[\!{\textstyle d}])\)}  
The factorized form cuts the number of tunables roughly in proportion to the chosen block size \(b:\;\sim\dots\dots\!\approx b\,d.\)

**Intuitively why this helps**  
The Cayley construction remains usable inside each block because every little rotational field inherits local validity guarantees—the question restates as “fixing attention head doesn’t lead into contradictory orientations across blocks”.  
When optimization proceeds layerly across transformers, every group rotates among itself while remaining silent regarding channels belonging outside its scope—this sparseness curbs overfitting simply because fewer hand‑shakes occur between distant neurons.\n\nHowever, this stratification also bears severe inductive restrictions invisible at first glance:\n\n* **Zero inter‑group communication** – The model never learns transformations coupling attributes situated in distinct index bands. If two signal streams residing side by side potentially interfere beneficially (for instance cross‑token attention patterns or shared positional information), their mixability is irremediably blocked.\n* **Arbitrary partitioning** – Determining proper cardinalities relies solely on numeric granularity rather than feature semantics; dividing vector coordinates purely by their ordinal positions does not reflect functional homogeneity often observed across layers.\n* **Lossy representational reach** – Classic linear maps like discrete Fourier transforms require mixing among arbitrarily distant coordinates (\(j'\)-th component interacts simultaneously with all others). A strict banded skeleton such as
\(x_{i+q(k)}=x_{i}+x_{i+q'}\)
fails precisely at those points where information relocation spans across larger strides.\n\nTo illustrate concretely one cost comes visible even when modelling synthetic unitary behaviour such as fast radial scalings or circulant shifts—all impossible under rigid band organisation yet naturally expressible within waiting well‑known structured families that embody long–range connections.\n\nConsequently while naïve dense rotations strike a mathematically clean baseline mere friends expecting enormous capacity—and correspondingly hazardous overfitting—a middle ground predicated through block–diagonality gives reasonable speedups yet cannot emulate many powerful global transformations any longer simply because its topology prohibits essential edge propagation.\n\nIn short, **the sheer density requirement renders standard OFT impractical on real foundation models**, whereas contracting it into unnaturally engineered spacing sacrifices important expressive directions—a tension at which subsequent contributions propose clever sparse factorizations allowing low‐parameter yet globally rich bijections.”
