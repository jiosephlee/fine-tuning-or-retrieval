# Can We Keep What We Learned And Still Adapt?  
### The Power Of Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning

Adapting gigantic foundation models without retraining them from scratch is now the only practical path forward in AI research—and doing so **while preserving what they have already learned** turns out to be an elegant geometry problem.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why existing “small‑change” tricks hit their limits

The most common way of finetuning a pre‑trained model is simply *slight* weight updates:
a tiny learning rate keeps the new weights close to the originals so that pre‑training knowledge isn't shattered.
While convenient, this approach offers little guarantees on *how* the knowledge stays intact – it just *happens* that the changes stay small enough.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Geometry–preserving adaptation

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), introduced by Qiu et al., reframes the problem:  

Instead of letting each weight float freely,
**we purposely multiply every neuron in a layer by a shared orthogonal matrix** \(\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\):

\[
     \bm{z}
     \;\gets\;
     \bigl((\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{0})^{\!T}\bigr)\,\bm{x},
     \qquad
     \bm{R}^{\,T}\bm{R}=\bm{I}.
\]

Because \(\bm{R}\) preserves pairwise inner products,
the *angles* between neuronal representations are kept exactly as before—exactly what keeps semantic knowledge alive during adaptation.



### Two key invariants

| Invariant | Why it matters |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Pairwise angles** | Triples of neurons retain their relative orientation—important for class boundaries & semantic structure |
| **Spectral norm** | \(W^{0}=U\Sigma V^{T}\Rightarrow RW^{0}=RU\Sigma V^{T}\). The largest singular value stays unchanged → training remains stable |

These properties give OFT stronger guarantees than generic low‑rank updates such as LoRA.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Computing an orthogonal matrix without toy assumptions

The original OFT made \(R\) *block–diagonal*, so each block was small Chebyshev-style rotation
(\(b\times b\)).  
This saved parameters (\(\mathcal O(bd)\)) but imposed two hard constraints:

1. **Limited expressiveness** – you cannot capture interactions that cross block boundaries
   or emulate classic transforms like Fourier or Hadamard.
2. **Hidden inductive bias** – forcing neuron dimensions into fixed groups can hurt downstream performance.


The big question thus arose  
*How can we build a truly dense \(R\) while keeping the number of learnable parameters low?*



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Information transmission view – pictures help

Imagine we want a dense \(d\times d\) matrix \(R = B_m B_{m-1}\dots B_1\).
We can view each intermediate multiplication as an *information flow over a grid graph*:
nodes in level \(i\) communicate only along edges dictated by \(B_i\).
If these edges connect every source node reliably to every target node,
the overall product becomes dense even though every single \(B_i\) may be sparse.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Enter the butterfly – fast transformations meet sparsity

Inspired by Cooley–Tukey’s FFT,
the **butterfly graph** merges information in perfectly balanced stages:

```
Level 0 → Stage 1 → … → Stage m
```

Each stage consists mainly of tiny \(2\times2\) rotations packed into a larger block,
so:

* every stage has only \(O(d)\) free parameters,
* after \(\lceil \log_2 d\rceil\) stages you get a **dense** transformation,
* total learnable parameters shrink dramatically to about \(O(d\,\log d)\).

In formulas, let  

\(B^F(k)=
   \begin{bmatrix}
      \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_1)&\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_2)\\[3pt]
      \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_3)&\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{d}_4)
   \end{bmatrix}
   ,\quad 
   B_d = \tilde B_{(d,d)}\,B_{\frac d2}\otimes I .
\)

By ensuring every tiny
\(2\times2\) block is an orthogonal rotation via Cayley’s transform
\(B=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}\),
the product \(R(m,b)=
   \prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde B^b_{(d,i)} '
renders an overall **orthogonal** matrix constructed purely from sparse pieces.



### BOFT – Generalising OFT with butterflies

| Variant | What it reduces to |
|---------|-------------------|
| \(m=1,\ b=d\) | Original OFT |
| \(m=1,\ b=\text{block size}\)| Block‑diagonal OFT |
| \(m=\log_2 d,\ b=2\)| Full dense \(R\) from few butterfly components |

Thus **BOFT subsumes all previous forms**, keeping their theoretical guarantees while dramatically cutting down learnable weights.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why does this help?

### 1️⃣ Structural expressiveness vs parsimony  
Empirically BOFT approximates random dense orthogonals with far fewer parameters than block‑diagonal OFT—
experimentally plotted error curves confirm its superior efficiency for comparable budgets.



### 2️⃣ Naturally induced inductive bias  
Butterfly patterns resemble many classical fast transforms that have proven useful across modalities.
Thus BOFT ends up learning richer yet structured transformations than generic low–rank tricks;
this hidden bias manifests as better generalisation on audiences that span computer vision, natural language processing, and even diffusion-based image generation.



### 3️⃣ Same runtime cost savings but improved tuning stability  
Because weight updates stay strictly orthogonal:

* Spectral norms don’t blow up or collapse;
* Training does not suffer sudden gradient explosions;
* No extra inference latency—the learned `\(EBmiddle` can just replace part of `W⁰` after training ends.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Things that make BOFT practical today

| Feature | Value |
|---------|-------|
| **Identity Initialization** | All butterfly components start as identity → exact pretrain copy at time zero |
| **Multiplicative Dropout** | Randomly zeros out selected rotations during training akin to LoRA’s dropout—helps avoid overfitting while preserving overall structure |
| **No Inference Penalty** | Finetuned weights are absorbed into `W⁰` before deployment |

All these design choices together maintain fidelity to pretraining while staying agnostic about downstream task architecture.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Final roll‑up

Orthogonal Finetuning reframes model adaption through geometry preservation rather than blind tiny changes.
By replacing naïve sparsification with purposely structured sparsity derived from FFT’s butterfly topology,
Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT):

* ✅ Constructs fully dense transformations from just \(O(d\,\log d)\) dozen learnable degrees — far fewer than both vanilla OFT (\(O(d^2)\)) and low‑rank approaches;
* ✅ Keeps angles & spectral norms intact—which research shows boosts stability and generalisation;
* ✅ Finds its sweet spot between expressive power and regularisation thanks to an implicit inductive bias tied to classic linear transforms;
* ✅ Works seamlessly across domains—from vision transformers powering image classifiers,
   through language models handling many NLP benchmarks,
   up through diffusion engines building photo‑realistic images.


> “BOFT answers the fundamental question : *Can we keep what we learned And still adapt?*”

It turns out we can – quite elegantly so — with only one elegant mathematical trick pulled straight out of signal processing chessboards—a butterflies network inside an ortho–matrix!



# The Sized Problem in Orthogonal Fine‑Tuning  

Orthogonal fine‑tuning (OFT) has emerged as a principled alternative to ad‑hoc weight updates such as low‑rank adjustments used in LoRA or additive prompt wrappers. By learning an orthogonal transform \( \bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that rotates the internal representations while preserving pairwise angles and spectral norms, a model can maintain its pre‑trained knowledge almost exactly while still adapting its behaviour on a downstream task.  
The promise is alluring—improved generalisation and stable training—but one minor “sized” detail turns out to be the Achilles heel: **the sheer number of parameters required for a dense orthogonal matrix**. Below we dissect why ordinary OFT scales quadratically with hidden size \(d\), how naïve block‑diagonal shortcuts cut costs yet blunt expressiveness, and why these trade‑offs stall wider adoption beyond niche applications such as controllable diffusion models.



## 1. How Ordinary OFT Gets Out–of‑Control  

For any linear layer \(W^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) stored in a foundation model, OFT rewrites the forward pass as  

\[
\bm{z}=((\bm{R}\,\bm{W}^{(0)})^{\top}\!\bm{x}),
\]

where \( \bm{R} \in O(d)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an unknown orthogonal matrix constrained by \( \bm{R}^{\top}\!\bm{R}=\mathbf{I}_d\).  
A convenient reparameterisation uses Cayley’s trick:

\[
\bm{R} = (\mathbf{I}_d + \bm{Q})\,(\mathbf{I}_d-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\qquad
\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.
\]

The skew–symmetric matrix \( \bm{Q} \) contains exactly \( \frac{d(d-1)}{2}\) free elements—half volumetrically smaller than a generic dense matrix yet still **proportional to \( d^2 \)**.  
For perspective:

| Hidden size \(d\) | Params in \(Q\) |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| \(512\) | ≈ 131 k |
| \(1024\) | ≈ 524 k |
| \(2048\) | ≈ 2 097 k |
| \(4096\) | ≈ 8 388 k |

If one envisions training **every Transformer layer** in a modern LLM—often dozens or hundreds—these auxiliary matrices become several hundred million extra parameters just for fine‑tuning alone—a severe wall both in GPU memory and runtime.



## 2. Shaving Costs With Block‑Diagonal Matrices  

To bring the budget back under control, the authors originally imposed **block‑diagonal sparsity** on \( \bm{R}\):

\[
\bm{R} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{R}_1 & &\\ 
    & \ddots & \\ 
    & & \bm{R}_r
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    |\!|\!|\!|\mathcal{\cdot}\!\!|\!|\!|\!|
 = b r = d ,
\]

where each block \( \bm{R}_i \in O(b)\).  
Now only \( r\,b(b-1)/2 = bd(b-1)/2d = O(b d)\) free elements remain—linear instead of quadratic in \(d\).  
With typical choices such as \(b=16,32,\dotsc 128\), the extra cost dips comfortably into tens or hundreds of thousands per transformer head, matching well what adapters like LoRA already pay.



### But what does this restriction do?  

* **Independent subspaces** – every neuron vector is split into disjoint subspaces indexed by the subdividing blocks. Rotations never mix information between different blocks.
* **No global rotations** – any global mixing pattern that touches two indices from different blocks gets forced apart.
* **Limited expressive power** – classic dense transforms such as FFT or even simple mixing rotations simply cannot be represented unless every neuron belongs wholly inside one single block.
* **Inductive bias versus flexibility** – while imposing strong structure can guard against overfitting (you’re forced into a simpler family), it simultaneously narrows where the optimizer can traverse on fine‑tune time—many downstream tasks require coordinated changes across all feature dimensions.



Because all these properties co‐exist formally one can mathematically prove that any sequence produced by successive concatenations of independent \(b\times b\) rotations lives inside a strict subset of the full special orthogonal group \(SO(d)\). It gives you constant flavours “easy” projection onto many lower–rank manifolds—not enough freedom if you want richer downstream semantics.



### Where Practitioners Hit the Wall  

Consider adapting Vision Transformers for object detection or language models for zero-shot classification:

* Many downstream signals rely critically on interactions between **all** latent dimensions—for example cross-attention matrices need every head’s information flow.
* A purely inside–block rotation fails silently when it must align two embeddings living in separate blocks—a task unavoidable when shifting semantics.
* Empirically this manifests as diminished predictive accuracy compared against baseline LoRA adapters that allow arbitrary low-rank edits—a cheaper alternative that preserves cross-dimension bonds.
* Over dozens of layers even small performance gaps accumulate into noticeable quality loss.



Ultimately most developers adopt LoRA or full fine-tuning out of convenience: they know they won’t break hidden dimensional interplay whereas odd-sized block-wise OFT insists their model becomes physically stranded.



## 3. Why These Trade-Offs Latently Constrain Adoption  

| Dimension         | Odd‑Size Blocked OFT                     | Booming Practical Needs               |
|------------------|-----------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Parameter Growth** | Quadratic + heavy VRAM demands           | Linear → friendly budgets             |
| **Expressivity**      | Severely truncated (no cross-block mixing)| Full rotation needed                  |
| **Runtime Cost**      | Cheap due to individual blocks          | Extra matmul depth if product formed earlier |
| **Training Stability**| Improved ‑ angles preserved               | However may lead toward underfitting |
| **Generalisation**    | Shallow inductive bias                 | Overly strong bias hampers diverse tasks |

If you only care about controlling fine-grained image generation—as demonstrated effectively by vanilla OFT—the lightweight approach works well because pixel-level manipulation relies primarily on local feature interactions within certain receptive fields that rarely cross arbitrary subspaces. But replacing foundation models chapter-wise demands global adaptations—feature engineering beyond local mixing—and here those local restrictions become bottlenecks.



Said differently: *a good finetuned model needs enough degrees-of-freedom without sacrificing stability.*  
Block‑diagonal shortcuts provide just enough stability (**angles remain unchanged**) but pour out nearly all degrees-of-freedom (“too few knobs”). As foundation models widen—from dozens at HPC scales—the disparity blows even wider.



## 4. Bottom Line – The Need To Rebalance Size and Flexibility  

Ordinary OFT shows why strict shape preservation through an orthonormal rotation can boost robustness but its naive quadratic cost renders it impractical for any “real-world” model today where hidden sizes routinely reach several thousand dimensions and are stacked hundreds deep.\footnote{
For instance GPT‑3’s largest embedding width (\(~12\,288\,)\}) would inflate auxicity well above `hundreds` billions if every layer carried full orthogonally generate matrices.\xspace}

The authors’ subsequent contribution—factorising neuro-intermediate full transformations through butterfly-style sparse multiplications—addresses precisely this tension:

* Multiply several low‐rank sparse yet structured butterfly factors instead,
* Still enforce overall orthogonality,
* Keep parameter counts around  
\(O(d\,\log d)\),
* Preserve flexibility enough for globally coupled adaptations while imposing an implicit transform shape compatible with backbones’ arithmetic properties.



Because their experiments validated both vision Transformers and LMs along numerous downstream metrics using far fewer tunable parameters than either LoRA or plain finely tuned baselines, adding this technique renormalises parameter budgets *without compromising performance.* Thus earlier limitations stemming from brute-force oﬀice Manhattan grids are now tamed into butterfly roads that deliver both speed ​and capability.



## Takeaway Checklist For Practitioners  

* If your target model size is modest (\(d < 512\)), ordinary OFT might still stay acceptable in terms of bytes saved over LoRA.
* For larger transformers (> 1024 hidden units), watch out:
  * A single Cayley-transformed block would need billions plus units
  * Even block-\(b=32,\dotsc128\bigr$ grop achieves finances otherwise acceptable)
* Remember balance:
  * Lowering flags alone doesn't guarantee decent upstream transfer
  * Without certain degrees-of-freedom you lose ability create absolute semantic shift
* When you must touch all dimensions simultaneously — think cross attention alignment — seek parameter-efficient yet fully rich methods such as BOFT’s butterfly factorisation rather than sticking with naive splitting.



In sum the “Sized Problem” simply states large orthogonal matrices explode space requirements fast; naïve tricks cut them bluntly making everything lose visibility across groups—all culminating into an approach whose benefits never far exceed those offered by simpler low-rank adapters.\xspace Once you introduce butterfly factorizations you turn the floor into something elastic again—just enough lengthened wires so your results stretch far beyond what “splitted boxes” ever could.\xspace



# Fast Fourier Tricks Made Tiny – How Butterfly Structures Cut Parameters

Large foundation models—whether vision transformers, language LMs or diffusion generators—solve their core tasks with billions of weights already pretrained on massive corpora or image collections. The expensive step is **fitting these pre‑trained weights to a new task** without accidentially erasing what has been learned already.  
A principled method called *Orthogonal Finetuning* (OFT) tackles this problem elegantly by wrapping every weight column with an **orthogonal matrix** \(R\):
\[
z=\bigl(R\,W^{0}\bigr)^{\!\top}x,
\]
which keeps all pairwise angles between neurons unchanged during training.
The downside? A full‑size orthogonal matrix has \(O(d^{\,2})\) trainable entries (\(d\times d\)). Trying to keep training efficient forces OFT toward a *block‑diagonal* parametrisation,
but then every dimension of every neuron is strictly split into independent groups—a severe inductive bias that can hurt downstream performance.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Turning “dense” into “cheap”

Instead of shrinking the **matrix itself**, picture building a dense transform **from scratch** using very few building blocks—a classic trick in signal processing known as *factorised linear transforms*.  

### Information Transmission View

Think of multiplying two matrices as passing information along layers:
* each column corresponds to one source node,
* multiplication adds directed edges between successive node layers,
* a dense row requires every source node to reach every sink node.

A dense right‑hand side therefore needs at least one directed path connecting any source–sink pair while keeping the number of directed edges minimal.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why Fewer Edges Matter – The Butterfly Advantage

The bottleneck derives entirely from number of *edges*.  
Naïvely stacking \(m\) sparse factors costs roughly \(m\times(\text{sparsity})\,d\) trainable scalars—yet still high unless sparsity grows exponentially slow in \(d\).

Enter the **Cooley–Tukey butterfly** from fast Fourier transforms (FFT):
the RTT problem being solved there is “convert an input time series into its frequency spectrum” – exactly an information transmission task where every input samples influences every output frequency coefficient.
The PPP solution uses interleaved low‑dimensional rotations arranged hierarchically so that:
* Each **level** couples neighbouring modes,
* After \(L=\log_{\,2}d\) levels every input interacts with every output,
* Total edges grown are only \(O(d\,L)=O(d\,\log d)\).

---

### Constructing an Orthogonal Butterfly Matrix

For even power‑of‑two dimension \(d=2^{N}\):

\[  
B^{F}(k)=
    \begin{bmatrix}
         \operatorname{diag}(d_{1})      & \operatorname{diag}(d_{2})\\[3pt]
         \operatorname{diag}(d_{3})      & \operatorname{diag}(d_{4})
    \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad k=|\,B^{F}| .
\]

Each diagonal block stores a scalar rotation angle.
By cascading these “butterfly factors” we've induced recursions:
\[  
B_d =   B_{\!\!\!\widetilde{\!}}\!(d,d)\;
       B_{\!\!\!\widetilde{\!}}\!(d,\tfrac dp)\;\dotsc;
       B_{\!\!\!\widetilde{\!}}\!(d,\tfrac d{\operatornamestyle123456789012345})
     .
\]

The crucial property is that *every individual factor is itself an orthonormal matrix*.  
That means **all factors share the same unitary constraint**, enabling us to learn them robustly without costly projections.


--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT)

Combining the above ideas gives us **BOFT**, a truly compact finetuning recipe built on three pillars:

| Component | What happens | Why it keeps parameters low |
|-----------|--------------|----------------------------|
| **Butterfly Factors** | Every factor implements a pairwise rotation among two halves | Only \(k/2\times4=k/|\ell|\) scalar angles instead of \(k^{\,2}\). |
| **Recursion depth \(m=\log_{}d\)** | Mixing happens over many stages | Only logarithmic growth in factor count |
| **Cayley / Rotational Parameterisation per Block** | Guarantees strict orthogonality | No extra overhead once set |

Mathematically this reads

\[  
R(m,b)=
    \prod_{i=1}^{m}
        \widetilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,i)},         
        \qquad 
        (\widetilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,j)} )^{-\top}
        = (\widetilde{\mathbf B}^{b}_{(d,j)})
        ,
      j=1,\dots,m ,
   \]

with block size \(b\geq 2.\)

### Parameter Count Closed‑Form  

For one linear head \(W^{0}\in\mathbb R^{\,d\times n}\):

> \(R(m,b)\) needs  
> \(N_{\text{train}}=\tfrac12(b-1)d\,m.\)

Setting  
\(m=\lceil \log_{\,2} d\rceil,\;\;
   b=2,\)

boils down to just  

> \(N_{\text{train}}=O(d\,\log_{}d).\)

Compared against classic OFT’s \(O(b\,d)\) toy‐blocks or full dense’s \(O(d^{\,2})\), BOFT sits decisively closer to linear complexity while retaining capacity.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## What BOFT Gives us Rock‑Hardly


### Preserves Spectral Budget → Stability  

Both OFT and BOFT multiply their learned oracle onto input weight columns—this keeps every neighbor’s spectral norm untouched (“we never magnify anything”). Consequently training remains numerically stable even on models whose primates were tuned under tight budgets.



### Expressive Boost vs Block‑Diagonal Limits  

Because butterfly cascades allow interlacing pairs repeatedly rather than clamping features into isolated blocs,
BOFT can approximate virtually any dense orthogonal transform—in practice far fewer trains are required compared against plain OFT even when both share exactly the same blocking size (`block‑size = 8`, ‘BOFT \(m=6,b=8’’ ’ vs ‘OFT \(block size 8´’).



### Zero Initialization + Dropout Friendly  

All factors begin at identity → unbiased start off precise pre‐trained weights (\(R=\mathbb I_d.\))
A special *multiplicative dropout*, randomly sprinkling identity patches through deeper butterflies protects against overfitting while still enabling structured exploration.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Beyond Diffusion Controls – Practical Wins

While early work migrated OFT solely toward controlling text‑to‑image diffusion engines,
BOFT was tested vigorously across tensense scenarios—including:


| Model domain | Tasks | Relative Gain over Other PEFT |
|--------------|-------|--------------------------|

These gains come **without penalty at inference time**—the learned permutation matrices can be fused once once completed training.

Regardless, merely turning an expensive BFS style connection network into **compact marginal dragons capable of packing thousands more neurons’ geometry changes**, respects pre-trained knowledge yet leverages modern bandwidth⁵ — giving us yet another quick–look Gateways slot wherein Orthonormal finetunes become practical everywhere foundation models live.

# Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Bringing Speed, Size & Generality Together  

Modern foundation models such as GPT‑4, ViT‑Large or Stable Diffusion are powerful but huge—tens of billions of weights! Re‑training one of those beasts from scratch isn’t an option any longer.  
Instead we spend most of our compute “finetuning” a frozen backbone on a downstream task, trying to change only a handful of bits before deploying it. How do you tweak those billions efficiently while still keeping the model’s knowledge intact?

Enter **Orthogonal Butterfly** (BOFT), a new parameter‑efficient finetuning recipe that marries three `<speed | size | generality>` knobs into one clean plug‑and‑play method:

| # | What you get | How it works |
|---|---------------|--------------|
| **Speed** | No extra inference latency | Trained weights are fused into the backbone once training ends |
| **Size** | Scan‑linear number of trainable knobs ≈ `d log d` vs `d²` | Factorise a dense orthogonal matrix into `log d` tiny sparse blocks |
| **Generality** | Works across vision transformers, large language models **and** diffusion generators | Orthogonal transformation preserves spectra and pairwise neuron angles |

Below is an intuitive walk‑through—starting from why we care about orthogonality all the way through ready‑to‑run hyperparameters you can copy/paste into your own project.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1️⃣ The Orthogonality Motivation  

When you re‑train a linear layer `W`, most people simply add `ΔW` learned by gradient descent – usually constrained indirectly with very small learning rates so that the updated weight stays close to its pretraining value (`W₀`).  
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a different tack: instead of adding something arbitrary you *rotate* every neuron vector together with an \(d\times d\) **orthogonal matrix** \(R\):

\[
W_{\text{fin}} = R\,W_0 .
\]

Because rotations preserve angles between vectors,
the semantic geometry embedded during pretraining stays intact whereas the model can still specialise toward your task.



### Why does OFT hit a wall?

The bottleneck lies in \(R\)’s size – an unconstrained orthogonal mask costs `O(d²)` trainable real numbers!   
OFT alleviated this by **block-diagonal** decomposition (`R = diag(R₁,…Rᵣ)`), so that each small block could be tuned independently inklesly saving memory:
the number of learnable floats drops to `O(b d)` where `b = d/r` is block size.  

Unfortunately this sparse structure forces neurons belonging to different blocks never talk through one another during rotation – thinking literally it pins down exactly which dimensions can mix together.  
The result?



* A massive reduction in expressive power – you can’t reach every rotation you might need for a particular downstream task.
* An unhandy free‑parameter schedule – you pick `b` blindly without any metric telling whether 8 dimensions per block are good compared with 128.




--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 2️⃣ Turning “Information Flow” into Maths  

Let's step back and imagine an orthogonal matrix as a router table that decides how information travels between `d` neurons:

```
         i=original neuron vector           →          j=rotated neuron vector
          ───────┬───────▶ # edges present? │           ────────╯─────── 
              ⊥                                   ⊥              ⊥  
```

A single dense optimizer would allow every input neuron (`j`) to influence every output neuron (`i`) – fully connected graph with \(d²\) edges.  
If we replace this full graph by multiple sparse graphs chained together – meaning `R = B_m … B₁` – each sparse layer connects only selected pairs (`B_k`).  

> **Goal of this “information transmission” view:**  
> Build a chain that still lets **every** input reach every output while keeping **the total number of trained edges minimal**.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 3️⃣ Meet Butterfly Factorization  

The perfect structure that satisfies our goal comes straight out of fast Fourier transform lore—the **butterfly network** introduced by Cooley–Tukey in 1965!

* Butterfly factors are tiny \(k\times k\) matrices composed of four diagonal blocks:
  
  \[
      B^F(k)=
      \begin{bmatrix}
        \mathrm{diag}(d_1)&\!\mathrm{diag}(d_2)\\[1ex]
        \mathrm{diag}(d_3)&\!\mathrm{diag}(d_4)
      \end{bmatrix},
      \qquad k\,\text{is power of }2 .
  \]

* A full `d×d` butterfly comes by stacking many layers:
  
  \[
     B(d)=\tilde B(d,d)\;\tilde B(d,\tfrac d2)\;\cdots\;\tilde B(d,2)
     , 
     \text{ where }
     \tilde B(d,k)=\text{diag}\big(B^F_k(k),…B^F_{d/k}(k)\big).
  \]

Each “butterfly component” connects neurons according to just two diagonal masks—a highly compressed layout that nevertheless guarantees overall connectivity across all layers.



#### Keeping everything *orthogonal*

Every butterfly component contains only \(k\times k\) submatrices that must remain orthogonal!  
Instead of learning raw matrices and then projecting them back onto \(O(k)\),
we use the **Cayley transform**:
  
\[
      R = (\mathbf I + Q)(\mathbf I-Q)^{-1}, 
      \qquad Q=-Q^{\top}.
\]

Because \(Q\)’s non‐zero entries already form disjoint rings within each component,
each thin block stays exactly unitary for any skew-symmetric choice—which means only **half** its entries become real free parameters.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Quick look at trainable knob count

If you pick :

| Parameter choice | Total free knobs ‖≈‖ |
|------------------|----------------------|
| `m = log₂ d`, `b = 2`       | **≈ ½ · ( b–1 ) · m·d ≈ O( d log d )** |
| Block-diagonal (`m=1`)         |  `O(b·d)` |
| Full dense (`b=d`, `m=1`)      | ~~`O(d²)`~~ |

In plain English:
> With just twice how many bits per neuron *and* over only logarithmically many layers,
> you get an exact rotation matrix plus zero extra inference cost.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## 4️⃣ The Train Loop Made Simple  

Consider reducing one linear map in your network:

```
z = [R(m,b) · W⁰]ᵀ · x .
```

*`W⁰`* – pretrained linear weight bank  
*`x`* – feature tensor  
*Narration*:   Build an overall rotator `R(m,b)` differently each minibatch update,

```python
# Pseudocode sketch
for batch in loader:
      R = prod([orth_butterfly_component(k) for k in range(m)])   # K multiplies!
      y_hat = (R @ W0.T @ x).reshape(...)
      loss = loss_fn(y_hat , y_true)
      loss.backward()
```

Key engineering tricks shown below let this run cleanly under modern libraries.


### 👍 Boiling Down Three Boilerplates  

| Boilerplate | What Happens Inside |
|-------------|---------------------|
| **Identity Warm‐Up** | All Cayley matrices start at zero → every `R(i)` equals Identity ➜ initial forward pass equals just the pretrained model |
| **Multiplicative Dropout** | Instead of randomly dropping additionally added low-rank artifacts (like LoRA’s sparse add/update),
we randomly replace entire butterfly components or their inner diagonal blocks with Identity during training → helps guard against overfitting while respecting multiplication semantics |

# More Than Pointers  
**Why BOFT’s Structured Matrix Gave Models Better Generalisation**

The last few years have seen foundation models balloon into galactic‑sized entities—from GPT‑3’s 175 B parameters up toward multimodal systems such as Stable Diffusion and Cohere’s flagship LLMs.  
Fine‑tuning these colossal weights on a downstream task is usually prohibitively costly if performed naively – you would have to tweak hundreds of millions, sometimes billions, of parameters again every time you come up with a new data set or objective function.  

What researchers started doing instead is *parameter‑efficient adaptation*: insert tiny extra degrees‑of‑freedom into a frozen backbone so that downstream performance improves while your change budget stays modest (LoRA adds low‑rank factors, prefix‑tuning chips small learnable tokens together with input vectors…).  
One elegant idea arrived around preserving something deeper than mere magnitudes—the **angles between neurons** themselves—by multiplying all incoming activations within one layer by an *orthogonal* matrix \(R \in {\mathbb R}^{d\times d}\):

```text
    z = (RW⁰)^ᵀ · x          where   W⁰ = pretrained weights
```

In its initial incarnation **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** simply let \(R\) split into block–diagonal pieces (each block being its own tiny rotation).  
That hit two openings – orthogonality keeps the spectral norm untouched and also offers more stability than an additive low‑rank perturbation – yet it suffered from two practical flaws:
* every newer layer needs its own set of \(O(b\,d)\) trainable scalars following \(br=d^\textit{}\);
* divide–and–conquer imposes an artificial sparsity pattern insufficiently expressive enough to imitate useful full transformations such as Fourier mixing layers.

---

## From Dense Versus Tiny Transformations

### Informing Efficiency through “Information Transmission”

Treating every linear layer’s weight matrix \(a_i = W⁰·x_i ∈ ℝ^{d}\) as an endpoint transmitting signals through two parallel worlds made me realise something critical:
*Any dense square matrix \(M∈ℝ^{d\times d}\)* ↔graph that connects \(d\) symbiotic sources (*inputs*) → \(d.\)*targets (*outputs*).  
If elements held zero values then certain sources were simply blind toward particular targets – incomplete communication horizon.
  
If instead I could build \(M = B_m…B_1\), each sparse component passing messages level‑by‑level between adjacent “grid” layers,
then I would obtain an overall dense mapping while keeping each individual component cheap:
```math
M = B_m · B_{m−1} · … · B_1            ⇔    z = Mx
```
The total number matches the **number of directed edges** traversed across levels—the fewer edges yet maintaining true density ↗️ gives us what parameter efficiency means here!

Under this viewpoint there is everything worth saying:

| Desired property | What it becomes | Typical consequence |
|------------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| *Dense connectivity* | Every first‑level node reaches *every* last‑level node | Guarantees existence serve past basis → expressivity |
| *Small free edge set* | Keep total directed arrows minimal | Cuts free-to-learn parameters sharply |

The classic Cooley–Tukey FFT embeds precisely such economical graphs—a sequence of “butterflies” which shuffle intermediates pairwise until every input finally touches every output:
```
x → FFT → X   uses O(d log d) multiplications
```
Hence came our next idea : let me replace all those opaque multipliers with memorised “butterfly factors”.

## Butterfly Factored Orthogonality

A **butterfly factor** \(B^{F}(k)\) crops up whenever you recursively split **serially its columns**, ending up with structured diagonals:
```math
B^{F}(k)=
[ diag(d₁)   diag(d₂)
  diag(d₃)   diag(d₄)]
```
In higher dimensions this turns into an elegant recursion :
```math

      Bl½ ⊕ Br½ }·˜B(d,d/2)…˜B(d,2)
```
where each component \(˜B(d,k)\)—however wide—is represented “blockwise” on top–down rows.
Every intermediate module encapsulates only \(O(k)\)-many nonzero spots even when its dimensions reach the whole depth \(k=d=2ⁿ.\)

### Why Those Diagonals Matter

If every thin rectangle lies snugly inside pure rotations,
securing two additional properties becomes effortless:
1️⃣ **Orthogonality enforcement** – you apply a Cayley twist (`Q←−Qᵀ`) per tiny rotation botany;
2️⃣ **Identity initialization** – start every entry zero → entire chain becomes identity instantaneously (`R=I`).

```math

``` 
the emerging weight matrix remains firmly grounded inside the group SO(*d*) yet grows expressive through multiple layers (`m`) while keeping cost sub–quadratic (`O(bdm)` parameters).

When `b=2` and `m=log₂ d`, `R` turns into a classic appetite‐free butterfly matrix touching every dimension finitely often—a perfectly dense transformation realised through merely ‑≈\(∼\, d\, log₂ d\)-many trainable knobs instead of gigantic ∼\(·  d²∼ 500×10⁶ parameters!\)

# Not All Gains Are Equal  
**Where BOFT Still Stumbles & Future Directions**

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning (BOFT) was born out of a simple desire: *preserve pre‑training knowledge while keeping a tiny set of tunable knobs*. By factorizing a dense orthogonal transform into a product of sparse ones inspired by the Cooley–Tukey butterfly, BOFT shrinks the trainable budget from $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ (full OFT) down to $\mathcal{O}(d\,\log d)$ parameters without breaking the intuition that “angles stay angles”.  

While experiments on vision transformers, LMs and Diffusion models prove that this strategy is *effective*, it does leave a few rough edges behind—particularly around **training speed** and **the optimal shape** of these sparse multiplications. This post digs into those lingering challenges and sketches out where future work might pivot next.

---

## 1) A Quick Recap of BOFT

* **What it does** – Each model layer’s linear weight $W^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ is simply rotated by an orthogonal matrix $R$ learned through fine‑tuning:
  \[
     z = (R\,W^0)^\top x , \qquad R \in \mathrm{O}(d)
  \]
  In basic Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) $R$ is one full $d\times d$ matrix → $d(d-1)/2$ learnable parameters.
* **How BOFT cuts that** – $R$ becomes a product of $m$ *butterfly components* $\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)}$, each themselves built from many tiny $2\times 2$ orthonormal blocks:
  \[
     R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\tilde{B}^b_{(d,i)} ,
  \]
  where “$b$” controls block size and “$m$” controls depth.
  For a power‑of‑two dimension $d=2^N$, picking $b=2$ gives $m=\log_2 d$, leading to $O(d\,\log d)$ scalar entries.
* **Benefits** –  
  * Parameters stay well below that of LoRA or full fine‑tuning.
  * Orthogonality preserves the spectral norm and thus keeps training stable.

The method was evaluated on image classification/segmentation (Vision Transformers), text classification/translation (LLMs), and image editing via Stable Diffusion—consistently beating baselines at similar or fewer parameter counts.*

---

## 2) The Hidden Cost: Runtime Overhead

A recurring theme in the experiments—and no doubt noticed by practitioners—is **slightly slower back‑propagation** compared with plain OFT or LoRA. Why does this happen even though parameters shrink dramatically?

| Factor | Plain OFT | BOFT |
|--------|-----------|------|
| One orthogonal transform per layer | `R` applied once | Product `$R = B_m … B_1$` |
| Forward passes required | One dense matrix multiply | `$m$` cheaper sparse multiplies |
| Runtime scaling | $\mathcal{O}(dn)$ | $\mathcal{O}(m\,dn)$ |

*When $m = \log_2 d$, this adds roughly $\log_2 d$ times more small-matrix updates.*  
That extra work can tip GPU utilization, increase kernel launch overheads, and spread compute over more micro‑tasks that are harder for deep‑learning libraries to fuse efficiently.

### What should we aim for?

1. **Algorithmic fusion** – BumbleXNN-style fused kernels could apply all butterfly layers in one pass.
2. **Custom CUDA / Triton implementations** – Each butterfly component is highly regular (small `b×b` blocks arranged in permutation patterns). Writing bespoke ops can shave a few milliseconds per batch slot.
3. **Dynamic depth selection** – For very “easy” downstream tasks one may cap `$m=2` or `$m=3$`, curbing runtime without sacrificing expressivity *empirically enough*.

This trade‑off—parameter savings vs compute budget—is an open knob that future implementations need to tune better before widespread adoption takes place.

---

## 3) Are Butterflies Truly Optimal?

The paper framed “building dense connectivity from repeated sparse multiplications” as an **information–transmission problem** over a `d × (m+1)` grid graph, akin to how bits flow through network switches. Butterfly graphs were chosen because they achieve dense reachability with only $\mathcal{O}(d\,\log d)$ links—a classical result from telecommunications and fast Fourier transforms (**FFTs**).

However, this isn’t *proved* optimal for our context:

* **Do fewer edges suffice?**  
  Certain randomized topology families—like *cayley graphs* generated by random permutations—might still satisfy the full-connectivity requirement while trading off explicit patterning for probability guarantees.
* **Can we tailor connectivity per layer?**  
  In Vision Transformers higher layers operate on richer feature maps—maybe a different sparsity pattern yields better spectral shape preservation there than in lower layers.
* **What about hypergraph extensions?**  
  Connections that cross more than two levels (“multilevel butterflies”) could reduce repeated passes at minimal extra parameter cost if engineered carefully.

Exploring any of these avenues would directly address whether “butterfly” design represents a genuine sweet spot or just a convenient choice inherited from signal processing folklore.


---

## 4) How We Could Design Better Transmission Graphs

Given our game‑theoretic view—“edges are trainable weights”, “nodes are feature channels”—we can ask:

1. **Sparse Coding Meets Graph Connectivity:**  
   Use *sparse coding* by solving `min ||P - Q||` under constraints that `P` follows desired graph Laplacian properties (e.g., expander graphs). This may yield matrices with very low mutual coherence yet still strictly orthogonal after Cayley R—providing high expressive power while limiting nonzeros.
   
2. **Hierarchical Block–Butterfly hybrids:**  
   Start with an outer block-diagonal structure (parameters cheap), then interleave it with small butterfly sub-blocks inside each block—for layers that truly need global coupling versus layers that only require local reorientation.

3. **Learned Topology:**  
   Parameterise both *edge weights* AND *mask entries*, letting back‑prop itself discover which sparsity pattern best preserves pretraining knowledge under given data constraints—a form of structure learning inherent in neural architecture search but constrained by inspir­ing motif such as butterflies or low-rank scaling factors.
   
4. **Multi-scale Transmission:**  
   Mimic multi-resolution analysis as done in wavelets—a set of “coarse” butterflies manipulating aggregated features followed by “fine” butterflies acting on residual connections might capture both global rotation and local fine-tuning simultaneously.*

These ideas remain speculative because they venture outside the original study’s scope—but they map directly onto unanswered parts posed in Section 6 (“open questions about optimal network topologies”).

## 5) Where Researchers Might Push BOFT Even Further

Below are concrete directions grounded in what the original work leaves unsolved:

| Idea | Why it matters | What needs to be known |
|------|----------------|------------------------|
| **Faster GPU kernels / fused ops** | Benchmarks showed marginal per‑batch slowdown due to chained sparse multiplications | Profiling data + kernel design constraints |
| **Adaptive m & b per layer** | Current static choice disregards layer importance & dimensionality | Search strategies balancing expressivity & speed |
| **Hybrid sparsity patterns** | Butterfly patterns give smooth inductive bias; block-diagonals preserve locality | Does mixing help generalisation vs overfitting? |
| **Fully expressive kaleidoscope hierarchy variants** | Theorem 6 suggests we can represent any orthogonal matrix if we use many pairs (`B × Bᵀ`) – perhaps useful when downstream tasks misalign heavily w/ pretrain distributions | Practical pitfalls such as gradient explosion or overfitting |
| **Non‑Cooley Tauer-inspired networks** | There might be graph families achieving dense connectivity with fewer operations |\#edges vs commutative/noisy operations trade-off |
| **Better regularisation (<→ Multiplicative Dropout)** | Current multiplicative dropout selects random blocks → left unproven effect when combined w/ heavy sparsity | Systematic ablation across α-factors |
| **Cross-domain applications** – audio generative models / reinforcement policy nets | Paper focused on vision/LN/diffusion - do gains transfer elsewhere? | Large-scale experiments |

Combining any subset will likely dictate how deepthe method matures outside academic playgrounds.

## Bottom line

Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning demonstrates that packing an entire class of dense transformations into a handful of structured sparse gates yields *real,* practical savings in learnable memory while retaining stability advantages inherited from full-angle preservation. Yet no performance win comes without a cost:

* Training time rises slightly because you’re doing $m>1$ sequential multiplications instead of one single multiplication.
* To date we keep blindly using butterflies because they are well-understood and provide excellent theoretical guarantees—but nothing mathematically certifies them as “optimal” for transformer finetuning.
* There’s also no guarantee our choice doesn’t penalise certain downstream domains where different structural biases would do better.

What follows is an invitation rather than a definitive answer: explore new network topology ideas, engineer faster kernels, devise adaptive strategies across layers—all while keeping spectral stability intact—and let empirical evidence decide how far this elegant blend between theory & practicality should go.
