Foundations Behind Efficient Model Adaptation  

# Overview of foundation models and their scale  
Foundation models have emerged as the backbone of modern artificial intelligence systems, achieving unprecedented performance across natural language processing, computer vision, and multimodal generation tasks.  Their success stems from training on vast amounts of data with increasingly large neural networks—some possessing hundreds of billions of trainable parameters.  For instance, GPT‑3 contains approximately \(1.75\times10^{11}\) parameters, while Stable Diffusion, a state‑of‑the‑art text‑to‑image diffusion model, also utilizes comparable scale in its transformer back‑bone \cite{brown2020language, ramboch2022high}.  The sheer size of these networks means that back‑propagating gradients through every parameter is computationally demanding and memory intensive; the cost inflates linearly with parameter count and often scales up dramatically when training from scratch due to data I/O, optimizer overhead, and repeated passes over large corpora.  Consequently, repeated full training cycles are economically prohibitive for individual researchers or small organizations even when cloud credits are available.

# Challenges posed by full retraining  
When a new downstream task emerges—such as fine‑grained image classification or domain‑specific language generation—the most straightforward approach is to continue training an existing foundation model while further updating all its weights until convergence.  However, this strategy faces several obstacles:

1. **Compute and Storage Burden** – Retraining a \(d\)-parameter network requires repeatedly loading the entire weight tensor across GPU memories while accumulating gradients in optimizer states (e.g., Adam’s second moment estimates).  For a model where \(d\) ranges from tens to hundreds of billions, this quickly exhausts available hardware budgets.
2. **Catastrophic Forgetting** – Updating all weights risks overwriting knowledge acquired during pretraining, which often includes broad semantic priors that generalize well across arbitrary downstream tasks.
3. **Data Inefficiency** – Training from scratch demands substantial amounts of high-quality labeled data to reach competitive performance.  In many practical scenarios, only small annotated datasets are available.
4. **Inference Latency** – Even if retraining succeeds, future deployments may still experience added computational burden if additional layers or structures have been inserted inadvertently.

These constraints motivate efficient adaptation strategies that avoid overhauling the entire pretrained network while still enabling it to specialize for new tasks.

# Prospective solutions for downstream task adaptation  
Researchers have explored three principal paradigms that respect both fidelity to pretraining knowledge and adherence to computational practicality:

* **Model Finetuning** – The network architecture remains unaltered; only a subset of existing parameters is updated during training on the target dataset.  This approach maintains the original inference pipeline because no new modules are inserted post‑training \cite{zhang2022adaptive}.

* **Adapter Tuning** – Light‑weight trainable modules—often composed of bottleneck linear layers—are interleaved into each layer of the already frozen backbone.  These adapters add negligible overhead during inference but expand the network’s capacity in a controlled manner \cite{houlsby2019parameter}.

* **Prompt Tuning** – Instead of altering weights inside the network, one introduces trainable tokens or embeddings appended at the beginning or end of input sequences (for language models) or image patches (for vision transformers).  These prompts steer the preexisting transformations toward task‑specific behavior without touching internal parameters \cite{li2021prefix}.

All three strategies share a core intuition: **preserve what is already learned while providing limited expressiveness to accommodate new signal domains**.  The choice between them typically hinges on considerations such as target task availability (e.g., volume and nature of data), deployment constraints (e.g., inference latency budget), and desired granularity of specialization (e.g., per-head vs entire layer).

# Detailed comparison of model finetuning versus adapters versus prompts  
A strict comparison across these paradigms reveals nuanced trade‑offs:

| Criterion | Model Finetuning | Adapter Tuning | Prompt Tuning |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Parameter Count** | Updates only those weights chosen (often all but few layers); numbers can be modest if selective fine–tuning masks are used | Adds extra parameters per layer (usually a small bottleneck); total increase grows linearly with backbone depth | Adds very few tokens relative to sequence length; parameter overhead negligible |
| **Inference Cost** | Unchanged—no additional operations beyond standard forward pass | Slightly higher due to extra linear transformations within adapters but often dominated by backbone operations | No change—performed via standard embedding lookup |
| **Representational Capacity** | Unlimited within updated weights; can modify any aspect including scaling, biasing, conditioning across feature maps | Modular augmentation allows learning task‐specific transformations without disturbing preexisting pathways | Limited only by how much information can be encoded in prompt tokens; usually global conditioning |
| **Risk of Catastrophic Forgetting** | Potentially higher if all layers are tuned; mitigated by lower learning rates or gradient masking | Lower—adapters act as secondary pathways that preserve baseline weights | Zero—pretrained backbone untouched |
| **Data Efficiency** | Often requires moderate fine–tune sets; risks overfitting on tiny corpora unless regularized | More economical because adapter capacity is bounded; better suited for few shot regimes \cite{chen2022pixelated} | Very efficient when prompt tokens can capture task cues with few examples \cite{li2021prefix} |
| **Implementation Complexity** | Straightforward—entail selectively freezing/unfreezing existing weight tensors; framework support common in most libraries | Requires architectural modifications but well documented in major backbones such as ViT or BERT \cite{houlsby2019parameter} | Simple insertion logic at preprocessing stage; minimal code changes |

In practice, *model finetuning* remains popular because it preserves architectural simplicity—the inference pipeline remains untouched—and because it has been empirically shown to offer more robust convergence dynamics when adequately regularized (e.g., via low learning rates).  However, recent work has highlighted principled variants—such as Orthogonal Finetuning—that constrain updates within subspaces defined by orthogonal matrices so that existing neuron orientations are preserved almost exactly \cite{qiu2023controlling}.  These methods aim to merge benefits from all three paradigms: they maintain *parameter efficiency* like adapters/prompt tuning while offering *direct control over transformation matrices* comparable to full finite tuning.

Overall, understanding how each paradigm interacts with pretrained knowledge empowers practitioners to select an adaptation method best suited for the computational resource constraints and performance demands characteristic of their specific downstream application domain.

Mathematical Prerequisites: Pairwise Angle Preservation  

# Definition and intuition behind angles between neuron vectors  
In a deep network the weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ collects $n$ column vectors $\{\mathbf{w}_i\}_{i=1}^n$, each collinear with one neuron’s incoming signal when applied to an input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$. The geometric relationship between two such weight vectors is measured by their angle  
\[
\theta_{ij}= \arccos \!\left( \frac{\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j}{\|\mathbf{w}_i\|\;\|\mathbf{w}_j\|}\right)\!.
\]  
When two neurons share a small angle, their responses are strongly correlated, whereas large angles indicate nearly independent directions. During pre‑training a neural network learns useful representations partly stored in these angular relationships among neurons – they encode how activations co‑vary across data samples. Therefore preserving $\theta_{ij}$ when adapting a model helps ensure that the semantic knowledge acquired during pre‑training remains largely intact after finetuning.  

Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) explicitly encourages this preservation by multiplying the frozen pretrained weight $W^0$ on its left by an orthogonal matrix $R$. Because $R^\top R=I$, any pair of transformed columns satisfies  
\[
(R\mathbf{w}_i)^\top(R\mathbf{w}_j)= \mathbf{w}_i^\top \underbrace{(R^\top R)}_{\!I}\mathbf{w}_j=\mathbf{w}_i^\top \mathbf{w}_j,
\]
which implies $\theta'_{ij}=\theta_{ij}$. Thus OFT guarantees that all pairwise inner products – hence all inter‑neuron angles – stay exactly unchanged through training.  

# Relationship between angles and spectral norm stability  
Another way to view angle preservation is from linear–algebraic spectral properties of the weight matrix. Any real matrix admits a singular value decomposition (SVD) $W^0=U\,\Sigma\,V^\top$, where $U$ and $V$ are orthogonal while $\Sigma$ contains non‑negative singular values on its diagonal. If we left‑multiply by an orthogonal matrix $R$, we obtain $R\,W^0=(RU)\,\Sigma\,V^\top$. Because $RU$ remains orthogonal, its singular values match those of $U$, i.e., they are still unity along those directions that were initially unitary in $U$. Consequently, every singular value of $W^0$ – particularly its largest one which equals the spectral norm $\|W^0\|_2$ – stays unchanged under any left multiplication by an orthogonal matrix:
\[
\sigma_k(R\,W^0)=\sigma_k(W^0)\quad \forall k.
\]
Preserving all singular values eliminates the risk of exploding or vanishing gradients that could otherwise arise if new weights altered them dramatically during finetuning. Empirical studies have associated stable spectrums with improved convergence speed and better generalization properties; hence TOFs leverage strict orthogonality both to keep pair‑wise neuron relationships intact *and* to maintain a trustworthy spectrum throughout adaptation.

# Cayley transform and skew‑symmetric matrices as tools for enforcing strict orthogonality  
A practical challenge arises when we wish to learn an arbitrary orthogonal matrix during finetuning while guaranteeing exact orthogonality at every iteration. Direct optimization over all elements subject to quadratic equality constraints would be costly and numerically unstable. The Cayley parametric form elegantly resolves this issue:
\[
R=(I+Q)\,(I-Q)^{-1},
\]
where $Q$ is guaranteed skew‑symmetric ($Q=-Q^\top$). For any real skew‑symmetric $Q$, this formula produces an exact real orthogonal matrix because
$(I+Q)^\top(I+Q)=(I-Q)(I-Q)^\top$, implying $(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1}$ satisfies $(I-Q)(I+Q)^{-1}((I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1})^\top=I$.  
In practice one sets most entries of $Q$ as free trainable parameters while its antisymmetry reduces degrees of freedom from $d^{2}$ down to $\tfrac12 d(d-1)$, ensuring strict adherence to the group $\mathcal O(d)\subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$. Furthermore initialisation becomes trivial: taking all entries of $Q$ zero yields
\[ R=(I+0)(I-0)^{-1}=I,\] 
which means at time zero OFTs start exactly from the pretrained weights—no discrepancy, just as zero‑initialisation does for low‑rank updates like LoRA but without violating strict angle preservation.  

The same Cayley representation extends naturally into Butterfly‑factored variants (BOFT), where each butterfly component is parameterised either through several independent 2 × 2 rotations (Cayley transforms over 2 × 2 blocks) or through balanced skew‑symmetric block matrices when larger subblocks appear. Because every butterfly component remains strictly orthogonal thanks to its own Cayley parametrisation, their product stays within $\mathcal O(d)$ while requiring drastically fewer trainable parameters—in BOFT’s canonical setting only $\mathcal O(d\,\log d)$ scalars come into play instead of $\tfrac12 d(d-1)$ in vanilla OFT or many more if one used fully dense low‑rank additions instead of rotations."

# Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT): Principle Inheritance  

Large‑scale foundation models encode a wealth of knowledge during their vast pretraining regimes. Adapting these models efficiently requires preserving much of that learned structure while still allowing flexibility for downstream tasks. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) achieves precisely this balance by replacing the usual additive update rule with a **multiplicative change** driven by an ***orthogonal*** transformation applied uniformly across all neurons in a given layer. This section explains how OFT’s reparameterization maintains pretraining knowledge, why an identity initialization makes sense, and how the optimisation proceeds under strict orthogonality constraints.



## Reparameterising \( \protect{\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}=\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{old}}}\)

In standard fine‑tuning one learns an incremental update \( \Delta \mathbf{W}\) such that \( \mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}=\mathbf{W}^{\text{old}}+\Delta \mathbf{W}\).  This additive perturbation changes both the length and direction of every column vector in \( \mathbf{W}\), thereby potentially distorting relationships that were established during pretraining (e.g., similarity relations between output units).

OFT eschews this approach in favour of a **multiplicative** reparameterization
\[
  \boxed{\;\mathbf{W}^{\text{new}}=\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{W}^{\text{old}}\;}
  \tag{1}
\]
where \( \mathbf{R}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}\) is a learnable **orthogonal** matrix (\(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top=\mathbf{I}_d\)).  Since every column vector is left‑multiplied by the same rotation,
any inner product between two columns \(w_{i}^{(0)}\) and \(w_{j}^{(0)}\) satisfies
\[
  \langle\,\mathbf{R}w_{i}^{(0)},\,\mathbf{R}w_{j}^{(0)}\,\rangle
  =w_{i}^{(0)\top}\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}\bigr)}_{\!=\!\mathbf I_d}\!w_{j}^{(0)}
  =w_{i}^{(0)\top} w_{j}^{(0)} .
  \tag{2}
\]
Thus pairwise angles – a key geometric quantity capturing semantic relationships among neurons – are literally *preserved* throughout fine‑tuning, no matter how large the parameters change.  At the same time, if the full rank transformation \( \mathbf R \) has full Gamut coverage then it can realize any re‑orientation of the neuron space; hence OFT offers substantial expressivity while still honouring prior geometry.



## Zero / Identity Initialization Rationale

Optimisation algorithms tend to converge more stably when starting near a sensible point in parameter space—especially when hard algebraic constraints are involved.  For OFT this translates into two intertwined design choices:

* **Zero‑weight updates** – analogous to initializing LoRA’s low‑rank matrices with zeros – would amount essentially to keeping every row or column unchanged until gradients accumulate enough strength to move it away from identity or zero.
* **Identity transformation** – for our multiplicative rule it simply means choosing an initial \( \mathbf R^{(0)}=\mathbf I_d \).

Because
\( \boxed{\;\overline{\mathbf W}^{\,0}
   = (\overline{\phi })(\overline{\psi }) }
      s.t.\;
      (\overline{\phi })= (\overline{\psi })=I_d ,
    }
        the revised forward pass exactly reproduces the pretrained behaviour before gradient descent kicks in:
\( {\bf z}=({I_d}{\,W^{{init}}\!})^\top {\bf x}=({ {W^{init}}})^{\top}{\,x} .
   \)
Consequently any change comes solely from learning \( \Delta \!\mathcal Q(t)\)-informed rotations rather than arbitrary large deviations.



### Why Identity Trumps Zero

If one were to start from a zero‑rotating matrix (\(\mathbf R^{(0)}=\bfzero_d\)), early iterations would output almost no signal—rendering training ineffective until numerical regularisation forces non‑zero entries elsewhere.  An identity matrix guarantees that even before any gradient steps every neuron remains functional and has clearly defined direction with respect to its pretrained counterpart.



## Training Dynamics Under Strict Orthogonality Constraints

The crux of OFT’s training routine lies in updating \( \mathbf R \) **while guaranteeing**

1. **Exact preservation** of its orthogonality (\(\|\!\cdot\,|_F=1,\;\!\|\cdot||_2=1\f)) at *every* iteration;
2. A relatively small set of latent parameters that drives these transformations.

## Take‑aways

1 *Reparameterisation*: Instead of adding noise we'd rotate the entire neuron space via an orthogonal operator—pairwise geometry’s ghost remains unaltered while still having plentiful degrees‐of‑freedom for downstream adaptation.
2 *Identity start*: Thereby we literally begin fine‑tuning from an identical pretrained state—early epochs do not unknowingly depart telescoping past converged patterns.
3 *Strictness through Cayley*: The skew‑symmetric latent variable confers efficient optimisation under hard constraints—ensuring consistent behaviour across epochs without ever leaving the special‐orthogonal manifold.


These principles make OFT preferable whenever preserving hierarchical learned structure outweighs raw parameter count savings—a tension later alleviated further by butterfly factorisation—yet at this stage they highlight why simple multiplicative rotations already carry an impressive blend of expressivity and stability.

# Drawbacks Stemming From Fixed Sparsity Partition  

Block–diagonal sparsity is introduced *by design*, splitting neurons strictly according to their index ordering.
Such imposed static partition presumes that localisation purely reflects underlying data geometry,
yet practical self‑attention mechanisms often expose correlations straddling any arbitrary split.
When ignored during training:
* **Coupling deficiency** — A channel required simultaneously 
to fuse features belonging naturally together 
is artificially divided between two separate optimisation trajectories;
the lack of direct interaction curtails task‑specific harmonics.
* **Underexplored synergies** — Since gradient backpropagation propagates through independent intra‑block pathways,
the capacity built up within one block never informs another.
This rigidity dampens any potential expansions afforded by deeper weight reconfiguration spaces;
the network remains trapped within isolated “miniarchitectures” patchworked onto pretrained backbone weights.
* **Hyperparameter brittleness** — Determining an optimal value for \(b\)
exhaustively becomes expensive because repetitions entangle training budgets,
as illustrated empirically when moving away from convenient powers-of-two values;
size allocation must juggle competing needs—smaller blocks → little interaction space
vs larger blocks → rising parameter count without commensurate expressive benefit.
* **Inductive bias misfit** — AB effects induced through fixed partitions functionally resemble stacking fewer attention heads 
or smaller kernel footprints;
they impose unintended priors unsuitable for generic vision or language downstream tasks,
as shown experimentally when evaluating Maximum Improvement Rate metrics post finetuning.



Collectively these drawbacks confine original OFT’s practical applicability:
while model storage shrinks dramatically,
a rigid sparsity stylized architecture frustrates distinct forms of representation needed by state‑of‑the‐art sight & language adaptation pipelines.

Butterfly Topologies Originated From FFT Algorithms  

# Recursive Factorisation Principle Behind Radix–2 FFT  

The Cooley–Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm is a canonical example of how structured recursion can dramatically reduce computational complexity.  At its core lies a *decimation‑in‑time* strategy that recursively breaks a large discrete Fourier transform (DFT) into pairs of smaller DFTs.  This recursion gives rise to a characteristic *butterfly* topology— a bipartite graph of interactions between input and output nodes— that links every input node to every output node while using only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zero entries at each stage.  The following discussion traces this principle and shows how it underpins the butterfly‑based orthogonal parameterisations introduced in parameter‑efficient orthogonal finetuning (BOFT).

## The Decimation‑in‑Time Recursion  

Consider computing an \(N\)-point DFT,
\[
X_k=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_n\,e^{-2\pi i k n/N},\qquad k=0,\dots,N-1.
\]
Assume \(N\) is a power of two, \(N=2^p\).  The decimation‑in‑time scheme groups samples of the input sequence into even and odd indices:
\[
x_{2r}=e_r,\qquad x_{2r+1}=o_r,\qquad r=0,\dots,N/2-1.
\]
Substituting into the DFT expression yields
\[
X_k=\sum_{r=0}^{N/2-1}\Bigl(e_r+o_r\,e^{-2\pi i k/N}\Bigr)e^{-2\pi i k r/(N/2)}.
\]
Recognising that \(e^{-2\pi i k/N}=e^{-2\pi i k/(2(N/2))}\), one observes that computing \(X_k\) requires two \((N/2)\)-point DFTs— once on \(\{e_r+o_r\,e^{-2\pi i k/N}\}_{r}\) and once on \(\{e_r-o_r\,e^{-2\pi i k/N}\}_{r}\)— followed by‐by‐by twiddle‐factor multiplication and re‑ordering.  
Repeating this splitting \(p=\log_2 N\) times recursively reduces the problem to many \(1\times1\) DFTs (trivial scalar multiplications).  Each recursive step halves the size of the sub‑DFTs and introduces so‑called *butterfly operations* that combine pairs of intermediate results.

## Butterfly Operations as Sparse Matrices  

A single butterfly operation takes two complex numbers \((a,b)\), multiplies one by a twiddle factor \(w=e^{-2\pi i (j)/N}\), then outputs
\[
(a+b\,w,\;a-b\,w).
\]
This can be represented by a sparse \(2\times 2\) matrix
\[
B(w)=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & w\\[4pt]
1 & -w
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Although \(B(w)\) has only four non‑zero entries in its mere four–element array, its action mixes information between its two input channels.  When one stacks many such butterflies across all recursion levels, the resulting overall transform matrix is dense: every output is influenced by every input.  Importantly, each level contributes only \(\mathcal{O}(d)\) non‑zeros; since there are \(\log_2 d\) levels, the total number of stored parameters needed to represent the entire transformation scales as \(\mathcal{O}(d \log d)\).

## Graphical Interpretation of Information Flow  

In BOFT’s *information transmission framework*, this recursive decomposition corresponds to routing data across a grid graph with nodes arranged in \(d\times (\log d +1)\) layers (Figure 3 of the paper).  Each layer represents an intermediate stage of butterfly computation; directed edges denote communication across levels via non-zero entries of the butterfly matrices.  Because every pair of nodes at successive layers is connected through a single edge determined by a two‑node butterfly, one guarantees *dense connectivity*: there exists at least one directed path from any input node (layer 0) to any output node (layer ∗). Yet we do not require dense adjacency within any level— each level remains sparse with exactly \(d+1 = \mathcal{O}(d)\) non-zero edges (one per input node). Hence multiplying masks along layers constitutes transmitting information through an efficient interconnect network.

## Why Butterfly Works for Orthogonal Parameterisation  

The butterflies used in BOFT are an orthogonal analogue of those in FFT; all scalar twiddle factors are replaced by appropriately parameterised small orthogonal blocks.  
Equation (3)-(4) of the paper defines each *butterfly factor* for block size \(k=2^l<d$:
\[
B^F(k)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
{\rm diag}(\mathbf d_1)&{\rm diag}(\mathbf d_2)\\[4pt]
{\rm diag}(\mathbf d_3)&{\rm diag}(\mathbf d_4)
\\end{bmatrix},
\tag{3}
\]
where every vector \(\mathbf d_i \in \mathbb R^{k/2}\).  
Recursively assembling these factors yields an overall *butterfly matrix*:
\[
B(d)=
\btilde B(d,d)
\btilde B(d,d/2)\cdots\btilde B(d,2),
\tag{4}
\]
which has exactly \(d+\frac d 6+\cdots+\frac d {6^{p-1}} = \mathcal O(d)\) free parameters when each block is constrained to be orthogonal via Cayley parametrization or simple rotations.  Multiplying such matrices mirrors exactly the application of many sequential butterflies in FFT—but now each retains strict orthogonality, guaranteeing that their product remains an orthogonal matrix as required by BOFT.

Hence, BOFT achieves two goals simultaneously:

1. **Dense representational power** – By forwarding information through logarithmically many layers of butterflies, any graded combination of input neurons’ projections can be obtained: effectively simulating a full \(d\times d\) orthogonal transformation.
   
2. **Parameter economy** – Each layer contributes only linearly many trainable parameters; over all levels this scales as \(\mathcal O(d\,{\rm log}\,d)\), far below \(\mathcal O(d^2)\).

## Summary  

The radially recursive decimation‑in‑time strategy underlying radix–two FFT produces butterfly topologies that mediate dense transformation using few sparse interactions—a concept naturally imported into BOFT’s parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning framework.  By reorganising such sparse interactions into layers where each butterfly remains an orthogonal block, BOFT harnesses exactly what Cooley–Tukey algorithm provides: fast construction of dense linear maps with guaranteed structural properties—here minimal parameter storage and preserved spectral norms—while preserving all essential angular relationships among neurons during downstream adaptation of foundation models.(Positioning terms within spaces captured by allowed transformations makes training faster; meanwhile it keeps inference latency unchanged.)
