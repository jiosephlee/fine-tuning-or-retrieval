Introduction to Text‑to‑Image Diffusion

# Emergence of large‑scale latent‑space diffusion models  
Recent advances in generative modeling have brought large text‑to‑image diffusion pipelines into prominence.\nStable Diffusion \cite{rombach2022high} and Imagen \cite{saharia2022photorealistic} exemplify this trend, delivering photorealistic outputs when conditioned on textual descriptions written in natural language.\nThese networks typically learn a high‑dimensional latent representation of images and employ a denoising diffusion process governed by a powerful cross‑attention mechanism that ties visual embeddings to language embeddings extracted by pretrained CLIP encoders.\nThe resulting ecosystems have been built upon billions of paired image–text examples annotated either directly through human curation (as in CLIP) or via unsupervised large taxonomies (as in LAION), allowing the model parameters—oftentimes exceeding several hundred million—to encode an immense volume of visual knowledge.\nBecause training such massive architectures demands extensive compute budgets and extensive data curation pipelines, researchers usually freeze many components during downstream deployment while seeking efficient ways to adapt the learned behavior without retraining from scratch.\nThis growing landscape creates compelling opportunities for targeted fine‑tuning protocols that respect both memory constraints and real‑world deployment speed.\n
Latent‐space photorealistic generators currently perform most competitive results because operating directly in continuous latent vectors reduces computational overhead compared against pixel‑space generation.\nThe mapping between distributional priors over noisy latents and realistic images becomes manageable through teacher–student architecture—GLIDE employs VQ–GAN encoders for quantized latents \cite{esser2021taming}, whereas Stable Diffusion uses an auto‑encoder trained jointly with a U–Net denoiser network on top of perplexity‐based masks.\nThe downstream richness arises from these fusion techniques because operations such as self‐attention across time steps propagate conditioning signals coherently through the network layers thereby mediating semantic concepts encoded by language tokens.\nThe combination of expressive nonlinearities (Swish/SiLU activation), residual blocks carrying skip connections along noisy progressive steps,\nand recurrent cross-attention underlines why these predictors capture fine semantic details yet preserve infinities contained within learned internal representations.\nIn short, these capabilities motivate research into tailoring variants of generative priors for niche industry requirements without bagging architectural redesigns.\n

2 Representations Inside Neural Networks

Statistical characterization of how neurons are organized inside a network is essential when attempting principled finetuning or designing regularizers. In particular, recent work has shown that neuron weights tend naturally toward living on a unit hypersphere when they are implicitly normalized by cosine similarity during forward propagation or training analysis. This observation motivates treating each neuron as a point on an $S^{d-1}$ sphere and reason about learning dynamics in terms of angles rather than magnitudes. Two key findings underpinning this view are (a) directional relationships overwhelmingly dominate semantic capacity while raw magnitudes contribute little information, and (b) preserving pairwise angular relationships measured by a global scalar called **hyperspherical energy** can help retain performance after finetuning. The following sections dissect these ideas mathematically and empirically so you may see how they emerge from concrete definitions used in modern diffusion‑based generative models.

# Vector form of fully connected layer weights

A fully‑connected layer can naturally be written as a matrix $\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ where $d$ is the dimensionality of both input vectors $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and output neurons, while $n$ denotes the number of neurons (or output channels). Each column $\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ represents one neuron’s receptive vector; feeding an input $\bm{x}$ yields an $n$‑dimensional feature map $\bm z=\bm W^{\top}\bm x$, i.e.
$$
\bm z = 
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
w_{1}^{\top}\\ \vdots \\ w_{n}^{\top}
\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm W^{\top}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\ \vdots \\ x_{d}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm x}.
$$
In many large‑scale back‑propagation regimes this forward pass is effectively governed by inner products $w_{i}^{\top}x$, which are linear functions of both magnitude and direction but become dominated by angle once filters grow large during training. Thus treating each $\bm w_i$ as a directed vector captures all relevant information if we deliberately normalize them onto a shared manifold—namely, the unit sphere—by setting $\hat{\bm w}_{i}=\bm w_i/\lVert \bm w_i\rVert$. This “vector‐on‑hypersphere” representation is foundational for defining angular similarities later on.


# Cosine similarity between two neurons

Once neurons are projected onto $S^{d-1}$ via $\hat{\bm w}_i$, their relative orientation can be quantified by **cosine similarity**:
$$
\cos(\theta_{ij}) = \langle \hat{\bm w}_i,\hat{\bm w}_j\rangle 
= \frac{\lVert \hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\rVert^2}{2},
$$
where $\theta_{ij}\in[0,\pi]$ indicates the acute angle between them. Because all vectors lie on the unit sphere, $\lvert \cos(\theta_{ij})\rvert$ captures exactly how parallel or antiparallel two neurons are—an entirely angle‑based notion devoid of scale information.


The inner product used in standard forward propagation reduces mathematically to $\,w_j^\top x = \lVert w_j\rVert\,\lVert x\rVert\,\cos(\alpha_j)$ where $\alpha_j$ is the angle between $w_j$ and $x$. During training iterative weight updates change both magnitude $\lVert w_j\rVert$ and direction $\hat{\bm w}_j$. Empirical evidence later shows updating solely directions—or equivalently rotating all neurons together—can alter semantics more cleanly than altering magnitudes.


# Defining hyperspherical energy as sum over pairwise cosine distances

To summarize local geometric configuration among many neurons on a single layer one may aggregate all pairwise cosine similarities into a single scalar called **hyperspherical energy**:
$$
E_{\text{he}}(\bm W)
:= \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^n 
\Bigl[\bigl(2\,\bigl(1-\langle \hat{\bm w}_{i},\hat{\bm w}_{j}\rangle\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr]^{-\,1}
= \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^n 
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^{-\,1}.
$$
The summand equals $1/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ which goes high when two normals become close (small Euclidean distance on sphere) – i.e., when two neurons become nearly parallel – thus penalizing tight clusters and rewarding angular spread. When all rows are uniformly spread around $S^{d-1}$, every inter‑neuron distance is similar and $E_{\text{he}}$ attains its minimum value zero if one counts only relative differences under an orthogonal transformation (as discussed below). Importantly, since each term depends only on direction and dot product at most linearly on magnitude before normalization, hyperspherical energy is invariant under global scaling or singular orthogonal rotations applied identically across all rows.


An explicit property pivotal for finetuning theory here is:

> **Invariance under Orthogonal Transformations**  
> Let $\operatorname{O}(d)$ denote the set of real orthogonal matrices ($QQ^\top=I$). For any orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$,  
> $$E_{\text{he}}\!\bigl(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W\bigr)=E_{\text{he}}\!\bigl(\mathbf W\bigr),$$  
> because multiplying every row by an orthogonal matrix multiplies each row vector by same rotation/reflection without changing pairwise angles among them or their norms.


Consequently any procedure that applies a *layer‑shared* orthogonal transform preserves hypothetically critical relational structure captured by $E_{\text{he}}$, even though every individual row has been rotated.



# Why energy matters – empirical evidence linking it to downstream quality

The hypothesis driving orthogonal finetuning is precisely that **preserving hyperspherical energy** maintains generative fidelity after fine-grained adaptation such as subject‑driven generation or additional control signals embedded into diffusion steps.
Two types of evidence tilt this assertion:

1. **Post‑hoc Recall Tests**  
   In toy autoencoder experiments built around flower images—a simple representation demonstrates that extracting *directions* only suffices reconstructing inputs while discarding magnitude yields almost perfect images post‑training. A formal reconstruction measurement showed variations across three methods:
      * raw inner product (training mode);  
      * magnitude alone;  
      * angle alone after projection onto unit sphere.
   Numerical results reported missing amplitude did not spoil semantic fidelity whereas mounting direction alone carried nearly all content (see Figure 1). Thus neuron direction encodes semantics while hypotesis tests confirm directional preservation correlating strongly with generative realism.


2. **Finite‐dimensional Diffusion Finetunes**  
   When performing subject‑driven finetuning using DreamBooth style objectives aimed at reconstructing few training samples while preserving prior generations ("class‑specific prior preservation"), minimal loss metrics alone poorly correlate with final visual performance because small Euclidean changes may still scramble angles arbitrarily leading to identity loss or hallucinations. Adapting these networks with orthogonal matrices per layer ensures minimal Movement Excluding scale adjustments (\[Equation (…)\]), producing far faster convergence rates—in subject driven tasks about one tenth the iterations needed by LoRA or baseline DreamBooth—and higher CLIP/ DINO based identity scores across multiple random seeds (Table X).

3. **Control Consistency Quantities**  
   For controllable generation conditioned on Canny edges or segmentation maps there arises an objective: compute *control consistency*, i.e., regenerate control features from generated image via off‑the–shelf edge detectors/segmentation models then compare against original inputs using IoU/F₁ metrics per class or landmark errors ($L_2$ distances). Experiments show orthogonally finetuned models achieve unprecedented consistency while remaining faithful at image fidelity scores comparable or surpassing ControlNet/ T₂I‑Adapter baselines (Table Y). This result proves maintaining inter–neuron angular patterns protects mapping accuracy between external control signals and generated outputs.


Taken together these observations suggest preserving hyperspherical energy during finetune acts as a robust regularizer ensuring cognitive structures derived during pretraining persist intact even as the network adapts new tasks. Hence orthogonal finetuning—by fixing offls’ geometry—is theoretically sound (by invariance) and empirically effective (§4–5).

# 3 Why Rotating Neurons Works  

The core intuition behind Orthogonal‑Finetuning is simple yet powerful:  
neuron directions encode the semantic concepts recognised by a trained model  
while their magnitudes play a far smaller role.   
By rotating all neurons in a layer together—an operation that preserves every
pairwise angle—one can steer the network toward new behaviours without
breaking the internal geometric structure learned during pretraining.
Below we spell out the empirical evidence supporting this view,
the intuition gained from signal‑processing analogies,
and finally prove mathematically why an orthonormal change keeps **hyperspherical
energy**—the sum of all pairwise cosine similarities—unchanged.



## # Toy Auto‑Encoder Experiment  

A light‑weight convolutional auto‑encoder was trained on a set of flower
images using the standard linear inner–product \(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\)
to compute feature maps inside every layer.\(^1\)
After convergence we performed three different “read‑outs’’ on a held‑out
image without retraining:
\[
\underbrace{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}_{\text{inner–product}}
\,;\qquad
\underbrace{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}_{\text{magnitude}}
\,;\qquad
\underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}}
          _{\text{angular (cosine)}} .
\]
The outcome was striking.
The reconstructed images obtained using only angular information were nearly
identical to those produced with raw inner products,
while reconstruction based solely on magnitudes was essentially noise–like.
In other words, once trained,
the learning system had turned most of its representational power into
the *direction* fields \(\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\),
with \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}\).
Magnitude variations contributed very little usable semantic signal.
This toy experiment shows empirically that manipulating neuron **angles** is
the route through which a neural network alters its behaviour.



## # Fourier Phase Analogy  

Fourier analysis offers another familiar illustration.
Any two‑dimensional image \(I(\boldsymbol{x})\) can be decomposed into sinusoidal
basis functions:
\[
I(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{u,v}A_{u,v}
e^{\,2\pi i(u x+v y)} ,
\]
where \(A_{u,v}=M_{u,v}e^{i\phi_{u,v}}\)
separates into *magnitude* \(M_{u,v}=\|A_{u,v}\|\)
and *phase* \(\phi_{u,v}=\arg(A_{u,v})\).
If one keeps the magnitudes \(M_{u,v}\) fixed at random values yet restores
the original phases \(\phi_{u,v}\), the resulting image is impressively close
to \(I(\boldsymbol{x})\);
conversely, replacing phases with random values while keeping magnitudes fixes
loses almost all object identity information.
Thus **phase shifts**—the analogues of neuronal direction rotations—carry most
of an image’s structural meaning.
This perspective aligns beautifully with our observations:
rotating neurons changes their directional codes, reshaping semantic content
while leaving *overall* total energy unchanged.



## # Invariance Proof  

Let us formalise why an orthonormal transformation leaves *hyperspherical
energy* invariant.
Consider a fully connected layer whose weights form an \(d\times n\) matrix

\[
\bm W=\bigl[\bm w_1,\dotsc,\bm w_n\bigr] ,
\qquad 
\bm w_i\in\mathbb R^{d}.
\]

Normalising every column gives \(\hat{\bm w}_i=
\dfrac{\bm w_i}{\,\lVert \bm w_i\rVert_{\!2}}\).
The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) associated with this layer is defined as

\[
 \operatorname{HE}(\bm W)
   := \sum_{\substack{i,j\\ i<j}}
      \frac{1}{\, \lVert \hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\rVert_{\!2} } .
   \tag{1}
\]

A convenient equivalent form replaces Euclidean distances between unit vectors
by their cosine similarities:
since \( 
    \lVert \hat{\bm u}-\hat{\bm v}\rVert_{\!2}
      = \sqrt{\, 2(1-\!\langle \hat{\bm u},\,\hat{\bm v}\rangle ) }
    \)
each term becomes an increasingly large value when adjacent neurons become
more parallel.
Hence minimizing HE tends toward uniformly spread neurons,
while here we will exploit its *rigidity*  
to preserve pre–trained inter‑neuron relations.



### Orthogonal Transformation

Let \(R^\top R = I_d = RR^\top\)
be any real orthogonal matrix (\(R^\top = R^{-1}\)).
We claim

> **Lemma.**  
> For any \(R \in O(d)\),
> \(\operatorname{HE}(R\,W)=\operatorname{HE}(W)\).

*Proof.*  
Rotations do not alter norms:
for every column,

\(  
   \lVert R\,\hat{\bm w}_i\rVert_{\!2}
    = \sqrt{
           (R\,\hat{\bm w}_i)^\top(R\,\hat{\bm w}_i)
          }
    =   \sqrt{
           \hat{\bm w}_i^\top(R^\top R)\,\hat{\bm w}_i}
    =   \sqrt{
           \hat{\bm w}_i^\top I_d\,\hat{\bm w}_i}
    =   1 .
```
Hence \(R\,W=RW=(Rw_1,\dotsc,rw_n)\) has exactly the same unit‐norm columns,
so its normalized version is simply 
\(R[\hat{\bm w}_1,\dotsc,\hat{\bm w}_n]\). 

Now evaluate any term in Eq.(1):

```
 ⟨R\,\hat{\bm u},\,R\,\hat{\bm v}⟩  
   = (R\,\hat{\bm u})^\top(R\,\hat{\bm v})
   = \hat{\bm u}^\top(R^\top R)\,\hat{\bm v}
   =        \langle \hat{\bm u},\,\hat{\bm v}\rangle .
```

Thus **every cosine similarity—and consequently every Euclidean distance—
between pairs of neurons remains identical** after applying \(R.\)** Consequently every summand in Eq.(1)
is untouched, implying HE(RW)=HE(W).
∎



### Consequence for Finetuning

If a pre‑trained weight matrix \(W^0,\!)
 has some value HE\(^{0}=E^0=\operatorname{HE}(W^0).\)
Any finite rotation/reflection \(R:\mathbb R^{d\times d}\rightarrow O(d)\)
satisfies  

\(E := E^0.\)

Hence any finetuned weight matrix constrained to equal
\(W=W_0’=RW^0+S?\!            // actually RW^0\nor more generally,

`RW^0` exactly inherits all pair-wise cosine similarities,
so its hyperspherical structure matches halfmodologically found shape;
therefore all higher‑level processes relying upon these relational cues --
exploited during text‑driven sampling cycles -- remain intact.
This invariance explains why rotating entire layers works efficiently:
semantic ordering stays correct while absolute orientations are free for adaptation.

`OFT`: Core Finite‑Matching Procedure  

# Objective operation  
Orthogonal Finetuning seeks to keep the *hyperspherical energy* of every neuron layer unchanged while allowing the model to learn new behavior required for downstream text‑to‑image tasks such as subject‑driven generation or controllable generation. Hyperspherical energy captures how uniformly neuron vectors spread over the unit hypersphere, quantifying pairwise angular similarities rather than raw Euclidean norms. For a fully connected layer whose weight matrix is denoted $\mathcal W=\{\mathbf w_1,\dots ,\mathbf w_n\}\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$, we first map every neuron vector onto the unit sphere,
\[
\hat{\mathbf w}_i=\frac{\mathbf w_i}{\|\mathbf w_i\|}\,, \qquad i=1,\dots ,n\, ,
\]
and define
\[
\text{HE}(\mathcal W)=\sum_{\substack{i,j\\i\neq j}}\frac{1}{\bigl\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\bigr\|}\!.
\tag{E}
\]
Because $\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|=\sqrt{2(1-\cos \theta_{ij})}$ depends solely on the angle $\theta_{ij}$ between neurons $i$ and $j$, HE purely accounts for relational orientation within a layer–exactly what most semantics embeddings encode thanks to what prior work has termed “angular similarity.” Empirically, changing neuron directions while fixing magnitudes preserves semantic expressivity more reliably than arbitrarily scaling weights; this was shown both in toy experiments involving autoencoders and through analogy with phase spectra delivering full image reconstruction when magnitudes are replaced randomly.

The core optimisation goal therefore becomes minimizing how much HE differs after finetuning,
\[
\min_{\mathcal W}\;\bigl|\text{HE}(\mathcal W)-\text{HE}(\mathcal W^0)\bigr|\!,
\tag{F}
\]
where $\mathcal W^0$ denotes the pretrained parameters already proven worthwhile on text guidance tasks. By demanding equality—or arbitrarily tiny discrepancy—in HE before and after training, OFT guarantees that neuron angles remain unchanged up to an admissible transformation therefore maintaining internal relational geometry required for faithful generation from textual prompts while letting the model adapt semantics elsewhere via its decoder networks or integrated conditioning modules.



# Equivalence class  
Equation (F) admits an infinite family of optimizers because angular similarities are invariant under simultaneous left‐multiplication by any full‑rank orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$. Explicitly,
if $\mathcal W=\mathbf R\, \mathcal W^0$, with $\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I$, then every individual angle satisfies 
$\cos(\angle(\hat{\mathbf r_i},\hat{\mathbf r_j}))=
   \cos(\angle(\hat{\mathbf w}^{\,0}_i,\hat{\mathbf w}^{\,0}_j))$,
hence $\text{HE}(\mathcal W)=\text{HE}(\mathcal W^0)$ and equation (F) attains its minimum value $0$. Thus **the entire set**
\[
E=\bigl\{\;\mathbf R\, \mathcal W^0:\;\mathbf R^\top \mathbf R=\mathbf I\bigr\}
     =O(d)\cdot \mathcal W^0
     ,
\]
constitutes an equivalence class whereby all members share identical hyperspherical energies—and consequently identical internal angular structures—yet differ dramatically in orientation space thanks to reflection or rotation degrees of freedom inherent in $O(d)$.



This mathematical nuance translates directly into practical benefits during finetuning:
any choice within $E$ produces identical initial contractive behaviour (the forward pass equivariantly recomposes features), yet downstream losses steer $\mathbf R$ toward orientations fulfilling task‐specific objectives without altering HE’s invariants. When combined with auxiliary regularizers used throughout subject‑driven generation (e.g., reconstruction loss plus prior preservation) or controllable generation (control consistency loss), one obtains *fast convergence*: gradients mainly adjust φ directions smoothly respecting camouflaged constraints versus arbitrary magnitude tweaks.



# Implementation scheme  
In practice, OFT implements this elegant theory by endowing every trainable attention or linear module with one *layer–shared* orthogonal transform $\mathbf R$, initialized identically at every iteration so that initially $\mathcal W=\mathcal W^0$. Concretely,

1. **Identity initialization**  
   At epoch zero set $\mathbf Q=\boldsymbol 0$, whence  
   \[
   \mathbf R=\frac{\boldsymbol I+\boldsymbol Q}{\,\boldsymbol I-\boldsymbol Q\,}=\,\boldsymbol I\, .
   \]
   Consequently every modified forward pass equals its pretrained counterpart,
   guaranteeing exact reproductions before learning begins.



2. **Gradient‐based evolution**  
   For backpropagated losses built upon reconstructions, control matches or CLIP fidelity metrics, gradients flow conventionally through all network parameters—including the entries explicitly constrained to form $\boldsymbol Q$. To keep computation modest yet expressive enough for large hidden dimensions $d$, OFT adopts two complementary strategies:

   * **Cayley parameterisation**  
     Any skew‐symmetric matrix $\boldsymbol Q=-\,{\boldsymbol Q}^{\!\top}$ satisfies  
     \[
       (\boldsymbol I+\boldsymbol Q)(\, \boldsymbol I-\boldsymbol Q\, )^{-1}\in O(d),
     \]
     an inexpensive differentiable cubic warping compared against naïve Gram–Schmidt procedures whose cost balloon quadratically.



   * **Block–diagonal reduction**  
     A single large $d\times d$ rotation can be decomposed into $r$ smaller blocks,
     \[
         \mathbf R=
          {\rm diag}\!\bigl(\!\underbrace{{}^{r}\!},

OFT : Efficient Parameterisation  

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models contain millions of neuron weights whose directions encode semantic meaning very faithfully—their pairwise angles remain largely unchanged during training and therefore preserve what has been learned during pretraining.  
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) exploits this fact by adapting every layer through an **orthogonal transformation** applied multiplicatively to its weight matrix \(W^{0}\).  
Because any such transformation preserves all pairwise angles (\(\mathrm{HE}(W)=\mathrm{HE}(W^{0})\)), the transformed network keeps the hyperspherical energy exactly unchanged while flexibly redirecting neurons towards a downstream task’s needs.

Below we explain how OFT makes this transformation learnable while remaining computationally lightweight,
its trade–off knob “block size” which lets us control flexibility,
how many trainable variables this entails compared with competing methods,
and why this design ultimately reduces finetuning time without sacrificing fidelity.



### # Cayley mapping  

Orthogonality supplies strong regularisation—it would otherwise require enforcing quadratic constraints on every entry of \(W\), an expensive operation during optimisation.
A classic trick sidesteps this problem entirely via **Cayley’s parametrisation**:
given any skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q=-Q^{\top}\),

\[
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
\]

is guaranteed to lie in the special orthogonal group \(\mathrm{SO}(D)\); i.e.,
\(R^{\top}R=RR^{\top}=I,\;\det(R)=+1.\)

All continuous degrees of freedom required for STO reachability come exclusively from entries below the main diagonal of \(Q;\;
={'#'\ == }\;\dim(\mathrm SO(D))=\tfrac12D(D{-}1).\)
Thus learning merely eighteen dozen scalar entries suffices even when transforming extremely high‑dimensional layers  
(e.g., a layer operating on 768 features needs \(768\times767/2\simeq293\,K\) learnable variables).

Cayley’s map is differentiable everywhere except when \((I-Q)\) becomes singular—an event avoided during training because optimisation initially places \(Q\simeq0,\Rightarrow R\simeq I,\det(R)\simeq +\,.\).
Furthermore an initial zero matrix guarantees that every finite step starts at the pretrained model,
and ordinary backpropagation propagates gradients through the inverse
by standard automatic differentiation engines.

In practice neural architectures expose large input dimensions which makes handling billions of rotation terms prohibitive.

each channel often processes information independently.
Thus an optimal compromise is achieved by splitting the full rotation into independent pieces applied separately along non‑overlapping subspaces:
for a chosen integer “block size” \(b=r^{-\,}Z/d,\)

\[

If every bloc performs an individualized rotation,

COFT：Constrained Rotational Updates  

In orthogonal finetuning, the weight matrix at layer ℓ is modified by multiplying its pretrained weights \(W^0_\ell = [w_1^0 , w_2^0 , … , w_n^0]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) with an orthogonal matrix \(R_\ell\in O(d)\):
\( W_\ell = R_\ell\,W^0_\ell.\)
The forward pass remains unchanged because only the angles among neurons are altered while their norms stay fixed.
The unconstrained choice \(R_\ell=A_\ell/\!\!\sqrt{A_\ell^\top A_\ell}\) built via Cayley transformation satisfies exact orthogonality, yet an arbitrary rotation could move neuron directions far away from their initial positions and consequently upset delicate semantic structures learned during pretraining.
Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT) remedies this by enforcing an additional radius bound that anchors every trained matrix close—yet still flexibly—around its identity counterpart.



# Rigorous Constraint  

The essential requirement introduced in COFT is  
\[   
   \bigl\lVert R-\mathbf{I}\bigr\rVert_{\!*}\;\leqslant\;\varepsilon ,
   \\]
where \(\mathbf{I}\in{\mathbb R}^{d\times d}\) denotes the identity and  
the operator norm obeys \(0<\varepsilon<1.\)
Geometrically this stipulates that every column vector of \(R-\mathbf{I}\)—that is, every displacement vector from an original neuron direction—is confined inside an \(d\)-dimensional hypersphere centered at zero with radius equal to \(ε.\)
By restricting how far each weight vector may twist away from its pretrained orientation,
the finite‑step updates produced during back‑propagation cannot erupt into wildly different semantic regimes,
and therefore convergence becomes more reliable.
This direct diameter control replaces implicit assumptions behind tiny learning rates
which otherwise act as an informal multiplier bound whereas here an explicit inequality governs every rotation.

# Re‑scaled OFT : Amplifying Flexibility  

Large text-to-image diffusion models rely heavily on the internal representations learned during pretraining. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) preserves these representations by rotating neuron weights jointly while maintaining their pairwise angles and thus their hyperspherical energy. However, a rotation alone may leave some representational capacity unused: every neuron’s magnitude remains fixed at its pretraining value. To widen the behavioural repertoire of a finetuned diffusion model without disturbing its well‐balanced geometry, the Re‑scaled Orthogonal Finetuning (Re‑scaled OFT) augments OFT with an element-wise scaling matrix $\\boldsymbol{D}$. This section explains how this simple addition works, how it can be implemented efficiently, and why it consistently boosts performance at almost no extra computational cost.

## Diagonal Scale \(\\boldsymbol{D}'\)

In standard OFT every weight vector \(\\mathbf{w}_i\\) is transformed by an orthogonal matrix:

\[
\\mathbf{z}\\;=\;(\\boldsymbol{R}\\cdot\\boldsymbol{W}^0)^\\top\\mathbf{x},
\]

where \(\boldsymbol{W}^{0}\in\\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\) holds the pretrained weights,
\(\\boldsymbol{R}\\in \\mathbb{O}(d)\) is layer‑shared and learned.
Because orthogonal transformations preserve pairwise dot products after
normalization,
\[
\\mathrm{HE}(\\boldsymbol{W})\;=\;
\\sum_{i\\neq j}\\Vert \\hat{\mathbf w}_i- \\hat{\mathbf w}_j \\Vert^{-1}
\]
remains exactly equal to that of \(\\boldsymbol{W}^{0}\):
all angular relationships stay unchanged and thus so does “hyperspherical
energy”.

The missing flexibility lies in magnitudes:
the Euclidean length of each neuron \(|| \\mathbf w_i ||\) never changes.
Scale changes do not interfere with angles because when computing
\(\\hat{\mathbf w}_i= {\\mathbf w}_i / || {\\mathbf w}_i ||\),
magnitudes cancel out.
Consequently adding diag­onal scalings does **not** alter hyperspherical
energy:
\[
\\mathrm{HE}(\\boldsymbol R\, \\boldsymbol W^0 \\boldsymbol D)
= \\mathrm{HE}(\\boldsymbol R\, { \\boldsymbol W^0})
= \\mathrm{HE}(\\boldsymbol W^0),
\]
since multiplying \(\\boldsymbol W^0\) on the right by a diagonal matrix
\(\\boldsymbol D =  \mathrm {diag}(s_1,\dots , s_n)\) only rescales
each column independently–the direction of each column stays untouched,
hence all normalized vectors remain unchanged.
Thus we inherit exactly the same preservation property of standard
OFT while gaining an additional degree of freedom: one positive scalar
\(s_i>0\) per neuron describes how much it should be amplified or dampened.
This diagonal scaling serves as a lightweight companion that can adapt
the magnitude distribution of neurons across layers,
allowing subtle adjustments that improve expressivity without breaking
the careful geometry preserved by orthogonality.



## Implementation Hint

The forward pass of Re‑scaled OFT replaces Eq.(6) from the main method
with:

\[
   \\mathbf z=({\\boldsymbol R}\\,{\\boldsymbol W}^0)\,{\\boldsymbol D})^{\\top}\\mathbf x,
   \qquad 
   {\\boldsymbol D}= {\rm diag}(s_1,\ldots ,s_n),
   \;\;\;\;
   s_i>0 .
\]

During training both \( \\boldsymbol R \) (parameterized via Cayley’s transform)
and all diagonal entries \(s_i\) are optimized simultaneously by gradient descent.
Because every neuron now has its own learnable scalar factor,
a straightforward way to enforce positivity is to keep an unconstrained
parameter \(a_i\in \\mathbb R\) per neuron and set

\[
   s_i = {\rm exp}(a_i)\quad  \text{or}\quad 
   s_i = {\rm softplus}(a_i).
\]

In practice initializing all \(a_i=0\), i.e.,
\(s_i=1,\;\forall i\), guarantees that learning starts from exactly
the pretrained configuration—identical to vanilla OFT—while allowing the optimizer
to decide whether amplifying or attenuating any particular neuron helps during finetuning.
Because only \(n\) new scalars are introduced per layer,
the parameter budget barely grows compared with standard OFT;
for example, adding one scalar per channel amounts to roughly one percent gain in memory usage.



## Result

Extensive experiments confirm that Re‑scaled OFT consistently outperforms plain OFT across both considered downstream settings—subject-driven generation and controllable generation—
while keeping training time, convergence behaviour and inference speed unchanged.
In subject-driven generation tasks governed by DreamBooth’s loss,
the inclusion of the scaling matrix afforded higher CLIP‑I and DINO scores,
improving subjective fidelity without sacrificing prompt adherence.
Likewise, on controllable generation benchmarks (Canny edge → image,
segmentation map → image, landmark → face),
Re‑scaled OFT achieved noticeably lower control error metrics (e.g.,
reduced landmark deviation or higher segmentation IoU)
and produced sharper images overall.
Because this gain comes from merely adding one scalar per output feature,
it imposes essentially negligible computational overhead:
matrix multiplications required during inference remain identical except for an additional inexpensive element-wise product with \( {\\boldsymbol D}\),
and gradient updates involve trivial Jacobians through exponentiation/softplus.
The resulting performance trajectory shows smooth convergence identical
to plain OFT’s fast learning curve while surpassing its final metrics by a modest margin—
precisely “all‐but‐negligible” extra costs as reported in the supplementary analysis.



In summary, augmenting Orthogonal Finetuning with a simple diagonal scale term enables diffusion models to fine-tune both directions and magnitudes of neurons while preserving hyperspherical geometry.
This modest adjustment unlocks additional expressive power at almost no price:
a single additional scalar per neuron – revealing an elegant way to sharpen finetuned diffusion models without compromising stability or efficiency.

## # 4‑A Subject‑Driven Generation

When asked simply “draw my friend wearing a hat”, simply shifting neurons arbitrarily destroys subtle inter‑feature relationships that were carefully shaped during massive pretraining rounds (see Sec.~3).  
Instead whether one rotates each neuron's direction **a little**, all intra‑layer angular relationships stay intact—i.e., the *hyperspherical energy* remains exactly the same—and therefore reconstruction fidelity remains unimpaired even while adapting locally for novel subjects.

• A effective consistency metric built upon backward extraction provides an objective barometer unprecedentedly transparent for benchmarking individualized controls;



These outcomes collectively show why larger scaling artifacts such as arbitrary rank addition do not always help when preserving delicate topological relationships – whereas orthogonality builds synergy explaining leaps possible without sacrificing speed.

**5 Discussion AND Practical Lessons**

Large‑scale text‑to‑image diffusion models capture an enormous amount of visual knowledge during pretraining, yet adapting them efficiently without erasing that knowledge remains a central challenge.  
The Orthogonal Finetuning strategy highlighted in this work offers practical insights into why an appropriately constrained update yields fast convergence, preserves generative fidelity, and incurs no runtime cost during inference. In what follows we unpack these lessons from several angles—parameter economy, training stability, architecture‐agnostic behavior, and the compositional potential of learned rotations—all grounded entirely in the formulation introduced above.

---

### # Parameter Efficiency Comparison

Full fine‑tuning modifies every weight entry of a diffusion network.  
If a convolution has \(d=128\) input channels mapping to \(n=128\) output channels, its weight tensor contains \(d\,n = 16\,384\) parameters—a substantial fraction of modern models.  
In contrast, Orthogonal Finetuning turns every fully connected layer from
\(\mathbf{W}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\)
into
\(\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^{0}\)
where \(\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) is an orthogonal matrix shared across *all* neurons in that layer
(Section 3).  
The number of trainable entries thus scales with
\[
|\!|\mathbf{R}|\!| = \tfrac{d(d-1)}{2},
\]
since an orthogonal matrix admits \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}{2}\) free parameters once one accounts for its unit‐norm columns and pairwise orthogonality constraints.
For \(d=128\) this amounts to \(8\,128\) parameters—a markedly smaller set than both full fine‑tuning and many adapter approaches.

LoRA illustrates another route toward parameter sparsity: an incremental low–rank update
\(\Delta\mathbf{W}= \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\)
with ranks \(r'\ll d,n\).  
With the same rank \(r'=8\), LoRA would introduce
\(r'(d+n)=8(128+128)=2\,048\)
trainable entries—a number not far away from Orthogonal Finetuning’s cost but achieved via an entirely different structural bias.
Furthermore, the block‑diagonal reparameterization proposed for Orthogonal Finetuning reduces \( |\!|\mathbf{R}|\!| \) proportionally by a factor \(r^{2}\),
allowing one to trade off between expressivity and parameter budget without compromising hyperspherical energy preservation.
Empirical results confirm that Affine Rotations trained under these constraints retain high semantic fidelity while still requiring more adaptations than even LoRA’s sparse updates—a testament both to their expressive power and their disciplined nature.



### # No Runtime Penalty

One primary complaint against adapter–based augmentations is an unavoidable inference overhead:
additional convolutional blocks or linear projections must still be evaluated at every sampling step.
Orthogonal Finetuning sidesteps this issue entirely:
once training concludes,
the new weight matrix simply becomes
\[
\mathbf{W} = \underbrace{\mathbf{R}}_{\text{orthogonal}}\cdot \underbrace{\mathbf{W}^{0}}_{\text{pretrained}}
            \quad .
\]
The product is computed once—at model deployment—and replaces the original weights.
During sampling no extra forward passes are required beyond what Stable Diffusion already performs;
the model’s functional graph remains unchanged.
Thus Orthogonal Finetuning achieves complexity equivalence at inference-time,
a decisive advantage when deploying on edge devices or maintaining low-latency pipelines.



### # Robustness

Full fine‑tuning or purely unconstrained Orthogonal Finetuning can sometimes drive the network into regimes where popper generations collapse,
a phenomenon attributed largely to large angular deviations from pretrained directions.
Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning augments Equation (6) with
\[
\lVert \mathbf{R}-\mathbf{I}\rVert_{F}\;\leqslant;\;\epsilon ,
}
]
wherein \(\epsilon>0\) bounds how far any neuron may rotate from its original orientation.
In practice this manifests as attractor dynamics favoring smooth adjustments;
empirically COFT consistently avoids sudden drops in generation quality observed under unrestricted finetuning models,
particularly when the training data are scarce (< 10 images per subject).
The imposed radius guarantees that even if multiple epochs attempt aggressive exploration,
the network remains tethered close enough to its initial hypothesis so that semantic representations embedded in hyperspherical angles are largely untouched.



### # Connection | Contrast

Adapters such as LoRA employ additive low–rank corrections:
\( \mathbf{\Delta W} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{\top} \),
and continuously blend original weights into updated ones through scalar hyperparameters.
Orthogonally finetuned weights instead enact a multiplicative global rotation:
\( \hat{\mathbf{W}} = (\underbrace{\mathbf{I}+(\mathbf{\Delta R})}_{=\mathbf{R}})\,\mathbf{W}^{0}
   =   \bigl[\;\underbrace{\bigl((\Delta R)\bigr)}_{\text{sparse?}}\bigr]\;\cdot\,\!
         (\underbrace{\!1}_{=\,I})\,,
}
].
When \( (\Delta R)= (\mathbf{R}- \mathbf{I}) \) has very small Frobenius norm,
its effect on any individual neuron does resemble a linear projection much like LoRA’s update,
yet because it acts *multiplicatively on all neurons simultaneously*, angular relationships—the core determinant of semantic encoding—are automatically preserved (Section 3).
LoRA cannot guarantee constant pairwise cosine similarities since its updates alter skewed components unspecifically;
by contrast OFT maintains hyperspherical energy exactly (or within $\epsilon$).
This key conceptual distinction explains why obstacles such as collapsed samples emerge more readily under additive solutions yet stay muted under targeted rotational adjustments.



### # Potential for Compound Rotations

A striking theoretical property emerges when several downstream tasks are sequentially learned:
each task produces an associated orthogonal matrix
\( \tilde{\boldsymbol R}_{k} \)
constrained by its own ε–ball around identity.
Because multiplication of two orthogonal matrices remains orthogonal,
the composite transformation
\(   {\boldsymbol R}_{\,12\dots K}=
     {\boldsymbol R}_{K}\,\dots\,{\boldsymbol R}_{1}
     =
    {\boldsymbol Q}(?) 
     ,
}
]
continues preserving hyperspherical energy along every intermediate step.
If each constituent satisfies
\(    \lVert {\boldsymbol R}_{k}-{\bf I}
        \rVert_{F}\leqslant
        \epsilon_{k},
    }
}
],
then by triangle inequality their cumulative deviation obeys
\(    
    \lVert {\boldsymbol R}_{\,12\dots K}-{\bf I}
      _F 
      
    & 
      <= 
         K\,E_{\max},   }
where \(E_{\max}=max_k\,{\epsilon_k}\).
Hence a bounded γ-setting ensures that even after learning dozens of specialized rotations—
for example one aligning representations toward facial landmarks,
another towards object pose—
the overall generator still hovers within controllable angular distance from its pretrained state.
This observation opens avenues for multi‑task compounding where one may concatenate learned rotational priors instead of retraining from scratch or deploying numerous adapters.

**Wrap‑up**

Through concise mathematical reasoning coupled with concrete empirical results,
Orthogonal Finetuning demonstrates that focusing on angular integrity—rather than wholesale weight reshaping—strikes an elegant balance between flexibility and safety during downstream adaptation.
Its light parametrics, zero overhead at test time, robustness guarantees via proximity constraints,
clear contrasts against additive adapters,
and compositional promise together make it an inviting baseline whenever high‐fidelity visual control must be infused into existing diffusion engines without sacrificing their extensive prior learning.

# 6 Open Questions & Future Directions

The Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) framework introduced earlier presents an elegant way to control large text‑to‑image diffusion models through layer‑shared orthogonal transformations. Its theoretical guarantee of preserving hyperspherical energy, its empirical stability and speed of convergence, and its negligible inference overhead make OFT an attractive alternative to existing finetuning strategies such as LoRA or ControlNet. Notwithstanding these strengths, several fundamental limitations remain unresolved, and addressing them offers promising avenues for research at the intersection of deep learning theory and practical system design. This chapter discusses three prominent open problems that stem directly from the current OFT formulation and experiments.

## # Scalability constraints imposed by graph representations

A critical bottleneck in applying OFT to state‑of‑the‑art diffusion models is the computational cost associated with maintaining orthogonality during training. In Section 4.3 of the original manuscript, the authors mitigate this issue by employing a Cayley parameterization
\[
\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1},
\quad \bm{Q}^\top = -\bm{Q},
\]
which guarantees that $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal as long as $\|\bm{Q}\|<1$ (ensuring that $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})$ is invertible). However, computing the inverse $(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ at every training step incurs $\mathcal{O}(d^3)$ complexity, where $d$ is the neuron dimensionality of a given layer. Even when combined with block‑diagonal simplifications introduced later (Section 4.4), which reduce this cost to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$ for $r$ blocks, modern diffusion models contain layers whose $d$ exceeds $10^5$. In practice, gradient updates become memory‑bound not because of the number of trainable parameters—OFT keeps this number modest—but because each update requires an expensive matrix inverse.

An additional angle on scalability originates from *graph* representations of orthogonal matrices (e.g., representing a full rotation as a product of elementary Givens rotations). One can view each Givens rotation as an edge in a sparse graph whose nodes correspond to coordinate axes. The overall transformation then amounts to multiplying many sparse rotations together—an operation that could be executed with linear complexity if carefully scheduled. While such graph‐based decompositions are well‑studied in numerical linear algebra (e.g., QR factorizations), their integration into stochastic optimization pipelines remains largely unexplored for deep learning frameworks like PyTorch or JAX where backpropagation must be performed efficiently across thousands of parallel GPUs.

Thus three intertwined research questions arise:

1. **Fast differentiable inversion** – Can we derive an alternative form of Cayley’s transform that eliminates explicit inversion yet preserves exact orthogonality? For instance, iterative refinement schemes such as Newton–Schulz could approximate inverses while allowing gradients to propagate through fewer operations.
2. **Sparse matrix representations** – Is there a compact graph‑based factorization (e.g., Rasch decomposition, Householder reflectors) that reduces both latency and memory bleed during backpropagation? How do such factorizations interact with automatic differentiation engines?
3. **Dynamic block scheduling** – In block‑diagonal OFT, choosing $r$ trades off flexibility against computation time. Could an adaptive scheduler select block sizes per layer on the fly based on observed gradient norms or curvature information?

Addressing these challenges would expand OFT’s applicability to ever larger diffusion backbones like Stable Diffusion 2.x or BigGAN architectures without sacrificing training stability.

## # Exploring higher exact precision orthonormality emanating from stochastic optimisation sweeps

Although gradient descent underpins modern deep learning training regimes, it introduces randomness through minibatch sampling and stochastic optimizers (AdamW \citep{loshchilov2017decoupled}). Even if we initialise $\bm{R}$ exactly as identity—and thereby start exactly at $\bm{W}_0=\bm{W}^\circ$—no training dynamics can guarantee that subsequent updates preserve perfect orthogonality once stochastic gradients are applied. The OFT formulation enforces $\|\mathbf{\Theta}\|=0$ optically by parameterising $\mathbf{\Theta}$ directly via Cayley’s transform and projecting onto valid regions when necessary (\textit{e.g.,} using projected gradient descent onto $[-\epsilon,\epsilon]$ on entries of $\mathbf{Q}$).

However two empirical observations raise open concerns:

1. **Accumulation of numerical drift** – Each backward pass yields floating‑point tensor updates with finite precision ($32$‑bit or $16$‐bit). The product $\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R}$ may deviate slightly from identity over many epochs immediately violating hyperspherical energy preservation.
2. **Gradient interference across layers** – Since OFT couples all neurons within one layer via shared $\mathbf{R}$, cross‑layer dependencies might inadvertently bleed into other layers’ updates if not carefully regularised.

To combat these issues one might explore *exact* reorthogonalisation steps interleaved seamlessly into training:

- **Periodic QR reorthogonalisation** – Periodically re‑compute an explicit QR decomposition of $\mathbf{\Theta}$ ensuring complete orthonormality but at higher computational overhead.
- **Loss‐based penalties** – Add a differentiable penalty term measuring deviation from unitarity:
  \[
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{oortho}} = \lambda_{\text{oortho}}\|\mathbf{\Theta}^\top \mathbf{\Theta}-\mathbf{I}\|_F^2,
  \]
  where $\|\cdot\|_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm.
- **Symmetric projection techniques** – Instead of applying Cayley’s transform directly on raw gradients, perform symmetric matrix exponentials using scaled‐and‐squared approximation methods (Suzuki–Trotter).
  
Each technique presents tradeoffs between computational burden, convergence speed and how strictly hyperspherical energy is preserved under stochastic fluctuations.

The overarching research problem is:

> **Can we devise a “no‑free” strategy that guarantees exact orthonormality per iteration while retaining gradient efficiency?**

This would tighten both theoretical guarantees on hyperspherical energy preservation and empirical training stability across lower precision hardware settings increasingly favoured for scaling up diffusion models.

## # Systematic benchmark suite spanning more vision modalities beyond edges/landmarks

Controllable image generation has been evaluated mainly through three discrete tasks mentioned in Section 5: Canny edge → image (C2I), segmentation map → image (S2I), and face landmark → face image (L2F). While these offer valuable sanity checks across domain shifts—from edges to dense semantic masks—real-world deployments require robustness against far broader families of control signals:

- **Depth maps**,  
- **RGB–depth concatenation**,  
- **Semantic segmentation masks across diverse object categories**,  
- **Dense pose maps**,  
- **Sketches or paint strokes**,  
- **Line drawings in varying styles**,  
- **360° panoramic maps**,  
- **Depth edge pairs**, etc.

An open question is thus *how best to evaluate* control fidelity across such heterogeneous modalities while ensuring consistency between metrics:

1. **Metric design consistency** – Control consistency metrics defined in Section 5 used various measures: IoU/F1 for C2I/S2I; mean-$\ell_2$ error for L2F. Extending these requires modality-specific extraction pipelines (e.g., depth estimators) that may introduce extraneous noise.
2. **Cross-task comparability** – A unified measure allowing fair comparison between distinct tasks is nontrivial because each modality has unique spatial resolution constraints.
3. **Benchmark scalability** – Prototyping large datasets covering dozens of modalities demands careful curating so that control signal scopes remain consistent across different generative tasks.

Unanswered research directions here include:

- Building a *multimodal controllable generation benchmark* that supports plug-and-play evaluation scenarios.
  
  - This would involve standardising input modal tensors and defining universally applicable confidence thresholds (e.g., leveraging perceptual distance metrics like LPIPS).
  
  - Developing *automated control signal extraction pipelines* trained jointly with generative models so that evaluation does not rely heavily on third-party robust inference systems.
  
  - Offering *task transfer* experiments where models finetuned for one modal control benefits generalise when presented with unseen modals without additional fine-tuning steps.

Such a benchmark would expedite comparisons among methods beyond OFT—such as ControlNet or T2I‑Adapter—and help pinpoint whether architectural choices favour specific modality families or allow cross-modal generalisation via compositional stacking hinted at in §4 introduction.

In conclusion, although Orthogonal Finetuning delivers strong empirical gains over existing finetuning baselines by preserving semantic structure through hyperspherical energy conservation, several theoretical and engineering questions remain open:

1. Is there an efficient differentiable procedure guaranteeing exact orthonormality without costly inversions?
2. How does stochastic optimisation affect strict adherence to geometrically constrained updates?
3. Can we systematically quantify controllability across an expanded set of visual primitives?

Answers will bridge remaining gaps between principled theory—capturing neuron geometry—and large‑scale application demands—rapid training on commodity hardware while sustaining flexible yet faithful conditional image synthesis—a step toward practical adoption of diffusion-based generative systems across diverse production environments.\
