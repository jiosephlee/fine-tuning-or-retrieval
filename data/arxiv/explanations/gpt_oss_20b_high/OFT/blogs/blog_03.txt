# Tell Me What I Want – Controllable Image Generation With Orthogonal Finetuning  

Large diffusion models turn words into life‑like images almost instantly, but when you need *exact* placement or shape it’s almost impossible to steer them just with text prompts.  
Add a handful of extra signals – a sketch edge map, a semantic segmentation layout or even a portrait landmark diagram – your model suddenly remembers *exactly* what you asked it to respect while still looking photorealistic.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Why edges sometimes beat words  

A standard off‑the–shelf diffusion pipeline already knows how human bodies look, how cars bend at joints or how hair gathers light—but it keeps the relationship between neurons fixed during training.  
When you give it an additional “control signal” (for instance a set of edges), it tries to reconcile two incompatible constraints:
1) follow your prompt  
2) match every pixel specified by the auxiliary input  

Because gradients usually move all weights together—keeping their directions roughly unchanged—late training rounds almost become blind spots; subtle edge geometry disappears or gets exaggerated until visual artefacts appear.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Enter Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)

> **Core idea:** instead of tweaking every neuron weight freely (as LoRA or plain SGD does), rotate *all* neurons in one layer at once so their pairwise angles stay unchanged—this keeps the internal meaning network learned during pre‑training intact while allowing shape changes limited enough for steady learning.



### Preserving Hyperspherical Energy  

Every neuron’s direction lies on a unit hypersphere after normalisation (`$\hat{\mathbf w}_i = \mathbf w_i/||\mathbf w_i||$`).  
The *hyperspherical energy*  
$$
HE(\mathbf W)= \sum_{i\neq j}\|\hat{\mathbf w}_i-\hat{\mathbf w}_j\|^{-1}
$$  
measures how “uniform” those directions are—a proxy for semantic richness learned during pre‑training.
Conjugating weights with an orthogonal matrix `$R$` leaves all pairwise differences untouched, therefore `$HE(\mathbf WR)=HE(\mathbf W)$`.  
That means **the fine details your image needs stay alive** while moving along exactly those orientations your prompt wants.



### How is it cheaper than LoRA ?  

LoRA adds two small rank‑$r'$ matrices (`$\Delta W=AB^T$`) which grows linearly with `$n+m$`.  
OFT records only one orthogonal block per layer → `$d(d/r-1)/2$` parameters per layer (and usually far fewer thanks to block sharing).  
At inference time nothing changes – combining `$R`and`W⁰`once gives you your tuned weight.



### Constrained variant (COFT)

A tiny tweak asks `$R$` never to stray too far from identity (`||R−I||≤ε`).  
It’s a graceful hard gate around unconditional rotation that prevents drift toward catastrophic loss curves seen when plain SGD learns weights too aggressively.

## Measuring “stickiness”: the Control Consistency Metric  

Control is easy if you only look at the prompt—it looks good intrinsically—but does it actually honoured the extra signal?
The authors created a symmetric test:

| Task | Need | Signal Estimator |
|------|------|------------------|
| Canny → Image | Detected edges | Off‑the–shelf edge solver |
| Segment → Image | Semantic mask | Pre‑trained segmenter |
| Landmark → Face   | Facial keypoints | Face pose predictor |

For each generated picture we run the relevant estimator again **inside** our own codebase → “reconstructed” signal → compare against what was supplied originally:

* **Edge work:** Intersection over Union + F1
* **Seg work:** Mean IoU + overall pixel accuracy
* **Face work:** Mean Euclidean distance between predicted versus supplied landmarks

Because estimators are deterministic close‐to ground truth procedures this gives a straight view wherever prompts fail silently.
