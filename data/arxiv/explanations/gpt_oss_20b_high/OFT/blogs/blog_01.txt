\title{Blogs about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Spin the Lens: Introducing Orthogonal Finetuning – The Secret to Controlling Diffusion Models  

Diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion and Imagen have dazzled us with photorealistic images generated from simple text prompts.  
Yet when it comes to *fine‑grained* control – e.g., keeping a specific person’s face while moving it into a new scene, or forcing an image to follow a sketch – most methods falter or hurt the model’s original quality.  
The paper *“Controlling Text‑to‑Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning”* offers a principled solution that keeps the generative power intact while giving designers a clean knob for controlling the output: **Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)** and its tighter cousin **Constrained OFT (COFT)**.  

This post walks through the intuition behind the key idea, why angular structure matters more than raw weight values, how OFT preserves that structure through rotations on a sphere, and what dividends these tricks pay on two canonical downstream tasks: subject‑driven generation and controllable generation.

---

## 1. The Problem Space  

At its core, adapting a massive pretrained diffusion model (hundreds of millions of parameters) to a new task usually means throwing a tiny learning rate at every weight so that the model moves only ever so slightly away from its original state.  
Two flavours dominate this landscape:

| Goal | Typical Approach |
|------|------------------|
| **Subject‑driven generation** (e.g., “paint my pet dog in a vineyard”) | DreamBooth-style **finetuning** with a reconstruction loss plus class‑preservation loss |
| **Controllable generation** (e.g., “draw this sketch as an image”) | Adapter methods such as ControlNet or T2I‑Adapter that add lightweight extra modules |

Both families suffer from either **instability** during finetuning or **blow‑up** of semantic fidelity once enough epochs pass—think flickering identities or loss after few iterations on large datasets.



---

## 2. Angle Over Magnitude – Why Rotations Matter  

### 2.1 Neurons Live on a Sphere  
After normalising each neuron vector \(w_i\) by its Euclidean length \(\|w_i\|\), every neuron becomes a point \(\hat w_i\) on the unit hypersphere \(S^{d-1}\) where \(d\) is its dimension.
The *relationship* among neurons is captured entirely by angles or equivalently by cosine similarities:
\[
  \langle \hat w_i , \hat w_j\rangle = \cos \theta_{ij}.
\]
Empirical work shows these angles carry almost all semantic information—infinite magnitude information can be discarded without hurting performance if you keep angles intact (see Figure 1 in the paper).

### 2.2 Hyperspherical Energy – A Batch Summary  
To summarise pairwise relationships across neurons we use **hyperspherical energy**:
\[
  \operatorname{HE}(W)\;=\;\sum_{i\neq j}\bigl\|\hat w_i-\hat w_j\bigr\|^{-1},
\]
which is essentially adding up *inverse chords* between all neuron pairs.
A low value indicates that neurons are evenly spread out (high uniformity) while large values indicate clustering.


### 2.3 Why Rotate But Don’t Shake?
If you rotate every neuron by an *orthogonal matrix* \(R\), each individual angle \(\theta_{ij}\) is preserved because
\\[
R^{\top}R=I
\\]
ensures all dot products between pairs stay unchanged.
Hence the entire hyperspherical energy exactly matches its pretrained counterpart.
Conversely, scaling or adding arbitrary perturbations would change angles—potentially wrecking what makes our diffusion model generate realistic images.



---

## 3. Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) – What It Does  

### 3.1 Single Operating Principle  
When finetuning layer \(l\), instead of learning new weights \(W^{(l)}\) directly,
we *learn* an orthogonal matrix \(R^{(l)}\in O(d)\) and set
\\[
W^{(l)}_{\text{fin}}=R^{(l)}W^{(l)}_0,
\\]
where \(W_0^{(l)}\) is the pretrained weight matrix.
This updates neuron *directions* simultaneously while keeping their norm fixed—a global rotation per layer.



### 3.2 Parameter Efficiency  
A naïve orthogonal matrix has \(O(d^2)\) parameters.
To keep finetuning lightweight we use **block‑diagonal** forms:
\\[
R^{(l)} = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(R_1^{(l)},\,R_2^{(l)},\,{\dots\,},\, R_r^{(l)} \right),
\\]
with each block being \(d/r\times d/r\).
The number of trainable parameters shrinks to roughly
\(O(d^2/r)\), often far smaller than LoRA’s fan‑in × fan‑out cost.



### 3.3 Efficient Orthogonality via Cayley Transform  
While imposing strict constraints \(R^\top R=I\) is possible with Gram–Schmidt,
the authors adopt **Cayley paramterisation**:
\\[
R=\bigl(I+Q\bigr)\bigl(I-Q\bigr)^{-1},
\\]
where \(Q=-Q^\top\).
Optimising over skew‑symmetric \(Q\) guarantees orthogonality yet keeps gradients cheap.
Only when needed does one compute an inverse inside the block areas.



---

## 4. Tightening Things Up – Constrained OFT (COFT)

Even though rotations preserve angles perfectly,
the *alignment* relative to the pretrained orientation can still drift arbitrarily far during finetuning.
COFT adds an explicit radius bound around identity:
\\[
   \|\,R-I\,\|\leq\varepsilon .
\\]
This caps how far each layer can rotate—making training even more stable without sacrificing expressiveness.
In practice COFT converges faster and tolerates larger learning rates / longer runs.



---

## 5. Plugging into Existing Frameworks

All experiments were carried out *on top of Stable Diffusion v1.5*.  
OFT/COFT are dropped into DreamBooth’s fine‑tuning pipeline for subject guidance,
and into ControlNet/T2I‑Adapter settings for control signals such as Canny edges,
segmentation maps and facial landmarks.
Because only orthogonal matrices change weights en bloc,
the final finetuned model consists solely of modified base weights—no auxiliary modules—hence **no run‐time overhead**.

## 6. Empirical Evidence

### 6.1 Subject‑Driven Generation  

| Method | DINO | CLIP‑I | CLIP‑T | LPIPS |
|--------|------|--------|--------|-------|
| DreamBooth | *baseline* | *N/A* | *N/A* | *N/A* |
| LoRA      | lower      | lower   | lower   | similar |
| **OFT**   | higher     | higher   | comparable | slightly better |
| **COFT**  | higher     | higher   | comparable | slightly better |

Key takeaways:

* **Stability:** After just ~400 steps both OFT variants match DreamBooth’s performance while LoRA collapses even by ~2000 steps.
* **Convergence:** COFT achieves stable semantics quickly regardless of learning rate—relicating dreaming architecture from Classical optimization literature.
* **Quality:** Human AM anecdotes confirm sharper facial features and faithful adherence simultaneously – something DreamBooth tends to sacrifice with long runs.


### 6.2 Controllable Generation  

Three benchmark tasks were tested:

| Task                              | Metric             | Best Value (ours vs baselines) |
|-----------------------------------|--------------------|--------------------------------|
| Canny → Image                      | IoU / F1           | ours : high / high            |
| Segmentation Map → Image          | mIoU / Accuracy    | ours : high / high            |
| Landmark → Face                    | ℓ₂ distance       | ours : tiny error             |

Notably:

- **Speed:** Our COFT model converged within ≈8 epochs whereas ControlNet needed ~20–30 epochs for comparable control fidelity.
- **Data Efficiency:** Using only 5 % of ADE20K still let COFT match full dataset baselines—a striking finding for resource-constrained setups.
- **Consistency Metric:** We proposed measuring how close one recovers the input control signal after synthesis—a practical headway moving beyond subjective visual checks.



---

## 7️ Lessons Learned

1. **Angle preservation ≠ Euclidean shrinkage** – Many prior tweaks rely on small Euclidean changes via tiny LR updates; OFT sidesteps that assumption entirely.
2. **Orthogonality pays off** not just because it spreads neurons but because it respects their existing geometry—keeping semantics stable while granting “twist” flexibility used by downstream cues.
3. COFT demonstrates that adding whiteboard constraints helps early gradients find good directions without excessive over‐parameterisation—mirroring regulariser ideas from traditional machine‑learning optimisation empires.

## 🚀 How You Can Try It

If you’re already comfortable modifying HuggingFace pipelines or private Stable Diffusion builds:

1. Replace your `LoRA` hyper‑parameter block with an `OFT` block confessing `num_blocks=r` (usually `r=8`).
2–4ish gives you roughly `r*d/d` learnable parameters per layer—tiny compared with even whole `LoRA` ranks!
3 . Train following DreamBooth or ControlNet's official scripts—they will work unchanged because everything else remains untouched.
4 . After finetuning you’re done—just drop back into your original inference loop—the base weights are implicitly rotated already so no new forward step is required.

## 📌 Closing Thoughts & Open Questions

Orthogonal Finetuning uncovers an elegant rheostat for diffusion models: rotate neurons within layers while respecting pre-trained geometry and immediately reaping stronger downstream control without additional inference cost.
Open research paths include:

* scaling up iceberg-lab/layouts where Cayley inverse becomes expensive – could batched Pseudo‑inverse approximations make it fast?
* compositionality via stacking several independent rotation blocks from different downstream tasks—does their product preserve multi-task knowledge?
* better parameter sparsity schemes beyond block diagonals yet still guaranteeing truthful angle preservation.


With these knobs at hand our difference between “something works” and “something works *permanently*, well into a production footing” will just keep getting tighter.

Happy tinkering!
