\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 5: OFT : Efficient Parameterisation  

Large text‑to‑image diffusion models contain millions of neuron weights whose directions encode semantic meaning very faithfully—their pairwise angles remain largely unchanged during training and therefore preserve what has been learned during pretraining.  
Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) exploits this fact by adapting every layer through an **orthogonal transformation** applied multiplicatively to its weight matrix \(W^{0}\).  
Because any such transformation preserves all pairwise angles (\(\mathrm{HE}(W)=\mathrm{HE}(W^{0})\)), the transformed network keeps the hyperspherical energy exactly unchanged while flexibly redirecting neurons towards a downstream task’s needs.

Below we explain how OFT makes this transformation learnable while remaining computationally lightweight,
its trade–off knob “block size” which lets us control flexibility,
how many trainable variables this entails compared with competing methods,
and why this design ultimately reduces finetuning time without sacrificing fidelity.



### # Cayley mapping  

Orthogonality supplies strong regularisation—it would otherwise require enforcing quadratic constraints on every entry of \(W\), an expensive operation during optimisation.
A classic trick sidesteps this problem entirely via **Cayley’s parametrisation**:
given any skew‑symmetric matrix \(Q=-Q^{\top}\),

\[
R=(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1},
\]

is guaranteed to lie in the special orthogonal group \(\mathrm{SO}(D)\); i.e.,
\(R^{\top}R=RR^{\top}=I,\;\det(R)=+1.\)

All continuous degrees of freedom required for STO reachability come exclusively from entries below the main diagonal of \(Q;\;
={'#'\ == }\;\dim(\mathrm SO(D))=\tfrac12D(D{-}1).\)
Thus learning merely eighteen dozen scalar entries suffices even when transforming extremely high‑dimensional layers  
(e.g., a layer operating on 768 features needs \(768\times767/2\simeq293\,K\) learnable variables).

Cayley’s map is differentiable everywhere except when \((I-Q)\) becomes singular—an event avoided during training because optimisation initially places \(Q\simeq0,\Rightarrow R\simeq I,\det(R)\simeq +\,.\).
Furthermore an initial zero matrix guarantees that every finite step starts at the pretrained model,
and ordinary backpropagation propagates gradients through the inverse
by standard automatic differentiation engines.



### # Block size selection  

Although fully learning an arbitrary \(Z\times Z\) rotation seems powerful,
the associated memory/batch cost scales quadratically (\(\mathcal O(Z^{2})\)).
In practice neural architectures expose large input dimensions which makes handling billions of rotation terms prohibitive.
The key observation behind OFT’s scalability is that **many neurons never interact directly**—
each channel often processes information independently.
Thus an optimal compromise is achieved by splitting the full rotation into independent pieces applied separately along non‑overlapping subspaces:
for a chosen integer “block size” \(b=r^{-\,}Z/d,\)

\[
R=\operatorname{diag}(R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{r}),
   \qquad
   R_{i}\in \mathrm{SO}\!\left(\tfrac Z r\right).
   \tag{$*$}
\]

If every bloc performs an individualized rotation,
the network keeps unaffected interactions yet gains expressiveness in rotary transformations applied locally.
Choosing a larger value of \(r\)
(fewer neurons per bloc),
decreases how many learnable scalars sit behind every minibatch—even beside halving independence besides holistic symmetry—while simultaneously permitting finer control over different feature groups.
