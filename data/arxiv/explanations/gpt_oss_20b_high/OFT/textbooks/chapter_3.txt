\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 3: 3 Why Rotating Neurons Works  

The core intuition behind Orthogonal‑Finetuning is simple yet powerful:  
neuron directions encode the semantic concepts recognised by a trained model  
while their magnitudes play a far smaller role.   
By rotating all neurons in a layer together—an operation that preserves every
pairwise angle—one can steer the network toward new behaviours without
breaking the internal geometric structure learned during pretraining.
Below we spell out the empirical evidence supporting this view,
the intuition gained from signal‑processing analogies,
and finally prove mathematically why an orthonormal change keeps **hyperspherical
energy**—the sum of all pairwise cosine similarities—unchanged.



## # Toy Auto‑Encoder Experiment  

A light‑weight convolutional auto‑encoder was trained on a set of flower
images using the standard linear inner–product \(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}\)
to compute feature maps inside every layer.\(^1\)
After convergence we performed three different “read‑outs’’ on a held‑out
image without retraining:
\[
\underbrace{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}_{\text{inner–product}}
\,;\qquad
\underbrace{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}_{\text{magnitude}}
\,;\qquad
\underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{w}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|}}
          _{\text{angular (cosine)}} .
\]
The outcome was striking.
The reconstructed images obtained using only angular information were nearly
identical to those produced with raw inner products,
while reconstruction based solely on magnitudes was essentially noise–like.
In other words, once trained,
the learning system had turned most of its representational power into
the *direction* fields \(\{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\),
with \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{i}=\frac{\mathbf{w}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{w}_{i}\|}\).
Magnitude variations contributed very little usable semantic signal.
This toy experiment shows empirically that manipulating neuron **angles** is
the route through which a neural network alters its behaviour.



## # Fourier Phase Analogy  

Fourier analysis offers another familiar illustration.
Any two‑dimensional image \(I(\boldsymbol{x})\) can be decomposed into sinusoidal
basis functions:
\[
I(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{u,v}A_{u,v}
e^{\,2\pi i(u x+v y)} ,
\]
where \(A_{u,v}=M_{u,v}e^{i\phi_{u,v}}\)
separates into *magnitude* \(M_{u,v}=\|A_{u,v}\|\)
and *phase* \(\phi_{u,v}=\arg(A_{u,v})\).
If one keeps the magnitudes \(M_{u,v}\) fixed at random values yet restores
the original phases \(\phi_{u,v}\), the resulting image is impressively close
to \(I(\boldsymbol{x})\);
conversely, replacing phases with random values while keeping magnitudes fixes
loses almost all object identity information.
Thus **phase shifts**—the analogues of neuronal direction rotations—carry most
of an image’s structural meaning.
This perspective aligns beautifully with our observations:
rotating neurons changes their directional codes, reshaping semantic content
while leaving *overall* total energy unchanged.



## # Invariance Proof  

Let us formalise why an orthonormal transformation leaves *hyperspherical
energy* invariant.
Consider a fully connected layer whose weights form an \(d\times n\) matrix

\[
\bm W=\bigl[\bm w_1,\dotsc,\bm w_n\bigr] ,
\qquad 
\bm w_i\in\mathbb R^{d}.
\]

Normalising every column gives \(\hat{\bm w}_i=
\dfrac{\bm w_i}{\,\lVert \bm w_i\rVert_{\!2}}\).
The **hyperspherical energy** (HE) associated with this layer is defined as

\[
 \operatorname{HE}(\bm W)
   := \sum_{\substack{i,j\\ i<j}}
      \frac{1}{\, \lVert \hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\rVert_{\!2} } .
   \tag{1}
\]

A convenient equivalent form replaces Euclidean distances between unit vectors
by their cosine similarities:
since \( 
    \lVert \hat{\bm u}-\hat{\bm v}\rVert_{\!2}
      = \sqrt{\, 2(1-\!\langle \hat{\bm u},\,\hat{\bm v}\rangle ) }
    \)
each term becomes an increasingly large value when adjacent neurons become
more parallel.
Hence minimizing HE tends toward uniformly spread neurons,
while here we will exploit its *rigidity*  
to preserve pre–trained inter‑neuron relations.



### Orthogonal Transformation

Let \(R^\top R = I_d = RR^\top\)
be any real orthogonal matrix (\(R^\top = R^{-1}\)).
We claim

> **Lemma.**  
> For any \(R \in O(d)\),
> \(\operatorname{HE}(R\,W)=\operatorname{HE}(W)\).

*Proof.*  
Rotations do not alter norms:
for every column,

\(  
   \lVert R\,\hat{\bm w}_i\rVert_{\!2}
    = \sqrt{
           (R\,\hat{\bm w}_i)^\top(R\,\hat{\bm w}_i)
          }
    =   \sqrt{
           \hat{\bm w}_i^\top(R^\top R)\,\hat{\bm w}_i}
    =   \sqrt{
           \hat{\bm w}_i^\top I_d\,\hat{\bm w}_i}
    =   1 .
```
Hence \(R\,W=RW=(Rw_1,\dotsc,rw_n)\) has exactly the same unit‐norm columns,
so its normalized version is simply 
\(R[\hat{\bm w}_1,\dotsc,\hat{\bm w}_n]\). 

Now evaluate any term in Eq.(1):

```
 ⟨R\,\hat{\bm u},\,R\,\hat{\bm v}⟩  
   = (R\,\hat{\bm u})^\top(R\,\hat{\bm v})
   = \hat{\bm u}^\top(R^\top R)\,\hat{\bm v}
   =        \langle \hat{\bm u},\,\hat{\bm v}\rangle .
```

Thus **every cosine similarity—and consequently every Euclidean distance—
between pairs of neurons remains identical** after applying \(R.\)** Consequently every summand in Eq.(1)
is untouched, implying HE(RW)=HE(W).
∎



### Consequence for Finetuning

If a pre‑trained weight matrix \(W^0,\!)
 has some value HE\(^{0}=E^0=\operatorname{HE}(W^0).\)
Any finite rotation/reflection \(R:\mathbb R^{d\times d}\rightarrow O(d)\)
satisfies  

\(E := E^0.\)

Hence any finetuned weight matrix constrained to equal
\(W=W_0’=RW^0+S?\!            // actually RW^0\nor more generally,

`RW^0` exactly inherits all pair-wise cosine similarities,
so its hyperspherical structure matches halfmodologically found shape;
therefore all higher‑level processes relying upon these relational cues --
exploited during text‑driven sampling cycles -- remain intact.
This invariance explains why rotating entire layers works efficiently:
semantic ordering stays correct while absolute orientations are free for adaptation.
