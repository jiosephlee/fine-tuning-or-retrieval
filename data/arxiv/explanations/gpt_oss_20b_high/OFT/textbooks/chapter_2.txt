\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

Chapter 2: 2 Representations Inside Neural Networks

Statistical characterization of how neurons are organized inside a network is essential when attempting principled finetuning or designing regularizers. In particular, recent work has shown that neuron weights tend naturally toward living on a unit hypersphere when they are implicitly normalized by cosine similarity during forward propagation or training analysis. This observation motivates treating each neuron as a point on an $S^{d-1}$ sphere and reason about learning dynamics in terms of angles rather than magnitudes. Two key findings underpinning this view are (a) directional relationships overwhelmingly dominate semantic capacity while raw magnitudes contribute little information, and (b) preserving pairwise angular relationships measured by a global scalar called **hyperspherical energy** can help retain performance after finetuning. The following sections dissect these ideas mathematically and empirically so you may see how they emerge from concrete definitions used in modern diffusion‑based generative models.

# Vector form of fully connected layer weights

A fully‑connected layer can naturally be written as a matrix $\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ where $d$ is the dimensionality of both input vectors $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and output neurons, while $n$ denotes the number of neurons (or output channels). Each column $\bm{w}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ represents one neuron’s receptive vector; feeding an input $\bm{x}$ yields an $n$‑dimensional feature map $\bm z=\bm W^{\top}\bm x$, i.e.
$$
\bm z = 
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
w_{1}^{\top}\\ \vdots \\ w_{n}^{\top}
\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm W^{\top}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\ \vdots \\ x_{d}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm x}.
$$
In many large‑scale back‑propagation regimes this forward pass is effectively governed by inner products $w_{i}^{\top}x$, which are linear functions of both magnitude and direction but become dominated by angle once filters grow large during training. Thus treating each $\bm w_i$ as a directed vector captures all relevant information if we deliberately normalize them onto a shared manifold—namely, the unit sphere—by setting $\hat{\bm w}_{i}=\bm w_i/\lVert \bm w_i\rVert$. This “vector‐on‑hypersphere” representation is foundational for defining angular similarities later on.


# Cosine similarity between two neurons

Once neurons are projected onto $S^{d-1}$ via $\hat{\bm w}_i$, their relative orientation can be quantified by **cosine similarity**:
$$
\cos(\theta_{ij}) = \langle \hat{\bm w}_i,\hat{\bm w}_j\rangle 
= \frac{\lVert \hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\rVert^2}{2},
$$
where $\theta_{ij}\in[0,\pi]$ indicates the acute angle between them. Because all vectors lie on the unit sphere, $\lvert \cos(\theta_{ij})\rvert$ captures exactly how parallel or antiparallel two neurons are—an entirely angle‑based notion devoid of scale information.


The inner product used in standard forward propagation reduces mathematically to $\,w_j^\top x = \lVert w_j\rVert\,\lVert x\rVert\,\cos(\alpha_j)$ where $\alpha_j$ is the angle between $w_j$ and $x$. During training iterative weight updates change both magnitude $\lVert w_j\rVert$ and direction $\hat{\bm w}_j$. Empirical evidence later shows updating solely directions—or equivalently rotating all neurons together—can alter semantics more cleanly than altering magnitudes.


# Defining hyperspherical energy as sum over pairwise cosine distances

To summarize local geometric configuration among many neurons on a single layer one may aggregate all pairwise cosine similarities into a single scalar called **hyperspherical energy**:
$$
E_{\text{he}}(\bm W)
:= \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^n 
\Bigl[\bigl(2\,\bigl(1-\langle \hat{\bm w}_{i},\hat{\bm w}_{j}\rangle\bigr)\bigr)\Bigr]^{-\,1}
= \sum_{\substack{i,j=1\\ i\neq j}}^n 
\|\hat{\bm w}_i-\hat{\bm w}_j\|^{-\,1}.
$$
The summand equals $1/\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ which goes high when two normals become close (small Euclidean distance on sphere) – i.e., when two neurons become nearly parallel – thus penalizing tight clusters and rewarding angular spread. When all rows are uniformly spread around $S^{d-1}$, every inter‑neuron distance is similar and $E_{\text{he}}$ attains its minimum value zero if one counts only relative differences under an orthogonal transformation (as discussed below). Importantly, since each term depends only on direction and dot product at most linearly on magnitude before normalization, hyperspherical energy is invariant under global scaling or singular orthogonal rotations applied identically across all rows.


An explicit property pivotal for finetuning theory here is:

> **Invariance under Orthogonal Transformations**  
> Let $\operatorname{O}(d)$ denote the set of real orthogonal matrices ($QQ^\top=I$). For any orthogonal matrix $\mathbf R$,  
> $$E_{\text{he}}\!\bigl(\mathbf R\,\mathbf W\bigr)=E_{\text{he}}\!\bigl(\mathbf W\bigr),$$  
> because multiplying every row by an orthogonal matrix multiplies each row vector by same rotation/reflection without changing pairwise angles among them or their norms.


Consequently any procedure that applies a *layer‑shared* orthogonal transform preserves hypothetically critical relational structure captured by $E_{\text{he}}$, even though every individual row has been rotated.



# Why energy matters – empirical evidence linking it to downstream quality

The hypothesis driving orthogonal finetuning is precisely that **preserving hyperspherical energy** maintains generative fidelity after fine-grained adaptation such as subject‑driven generation or additional control signals embedded into diffusion steps.
Two types of evidence tilt this assertion:

1. **Post‑hoc Recall Tests**  
   In toy autoencoder experiments built around flower images—a simple representation demonstrates that extracting *directions* only suffices reconstructing inputs while discarding magnitude yields almost perfect images post‑training. A formal reconstruction measurement showed variations across three methods:
      * raw inner product (training mode);  
      * magnitude alone;  
      * angle alone after projection onto unit sphere.
   Numerical results reported missing amplitude did not spoil semantic fidelity whereas mounting direction alone carried nearly all content (see Figure 1). Thus neuron direction encodes semantics while hypotesis tests confirm directional preservation correlating strongly with generative realism.


2. **Finite‐dimensional Diffusion Finetunes**  
   When performing subject‑driven finetuning using DreamBooth style objectives aimed at reconstructing few training samples while preserving prior generations ("class‑specific prior preservation"), minimal loss metrics alone poorly correlate with final visual performance because small Euclidean changes may still scramble angles arbitrarily leading to identity loss or hallucinations. Adapting these networks with orthogonal matrices per layer ensures minimal Movement Excluding scale adjustments (\[Equation (…)\]), producing far faster convergence rates—in subject driven tasks about one tenth the iterations needed by LoRA or baseline DreamBooth—and higher CLIP/ DINO based identity scores across multiple random seeds (Table X).

3. **Control Consistency Quantities**  
   For controllable generation conditioned on Canny edges or segmentation maps there arises an objective: compute *control consistency*, i.e., regenerate control features from generated image via off‑the–shelf edge detectors/segmentation models then compare against original inputs using IoU/F₁ metrics per class or landmark errors ($L_2$ distances). Experiments show orthogonally finetuned models achieve unprecedented consistency while remaining faithful at image fidelity scores comparable or surpassing ControlNet/ T₂I‑Adapter baselines (Table Y). This result proves maintaining inter–neuron angular patterns protects mapping accuracy between external control signals and generated outputs.


Taken together these observations suggest preserving hyperspherical energy during finetune acts as a robust regularizer ensuring cognitive structures derived during pretraining persist intact even as the network adapts new tasks. Hence orthogonal finetuning—by fixing offls’ geometry—is theoretically sound (by invariance) and empirically effective (§4–5).
