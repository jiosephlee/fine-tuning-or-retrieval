# Foundational Concepts in Sequential Language Modeling

Language modeling is one of the most celebrated applications of neural networks today because it seeks an answer to a profound question: *How can a machine capture dependencies that unfold over many symbolic steps so that it can predict what comes next*?

This introductory chapter builds intuition around three foundational ideas that underpin modern sequence models:  
(i) how probability distributions over sequences are expressed mathematically by decomposing them into nested conditionals over time,  
(ii) why training very deep feed‑forward architectures—no matter how wide—fail when confronted with long-range interactions due precisely to vanishing or exploding gradients during back‑propagation through time, and  
(iii) why introducing learnable *gates* into recurrent updates gives rise exactly to those mechanisms that solve both problems without destroying locality.  

In what follows every equation is rendered using LaTeX syntax ($...$), and all terms are defined explicitly so a newcomer can follow without having previously read an advanced source text.



## Conditional probability decomposition over time

In natural language a sentence \(x_1,\dots,x_T\) is viewed as an ordered list of tokens sampled from an unknown distribution \(p(\mathbf{x})\).
Predictive language models seek precisely this distribution:
\[
p(\mathbf{x}) = p(x_1,\dots,x_T).
\]
The fundamental insight borrowed from probability theory—and heavily exploited by all autoregressive transformers—is that any joint mass function can be factorised into a product of successive conditionals:
\[
p(\mathbf{x}) 
   = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p\!\left( x_t\,|\,x_{<t}\right),
   \quad
   x_{<t}\triangleq \left\{x_1,\dots,x_{t-1}\right\}.
   \tag{1}
\]
Formulation (1) rewrites an otherwise impossible global problem into a sequence of **next‑token predictions** conditioned only on *past* information—the exact point where hypothesis‑driven architecture must supply knowledge about previously seen symbols.
Concrete neural online predictors compute
\[
p_{\theta}\!\left( x_t\,|\,x_{<t}\right)
   = f_{\theta}\!\bigl(x_{<t}\bigr)_t,
\]
where \(f_{\theta}\) denotes a Parameterised Model whose hidden state grows monotonically along positions––the crux being how each representation retains enough past work so that ‘the future depends on everything we've seen so far’.  
The challenge emerges precisely at this juncture—the necessity for architectures which either leaf each token’s influence persistently through all subsequent steps or aggregate their effects efficiently until they become indistinguishable.



## Vanishing / exploding gradients in naïve sequence networks

A first instinct when approaching Eqn.~(1) is simply to place all past tokens into one high‑dimensional vector,
feed it through several fully connected layers,
then exponentiate sins[:].
In practice such an approach completely fails when sequences are even moderately longer than ten symbols.
Two reasons explain why a “black‑box” feed‑forward stack cannot satisfy Eqn.~(1).

### Back‑propagation through time causes extreme scaling

During training, every parameter is updated proportionally
to its partial derivative against a particular loss signal.
When a model processes symbols one after another,
each intermediate value participates repeatedly
in many multiplications—one per following timestep—
which yields chain–rule products such as
\[
\frac{\partial p_{\theta}}{\partial w}
   = 
   \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial w}
            =
   \prod_{k=1}^{T} 
    J_k(w), 
    \qquad J_k(w)\triangleq
    \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}.
    \tag{2}
\]
If each Jacobian \(J_k(w)\) has eigenvalues not equal exactly one,
their product easily under‑flows toward zero *or blows up toward infinity* as \(T\to\infty\).
The former modification silences updates – **vanishing gradients**:
the model cannot learn anything distant from its current step.
The latter generates numerical instability – **exploding gradients**.
Both behaviours prevent successful optimisation even for very compact neural circuits.



### Why value compression matters

Feed‑forward brute force only enjoys *constant-width* representations;
when depth increases linearly with length,
a static hidden vector must encode an increasingly longer history into fixed dimensions.
This compression forces key events either drowned out (**Elman‑style bottleneck**) or treated redundantly (**ReLU duplications**), none remedying catastrophic back‑propagation issues measured above.
In short, without structural means of preserving salient components between timesteps,
temporal signals will either degrade irretrievably or disturb the entire network’s representation.



## Motivations for gated recurrent units

A natural remedy comes when one places **gates**—learnable scalar nonlinearities—between successive state updates.
They directly oppose both failure modes simultaneously:

* **Constant error carousel** – The core idea behind classic Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), first formalised by Hochreiter & Schmidhuber~[(\citet{Hochreiter:91}), (\citet{Hochreiter:97nips})],
introduces an additive cell–state update:
\(c_t = f_t\,c_{t-1}+ i_t\,z_t.\)
Because addition leaves norm growth unattenuated,
the effect cumulated across timesteps refuses catastrophic dampening (*)‐like \( \exp(-T)\),
ultimately preserving local gradients even when sequences are thousands long.



* **Gates regulating information flow** – Three sigmoid tensors influence exactly which portions
of new data enter (`input gate`),
which old data survive (`forget gate`),
and what ultimately constitutes hidden processed signal (`output gate`).
Mathematically each gate accepts two linear combinations—
one between current embeddings \(x_t\)
and one between previous hidden outputs \(h_{t-1}\);
bias offsets then undergo logistic mapping:
\( i_t=\sigma(\tilde i_t), f_t=\sigma(\tilde f_t), o_t=\sigma(\tilde o_t).\)
These plug points allow every rate parameter
to stay comfortably bounded beneath \(e^0=1\),
hence individual scalar subsets may recover crucial histories without leaving differentiability crucial.



An analogous narrative appears
in very recent work on Extended Long Short-Term Memory (xLSTM).
There new classes—**exponential gates** (\(i_t=\exp(\tilde i_t)\), \(f_t=\exp(\tilde f_t)\))—become standardised opportune variants together with *normalisers*
(which maintain accumulation magnitude),
allowing revisions of past write operations (§2.sLSTM).
Meanwhile another class introduces **matrix memories** (\(\mathbf C\nicearrow d\times d\))
with covariance update rules (\(\mathbf C_t = f_t\,\mathbf C_{t-1}+i_t\,v_k^\top k_i\)),
thereby doubling representational depth (> scalar → matrix).

Both constructs do more than merely repack age-old recurrency;
they implement concepts directly requested by practitioners’ observations:
the ability **to revise** previously stored associations (*the “Nearest Neighbor Search” problem*)  
and assurance **capacity** sufficient for rare token retrieval (*“Rare Token Prediction” challenge*).  
These features go well beyond vanilla sigmoid replies yet still preserve local gradient flow introduced originally by Hochreiter et al.



### Takeaway

Once designers integrated learnable gates into temporal flows,
they broke two cascades simultaneously:

| Problem | Gate Pattern | How it helps |
|---------|--------------|--------------|
| Vanishing gradients | Additive cell state update coupled with bounded forget gate | Maintains discrepancy scores across arbitrarily many steps |
| Information bottleneck | Input/forget gates isolate useful signals | Guarantees precise re‑encoding while discarding irrelevant data |

Later extensions step forward:
exponential activations repair precision when memorising hard-to-reclaim keys;
matrix memories let trunks hold high‑dimensional cross‑pair alignments;
parallelisable variants remove any sequential dependence altogether—
consequently paving the way toward models rivaling Transformer scales  
without paying quadratic overhead.

Classic Long Short Term Memory Architecture  

# Constant Error Carousel Definition  
The modern LSTM unit was devised to counteract vanishing or exploding gradients in recurrent neural networks. Central to this design is the *constant error carousel*—a structure whereby information injected into the memory cell at time \(t\) remains linearly accessible at later time steps. In practice this manifests as an additive term on the previous cell state: after transforming both current input \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\) and last hidden state \(\mathbf{h}_{t-1}\) into respective contributions, they are gated separately before being summed into the new memory content \(c_{t}\). Because this sum is not multiplied by any contracting factors other than gating signals bounded between zero and one, it approximates an identity transformation more faithfully than a pure recurrence would allow, thereby preserving gradients over many updates and permitting learning across long temporal extents.

# Input/Output/Forget Gate Formulation  
Each LSTM employs three binary‑style gates—input (\(i_{t}\)), forget (\(f_{t}\)), and output (\(o_{t}\))—whose values lie in \([0,1]\). These gates are produced by linear transformations followed by sigmoid activations:

\[
\begin{aligned}
i_{t} &= \sigma \!\bigl( \tilde{i}_{t} \bigr), &
\tilde{i}_{t} &= \mathbf{w}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{t}+ r_{i}\,\mathbf{h}_{\, t-1}+b_{i},\\[4pt]
f_{t} &= \sigma \!\bigl( \tilde{f}_{\, t} \bigr), &
\tilde{f}_{\, t} &= \mathbf{w}_{f}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{ t}+ r_{f}\,\mathbf{h}_{\, t-1}+b_{f},\\[4pt]
o_{ t}&= \sigma \!\bigl( \tilde{o}_{\, t}\bigr), &
\tilde{o}_{\, t}&= \mathbf{w}_{o}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\, t}+ r_{o}\,\mathbf{h}_{\, t-1}+b_{o}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The weight vectors \(\mathbf{w}_{*}\) map inputs to gate pre‑activations from raw symbols or features; \(r_{*}\) scale influences from the previous hidden state ensuring temporal continuity; biases \(b_{*}\) provide learned offsets independent from data-driven signals. The parameters are shared across time steps so that the network can learn recurrent dynamics with concise modelling capacity.

# Cell‑State Update Equation  
Within each time step residual contributions from forgotten past memory and new material are combined additively:

\[
c_{\! t}= f_{\! t}\,c_{\! t-1}+ i_{\! t}\,z_{\! t},
\]

where \(z_{\! t}\) refers to the transformed current input value:

\[
z_{\! t}= \varphi(\tilde z_{\! t}),\qquad
\tilde z_{\! t}= 
      {\bf w}^{\top }_{\! z}{\bf x}_{\! t}+ r_{\! z}{\bf h}_{\, t-1}+ b_{\! z}.
\]

Here \(c_{\! t-1}\) acts as a tumbling buffer whose contents (information held over many steps) are modulated by \(f_{\! t}\); concurrently fresh material enters through \(z_{\! t}\)’s contribution regulated by \(i_{\! t}\). This form essentially performs a weighted sum wherein forget gate supports retention while input gate allows augmentation—exactly how vanishing gradients are mitigated through transparent additive updates.
The hidden output corresponding to each timestep follows:

\[
h_{\! t}= o_{\! t}\,\psi(c_{\! t}),
\]

such that only after squashing with an activation function do raw cell contents translate into observable states emitted downstream or feeding back into subsequent steps.

# Activation Functions Employed  
On two distinct levels LSTMs exploit non‑linearities suited for their roles:

*For cell inputs* \(z_\text{s},\ phy(\widetilde z)\): historically either hyperbolic tangent (\(\tanh\)) or other sigmoidal variants appear in literature, chosen to bound prerequisite values within a manageable range yet still provide smooth derivatives necessary for training via back‑propagation through time.
  
*For hidden outputs* post‑sandwich activation \(\psi(c_\text{s})=\psi(c)\): commonly chosen as another instance of \(0<k<2.\dots? Actually many implementations use scaled tanh or sometimes ReLU variants—but irrespective of exact form they thin out uncontrolled growth in cell accumulations.
  
All three gates utilize σ (sigmoid), ensuring outputs strictly between zero and one; this guarantees controlled passage of gradients even after multiple compositions: if any gate saturates near one or zero intermediate signal flows through only as allowed by gating logic—introducing exactly “menu-like” directional control within each memory step.
  
By enveloping linear transforms inside these constitutively bounded nonlinear elements the canonical LSTM achieves explicit gradient preservation over extensive temporal horizons while still supporting expressive transformations embedded between successive hidden states.

# Three Core Shortcomings of Standard LSTMs

Standard Long‑Short Term Memory networks have associated strengths that have peaked general success across diverse sequence learning settings—from language modelling to realtime reinforcement learning agents—which led practitioners today to adopt them even when Transformers are dominantly available[^1].  
Nevertheless, empirical studies revealed systematic failures whenever particular aspects of computation were pushed toward their extremes—large scale models, very sparse phenomena such as rare words or tails events, and convenient hardware acceleration were examined side‑by‑side against architectural prudence offered by traditional formalism.  
These investigations tightly held three interrelated shortcomings tied respectively to **storage revision**, **encoding capacity**, and **sequential dependency** that guided the creation of xLSTM’s extensions[^2].

---

# Revisions Discarded During Storage Updates (Nearest Neighbor Search Issue)

A hallmark property underlying efficient memory architectures is their ability to revisit previously committed information upon encountering stronger evidence later in the stream—a capability often framed as “storage revision”.  
Standard LSTMs encode every symbol exclusively through additive updates \(c_t = f_t c_{t-1}+ i_t z_t\), where \(f_t,i_t\in[0,1]\) bound any single contribution’s influence retroactively[^3].  
When applied naïvely to lookup‑heavy problems such as **nearest‑neighbor search**, this additive subnet simply ignores newer key samples when an older version remains latest—including cases where an updated datum abuts a more suitable match—because exponential decay enforced by \(f_t\) never allows later instances to override prior charges without setting \(i_{t'}=0\).

The practical impact appeared starkly in experiments visualized on “Nearest Neighbor Search” tracks (**Figure lstmProblems** left panel): mean squared error rapidly worsened once sequences grew longer than dozens of steps—the exact cue pointing toward an inability let late arriving vectors retroactively perturb stored responsibility without incurring prohibitive numerical instability[^4].  
Our repair strategy added two ingredients:  
* Exponentially activated input gates (\(i'_t =e^{\,\tilde i_t}\)) allow arbitrarily intense local boosts while guarded normalization (\(n_t=f_n n_{t‑1}+ i'_t\)) caps total accumulations against runaway growth[^5].  
* A stabiliser term (\(m_t=\max(\log f_t+m_{t‑1},\,\log i'_t)\)) guarantees gradient flow without overflow (`math.exp` deployment).

With these controls revised drafts preserved old memories unless field-wise pointer‑strength strictly outweighed powers contained within fresher gates—thereby turning classic “fixed” decoder neurons into dynamically adjustable archive peers typifying state‑tracking capabilities normally reserved only for linear RNNs augmented through recurrent connections[^6].

# Limited Scalar Storage When Predicting Rare Events

Another core limitation traced back directly through word‑frequency stratification experiments conducted on **Wikitext‑103** trains[^7].  
Standard cells collapse symbolic history everdimensional information into a single scalar register \(c_t \in \mathbb R\).  
This single dimension reduces vocabulary–to–hidden mapping toward RNN proximal equivalence machines preferring repeated “high‑probability” clusters while impoverishing niche behaviours associated with infrequent tokens.  

Empirical validation demonstrated precisely this phenomenon (`Figure lstmProblems`, right panel): perplexities climbed steeply when evaluating scanty-occurrence strata such as verbs appearing less than five times per corpus slice—a purely statistical manifestation stemming specifically from bounded internal memorisation space failing to represent diversity beyond its coding width.^[8]  

To confront it “xLSTM” introduced sLTM’s matrix reservoir \(C_t \in \mathbb R^{d\times d}\) trained via covariance update \(C_t=C_{t‑1}+v_{t}k^{\!\top}_t\).  
Matrix memories preserve separate orthogonal channels able simultaneously hold multiple high-dimensional vectors akin to adjusting key–value pairs on demand—the convolutional analogue replaced brute forces negating scarcel compression.^[9]  

With larger latent spaces networks learn richer positional sigatures imperative even when sampling infrequent elements—and consequently rarity informed representational dynamics translate directly into markedly lower perplexities at low‑frequency quarterly slots compared against vanilla LSTMs measuring up against Transformer baselines illustrating dramatic narrowing margin.^[10]

Design Principles Behind Extended Long Short‑Term Memory  

In its most elementary form a Long‑Short‑Term Memory (LSTM) network stores past information solely in a single scalar value \(c_{\!t}\). While this arrangement suffices for many sequence‑based problems, two observations obtained in recent experiments reveal intrinsic limitations inherent to this design framework. First, when encountering a new piece of information during an ongoing process an ordinary LSTM cannot preferably revise its older stored value because its gates are confined therein by sigmoid activations never exceeding unity—a fact demonstrated vividly on *Nearest‑Neighbour‑Search* tasks where an LSTM fails once it commits itself too early to an inaccurate pick [Fig.~\ref{fig:lstmProblems}]. Second, scoring poorly on rare tokens illustrates that confining semantic knowledge into one number severely restricts expressive power — the model must collapse several distinct semantic cues into competing components belonging to identical dimensions [Fig.~\ref{fig:lstmProblems}]. Finally, sequential dependence persists through hidden‑hidden connections \(h_{\!t}\!\rightarrow h_{\!t+1}\); this serial recursion precludes parallel processing along the time axis inherent in Transformer attention mechanisms.]

To remedy these shortcomings an amended structure known as **xLSTM** employs two fundamental extensions over canonical LSTMs―*exponential gating* together with a *matrix‑valued memory* which houses richer content unions while preserving computational tractability.]

Exponential Gating Mechanism  

# definition of input‑gate exponential activation  
In standard long short‑term memory units the amount by which newly produced information \(z_{t}\) contributes to the cell’s internal representation is regulated by an **input gate**  
\(i _{t}\in[0,\,1]\).  Conventionally a logistic function \(\sigma(\cdot)\) bounds this value between zero and one, thereby restricting how strongly new data can overwrite memories accumulated earlier.
The *xLSTM* constellation offers an alternative – *exponential* gating – whereby the same linear pre‑activation  
\[
\tilde{i}_{t}\ :=\ \mathbf w^{\top }_{i}\,\mathbf x_{t}
            \ +\ r_{i}\,\mathbf h_{\,t-1}
            \ +\ b_{i}
\]
is passed through an unbounded exponentiation:
\[
i _{t}\ :=\ \exp (\tilde{i}_{t}) .
\]
Because \(\exp :\mathbb R \rightarrow (0,\infty)\), \(i _{t}\) is no longer constrained.
Consequently new content \(z_{t}\) may be incorporated very aggressively when it is beneficial – for instance,
when a repeatedly occurring event deserves a larger hold‑on weight – while still being able to diminish its influence strongly when necessary.
This multiplicative freedom allows the unit’s *constant error carousel*,
\(c_{t}=f _{t}\,c_{\,t-1}+i _{t}\,z_{\,t}\),
to grow at an exponentially fast rate should ever a sequence fashion accumulation patterns requiring drastic amplification.
Such behaviour would be impossible under strict sigmoid bounds.
Nevertheless because unbounded scalar gains risk numerical explosion,
the xor-principle underlying *exponential* changes demands careful stability handling – available via both normalisation and an auxiliary **stabiliser** – explained next.



# definition of forget‑gate exponential activation  
The **forget gate** decides how much past evidence stored in \(c_{\,t-1}\) persists after time step \(t\).
In vanilla LSTMs its pre‑activation feeds into a sigmoid:
\(f _{t}= \sigma (\tilde f _ t)\).
This confines its effect strictly between “forget everything” (\(0\)) and “keep everything” (\(1\)).
Directly lifting this restriction again yields two mutually compatible variants:
either keep the classical sigmoid,
or replace it altogether:
\(f _{ t}
   =   \sigma (\tilde f _ t)\;
    {\rm OR}\;
    \exp (\tilde f _ t)\).
With an exponential pairwise replacement,
the translator also attains **opacity beyond one**.
A positive pre‑activation generates \(f _ { t} > 1\), meaning historical traces are amplified during propagation:
the universe shines brighter rather than dimming—a useful behaviour when one wishes memories not only survive precisely,
but become increasingly influential –
perhaps learning long patterns that repeatedly recur throughout a corpus.
Conversely most negative inputs collapse swiftly towards zero,
realising virtually infinite recall decay – sharper than any constant‑value decay achievable by sigmoids.
Thus this flexibility lets the model traverse scans ranging from full erasure
to cumulative reinforcement within one parameter family.



# normaliser state accumulating future forget gateways  
The great expressive power bestowed upon \(i _ t,\!f _ t\)
would otherwise bring quantisation instability:
each multiplication heightens other partial products across all time steps.
To rescale states prior their production into bounded representations,
xLSTMs introduce an auxiliary **normalisation accumulator**, denoted \(n _ t\).
Because every bias addition term \(i _ t\, z _ t\) enters linearly into \(c _ t\),
averaging its inflow over time suffices for uniform buckowing.
Formally,
\(n _ { t}
   =   f_ { t}\;\!n_{{}{\,t-1}}
     +   i_ { t}.
\)
Though superficially resembling another cell update path,
\(n _ t\) tracks separately––understanding explicitly how much storing has contributed at every timestamp.
When preparing the hidden output
\[
h_ { t}
   =   o_ { t}
     \odot
     {\tilde h }_ { t},
    {\textstyle{\smash{\quad}}\textwith ({displaystyle{\quad}})}
   {\textstyle{{}}}
{\bf where }\;
{\tilde h }_ { t}
   =   c_ { t}/{ n_ { t}}
 ,
the ratio \(c_ { t}/n_ { t}\)
yields an invariant estimate –
large increases in raw memorised quantity are automatically normalised against their history‑weight count,
thus preserving learned dynamics whilst protecting against runaway amplification.
Such scalar reshaping mirrors batch divisions common in attention architectures,

even tiny parameter perturbations inflate cell values catastrophically:

\(m_ {{}}
