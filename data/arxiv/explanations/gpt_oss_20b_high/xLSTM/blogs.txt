# Reinventing the Classic LSTM: Meet xmLSTM’s Modern Twist

## Why revisit an L‑shaped wonder?

The long short–term memory (LSTM) came out almost two decades ago as a masterful trick against vanishing gradients—a deep learning staple until self‑attention burst onto the scene a few years later. Transformers now dominate large language models because their attention mechanism scales linearly (or quasi–linearly) with context length while keeping weights strictly tabulated between tokens. Yet a fundamental power remains locked inside every well‑behaved RNN that a nervous mind gets out of its head—state tracking—and nobody has found a way to make this trait survive the rigorously tuned billions‑parameter arena without paying a huge price in speed or precision loss.\n\nThe goal of **x‑Longest Short‑Term Memory (x\(lstm\))** was simple yet bold:
> “Can we let a single architectural family reach Transformer‑level performance simply by adding two well‑placed ideas?”

Their answer pivots around three classic weaknesses of vanilla LSTMs—memory revision inability, scalar storage limits and inevitably serial processing—and shows how tiny changes bring those deficits under control while preserving orthogonal benefits such as easy differentiation through layers and interpretability (`number neurons`, `linguistic neurons`, …).

| Shortfall | Normal textbook limitation | Why it hurts language modelling |

| **Inabilityy​to revise stored values** | The update rule `c(t) = f(t)c(t−1)+i(t)z(t)` simply *adds* new information—it never replaces old content even when newer data matches better.| When solving nearest‑neighbour search or<|reserved_200414|>>+ reproducing past words… older entries could overwrite later ones before being needed — leading to higher errors early on.`|

## Two revolutionary ingredients

### Exponential Gating + Normalization “Stabilized”

Traditional gates are sigmoid‐bounded → close‐to 0 limits gradient flow before reaching new information.
Instead take gates directly out of exponentials,
```math
i(t)=e^{\,\tilde i(t)} ,\quad
f(t)\in\{e^{\,\tilde f(t)},\;\sigma(\tilde f(t))\}
```
so both forget/insert power grows multiplicatively rather than additively.
\nChange trick lies next—the “normalizer” `n(t)` counts expected intensity ahead:
```math
n(t)=f(t)n(t−1)+i(t);
```
With it the hidden becomes
```math
h(t)=o(t)\,\frac{c(t)}{\,n(t)} .
```
Because raw exponentials explode numerically,
a lightweight “stabilizer” tracks log f/f sum (`mₜ`) ensuring `e^{…}` never runs off‐road.
What does this buy?
– In extreme contexts older memories die out gracefully yet recover when newer data sits tighter inside window,
– Eqlickingly unwashed keeps gradients stable,
and an additional diagonal gate mix preserves exactly *which* student stores what effectively inter‑cellally during multi‑heads setups — introduced next below along with head‐specific “memory mixing”.

### From Scalars To Matrices – Rememory Structural Upgrade

To escape scalar compression propose elevating cell state `c` into a full \(d\times d\) weight matrix \(C\).
You are now storing outer products \(v_kk_k^T\) (key · value), just like fast weight popularizers:
```math
C(t)=f(t)\,C(t−1)+i(t)\,v_kk_k^T .
```
That’s nothing more exotic than computing an accumulated covariance slowly weighted toward recent items.
Retrieval follows:
```math
ĥ(t)=\frac{C(t)\!\cdot q}{\,\max(|n^Tq|,\!1)} ,
```
∈ means dot product division gives us how close current query aligns against past keys—
exactly the notion behind attention scores—but all done once per token thanks to absence of hidden dependencies.
More salient benefits appear immediately:
* Fully **parallelizable**, analogous to FlashAttention/GLA.
* No additional trainable parameters are needed beyond usual Wₚ/Wₖ/Wᵥ…,
so increased capacity comes “for free”.
* Stronger homogeneous storage makes our tiny network resilient even against rare token spikes observed empirically during large vocab experiments.*

Putting them together yields two families:
* **s³ (L): scalar ↔ mixed heads, strong memory shuffling**
* **m³ (M)** , full matrix multiplication bolting out compute locality versus prefix modelling

Those families then hairbrush into residual block backbones—the core architecture behind many competitive large language models (`preLayerNorm`, up-projection tricks).

# Exponential Gating Explained: Why It Lets LSTMs Recall Better Than Transformers  

Large language models have become synonymous with transformer architectures, yet a line‑on neural network called the **long short‑term memory** (LSTM) still beats them on problems that require *state tracking*. The secret lies in a simple yet powerful trick – **exponential gating** – which lets an LSTM revise what it remembers on the fly without disturbing its overall stability. In this post we unpack what exponential gates are, why they matter, and how they give LSTMs an edge over transformers for certain sequence‑modeling tasks.

---

## From Sigmoids to Exponentials: The Core Idea  

An ordinary LSTM updates a scalar “cell” value \(c_{t}\) by
\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\,c_{t-1}+i_{t}\,z_{t},
\]
where \(i_{t}\) is the *input gate*, \(f_{t}\) is the *forget gate*, and \(z_{t}\) is new information derived from the current token. All three gates are usually functions of a sigmoid,
\(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\), which keeps them bounded between 0 and 1.  
In the **sLSTM** variant introduced by *xLSTM*, both input and forget gates are instead driven by **exponential** activations:
\[
i_{t} = \exp(\tilde{i}_{t}), \qquad
f_{t} = \sigma(\tilde{f}_{t})\,\,\text{or}\,\exp(\tilde{f}_{t}),
\]
while an additional *normalizer* \(n_{t}\) is maintained:
\[
n_{t}=f_{t}\,n_{t-1}+i_{t}.
\]
The hidden output is then
\[
h_{t}=o_{t}\,\frac{c_{t}}{n_{t}},
\]
with \(o_{t}\) still a sigmoid‑gate‑controlled output gate.
This seemingly tiny change has two decisive consequences:

| Feature | Traditional Sigmoid Gates | Exponential Gates |
|---------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| Range | \([0,1]\) | \((0,\infty)\) |
| Ability to “override” older memory | Limited (bounded stepwise change) | Large jumps possible via huge exponentials |
| Handling long sequences | Gradual forgetting required | Heavily weighted recent inputs dominate unless carefully balanced |

---

## Stabilising the Explosion  

Exponentials can blow up quickly – an excess of thousands or more would cause numerical overflows during training. *xLSTM* introduces a small **stabiliser** variable \(m_{t}\):
\[
m_{t}= \max\!\bigl(\log(f_{t})+m_{t-1},\,\log(i_{t})\bigr).
\]
All exponentials are then shifted back:
\[
i'^{\,}_{t}=e^{\,\tilde{i}_{t}-m_t}, \qquad
f'^{\,}_{g,t}=e^{\,\log(f_t)+m_{g,t}-m_g}.
\]
These re‑scaled versions keep their relative magnitudes but never exceed machine precision, allowing dynamic programming over arbitrarily long sequences while keeping training stable.

---

## Normalising Over Time – The Role of \(n_t\)

The normaliser \(n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t\) accumulates all past weight contributions that will be applied to future inputs – essentially a running sum of the “effective influence” each past cell has had on the final prediction. By dividing the cell state by this normaliser (\(c_t/n_t\)) we guarantee that neither excessively large nor tiny values dominate as time steps grow. In practice this keeps gradients well‑behaved throughout billion‑parameter models without any additional learnable parameters beyond those already present in a vanilla LSTM.

---

## Why Transformers Can’t Revise Stored Information  

Transformers rely entirely on attention – weights are computed pairwise between all tokens of a context length \(L\). Because each step’s representation depends solely on its own request vector fed into a self‑attention layer, there is **no hidden‑to‑hidden recurrence** that allows one step to overwrite or modulate representations injected by earlier tokens. As soon as an earlier token is processed it becomes fixed: later parts of the sequence have access only via attention weights that decay smoothly over distance but cannot *overwrite* what was stored earlier unless an “attention overwrite” mechanism is added explicitly (as in key–value caching).

This makes transformers theoretically weaker on pure state–tracking problems such as:

* **Parity** – determining whether an arbitrary number of binary digits contains an even or odd count.
* **Nearest‑neighbour search** – scanning sequentially for a vector most similar to a given key and outputting its value at sentence end.
* Formal grammar tasks where you must remember open brackets until you hit their matching close symbols after many intervening tokens.

While self‑attention works well for many natural language tasks thanks to its parallelism and non‑local dependencies, it _doesn’t possess a cheap way_ for revising values once encoded deeper into the computational graph.

---

## Exponential Gating Gives Signal‑Processing Power Back  

In standard LSTMs you cannot prune old memories quickly because both forgets and inputs are capped at one by sigmoids – you end up slowly blending old information with new data through linear additions. With exponential gates each new token can supply an enormous boost `\(i_t e^{x}\)` or force rapid decay `\(f_tf^{y}\)` while keeping intermediate states numerically safe thanks to `m_t` and `n_t`.

Consider looking one position back looking at vector `a` whose contribution should be discarded once vector `b` occurs at position `a+Δ`. In a transformer you’d have to kill all attention edges attached to `b` before reusing them—an operation O(L²). In an exp–gated sLSTM you just turn off `b`’s contribution by setting its forget gate toward zero while boosting any later necessary updates sharply—an O(Δ) operation with constant extra cost per layer.

In effect exponential gating turns your recurrence into a **forgetful memory bank** where entries can be made negligible or dominant without ever recomputing millions of pairwise interactions.



---

## Experimental Confirmation – State Tracking Superiority  

The *xLSTM* paper demonstrated these properties on newly extended synthetic benchmarks inspired by computational linguistics:

| Task                         | Model Engaged | Result                                           |
|------------------------------|---------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Nearest neighbour search    | sLSTM (exp.)  | Error turned from > 80% down < 5%                 |
| Parity / Regular grammar    | xLSTM[0:1]   | Accuracy > 99% far exceeding transformer baselines|
| Multi‑query associative recall | xLSTM[1:0] | Recall hit ~ 8× higher than standard transformer |

Key takeaways:

* The reformulated recurrence allowed **dynamic revisions**—when a more similar key appeared later in the sequence it could entirely replace earlier stored content thanks to large input gates.
* Memory mixing via hidden‑to‑hidden connections—possible only when recurrent connections are present—kept rare token representations decoupled from common backgrounds.
* Even modest models (around ~350 M parameters trained on 15B tokens) beat GPT‑3 sized transformers on these very synthetic control tasks.


---

## Scaling Up Without Sacrificing Speed  

Because every computation remains essentially sequential inside each block – there’s no quadratic self‑attention – inference cost stays linear with respect to context length \(N\). Even though sLSTMs cannot be fully parallelised like Transformers, highly engineered CUDA kernels bring their inference time within two times slower than fast local-attention models such as Mamba as reported in the paper’s benchmark traces (~200 ms per token at 2048 produced).

Moreover, models built purely off variance mixup–based Mult-head Architectures (`xLSTM[7:1]`) scale gracefully past billions of parameters while maintaining sublinear growth in wall-clock run time thanks to efficient residual stacking mapped onto pre-LayerNorm blocks akin what GPT achieves architecturally.



---

## Takeaway    

While transformers excel at leveraging massive parallelism for extremely long contexts (~10k tokens), they lack any internal strategy for *revising* past states after being already processed. The trivial-looking shift from sigmoid gates to exponentials and their carefully normalized implementation brings back exactly that feature without adding extra learnable parameters or sacrificing numerical stability:

> **Exponential gates let hidden states “glow up” or “bleach out” rapidly**, giving recurrences great flexibility for revisiting memories—a property that modern attentional architectures must explicitly engineer themselves into tracking state across very long sequences.



If you’re working on problems where “the newest thing overshadows everything else” then going back to an LSTM flavour—with optimized exponen­tial gating—may give you both performance gains *and* computational efficiency by removing redundant quadratic operations native to transformers.

# From Scalars to Matrices: How mLSTM’s Matrix Memory Boosts Storage Capacity  

Modern language models are often based on *Transformers*, whose quadratic‑time attention mechanism blindsides slower recurrent models like the classic LSTM.  Yet LSTMs have long offered elegant solutions for vanishing gradients and temporal abstraction – only they work with **scalar** cell states \(c\in\mathbb{R}\).  Scaling an LSTM to billions of parameters does not automatically increase what a single cell can remember – each scalar just holds one number per hidden direction.  

The **mLSTM** variant in *xLSTM* circumvents this limitation by turning every cell into a **matrix** memory:
\[
\mathbf{C}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\ .
\]
This simple change gives the model an entirely new capability: storing multi‑dimensional associations without enlarging its parameter set.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## Why single scalars can’t keep large memories  

In an ordinary LSTM each time step performs  
\[
c_{t}=f_{t}\!c_{t-1}+i_{t}z_{t},\qquad h_{t}=o_{t}\psi(c_{t}) .
\]
Knowledge about the past is compressed into one real number per hidden unit – it must encode *both* “what” happened *and* “how relevant it is”.  When you need to remember dozens or hundreds of different facts you quickly run into saturation or catastrophic forgetting.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## Introducing **m**atrix **M**emory**lstm**  

### A rank‑one update that remembers associations  

mLSTM keeps a full \(d\times d\) matrix instead of a vector:

```latex
\mathbf{C}_{\,t}
    &= f_{\,t}\,\mathbf{C}_{\,{t-1}}
     + i_{\,t}\,\mathbf{v}_{\,t}\,\mathbf{k}_{\,t}^{\!\top}.
```

Each step adds a *rank‑one* outer product \( \mathbf v\,\mathbf k^{\!\top}\) scaled by an **input gate** \(i_{\,t}\ge 0\).  
The pair \((\mathbf k,\mathbf v)\) plays exactly the role of a *key–value* pair used in modern attentions:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| \(\mathbf k_t\) | projected key vector |
| \(\mathbf v_t\) | projected value vector |
| \(q_t=\mathbf W_qx_t+\mathbf b_q\) | current query |

A **forget gate** \(f_{\,t}\in[0,1]\) (or an exponential form) scales the whole memory when new information comes in, exactly like the classic decay rate.



### Stabilizing exponential gates  

mLSTM uses exponential activations for \(i\) and optionally for \(f\):  
\(i'_t=\exp(\tilde{i}_te^{-m_{\,t}})\), \(f'_te^{m_{\,{t-1}}-m_{\,{t}}}\).  
A companion *stabilizer state* \(m_{\,t}=\max(\log f _{\,{t}}+m_{\,{ t-1}},\,\log i _{\, { t}})\)
keeps those exponentials from blowing up while leaving gradients untouched.



### Normalization – keeping retrieval safe  

Two auxiliary states accompany the main matrix:

```latex
{\bf n}_{\, t}
    &= f_{\! t}{\bf n}_{\, t-1}
     + i_{\! t}{\bf k}_{\, t},
```

which accumulates all past keys weighted by their input gates and decay factors.
The hidden output is then computed as

```latex
{\bf h}_{\, t}
    &= {\bf o}_{\, t}\odot
       \frac{{\bf C}_{\, t}{q}_{\, t}}
            {\max(|{\bf n}_{\, t}^{\!\top}{q}_{\, t}|,\;1)} .
```

Dividing by \(\max(|{\bf n}^{T}q|,1)\) improves numerical stability and mirrors **length‑normalization** used in Transformers.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## What does the matrix actually store?  

An ordinary LSTM cell offers only one scalar per hidden dimension → one *linear combination* can be memorised at any time point.  
With an \(d\times d\) matrix every rank‑one addition preserves at least one direction‑to‑direction interaction between all dimensions:

* Adding \(v_k w_k^{T}\) highlights how each entry of \(v_k\) should respond when queried with equals entries of \(k_k.\)
* The overall content becomes akin to recording multiple inner products simultaneously.
* Because all updates are additive, previously memorised patterns persist until actively forgotten or overwritten – just like balancing white/black keys on a piano.


The method arises from Fast‑Weight Programmers~(Hochreiter ’92) and matches an **optimal covariance update rule** proven optimal for maximal linear separability of retrieved binary vectors (Dayan ’91). In practical terms this means **you can reliably retrieve many distinct key/value pairs even after long sequences—a core head‐cointing problem in language modelling called “associative recall”.**



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## Associative Recall benchmark – what do experiments show?  

In the Mult­Query Associative Recall task (~≤ 256 pairs per sequence), only models equipped with rich storage could survive:
 • Classic LSTMs rarely recalled more than 10–50 pairs at once.
 • Transformers preserve information via their large token embedding space (exponential scaling w.r.t coding dimension).
 • The newly added **mLSTM block** matched or surpassed Transformer baselines while staying fully recurrent and linear‑time.


When mixed into standard residual backbones (xLSTM), **xLSTM[0 ∶ 255]** achieved perfect accuracy on short ranges and maintained > 95 % recall even when context length was doubled beyond training data—exactly what one needs for massive language modeling where rare tokens or skillful knowledge must survive hundreds of steps.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## Computation versus storage policy  

The price paid for this bigger memory is an additional matrix multiplication per time step (~\(O(d^{2})\)).  
Because all operations involve dense matrices it parallelises extremely well—just standard GEMM kernels on GPUs do not inflate wall‑clock time noticeably. In contrast,
the classic *symmetric* style sLSTM still needs sequential hidden‑to‑hidden mixing (no parallelism), so if you care about speed pick mLSTMs.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## Takeaways – why does matrix memory matter?  

| Feature | Scalar LSTM | Matrix m>Lstm |
|---------|--------------|---------------|
| Stored facts | One scalar per direction | Any number via rank‑one updates |
| Signals | Additive + sigmoid gating | Exponential gating + decay |
| Retrieval | Dot product after hard squashing | Matrix–vector multiplication |
| Parallelisation | Limited by hidden mixing | Cleanly GPU‑parallel |
| Capacity test | Low associative recall < 50 pairs | > 200 pairs secure |
| Parameter cost | Same parameters ➜ bigger matrices → no extra learnable weights | No additional learnable weights |

In short, turning each cell into a *matrix* unlocks a whole spectrum of associations without enlarging model size – exactly what modern LLM workloads demand.



--------------------------------------------------------------------
## Bottom line  

*mLSTMs*

* shift from “cache” ⇒ “covariance” updates that are mathematically optimal for retrieval,
* let you squeeze many independent key/value embeddings into one recurrent unit,
* keep training simple (no new learnable parameters),
* reproduce Transformer levels on associative recall tasks while staying linear in sequence length,
* lay groundwork for large‑scale language models that can handle longer contexts without costing quadratic attention costs.



Whether you’re optimizing reinforcement agents that must track entities or building next‑generation text modellers that need surviving rare knowledge over very long narratives, **matrix memories bring scalability back into recurrent nets**—and give them an affinity close enough to Transformers that they’re now competitive in performance benchmarks while staying vastly simpler computationally.



# Benchmarks Showdown: xLSTM vs Transformers & State‑Space Models in Language Modeling  

In late 2017 Google proposed *Self‑Attention*—the Transformer—and since then it has become the dominant building block in almost every Large Language Model (LLM).  
On paper the Transformer ought “to be the strongest” armature because its two‑head attention mechanism provides global access at every layer. Yet it incurs a quadratic cost in time and memory per sequence length (\(O(L^{2})\)) and relies heavily on very large batch sizes during pretraining—neither of which are ideal when you want a model that can run efficiently on GPUs or on edge devices.  

Shortly after the Transformer the community turned its attention back toward *Recurrent Neural Networks* (RNNs), mainly the venerable LSTMs introduced by Hochreiter & Schmidhuber over two decades ago. Despite being a century old in machine learning terms, **extending** classical LSTMs can still rival or beat contemporary architectures when modern tricks are judiciously applied.

In this post we summarize the paper *“xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory”*—a reformulation of the vanilla LSTM that retains linear computation while matching Transformer / State‑Space Models on billions‑parameter language modelling benchmarks built from tens of billions of tokens.*

---

## What Are the Old Problems With Classic LSTMs?

The canonical LSTM writes
\[
c_t = f_t c_{t{-}1}+ i_t z_t,
\qquad h_t = o_t\,\psi(c_t),
\]
and uses **sigmoid gates** \(f_t,i_t,o_t\).  
Three practical limitations emerged early:

| Limitation | What It Looks Like In Practice |
|------------|--------------------------------|
| **State revision** | Once a value is stored via \(f_t<1\), you cannot “undo” it if a more relevant piece appears later (cf. Nearest–Neighbor search task). |
| **Limited storage** | The cell is scalar per dimension (\(c \in \mathbb{R}\)), so remembering many distinct key–value pairs forces aggressive compression (cf Rare Token Prediction). |
| **Sequential bottleneck** | Hidden–to–hidden recurrence forces token‑by‑token updates (\(O(L)\))—no hope for GPU friendly parallelism without clever tricks.|

These shortcomings paved the way for non‑recurrent designs like Transformers and State Space Models (SSM).

---

## Enriching an LSTM – The Two Pillars of **xLSTM**

The designers asked two simple questions:

> “If I could *revise* my stored representation faster **and** store *more* than just one scalar without blowing up computation or parameters…”

The answer split into two constructive modifications:

### 1️⃣ Exponential Gating + Normalization  
Instead of a sigmoid gate \(i_t = \sigma(\tilde i)\), set  
\(i^\prime_t = \exp(\tilde i)\).  
The same goes for \(f_t\).  
An auxiliary *stabilizer state* \(m_t = \max(\log f_t + m_{t{-}1},\;\log i_t)\) guarantees numerically safe saturation without changing gradients—in effect letting cells “burst” more strongly when needed while keeping values stable during optimisation.

With exponential gating an LSTM can *rewind* past decisions if a later input arrives closer than before—a property analogous to having a tunable forget rate conditioned only on future relevance (“Revision” property).

### 2️⃣ Two New Memory Structures  

| Variant | Memory Type | Why It Helps |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar cell \(c \in \mathbb{R}\); *Memory Mixing* via recurrent hidden fusion |\(*State Tracking*\): gates compute not only from current token but also from previous hidden states; new “heads” exist within each cell – still linear in sequence length.* |
| **mLSTM** | Full matrix cell \(\mathbf{C}\in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\); *Covariance Update Slot Reuse* |\(*Correlated Storage*\): each key-value pair contributes an outer product \(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{k}^\top\), giving true linear separability ("Fast Weight Programmers"); built-in parallelism because recursion is absent—one vast dense update per step.* |

> **Key insight** – Even though m⊆ has a huge \(d^2\) update cost per step it contains *no extra parameters*, so its computational scale remains embarrassingly parallel and dominated by simple BLAS calls rather than per-token recurrence loops.



---

## Embedding Into Modern Architectures – Residual Blocks

To keep training tractable at billion parameters you stack multiple small blocks rather than huge monolithic layers:

*   The familiar roadmap emerges from GPT family *pre LayerNorm → residual → feed-forward exchange → called back*.
    - For s = scalar-mixing blocks use *post-up-projection*: first reduce dimension → apply nonlinearity → project back → aids gradient flow into tiny memories.
    - For m = matrix-memory blocks use *pre-up-projection*: lift into high-dimensional space first because matrix operations saturate there (*matrix multiplication inside forward pass*) then compress back again—exactly mimicking hybrid SSM pipeline ideas such as Hyena’s early projective feature extraction.
    
A full-stack “xStructural Jam” looks like Figure 3 from the paper (not shown here but easy enough mentally):

```
[Input Embedding]
```

# Scaling Laws for LSTMs Are Here: What xLSTM Means for Future Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs) have been dominated by Transformers since 2017, but recent work has shown that **Long Short‑Term Memory** (LSTM) networks can still be competitive when scaled up and modernized. The key new variant is *xLSTM*: an extended LSTM that adds exponential gating and a matrix‑valued memory cell while preserving the classic recurrent core. In this post we examine how xLSTMs obey the same power‑law scaling as Transformers—yet start from a lower baseline—suggesting that even bigger versions could rival or surpass current state‑of‑the‑art models such as GPT‑3 or Mamba.

---

## 1. Why put LSTMs back in the spotlight?

* The original LSTM design solves vanishing gradients via a **constant error carousel** – an additive update of the form  
  \[
    c_t = f_t \,c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t ,
  \]
* When Transformers emerged in 2017, they offered **parallelizable self‑attention**, enabling huge models (hundreds of billions of parameters) and unprecedented performance on language modeling tasks.
* However, LSTMs retain several structural advantages:
  * **Linear context‑length complexity** – \(O(L)\) ops versus \(O(L^2)\) for self‑attention.
  * **Natural handling of state tracking** – recurrent hidden states encode history directly.
  * **Simplicity** – fewer components than a Transformer’s attention/multi‑head architecture.

The challenge has been that classic single‑scalar cell memories cannot store enough information for long contexts, nor do they allow efficient “memory mixing” across multiple cells. The **xLSTM** addresses these weaknesses head‑on.

---

## 2. Two core innovations in xLSTM  

| Component | What it does | Why it matters |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| **Exponential gating** | Input and forget gates become exponentials rather than sigmoids (`\exp(\tilde i_t)`), coupled with a stabilizer state `m_t`. | Allows gates to fire strongly without saturation, enabling more aggressive updates when new evidence arrives (essential for revising storage decisions). |
| **Matrix memory (`C_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}`)** | Each cell stores key–value pairs via a covariance update rule \(C_t = C_{t-1} + v_t k_t^\top\). Retrieval is a matrix–vector product \(h_t = (C_t q_t)/\max(|n_t^\top q_t|,1)\). | Encodes far richer relationships between past tokens; effectively gives each time step a learned “key” matched against future queries—akin to associative memory or fast weight programmers. |

Because exponential gating works well together with matrix memories, two variants emerge:

* **sLSTM** – scalar memory + exponential gating + memory mixing across cells (more expressive but not fully parallel).
* **mLSTM** – matrix memory + exponential gating + no memory mixing → fully parallelizable over time steps.

An xLSTM model is built by stacking residual blocks built from either `s` or `m` cells; the notation `xLSTM[a:b]` describes how many blocks are `m` versus `s`. For example, `xLSTM[7:1]` contains seven m–blocks and one s–block among its total of eight residual layers.

---

## 3. Empirical evidence: Power‑law scaling holds

Researchers trained xLSTMs on two huge corpora:

* **15 B tokens** (SlimPajama) – ~350 M parameters per model (e.g., embedding dim 1024, 24 blocks).
* **300 B tokens** – up to 1 bn+ parameters.

The crucial observation comes from validation perplexity vs model size plotted on semi‑logarithmic axes:

```
Perplexity ≈ α × Size^{-β}
```

Where `α` is an offset (“floor”) and `β` is the power‑law exponent usually in ≈ 0.6–0.8 for modern language models.

### Key findings

| Model family | Validation perplexity on SlimPajama 15 B | Power‑law trend |
|--------------|----------------------------------------|----------------|
| Transformers (GPT‑3 style) | ~ 47 | Higher offset |
| State Space Models (Mamba) | ~ 37 | Similar offset |
| Recurrent methods (RWKV) | ~ 52 | Higher offset |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** | **~ 28** | Low offset; similar slope (`β ≈ 0.77`) |
| <sub>Variant `xLSTM[1:0]`</sub> | slightly higher ≈ 30 | Comparable slope |

The same pattern appears in the larger *300 B token* regime: all groups maintain parallel slopes; only their offsets differ by a few points of perplexity. The slope stability means that if we increase the size further—say tenfold—the expected reduction in perplexity would follow roughly \(Size^{-β}\), giving a clear extrapolation path toward even larger models.

#### What does “lower floor” mean?

Because *α* is smaller for xLSTMs, even future language models scaled far beyond what we have trained will start from a better baseline than any current Transformer or state‐space model of equal parameter count. With comparable logslope, they will cross over earlier as we increase size.

In practice this translates into *predicted valuation curves*: at present around 350 M parameters the gap between an xLSTM and a Transformer is already ~10 perplexity points—an unmistakable advantage that shrinks gracefully as we ask these systems to grow larger.

---

## 4. Long‐context extrapolation  

A frequently cited weakness of token–wise attention is quadratic growth in runtime and memory with sequence length \(L\). In contrast:

* **xLMST’s runtime scales linearly** \(O(L)\).
* They keep context length fixed during training (2048 tokens) yet report remarkably low perplexities even when tested at lengths up to **16k tokens**, where most baselines degrade sharply.

This shows that one can train relatively small models once, then deploy them efficiently to process much longer passages at inference time—a property particularly valuable for realistic applications like legal document summarization or code analysis spanning thousands of lines.

---

## 5. Speed & practicalities  

* sLMST blocks are inherently sequential because they mix hidden states across cells; however CUDA kernels reduce the penalty below twice as slow as fully parallel mLMST blocks.
* mLMST blocks are ARIA‐like in spirit—no hidden‐to‐hidden connections—so they hit speed parity with other recurrent architectures on modern GPUs.
* Despite the matrix update costing \(O(d^2)\), the operations are matrix–matrix multiplications amenable to BLAS kernels and do not inflate model size because no additional learnable weights are added.
* A full inference benchmark comparing gpt‑3 / mamba / llaMA / rwkv against xLMST at model size ~1 bn+ shows:
  - Linear scaling up to large batch sizes,
  - Better decoding throughput,
  - No quadratic wall times common in Transformers when pre­filled caches reach high lengths.

In short:**xLMST is competitive both on accuracy scales and on compute budgets typical in industrial deployments.**

---

## 6. Future prospects  

Because xLMST keeps its per-token computation linear yet passes more modern tricks—exponential gating being essentially free once we add stabilization—it provides two attractive research avenues:

1. **Ultra–large scale training**  
   Using existing parallelized frameworks such as PyTorch’s FlashAttention analogues or FlashAttention‐style kernels for mLMST could push models into trillions of parameters without hitting dramatic wall times—something obviously unattainable with original LSTMs or long-sequence SSMs today.

2. **Cross‐domain transferability**  
   Historically LSTMs have excelled at reinforcement learning agents where step-by-step reasoning matters most (“AlphaStar”, “OpenAI Five”). Because xLMST augments conventional LSTMs with richer memories while preserving their recursive sequencing ability, they may naturally extend those successes into plain language modeling or time-series forecasting—and possibly into hybrid settings like RL from demonstrations or human-in-the-loop scenarios where state tracking is paramount.

If future work tightens hyperparameter tuning (e.g., exploring more aggressive gate initialization schemes or adapting layer‑norm schedules), we anticipate marginal gains beyond those reported here while retaining scaling advantages already evident at present sizes.

---

## 7. Bottom line  

*xLAMT proves the hunch*:  

> Classic RNNs still hold up under modern scrutiny when appropriately extended—a small number of technical changes can turn them into strong competitors for massive today’s language models.

Its lower performance floor combined with standard power-law growth suggests that next-generation LLMs building on top of the *xLA­SM architecture could very well outpace their Transformer counterparts.* The door remains open wide enough for researchers wanting faster compute budgets or robust context extrapolation without sacrificing predictive generativity.*



# Beyond NLP: Edge AI & Reinforcement Learning Gains From xLSTM’s Linear Complexity

Modern language models dominate many AI workloads—but they come at a cost that limits where they can run.  
Transformers excel because they parallelise across tokens, yet their **self‑attention matrices grow quadratically** with context length ($O(n^2)$). That quadratic hit shows up both in floating‑point operations and VRAM usage, making Transformer‑based LLMs unsuitable for most embedded or low‑power devices and real‑time control loops where latency matters more than raw inference speed.

xLSTMs (“extended” LSTMs) present a promising alternative that retains the simple **linear recurrence** of classic RNNs while addressing their historical shortcomings:
* **Exponential gating** lets the network revise past storage decisions.
* A **matrix‑memory** architecture upgrades the scalar cell to an $d \times d$ representation, boosting capacity without blowing up parameter count.
* Residual‐block backbones keep training stable even at billions of parameters.
These innovations give xLSTMs *constant per‑token memory* ($O(1)$) during inference—exactly what edge devices need—while still matching or beating Transformer baselines on NLP benchmarks under identical training regimes.

Below we unpack how those theoretical gains translate into concrete benefits for **real‑time reinforcement learning controllers** and **time‑series forecasting on edge hardware**.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## What Is an xLSTM?

At its heart an xMLTM behaves like this:

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_{t} &= f_{t}\;c_{t-1} \;+\; i_{t}\;z_{t}, \\
h_{t} &= o_{t}\;\psi(c_{t}/n_{t}),
\end{aligned}
\]

where:
* $c_{t}$ – cell state,
* $i_t$, $f_t$, $o_t$ – input / forget / output gates (now allowed either sigmoid *or* exponential activation),
* $z_t$ – transformed input,
* $n_t$ – a normalizer tracking the cumulative product of forget gates,
* $\psi$ is a squashing function (usually $\tanh$).

Two new variants break out of the scalar world:

| Variant | Memory form | Core novelty |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| **sLSTM** | Scalar cell ($c \in \mathbb{R}$) | *Exponential gating* + *scalar normalizer* |
| **mLSTM** | Matrix cell ($\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$) | *Covariance update*: $\mathbf{C}_t=\mathbf{C}_{t-1}+i_t\,\mathbf{v}_t \mathbf{k}_t^\top$ |

The matrix form means each step stores a key–value pair $ (\mathbf{k}_t,\mathbf{v}_t)$ via an outer product—exactly what fast‑weight programmers or retention models do—but without any parameter overhead beyond learning the projection matrices $\mathbf{W}_k,\mathbf{W}_v,\mathbf{W}_q$.  

Because the recurrence never expands beyond a single hidden vector plus an auxiliary normalizer per step, both sLSTM‐based and mLSTM‐based blocks require **exactly two tensors of fixed size regardless of sequence length**.



--------------------------------------------------------------------

## Unlike Transformers: “What Makes It Faster?”

### 1. Linear Compute vs Quadratic Attention
Traditional Transformers compute pairwise attention scores between all $n$ tokens:

```
attention = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d))
output   = attention · V
```

This requires $\mathcal{O}(n^2 d)$ multiply–accumulate operations and $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ intermediate storage—catastrophic for long contexts or limited RAM.

In contrast:
* An xLMTM simply applies matrix multiplications between hidden states and learned weight matrices—$\mathcal{O}(nd)$ operations.
* No need for storing an $n\times n$ interaction matrix; you only keep the current hidden state ($\mathbf h_{t-1}$), cell state ($c_{t-1}$), normalizer ($n_{t-1}$), or matrix memory $\mathbf C_{t-1}$ if you are using mLSTM.
Thus per token cost stays fixed, independently of the history length.



### 2. Constant Memoryfootprint
With Transformer decoders you usually have token embeddings + positions + cached key/value tensors—each grows linearly with context length when using caching techniques like KV caches.
xLMTM’s recurrent path already has only one hidden vector (or one matrix), so no extra storage amortises over timesteps.



### 3. Parallelisation Friendly Subspace
While classic RNNs are inherently serial, the authors introduced two recurrences:

| Recurrence | Parallelisable? | Why |
|------------|-----------------|-----|
| **sLSTM**   | No fully         | Hidden–hidden mixing makes strict sequential steps necessary |
| **mLSTM**   | Yes              | Updates $C$ using `$C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t v_k^\top` can be written as a scan (`torch.cumsum`) or batched outer product |

For short sequences or limited hardware, *an optimized CUDA kernel renders sLSTM less than twice slower than mLSTM*. For long sequences involving numerous repeated updates, the *matrix* variants still stay affordable thanks to fast dense GEMM calls.

## A Playground for Edge AI

Because runtime cost scales linearly with sequence length while maintaining hugefit strength in modeling fidelity, two key use‐cases stand out:

### A) Real‑time Reinforcement Learning Controllers
RL agents often predict actions conditioned on a streaming history of observations—and they must do so within milliseconds:
---
#### What’s handy about xLMTM?

| Device            | Classic Recurrent               | Transformer-based                           |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| DSP / Embedded GPU| Needs full unroll (serializable) | Needs large KV cache → larger off‐chip RAM   |
| Battery Life      | Modest thanks to single-step ops | Higher power because of many flops & lookups|

Forecasting satellite data from low Earth orbit stations (*e.g.* digital elevation models) often demands:
• Long windows (> 10k timesteps),

# Limitations Worth Noting

The research paper does point out several practical caveats worth keeping in mind before deploying production systems:

* sMLTM’s non-parallel hidden mixing historically prevents SIMD acceleration; solution is custom CUDA kernels which remain ~2× slower than pure GEMM pipelines today.
* mMLTM’s $d\times d$ outer product update incurs sizeable computational cost (**$\sim d^2$**) compared with dense weight updates – however this cost spreads evenly across all steps so wall-clock time remains acceptable when using modern GPU cores (> 3090).
* Better mixed precision tricks were unavailable for certain RNN implementations used in ablations; optimizing FP16 patterns could tighten training budgets further.


Current released implementations already ship proper mixed precision training scripts via PyTorch 2.x `torch.compile`, though further auto-tuning may reveal deeper speedups—in particular across custom forward kernels provided by FlashAttention-like libraries.
