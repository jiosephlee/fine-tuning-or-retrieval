# Scaling Laws for LSTMs Are Here: What xLSTM Means for Future Large Language Models  

Large language models (LLMs) have been dominated by Transformers since 2017, but recent work has shown that **Long Short‑Term Memory** (LSTM) networks can still be competitive when scaled up and modernized. The key new variant is *xLSTM*: an extended LSTM that adds exponential gating and a matrix‑valued memory cell while preserving the classic recurrent core. In this post we examine how xLSTMs obey the same power‑law scaling as Transformers—yet start from a lower baseline—suggesting that even bigger versions could rival or surpass current state‑of‑the‑art models such as GPT‑3 or Mamba.

---

## 1. Why put LSTMs back in the spotlight?

* The original LSTM design solves vanishing gradients via a **constant error carousel** – an additive update of the form  
  \[
    c_t = f_t \,c_{t-1} + i_t \, z_t ,
  \]
* When Transformers emerged in 2017, they offered **parallelizable self‑attention**, enabling huge models (hundreds of billions of parameters) and unprecedented performance on language modeling tasks.
* However, LSTMs retain several structural advantages:
  * **Linear context‑length complexity** – \(O(L)\) ops versus \(O(L^2)\) for self‑attention.
  * **Natural handling of state tracking** – recurrent hidden states encode history directly.
  * **Simplicity** – fewer components than a Transformer’s attention/multi‑head architecture.

The challenge has been that classic single‑scalar cell memories cannot store enough information for long contexts, nor do they allow efficient “memory mixing” across multiple cells. The **xLSTM** addresses these weaknesses head‑on.

---

## 2. Two core innovations in xLSTM  

| Component | What it does | Why it matters |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|
| **Exponential gating** | Input and forget gates become exponentials rather than sigmoids (`\exp(\tilde i_t)`), coupled with a stabilizer state `m_t`. | Allows gates to fire strongly without saturation, enabling more aggressive updates when new evidence arrives (essential for revising storage decisions). |
| **Matrix memory (`C_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}`)** | Each cell stores key–value pairs via a covariance update rule \(C_t = C_{t-1} + v_t k_t^\top\). Retrieval is a matrix–vector product \(h_t = (C_t q_t)/\max(|n_t^\top q_t|,1)\). | Encodes far richer relationships between past tokens; effectively gives each time step a learned “key” matched against future queries—akin to associative memory or fast weight programmers. |

Because exponential gating works well together with matrix memories, two variants emerge:

* **sLSTM** – scalar memory + exponential gating + memory mixing across cells (more expressive but not fully parallel).
* **mLSTM** – matrix memory + exponential gating + no memory mixing → fully parallelizable over time steps.

An xLSTM model is built by stacking residual blocks built from either `s` or `m` cells; the notation `xLSTM[a:b]` describes how many blocks are `m` versus `s`. For example, `xLSTM[7:1]` contains seven m–blocks and one s–block among its total of eight residual layers.

---

## 3. Empirical evidence: Power‑law scaling holds

Researchers trained xLSTMs on two huge corpora:

* **15 B tokens** (SlimPajama) – ~350 M parameters per model (e.g., embedding dim 1024, 24 blocks).
* **300 B tokens** – up to 1 bn+ parameters.

The crucial observation comes from validation perplexity vs model size plotted on semi‑logarithmic axes:

```
Perplexity ≈ α × Size^{-β}
```

Where `α` is an offset (“floor”) and `β` is the power‑law exponent usually in ≈ 0.6–0.8 for modern language models.

### Key findings

| Model family | Validation perplexity on SlimPajama 15 B | Power‑law trend |
|--------------|----------------------------------------|----------------|
| Transformers (GPT‑3 style) | ~ 47 | Higher offset |
| State Space Models (Mamba) | ~ 37 | Similar offset |
| Recurrent methods (RWKV) | ~ 52 | Higher offset |
| **xLSTM[7:1]** | **~ 28** | Low offset; similar slope (`β ≈ 0.77`) |
| <sub>Variant `xLSTM[1:0]`</sub> | slightly higher ≈ 30 | Comparable slope |

The same pattern appears in the larger *300 B token* regime: all groups maintain parallel slopes; only their offsets differ by a few points of perplexity. The slope stability means that if we increase the size further—say tenfold—the expected reduction in perplexity would follow roughly \(Size^{-β}\), giving a clear extrapolation path toward even larger models.

#### What does “lower floor” mean?

Because *α* is smaller for xLSTMs, even future language models scaled far beyond what we have trained will start from a better baseline than any current Transformer or state‐space model of equal parameter count. With comparable logslope, they will cross over earlier as we increase size.

In practice this translates into *predicted valuation curves*: at present around 350 M parameters the gap between an xLSTM and a Transformer is already ~10 perplexity points—an unmistakable advantage that shrinks gracefully as we ask these systems to grow larger.

---

## 4. Long‐context extrapolation  

A frequently cited weakness of token–wise attention is quadratic growth in runtime and memory with sequence length \(L\). In contrast:

* **xLMST’s runtime scales linearly** \(O(L)\).
* They keep context length fixed during training (2048 tokens) yet report remarkably low perplexities even when tested at lengths up to **16k tokens**, where most baselines degrade sharply.

This shows that one can train relatively small models once, then deploy them efficiently to process much longer passages at inference time—a property particularly valuable for realistic applications like legal document summarization or code analysis spanning thousands of lines.

---

## 5. Speed & practicalities  

* sLMST blocks are inherently sequential because they mix hidden states across cells; however CUDA kernels reduce the penalty below twice as slow as fully parallel mLMST blocks.
* mLMST blocks are ARIA‐like in spirit—no hidden‐to‐hidden connections—so they hit speed parity with other recurrent architectures on modern GPUs.
* Despite the matrix update costing \(O(d^2)\), the operations are matrix–matrix multiplications amenable to BLAS kernels and do not inflate model size because no additional learnable weights are added.
* A full inference benchmark comparing gpt‑3 / mamba / llaMA / rwkv against xLMST at model size ~1 bn+ shows:
  - Linear scaling up to large batch sizes,
  - Better decoding throughput,
  - No quadratic wall times common in Transformers when pre­filled caches reach high lengths.

In short:**xLMST is competitive both on accuracy scales and on compute budgets typical in industrial deployments.**

---

## 6. Future prospects  

Because xLMST keeps its per-token computation linear yet passes more modern tricks—exponential gating being essentially free once we add stabilization—it provides two attractive research avenues:

1. **Ultra–large scale training**  
   Using existing parallelized frameworks such as PyTorch’s FlashAttention analogues or FlashAttention‐style kernels for mLMST could push models into trillions of parameters without hitting dramatic wall times—something obviously unattainable with original LSTMs or long-sequence SSMs today.

2. **Cross‐domain transferability**  
   Historically LSTMs have excelled at reinforcement learning agents where step-by-step reasoning matters most (“AlphaStar”, “OpenAI Five”). Because xLMST augments conventional LSTMs with richer memories while preserving their recursive sequencing ability, they may naturally extend those successes into plain language modeling or time-series forecasting—and possibly into hybrid settings like RL from demonstrations or human-in-the-loop scenarios where state tracking is paramount.

If future work tightens hyperparameter tuning (e.g., exploring more aggressive gate initialization schemes or adapting layer‑norm schedules), we anticipate marginal gains beyond those reported here while retaining scaling advantages already evident at present sizes.

---

## 7. Bottom line  

*xLAMT proves the hunch*:  

> Classic RNNs still hold up under modern scrutiny when appropriately extended—a small number of technical changes can turn them into strong competitors for massive today’s language models.

Its lower performance floor combined with standard power-law growth suggests that next-generation LLMs building on top of the *xLA­SM architecture could very well outpace their Transformer counterparts.* The door remains open wide enough for researchers wanting faster compute budgets or robust context extrapolation without sacrificing predictive generativity.*
