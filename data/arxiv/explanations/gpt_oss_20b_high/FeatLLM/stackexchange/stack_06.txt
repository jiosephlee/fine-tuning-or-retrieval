\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### "Non‑negative weight constraint" – why apply `max(w_k,0)` instead of letting weights take any value?
Question:
`Equation (3) uses ReLU on w_k so all learned coefficients are positive.

Intuitively I guess preventing negative contributions protects against wrongly discarding true positives.

But metric-wise wouldn’t allowing negative values help learn hierarchy among rules?

Could you elaborate on the intuition/principle behind restricting weights
Answer:
**Why constrain every weight \(w_k\) to be non‑negative before multiplying it with the binary  
feature vector \(\mathbf z_k^{i}\)?**  

In FeatLLM every element of \(\mathbf z_k^{i}\in\{0,1\}^{R}\) simply tells whether *a particular rule generated by the LLM is satisfied* by sample \(x^i\).  
The role of the weight vector \(\mathbf w_k\) is therefore only **to rank** these conditions according to how useful they are for predicting class \(k\).

The authors deliberately project \(\mathbf w_k\) onto the positive orthant,
\( \max(\mathbf w_k ,\, 0)\), before taking its dot product with \(\mathbf z_k^{i}\). Their rationale “ensures that **the features generated by the LLM do not reduce the likelihood of a class during learning**” (Section 5)
unfolds into several intertwined motivations:

1. **Monotonic interpretation**  
Each engaged rule is meant to **provide evidence** in favour of its target label. Making its contribution always non‑negative guarantees that *the more highly–scored rules are satisfied*, *the higher* the logit,
hence the higher expected posterior after softmax:
\[ 
    \operatorname{logit}_k^i 
        = {\underbrace{\max(\mathbf w_{k},\, 0)}_{\geqslant 0}}
           {\displaystyle\times}
           \;\mathbf z_{k}^{i}.
\]
This yields an additive–evidence view analogous to classic decision‑list or associative‑rule systems where every triggered item merely adds credit toward its class.
A negative coefficient would mean “meeting this rule actually decreases confidence”, which contradicts how human experts would formulate outcomes usually inferred from an LLM’s textual reasoning:
“If disease \(X</span>\) occurs when age > 60”, then …—an affirmative causal claim, not an exclusionary one.*

2. **Avoiding accidental cancellation**  
With binary inputs permitting arbitrary signs inflates expressiveness: two strongly correlated rules could end up mutually cancelling each other.
In few‑shot settings such cancellations are fragile—a slight shift in a single label flips gradient directions dramatically, destabilising learning and producing noisy posteriors that depend heavily on random seed.
Constraining each coefficient to be ≥ \(0\\) removes this source of variance.
Empirically (Ablation Table 4) FeatLLM’s performance degrades markedly when coarsely summing unsigned filtered binaries versus allowing fitted weights,
while keeping them signed positively keeps training stable even when only tens of examples are provided.*

3. **Interpretability**  
A strictly non‑negative system mirrors attribute­importance readings:
“Rule j contributes \(w_{kj}\)” means “satisfying this antecedent increases our belief in class \(k\\)”*.  
If signs were allowed one would have pairs such as “Rule j” + “Rule l”
  weighing referee semi­negatively without any intuitive semantic story;
understanding why satisfying two seemingly useful conditions together lowers confidence becomes opaque—precisely what résumé‑style explanations seek avoid[ ](https://arxiv.org/pdf/<…>).*

4. **Hierarchy through additive strength**  
The authors’ concern about learning a hierarchical ordering between rules can still be realised under non‑negativity:\n\t• Thicker coefficients simply express stronger support while absence (\(w_j=\,{\displaystyle 0\\}) signals irrelevance rather than detrimentality.\nComplex logical patterns such as “[A AND B] OR C” are already expressed at feature level through separate Boolean tests;
keeping their aggregation purely additive avoids mixing genuinely suppressive effects,\n  which would otherwise have been captured implicitly via extra conjunction predicates anyway.*

5. **Alignment with empirical robustness tests**  
In Section 6 (“Effect of adding spurious correlations”) FeatLLM suffers far less degradation when unrelated columns are injected compared with baseline models.
A key contributor is precisely this positivity constraint:\n  irrelevant columns may inadvertently satisfy spurious “rules” producing false positives—having all coefficients forced ≥ \(0\\)
forces those noises either to be ignored (\(w_j={\,{\displaystyle 0\\})}) or contribute positively only after they truly correlate in hold‑out data;\n  there’s no danger that satisfying them causes an inadvertent drop in logit values—which could otherwise magnify noise effects during softmax normalisation.*

Taken together, these arguments explain why simply applying ReLU before evaluating logits—in contrast with letting arbitrary signed weights roam freely—provides predictable behaviour aligned with how human users expect tabular‐rule engines:
every hint extracted from an LLM should either boost confidence or remain neutral,
never demote it directly.*
