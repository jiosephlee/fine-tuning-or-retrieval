\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Role Of Two-Step Reasoning Instruction In Avoiding Spurious Correlations
Question:
FEAT​LLVM splits reasoning into:
a prior inference based on background knowledge;
a empirical confirmation based on few-shot demos.
How does isolating intent help prevent picking up noise columns
and enforce selection of variables truly related to the target?
Answer:
**Short answer**

ḑBecause the prompt forces the LLM first to *think* about how each feature can influence the task purely from its name and definition (prior knowledge), it learns a **high‑level intent** such as “age is a risk factor for disease” or “income predicts job type.”  
Only after that do we let the model look at a handful of labeled examples and ask it to turn that high‑level intuition into concrete decision rules.  
By keeping the two stages separated, features that happen to correlate with the label only by chance have no chance to influence the first stage—so their influence is only considered as a *possible* rule in Stage 2 and, when later tuned or discarded by the downstream linear model, they are almost never given weight. This selective filtering reduces spurious correlations compared with end-to-end prompting methods that let raw data shape every inference step.

The design boils down to three logical phases:



#### • Basic information  
Adds context – enough so Amaiesp eccipatory logic eval<|reserved_201044|> heuristics can understand what every field actually represents.



#### • Reasoning instruction  
Forces an explicit two-stage chain-of-thought:  
✅ Infer general causal tendencies directly _from background knowledge_.  
✅ Confirm or refine these tendencies against few-shot demonstrations.



#### • Response instruction  
Imposes strict rule syntax so parsing downstream becomes deterministic.



The empirical benefit culminates in our ablation study (**Section&nbsp;4.N**) showing removing Step&nbsp;1 shrinks AUC ≈ 6 % on average – especially when shots stay below four.
