# Background and Related Work

The growing interest in few‑shot learning for tabular data arises from practical constraints such as expensive labeling pipelines or scarce labeled examples that plague many real‑world scenarios—from medical diagnostics to financial risk assessment. A parallel line of research seeks to leverage the already abundant inductive biases embedded inside large language models (LLMs), which are pretrained on vast amounts of text and therefore encode rich world knowledge that might be useful for structured‐data problems. This chapter surveys both tracks—classic few‑shot tabular approaches followed by recently proposed language‑interfaced solutions—before positioning the *FeatLLM* framework within that landscape.

## Few‑shot learning fundamentals – transfer priors vs unlabeled augmentation

In its simplest form, few‑shot learning aims to fit highly expressive models on an exceptionally small training set \(k \ll N\). Confronted with this scarcity it is natural for a learner to pick up spurious patterns that do not generalise beyond the observed examples (\cite{bartlett2021deep}). Two complementary strategies have emerged to mitigate this danger:

* **Transfer priors** inject external prior knowledge into the learner so that its inductive bias aligns better with latent structure present even in tiny datasets. An example is unsupervised pre‑training on a large collection of unrelated tabular records followed by fine‑tuning on the target task (\cite{somepalli2021saint}). Pre‑training objectives such as reconstructing masked entries (\cite{arik2021tabnet}) or contrasting augmentations (\cite{bahri2022scarf}) force a model often to learn representations that capture global characteristics rather than memorising idiosyncratic correlations present only in a few labels.
* **Unlabeled augmentation** leverages external pets—existing datasets without annotations—to enlarge the effective sample space via synthetic generation or semi‑supervised training procedures (\cite{yoon2020vime}). “Synthetic‐distribution” mixups (~\cite{zhu2023xtab}) and auxiliary diffusion models (~\cite{zhang2023generative}) explore the manifold induced by unlabelled records so that downstream classifiers see smoother decision boundaries despite lacking abundant labels.\footnote{\textbf{Note}: Although several pioneering frameworks experiment with hybrid likelihood avenues—combining brief supervised runs with deep generative priors—the primary goal remains avoiding overfitting while preserving robust inductive structure inherited from cross-domain corpora rather than simply increasing signal noise.\xspace}

These paradigms manifest differently in practice depending on whether one has access solely to supervised signals or may bring along millions of unlabelled structured documents.\textit{}

## “TabPFN”, “SCARF”, “STUNT” and other prototypical approaches

A handful of research efforts crystallised around these general ideas:
* **TabPFN** trains a Bayesian neural network on *synthetic* tabular samples drawn from a mixture of distributions devised on holistic column types (\(C_{\text{id}}), C_{\text{num}}, C_{\text{text}}\). Once trained offline its evaluation procedure simply concatenates both training and test records into one prefixed input sequence keying‐in every feature value together (\(x^{\text{train}}, x^{\text{test}}\)). This has two advantages—no further optimisation at test time and an innate form‐weak prior over possible table layouts \(\rightarrow\) effective generalisation even under brutal sparsity.\footnote{\textbf{SP}: Indeed this purely Bayesian paradigm tightly couples prediction confidence propagation to known covariate dimensions while honouring deterministic training budgets.\xspace}
  
* **SCARF** adopts a contrastive objective positing two ``proxy’’ representations per record built through stochastic augmentations over categorical/granular columns.\(\mathrm{s}\) While unsure if fully supervised measurements were considered present beforehand \cite{s}, its distinguishes itself by liberating representations under absolutely unlabeled conditions—to minimise non‑linear entanglement between features thereby stabilising downstream classifiers.\footnote{\textbf{SP}: When applied naturally under little labelled data conditions it shortens intra‑task covariance shape divergence dramatically.\xspace}
  
* **STUNT** implements an implicit curriculum whereby a child network learns gradually increasing complexity deadlines using distributions curated from broadly distributed domains—its use case being clinical predictions where described `missingness patterns' strongly articulate selection bias.\(\mathrm{s}\)

Beyond these three frames exist hybrids such as *TransTab*, which leverages inter‑column semantics learned through transformer attention over column names alone (\cite{wang2022transtab}), contributing another source outside typical statistical frameworks; likewise *Lift* introduces multi‑task prompts indexing over pretrained object detectors fed into GLM backbones (\cite{dinh2022lift}), suggesting encoding raw numeric patterns via linguistic tokens directly supplied within prompts.\(\mathrm{s}\)
These exemplars share an idealized picture—the label space arrives later via sufficiency transformation functions applied either during training stage modifications or entirely inside probability spaces generated either online or offline.

# Dataset Definition  

In every experiment reported in this paper we start with a standard labeled tabular data set  
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{D}\;\triangleq\;\Bigl\{\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{\,i},\,\mathbf{y}^{\,i}\bigr)\Bigr\}_{i=1}^{N},
\end{equation}
where $N$ denotes the total number of instances available in the collection.  
Each observation $\mathbf{x}^{\,i}$ is a $d$‑dimensional real‑valued (or categorical) feature vector,
$\mathbf{x}^{\,i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.
The associated target $\mathbf{y}^{\,i}$ is represented as a one‑hot encoding over $c$
predefined classes:
$\mathbf{y}^{\,i}\in\{0,1\}^{c}$ and $\sum_{k=1}^{c}\!y^{\,i}_{k}=1$.
Thus every sample carries both a complete description in terms of measured attributes
and its correct classification label.



In addition to numeric data, every column comes bundled with a natural‑language name
and descriptor—details that are crucial when invoking a large language model.
Let  

\[
F=\bigl\{f_{j}\bigr\}_{j=1}^{d}
\]

denote the ordered collection of feature names as strings,
and let $\mathrm{desc}(f_{j})$ stand for any accompanying textual definition.
These descriptions are supplied verbatim in the prompts given to the language model;
they allow semantic grounding so that engineering rules can refer properly to each column type
(e.g., “the age variable” or “the diagnosis category”).

Because FeatLLM operates under very limited supervision,
only a small subset of the data—$k$ samples—is retained for training:
\(S=\left\{\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{\,s},\,(\,\mathbf{y})^{\,s}\bigr)\right\}_{s=1}^{k}\subseteq\mathcal D.\)
Even though $k$ can range up to several dozen shots,
it will always satisfy $k\ll N$, as described next.



# Few‑Shot Regime  

The term *few‑shot* refers precisely to this situation wherein only 
a handful $k$ ($<N$) labeled observations are made available during training.
Formally one writes \(k = o(N)\) or \(k \ll N.\)
A small value of $k$ has several consequences:

* **Statistical scarcity** – The empirical risk minimizer may overfit almost trivially,
since there is insufficient evidence to estimate complex decision boundaries.
* **Spurious correlations** – Random coincidences between particular values
of irrelevant attributes and labels become prominent,
as already seen in many works on few‑shot learning across modalities.
* **Computational considerations** – Model families that demand extensive tuning or many passes
through large data sets become infeasible when only very few labels exist,
necessitating extremely lightweight downstream learners such as linear models.



Insightfully, FeatLLM turns these drawbacks into opportunities:
by leveraging **prior knowledge embedded inside large language models (LLMs)**,
it generates *rules* that capture principled relationships between columns and target classes.
Because these rules are derived from both generic world knowledge
(the LLM’s pre‑training corpus) and empirical insight (the handful of labeled examples),
they tend not to rely on accidental correlations found in a tiny data slice.



# Rule‑Based Binary Feature Construction Roadmap  

Once an LLM has been prompted appropriately (details appear in Section 3),
it emits *rule sets*—synthetic conditions expressed in natural language—
that discriminate between different classes.
To make these statements usable by standard machine learning tools,
FeatLLM translates each rule into a boolean predicate applied to every instance.
For every class $c \in \{1,\dots ,c_{\textsc{total}}\}$ we gather 
a collection 

\[
Z_{c}\;\triangleq\;\bigl\{r_{c,r}\bigr\}_{r=1}^{R},
\]

where $R$ denotes either a fixed budget (e.g., ten) or whatever number
the system elects during generation.
When applied to an observation $\mathbf{x}^{\,i}$,
each predicate produces either 0 or 1 depending on whether $\mathbf{x}^{\,i}$ satisfies it:
 
\[
z^{\,i}_{c,r}
=\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } r_{c,r}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{\,i}\bigr)=\text{\tt True}\\[4pt]
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
      \tag{$*$}
\]

Collecting all $R$ outputs forms an \(R\times 1\) *binary feature vector*

\[
\boldsymbol{\!z}_c^{\,i}
=
   \begin{bmatrix}
     z^{\,i}_{c,1}\\[2pt]\vdots\\[2pt]z^{\,i}_{c,R}
   \end{bmatrix}
   \;\in\;\bigl\{\!0,\!1\bigr\}^{R}.
   \tag{$**$}
   \]

This construction is performed independently for each candidate class,
producing at most \(c_{\textsc{s}}R\) new descriptors per instance.
All such vectors are gathered into an augmented data matrix
(e.g., concatenating every $\boldsymbol{\!z}_c^{\,i}$ side‑by‑side),
which becomes the sole input fed into the lightweight downstream learner (typically a non‑negative linear model).

In words:
every row now records which *semantic patterns* posited by an LLM apply
to its particular record,
and these patterns become explicitly quantifiable signals—a far cry from raw numerical columns alone.
The succeeding training phase learns how much weight should be given 
to each pattern so as best predict its associated class probability.

Prompt Engineering For Rule Extraction  

# Basic information description  
In every run of FeatLLM the first fragment that reaches the language model serves two intertwined purposes: it tells the model what we are asking it to predict and it tells the model how a unit‑row in our table relates linguistically and semantically to its own columns.  

The task formulation has always been phrased in natural language question form so that the model can resort immediately to its vast training background on declarative interrogatives – e.g., “Does this patient’s medical record contain evidence of chronic heart failure?” followed explicitly by whether the answer should be “Yes” or “No”.  Each cell’s contents however carries its own type – numbers, dates, identifiers, categories – so every column name is accompanied by an explicit **type tag** obtained either from an accompanying metadata file or inferred heuristically when none was supplied.  When a column has a categorical domain, its possible values are listed next so that the model knows how many discrete alternatives exist and accepts combinations such as “Female”, “Male”, “Other” etc.  

Within this introductory stanza logic depends heavily on *contextual grounding*: highlighting feature types curbs type mismatch problems later during parsing (for instance insisting that an inequality never appears against a nominal column). A brief set rationalizes correctness checks after subtitle insertion before proceeding past look‑ups for administrative aspects (*e.g.* target label name).  

This initial board also contains **serialized demonstration samples** – short sentences formed from real data points serving as linguistic anchors (see §Serialization scheme). In particular every example includes both input feature keywords followed by their values (“age is 47”) and finishes with an explicit label (“Answer – Yes”). These exemplars forge an immediate bridge between statistical format *and* readable narrative enabling subsequent reasoning components downstream.

# Reasoning instruction  
Having presented a canonical view of problem specification and sample facts, the next chord stems from qualitative analysis – how does “the model reason”?  

This step is deliberately split into two logical sub‑steps communicated verbatim before any accidental reaction bends towards spurious patterns hidden deep within hundreds of columns.*

*Step I*: The virtual expert first consults **global causal gravities** stemming purely from prior medical landscapes‐or economic theories encoded throughout its corpus yet *without* looking at our provided examples yet (*denoted generically as */bulletHead:modelsamplelist)). This deliberate deferment acts like turning off memory piecemeal influences - preventing inadvertent exploitation of random statistical artifacts specific only to our tiny training set.*

*Step II*: Armed now with high‑level implications formulated in general terms—“a smoker’s average expired air tends towards lower thresholds”—the model ingests our few snapshots sequentially supplies them side‑by‑side under each identified category template (Yes/No) thereby converting abstract tendencies into concrete decision predicates specific for each class.*

Attention payingly mandatory here was acknowledging any **feature type** listed before (**Step I**) guaranteeing generated inequalities have consistent syntactic signatures (`>`, `<`, `==`, `!=`) matched against numeric columns whereas categorical columns receive membership tests (`∈ {A,B}`patterns*) – avoiding impossible expressions such as attempting arithmetic comparison over dates.*  

Choice parameters preceding mapping these findings forced explicit boundary lines stipulating *exactly twenty generic rules per desired outcome*, striking equilibrium between under‑and over‑parameterisation while preserving prompt aesthetics (*a comfortable compliance bracket fits comfortably nothing larger than thirty tokens since GPT‑3's context capacity drops below usable levels past 15K tokens.*)



# Response instruction  
When patterns settle after Step II we recruit another disciplined opinion piece—the LLM’s *outputer*. Reasoning had produced unstated potential classifiers which get deduced *clauses*. To funnel these clause strings cleanly into trainable bits downstream we command them through strict YAMLish grammars declared pre‑promptatively climatefully locked.*

Specifically users outrigh pointers tell GPT again eg,

> *“For each class return ten statements labelled sequentially ‘rule0’, ‘rule10’, ‘..., ‘rule9’. Conclude every rule element begins directly after colon space.”*

Hence learning generative confirmatory Yoh behaviour—

```yaml
class_A:
    rule0 : <condition>
    …
    rule9 : <condition>
class_B:
    ...
```

This eliminates semi‑formal cosmic ellipsis leaving precisely identifiable keys such that even marginal lexical variations (‘greater’ ↦ ‘>’) become invisible once further `.execute()` parse block parses left side expressions regardless lexical noise—a OOP procedure trivializing bridging syntactic shot propagation variability introduced during paraphrasing.*

With these exact pieces ready recursion loops STOP. At run–time ≥30 epochs weight tuning compute immediately high confidence logistic dependencies computed via Eq.\eqref{klogit}, rendering temporary reliance on LLM eradicated.



In summary FeatLLM’s prompt engineering pipeline takes four systematic strokes laid along logically coherent axes—capturing *task*, *data*, *reasoning*, *response*—culminating immediately measurable binary vectors suitable for efficient post‐processing without yet proving any perilous regressions along typical artificial neural network pipelines customary today.*

Language-to-Code Parsing Pipeline  

In *FeatLLM*, large language models serve primarily as expert feature engineers rather than direct predictors per sample. Once an LLM has produced natural‑language expressions describing conditions that distinguish classes—called **rules**—the next challenge lies in transforming these freeform sentences into machine‑readable predicates that can be evaluated efficiently over ordinary tabular data sets. This transformation constitutes the **language‑to‑code parsing pipeline** described below, which preserves all amenity offered by powerful LLMs while obviating continual API queries at test time.



# Noisy syntax  

Typical programs that convert human language to executable logic rely on handwritten regular expressions matching textual patterns such as "`>`, `<`, `≤`, `≥`". However phrased rules frequently incorporate verbalized operators—for instance “\(x\) has a value greater than \(5\)” instead of "`x > 5`". Because tabular feature names are embedded verbatim in these sentences (e.g., `'age' -- greater than -- '50'`), even slight variants disrupt naïve regex tokens expected by static parsers.  

In FabLLM this issue becomes evident when a rule read from the model contains phrases classicalized into unconventional punctuations (“greater than”, “below”, “at least”), or even alphabetical forms (‘above’, ‘below’) mandated by reduction steps taken while manipulating templates seen during training (`Serialize`). Differences are invisible when presented visually yet are critical during numeric comparison operations performed programmatically later on the data row fields derived from `f_i` definitions written out explicitly (`“f_1 is …”`). Consequently straightforward pattern matching such as `/[<>]/i` repeatedly fails because there simply are no single-character operator symbols left within these natural strings.



# Self-consistent code generation  

To sidestep erroneous hand-crafted regular expressions, *FeatLLM* harnesses *self-consistent* python code generation built directly atop its own inference cycle. The model receives an instruction package clearly articulated around a **function template** similar to  

```
def matches_rule(row):
    # row : dict mapping feature names → values
    return (...)
```

Accompanying documentation describes arguments (`row`) and desired return type (`bool`). Within its logic stem it reads all pertinent expressions extracted via rule inference (§ 3). Because transformers have been trained on vast amounts of source-code corpora—including stubs implementing short conditionals—they reliably translate semantically equivalent natural-language sentences into concise boolean logic loops (`return row["age"] > 50`). Crucially, adopting a uniform template permits **rigid output vocabulary**, allowing easy walking through returned codes later.


During inference LLMs deterministically map verbal statements back onto canonical operators (`“greater than” → ‘>‘`, `“less or equal” → ‘<=’`). When noise remains—a case noted empirically—the combination narrows down pattern variability allowing strictly statically typed transformations through minimal post-processing steps financed only by simple conditional checks.



# `exec()` mechanics  

Once rule snippets arrive—themselves being flawless python functions—their execution proceeds by feeding every input row sequentially through Python’s native `exec()` mechanism:

```python
def iterate_rows(rows):
    for r in rows:
        yield match_rule(r)
```

At encoding time different rows are annotated individually using their original column values assigned directly incompatible types:
*numeric* fields stay numbers,
*categorical* fields remain strings,
*missing* entries become `None`.  
The engine runs one unified callable across all rows producing one-hot truth values (`1`/`0`) within a two-dimensional matrix $\mathbf{Z}\in\{0,1\}^{N\times R}$ where $N$ denotes samples and $R$ boundaries matches total generated predicates (10 per target class × number of classes). This matrix replaces raw numeric columns in subsequent learning stages without requiring additional data transforms once produced.



The gathered binary tableau becomes immediately accessible behind-the-scenes [[linearly]](https://symp.org); logistic weights are subsequently clippedted [positive] domain ($w_k\leftarrow \max(w_k,\phantom{-}0)$), gaining semantically interpretable importance scores after training (`cross‐entropy` loss) on merely few-shot examples ($k< N$, sometimes $k=4$). Because every row undergoing evaluation experiences precisely identical deterministic flow—calling precisely one short parser function—the resulting likelihood vector ($p^i$) offers highly reproducible behaviour across trials.



# Security / about reproducibility  

Every script returned by *FeatLLM* contains only arithmetic comparators, basic logical connectors (`and`, `or`, `not`) applied directly over column references supplied at runtime—a restricted computational subset lacking file I/O or system call symbols inherited during tokenization phase dominated by public open-source repositories (< 10 % cryptographic commands).</br>
Executing such confined scripts consequently admits negligible risk when run within isolated memory frames managed internally by standard CPython runtime (`exec()` injection flag off). Hence practitioners may evaluate bulk datasets without exposing sensitive pathways avoiding inadvertent data leakage.</br>
Reproducibility follows effortlessly because repeated requests under identical temperature settings yield stable parsing functions once seeded deterministically—a property rooted in zero-temperature self-consistency among GPT models measured experimentally within *FeatLLM*. Subsequent application thus depends solely on pre-computed Boolean matrix $\mathbf{Z}$ coupled with learned weight vectors uploaded once during training stage.

Rule–Based Binary Features And Lightweight Classifier  

---

# Rule Pool  
In FeatLLM every few‑shot training instance is turned temporarily into natural‑language text and fed together with concise task information into a large language model (LLM).  
From these prompts the LLM produces *rules*, i.e., concise logical conditions written in English such as “*age > 50 and smoker*”.  
Once a set of rules is obtained for every target class \(c=1,\dots ,C\), each rule acts like a Boolean guard that can be evaluated against every table entry and thereby yields a **binary feature** indicating whether the entry satisfies this guard or not.  
For one sample \(x^{\,i}\) and one target class \(c\), let  

\[
\mathbf{z}_{c}^{\,i}\;\in\;\{0,\tfrac12,\dots,\,1\}^{R}
\]

denote the resulting vector whose component
\(z_{c,r}^{\,i}\) equals \(1\) if the sample satisfies rule \(r\), otherwise \(0\).
Here \(R=\texttt{numberOfRulesPerClass}\) is fixed (the authors chose ten)
and consequently every outcome has exactly \(R\) binary descriptors.
These vectors comprise what we call the **rule pool** – they form an intermediate
feature bank that isolates out-of-sample predictive structure inferred by
the LLM before any downstream supervised learning takes place.
Because every component simply records satisfaction of a human‑synthetic condition,
the resulting bank inherits clear semantic meaning freely interpretable
by end users or domain experts while simultaneously reducing memory footprint
compared to raw tabular columns.



# Positive Weighting Scheme  
Having constructed a high‑dimensional Boolean matrix
\(Z=\bigl[\,z_{c,r}^{\,i}\,\bigr]\),
we wish an inexpensive predictor able to convert these indicators into an estimate
of how probably a row belongs to each target.
A conventional approach would multiply independent.Weights however,
if left free could learn negative coefficients,
thereby **suppressing** evidence that has been confirmed.
In FeatLLM we deliberately enforce **monotonicity** through projection:
for every target \(c\),

\[
w'_{c,j}= \max(w_{c,j},\, 0)\quad(j=1,\dots,R)
\]

which simply keeps positive coefficients unchanged while clipping any negative
values at zero.
Consequently every learned coefficient always *raises* support for its
associated rule,
consistent with our intuition that confirmation should never worsen
a target’s likelihood when expressed through logical guards.
This clamping also keeps parameter sparsity desirable when data are scarce,
avoiding spurious dampening effects induced by weak samples.



# Softmax Over Logits  
With positively projected weights available,
the single‑shot predictor proceeds via an elementary linear map followed by
probabilistic normalization.
For sample \(x^{\,i}\) and target \(c\),

the logit is computed as  

\[
l^{\,i}_{c}=w'_{c}\!\cdot\, \mathbf{z}_{c}^{\,i}
          = \sum_{j=1}^{R} w'_{c,j}\;\!z^{\,i}_{c,j},
\]

where “·’’ denotes inner product;
here each application reduces essentially to summation weighted by learned confidences.
To transform these raw scores into coherent posterior estimates,
they pass through the soft‑max operator,

\[
p^{\,i}_{c}= \frac{\exp(l^{\,i}_{c})}
                 {\sum_{t=1}^{C}\exp(l^{\,i}_{t})}
             .
\]

After normalization we obtain a valid probability vector
\(p^{\,i}=(p^{\,i}_{1},…,p^{\,i}_{C})^\top\)
whose entries clearly represent how plausible each class is given the presence
or absence of every rule.
This probabilistic view provides well‑known calibration properties,
insuring comparability even when different numbers of Rules arise during
bagged repetitions.



# Training Regime  
The only parameters learned during supervised training are the vectors \(w'_c\)
for every target.
Feasible optimization is achieved adaptively following established best practices:
Adam optimizer sits on top,
run with learning rate \(\eta=0 .01\),
and trained until convergence or up until a cap of two hundred epochs 
(both limits were chosen experimentally without large tuning effort).
Loss minimization uses categorical cross‑entropy,
which computes discrepancy between predicted distribution \(p^{\,i}\)
and ground‑truth one‑hot labels exposed through those small few‑shot training set;
cross‑entropy faithfully penalises confident yet wrong predictions,
thus driving gradients toward correct coefficient adjustments.
Because rule shapes are deterministic once sampled,
no further fine‑tuning or back-propagation through LLM calls occurs;
once coefficients stabilize, inference system reduces entirely 
to inexpensive matrix multiplication plus soft‑max operation.



# Alternative Naïve Scoring  
Before resorting towards coefficient optimisation steps,
one could directly employ **unweighted summation** over satisfied rules:
for sample \(x^{\,i}\),

and target \(c:\)

here count\( _{c}^{\, i}= \sum_{j=1}^{R} z^{\, i}_{c,j}. 

This plain score treats every extracted guard equally potent;
a pivotal mild threshold can transform it into indicator predictions.
The authors carried out controlled comparisons (the ablative evaluation),
demonstrating that while naïve counting provides acceptable performance
on very coarse problems,
omitting coefficient learning systematically underperforms especially when
spurious associations between unrelated columns arise.
Consequently their final design preserves minimal computation cost yet offers notably higher driven accuracy thanks "
to statistical preferences captured by learned non‑negative weights.

By structuring knowledge furnished by large language models into **binary rule features**, constraining their linear combination through “positive attribution’’ penalties, normalising outcomes via **soft‑max**, and optimising coefficients solely on sparse labelled sets,
FeatLLM achieves powerful yet extremely lightweight inference –
a key building block enabling trustworthy tabular policy applications in few-shot regimes._END_
