# Motivation behind studying very low‑label regimes in structured data  

Most traditional tabular learning pipelines assume access to thousands or tens of thousands of labeled instances so that statistical estimators such as logistic regression or tree ensembles can acquire reliable parameter estimates. Yet real‑world domains such as clinical diagnosis, financial risk assessment, or predictive maintenance often provide only a handful of labeled samples because annotation is expensive or risky while unlabeled data are abundant nonetheless.\n\nIn low‑shot settings this scarcity induces severe overfitting: even modestly complex model families may resonate spuriously with noise present in the few training items,\nselectively capturing correlations that do not generalize.\nHence there has been sustained interest across disciplines—to build models that learn genuine predictive patterns while avoiding anecdotal associations produced by accidental chance among small sample sets.\n\nLarge Language Models (LLMs), pretrained on unsupervised corpora containing billions of tokens, encode diverse types of world knowledge—including symbiotic relationships among clinical biomarkers,\neconomic indicators,\nand statistical jargon.\nThis prior knowledge permits speculation about causative or correlative pathways without needing large amounts of task‑specific data!\nEarly studies \cite{enhancing_llms_for_tabular_applications}\nhighlighted how exploiting this implicit knowledge yields consistent performance gains over conventional machine‐learning baselines when only a few supervised pairs remain.\nNonetheless researchers continued grappling with practical obstacles before adopting constructor models analogous to what Fea­tLLM proposes.\n\n# Limitations of existing end‑to‑end LLM predictors  

When an LLM is leveraged directly for tabular prediction, most frameworks adopt one among two well‑known paradigms:\n\n* **In‑context learning**—prompting the model with several serialized examples followed by a query utterance targeting a novel record;\n* **Parameter‑efficient fine‑tuning**—injecting task metadata into continuous adapters or LoRA modules.\n\nBoth paradigms incur two types of bottlenecks:\n\n1. **Per‑sample inference overhead**  
    Every new test instance triggers an independent call into the model’s host infrastructure.\n    This latency translates directly into large wall‑clock times once stringently billed APIs are employed.\n    Moreover larger accelerator budgets become essential when model size grows beyond freely available options.\nn\n2. **Prompt capacity constraints**
