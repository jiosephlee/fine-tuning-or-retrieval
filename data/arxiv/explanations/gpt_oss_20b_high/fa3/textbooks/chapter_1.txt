\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

Chapter 1: Foundations of Transformer Attention  

# Query-Key‑Value Dimensionality  
In a single transformer head one normally begins by projecting an input sequence of length \(N\) into three distinct tensors:
\(Q,K,V \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}\), where \(d\) denotes the hidden dimension associated exclusively with that head.
These three tensors are often called query, key and value respectively,
and they reside side‑by‑side in memory so that every token produces \(d\)-dimensional query,
key and value vectors.
In practice a multi‑head block uses different linear projections \(W_Q,W_K,W_V\) per head,
but mathematically it suffices to think once–per‑head.
The fact that both queries and keys use the same dimension \(d\) is vital;
it permits a simple inner product between any query vector \(q_i \in \mathbb R^d\)
and any key vector \(k_j \in \mathbb R^d\), producing one scalar similarity measure.
Because there are \(N\) queries against \(N\) keys,
the naive computation requires evaluating \(N^2\) such scalar products – an important source
of both computational cost and memory traffic later on.



# Scaled dot-product Scores  
The raw similarity matrix between queries and keys is obtained through a scaled dot product:
\[ S = \alpha\, Q K^\top,\qquad 
   S_{ij}= \alpha\, q_i^\top k_j ,\]
where \(K^\top\) denotes the transpose over the token dimension.
The scalar factor 
\( \alpha=\frac1{\sqrt d}\) has been introduced empirically by Vaswani et al.,
and it mitigates numerical instability when entering an exponential function.
Indeed, without division by \(\sqrt d\), especially when \(d\) grows large,
the unscaled inner products tend towards high magnitude values,
causing unstable exponentials during softmax calculation.
Thus scaling ensures that most entries comfortably lie inside the range where  
\(e^{-x}\) behaves smoothly.



# Row-wised Softmax Normalization  
Once all pairwise scores have been assembled into matrix \(S\),
each row needs independent normalization so that it becomes a probability distribution:
\[ P=\mathrm{softmax}(S), 
   P_{ij}= 
   \frac{\exp(S_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(S_{ik})}.  \]
Because each score vector corresponds exclusively to one query,
the operation is performed independently 
row-by-row – i.e., every query attends over **all** keys according
to probabilities listed along its own row.
Here it becomes useful to reduce numerical range further:
before applying exponential one usually subtracts the maximum element
within that row – the quantity denoted rowwise‐maximum – which does not alter overall probabilities while guaranteeing non-negative exponents.



# Output Construction  
Having obtained probability weights \(P\),
the next step mixes these weights against their corresponding values:
\[ O = P V .                                                   \]
Thus every entry \(O_i = P_{i,:}\!V^\top\) becomes a weighted average over all values –
in other words an attentive readout associated
to token \(i\).
For multi-head attention these single-head outputs
\(O^{(h)}_i (h=1,\dots,h_{\rm heads})\)
are simply concatenated along channel dimension.
An optional learned projection follows;
in practice this concatenation-and-project step ensures that rope embeddings and positioniness remain manageable across heads.



# Gradient Flow  
During training one needs gradients w.r.t.\ three inputs \(Q,K,V.\)
Denoting upstream gradient wrt output by  
\(dO=\partial L/\partial O\),
the chain rule yields compact expressions:
1. propagate onto values  
   \( dV = P^{\!\top} dO ,                         \)
2. propagate onto probabilities  
   \( dP=dO V^\top ,                               \)
3. propagate through softmax  
   \( dS= {\rm dsoftmax}(dP),                     \)
   where for any single probability vector \(p=\mathrm{softmax}(s)\)
   the derivative follows  
   \( {\rm dsoftmax}(ds)= (\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(p\bigr)-pp^\top)\,ds .\\[2pt]
   E.g., differential turn out row-wise into an ‘outer product’ form after simplifying.\)

4–5 bring gradients through votes onto queries/keys – scaled back through α  
\( dQ=\alpha\, dS\,K,\quad 
   dK=\alpha\, dS^\top\,Q .                       \)

These relations follow purely symbolically from matrix‐calculus identities;
no evaluation tricks beyond those announcements are required.



# Complexity Analysis  
Consider one head only.
Computing scores requires multiplying an
\(N\times d\) by a \(d\times N\) matrix – about 
\(2 N^2 d\) floating‑point operations (multiply–add per scalar");
you still do another similar multiplication when forming probabilities against values –
another roughly half that arithmetic load—thus total forward FLOPs ≈ four times 
\(N^2 d.\)
When there are \(h_{\!heads}\) heads,
the inner products decouple across heads – hence total work scales linearly:
\[{\rm FLOPs}_{forward}
      =4\,N^{2}\,h_{\!heads}\,d .      \tag{1}                            \]
This matches empirical counts reported under performance experiments:
for example for given numbers (\(N,\ h_{\!heads},\,d)\)
plugging into equation (1) reproduces millions‐to‐billions layoffs quoted experimentally.



Side note – these equations ignore small overheads such techniques such as numerically stabilising subtraction,
midpoint reductions inside softmax,
or doing partial materialisation tricks inherent to FlashAttention kernels;
they merely reflect pure arithmetic growth.





---  

Together this discussion recapitulates how attention proceeds through its scalar stages—
from constructing weighted similarity matrices,
normalising them into probability rows,
to mixing values––and even clarifies how gradients propagate backward naturally via chain rule logic.
The quadratic cost inherent in (1) explains why hardware specialists pursue ever faster or lower‑precision variants –
exactly what FlashAttention pioneered – while keeping exact symbolic forms unchanged so researchers may reason directly about model behaviour regardless of implementation platform.
