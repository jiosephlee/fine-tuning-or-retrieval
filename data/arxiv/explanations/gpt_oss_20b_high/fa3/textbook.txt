Foundations of Transformer Attention  

# Query-Key‑Value Dimensionality  
In a single transformer head one normally begins by projecting an input sequence of length \(N\) into three distinct tensors:
\(Q,K,V \in \mathbb R^{N\times d}\), where \(d\) denotes the hidden dimension associated exclusively with that head.
These three tensors are often called query, key and value respectively,
and they reside side‑by‑side in memory so that every token produces \(d\)-dimensional query,
key and value vectors.
In practice a multi‑head block uses different linear projections \(W_Q,W_K,W_V\) per head,
but mathematically it suffices to think once–per‑head.
The fact that both queries and keys use the same dimension \(d\) is vital;
it permits a simple inner product between any query vector \(q_i \in \mathbb R^d\)
and any key vector \(k_j \in \mathbb R^d\), producing one scalar similarity measure.
Because there are \(N\) queries against \(N\) keys,
the naive computation requires evaluating \(N^2\) such scalar products – an important source
of both computational cost and memory traffic later on.



# Scaled dot-product Scores  
The raw similarity matrix between queries and keys is obtained through a scaled dot product:
\[ S = \alpha\, Q K^\top,\qquad 
   S_{ij}= \alpha\, q_i^\top k_j ,\]
where \(K^\top\) denotes the transpose over the token dimension.
The scalar factor 
\( \alpha=\frac1{\sqrt d}\) has been introduced empirically by Vaswani et al.,
and it mitigates numerical instability when entering an exponential function.
Indeed, without division by \(\sqrt d\), especially when \(d\) grows large,
the unscaled inner products tend towards high magnitude values,
causing unstable exponentials during softmax calculation.
Thus scaling ensures that most entries comfortably lie inside the range where  
\(e^{-x}\) behaves smoothly.



# Row-wised Softmax Normalization  
Once all pairwise scores have been assembled into matrix \(S\),
each row needs independent normalization so that it becomes a probability distribution:
\[ P=\mathrm{softmax}(S), 
   P_{ij}= 
   \frac{\exp(S_{ij})}{\sum_{k=1}^{N}\exp(S_{ik})}.  \]
Because each score vector corresponds exclusively to one query,
the operation is performed independently 
row-by-row – i.e., every query attends over **all** keys according
to probabilities listed along its own row.
Here it becomes useful to reduce numerical range further:
before applying exponential one usually subtracts the maximum element
within that row – the quantity denoted rowwise‐maximum – which does not alter overall probabilities while guaranteeing non-negative exponents.



# Output Construction  
Having obtained probability weights \(P\),
the next step mixes these weights against their corresponding values:
\[ O = P V .                                                   \]
Thus every entry \(O_i = P_{i,:}\!V^\top\) becomes a weighted average over all values –
in other words an attentive readout associated
to token \(i\).
For multi-head attention these single-head outputs
\(O^{(h)}_i (h=1,\dots,h_{\rm heads})\)
are simply concatenated along channel dimension.
An optional learned projection follows;
in practice this concatenation-and-project step ensures that rope embeddings and positioniness remain manageable across heads.



# Gradient Flow  
During training one needs gradients w.r.t.\ three inputs \(Q,K,V.\)
Denoting upstream gradient wrt output by  
\(dO=\partial L/\partial O\),
the chain rule yields compact expressions:
1. propagate onto values  
   \( dV = P^{\!\top} dO ,                         \)
2. propagate onto probabilities  
   \( dP=dO V^\top ,                               \)
3. propagate through softmax  
   \( dS= {\rm dsoftmax}(dP),                     \)
   where for any single probability vector \(p=\mathrm{softmax}(s)\)
   the derivative follows  
   \( {\rm dsoftmax}(ds)= (\mathrm{diag}\!\bigl(p\bigr)-pp^\top)\,ds .\\[2pt]
   E.g., differential turn out row-wise into an ‘outer product’ form after simplifying.\)

4–5 bring gradients through votes onto queries/keys – scaled back through α  
\( dQ=\alpha\, dS\,K,\quad 
   dK=\alpha\, dS^\top\,Q .                       \)

These relations follow purely symbolically from matrix‐calculus identities;
no evaluation tricks beyond those announcements are required.



# Complexity Analysis  
Consider one head only.
Computing scores requires multiplying an
\(N\times d\) by a \(d\times N\) matrix – about 
\(2 N^2 d\) floating‑point operations (multiply–add per scalar");
you still do another similar multiplication when forming probabilities against values –
another roughly half that arithmetic load—thus total forward FLOPs ≈ four times 
\(N^2 d.\)
When there are \(h_{\!heads}\) heads,
the inner products decouple across heads – hence total work scales linearly:
\[{\rm FLOPs}_{forward}
      =4\,N^{2}\,h_{\!heads}\,d .      \tag{1}                            \]
This matches empirical counts reported under performance experiments:
for example for given numbers (\(N,\ h_{\!heads},\,d)\)
plugging into equation (1) reproduces millions‐to‐billions layoffs quoted experimentally.



Side note – these equations ignore small overheads such techniques such as numerically stabilising subtraction,
midpoint reductions inside softmax,
or doing partial materialisation tricks inherent to FlashAttention kernels;
they merely reflect pure arithmetic growth.

Together this discussion recapitulates how attention proceeds through its scalar stages—
from constructing weighted similarity matrices,
normalising them into probability rows,
to mixing values––and even clarifies how gradients propagate backward naturally via chain rule logic.
The quadratic cost inherent in (1) explains why hardware specialists pursue ever faster or lower‑precision variants –
exactly what FlashAttention pioneered – while keeping exact symbolic forms unchanged so researchers may reason directly about model behaviour regardless of implementation platform.

# Why Long Context Matter

Large language models have become indispensable tools for a wide range of tasks that require coherent reasoning over massive amounts of textual or multimodal information. In real‑world deployments—from chat assistants that answer questions based on archived reports, to AI agents that plan complex actions over extended periods—the ability to process very long contexts can be decisive. Yet treating each additional token naïvely multiplies computation effort by squared sequence length due to the core self‑attention operation $\mathbf S=\alpha\,\mathbf Q\,\mathbf K^\top$. Understanding why such quadratic behavior is problematic—and what developers have tried historically—provides the motivation behind recent algorithmic breakthroughs such as {\sc FlashAttention‑3}.  
Below we explore concrete empirical motivations, early shortcuts that were adopted, modern architectural responses inspired by learners such as *LongT5*, and intrinsic limits observed when measuring transformer runtimes against parameter budgets which together reveal the urgency of efficient long‐context design choices.

---

## Empirical Examples from Chat Assistants over Multi‑Document Reasoning Patterns

Practical requirements for very long context first surfaced in systems where an assistant must synthesize knowledge spread across many documents simultaneously. Consider a smart help desk where an LLM is given query inputs along with dozens of knowledge‑base articles and internal notes – sometimes spanning hundreds of pages – then tasked with generating concise answers or recommending next steps based on that collective corpus. Chapter structure recognition, table extraction from PDFs, or academic literature review are further examples where disparate text fragments must be cross‑referenced before inference proceeds. These scenarios were highlighted directly in studies such as *LongT5*~\citep{guo2021longt5}, *Scrolls*~\citep{shaham2022scrolls} and *Yarn*~\citep{peng2023yarn}, which demonstrated training consistent performance across thousand‑token chunks—an order‐of‐magnitude more than conventional models did at inference time.  

The impact is twofold:

1. **Enabling Richer Reasoning** – By exposing all relevant passages simultaneously rather than stitching partial summaries back into separate passes, an assistant can capture subtle dependencies between earlier passages and later observations.
2. **Reducing Interaction Latency** – Rather than iterating bidirectionally between two tool calls (e.g., search “document A”, present snippet → search “document B”, adjust hypothesis), a model trained on millions of tokens can reply after a single pass consuming everything at once—a single latency head‑butt instead of repeated round trips which would otherwise dominate response time when dealing with complex dialogs tied together by extensive context histories % see user interaction case cit _sun2019bert4rec_ & _yao2022react_ 

This empirical evidence strongly motivates system designers who require inference engines capable of sustaining billions‐of‐operations per second while still keeping careful track of memory traffic per token – precisely where CUDA‐enabled attentions encounter their bottleneck fielded by devices such as Nvidia H100 GPUs operating at thick bandwidth ceilings yet frustrated by negligible SMEM usage owing purely noise on global memory reads/writes when sequence length balloons beyond one thousand tokens.

## Shortcuts Explored Historically

To tame self‑attention’s $O(N^2)$ complexity musicians—a combination of AI researchers—have adopted several shortcuts before their research ecosystem could embrace more fine-grained parallelism such as fused read–write kernels pioneered later:

* **Approximate Locality Tricks** – Early work introduced restricted query/key windows which keep similarity evaluation within a moving band around each token (often called “local” or “causal” attentions). In practice this means only evaluating $O(Nw)$ entries per token rather than $O(N^2)$. However it sacrifices cross‑segment interactions.
* **Selective Kernel Fusion** – Some research leveraged kernel fusion mechanisms keeping small blocks embedded back-to-back while using GPU memory hierarchies so all weight matrices residing sufficiently compactly remain on-chip—delivering modest savings but still demanding many \(N\times N\) reads/writes even if computationally trimmed down.
* **Sparse Attention Techniques** – Scholarly efforts explored various structural sparseness patterns designed specifically for highly parallel hardware (see ChYM [~20] / TALM [~21] style papers). Techniques include block-wise sparsity layouts or sampling-based approaches wherein at each head only few key–value pairs survive until final softmax computation.
* **Software Optimizations** – Methods such as *FlashAttention* [Dao et al., 2017] introduced tiled communication schemes fusing softmax reductions locally so intermediate matrices never traverse off‑chip SRAM.\hfill({call}) Further refinements produced *FlashAttention‑2*, which reorganized work distribution along sequence length dimension while leveraging WGMMA via asynchronous producer–consumer warp groups—giving signficant savings eventually refactored again into *FlashAttention‑3*, harnessing Hopper’s Tensor Memory Accelerator and FT8 units.\hfill(\sectionref)

While these strategies delivered visible speedups compared against naïve implementation paths—especially at moderate sequence lengths—they consistently fell short once typical industrial workloads pushed beyond several thousand input tokens because extra overheads reached saturating points manual manual retraining or special hardware customizations became demanding requirements beyond what any single abstract layer could guarantee.

# Standard Attention vs Earlier FlashAttentions

Understanding how conventional multi‑head self‑attention has been accelerated on modern graphics processors requires tracing two distinct design philosophies that evolved during recent years.  
The first philosophy—what practitioners have traditionally called **“standard” attention**—employs three consecutive SIMD matrix multiplications separated by stages whose outputs are written back to global device memory  
(\texttt{HBM}).  
The second philosophy—the lineage denoted **FlashAttention** (original prototype) followed closely in its successor **FlashAttention‑2**—introduces locality‑aware tiling together with an intertwined computation–communication schedule that eliminates almost all intermediate writes until a single output tensor is produced.



## Standard kernel flow  

In its elementary form an attention head receives three tensors,
$\mathbf{Q},\,\mathbf{K},\,\mathbf{V}\in{\mathbb R}^{N\times d}$,
and produces 
the per‑token representations 
$\mathbf{O}\in{\mathbb R}^{N\times d}$ according to  

$$
\mathbf{S}\;\leftarrow\;\frac1{\sqrt d}\,\mathbf{Q}\,\mathbf{K}^{\!\top},
   \qquad 
   \mathbf{P}\;\leftarrow\;\operatorname*{
      {\rm softmax}}\!\bigl(\mathbf{S}\bigr),
   \qquad 
   \mathbf{O}\;\leftarrow\;\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{V}.
   $$

On GPUs this procedure is usually split into separate CUDA kernels recorded directly with CUBLAS calls:
1. A **generalized–matrix multiply** (GEMM) generates $\mathbf S$.  
2. Since ${\rm softmax}$ involves an exponential followed by normalisation,
the vendor‐supplied routine rewrites every element,
shifts it by $\operatorname*{
      {\rm rowmax}}(\!\mathbf S)$,
calculates entry‑wise $z_{ij}=e^{s_{ij}-m_{i}}$,   
computes per‑row sums $l_{i}=\,{\sum}_{j}z_{ij}$,
and stores both the shifted scores $z_{ij}$ (forming an intermediate score matrix)
and their sums/utilisation factors those vectors scalar‐wise inverted later;
3. A second GEMM multiplies these probabilistic weights against $\mathbf V$, yielding $\mathbf O$.  

Because each of these stages touches nearly $N^2d$ elements—
the sheer size of $\mathbf S$ being $(N\times N)$ entries even when $d=64$
—and relies heavily upon DRAM bandwidth,
the *standard* route incurs significant latency stalls.
Every round trip through global device memory wipes out tenths—or often exceeding eighty percent—
of processor utilisation powerfully acquired when dense arithmetic units saturate their request queues.



## FlashAttention principle  

The driving insight behind **FlashAttention** was that many computations performed operations that never need fully materialized tensors once globally passed.
Instead they can be carried out locally whenever possible.
To realise this idea it introduces **blocked local‐softmax reduction** executed entirely in *shared* device RAM (`SMEM`) arranged cyclically so successive layers reuse buffers without introducing costly synchronisations.

During execution a threadblock handles one chunk  
$\!\boldsymbol Q_{\!i}\!\in\nolimits{\mathbb R}^{\,B_{\!r}\times d}$  
of query tokens while iterating through every chunk  
$\boldsymbol K_{\!j},\,\boldsymbol V_{\!j}
   \!\in\nolimits{\mathbb R}^{\,B_{\!c}\times d}$  
of keys/value pairs stored temporarily in registered SMEM slots.
For each pair $(i,j)$,
FlashAttention performs a **single packed GEMM**

$f_{ij}=    {\boldsymbol Q}_{i}{\,}{\,}{\,}
              {\boldsymbol K}_{j}^{\,!\top}
     \;\bigl(\equiv {\bf SS{-}GEMM}{b}_{ij}\bigr),$

after which it extracts per‑row maxima ($m_{i}$)
and computes exponentials locally (`exp(z)`),
obtains running partial sums ($l_{i}$),
and immediately accumulates the weighted contribution
      
$$
C_{ij}=Z_{ij}\,\boldsymbol V_{\!j},   \qquad (\equiv {\bf RS{-}GEMM}{b}_{ij}),
$$

into an **intermediate accumulation buffer**
representing truncated fragments of the desired output tensor
$\boldsymbol O_{\!i}$.
Only *after* every chunk has been processed does:
|–|It normalises accumulated weights across rows using accumulated maxima/sums|
|--|It writes out *one contiguous segment* $\boldsymbol O_{\!i}$ together with its corresponding log‑scale term $L_{i}=m_{i}+{\log(l_{i})}$|
into global device RAM once per CTA.
Thus every expensive DRAM round trip occurs **exactly twice** per input tensor –
during whole‐score computation entering SMEM buffers –
and never again during intra‑kernel updates,
dramatically slimming communication volume compared against vanilla split‑kernel pipelines.

Core Contributions Aligned in FlashAttention‑3  

# Warp‑Specialisation Logic  

In modern GPUs the execution model decomposes work into groups called warps – blocks of **32** threads that execute together under lockstep control instructions orchestrated by CUDA cores and special functional units known as Tensor Cores and what newly appears on Hopper is the Tensor Memory Accelerator (**TMA**). A “warp‑specialisation” design partitions a single cooperative thread array – i.e., one Compute Thread Array (CTA) – into two distinct subclasses: **producers** that handle all data movement operations and **consumers** that perform arithmetic work only once their operands become available.\  

In FlashAttention‑2 every thread independently performed loads from global memory (`GMEM`) followed immediately by Matrix–Matrix multiplications (`GEMM`). Because these loads were issued asynchronously via TMA yet still blocked one another when shared memory (`SMEM`) was shared among thread blocks or warps, portions of execution became idle waiting for data progression through multiple coherency stages – a classic hazard pin situation.\  

FlashAttention‑3 resolves this inefficiency head–on by grouping half any CTA’s warps purely as *producer* warps which carry out TMA loads into SMEM once pulled from GMEM and signal completion through simple acknowledgement steps supplied by a lightweight circular buffer managed synchronized via `bar.sync` barriers.\  
The remaining warps are assigned purely to *consumer* duties – i.e., performing triple‑buffer “Software Pipelined” GEMMs (`WGMMA` slow‐path) once blocks are present in SMEM.\

# Implementation Pathways for High Precision forward passes  

The FlashAttention‑3 kernel fuses every arithmetic operation that appears in an exact self‑attention pass into one monolithic launch hosted on an NVIDIA Hopper‑style GPU.  
A careful arrangement of data movement between global memory (HBM) and shared memory together with Warp‑Group Matrix Multiply–Accumulate (**WGMMA**) allows every threadblock (“CTA”) that works on one query tile \( \mathbf{Q}_i \) to both **produce** its inputs asynchronously through **Tensor Memory Accelerator** (**TMA**) loads and **consume** its outputs through fast GPU matmul units while also evaluating a numerically stable local softmax over rowslying inside every blocked fragment of the score matrix \( \mathbf{S}_i^{(j)} = \alpha\,\mathbf{Q}_i\,\mathbf{K}_j^\top\).
No intermediate matrices are written back to HBM during execution – instead all reduction statistics (\(\ell_k\), \(m_k\)) are kept locally until all key/value blocks have been hit, at which point a single post–processing step scales the accumulated partial outputs \( \mathbf{O}_i \) before committing them back to DRAM together with their log–sum–exp offsets \(L_k = m_k+\log(\ell_k)\).

Below we walk through each logical stage, making colour–codes match those found explicitly in Algorithm 2 (\cref{alg:flash3_wgmma}) while explaining why every operational choice matters from an intuition standpoint.

## Producer Pathload  

The producer flips its role once per CTA launch – it never participates directly in multiplication or softmax – thus it can devote **all** its threads exclusively to starting asynchronous *data movement* payloads so TMA can overlap its traffic with other checks happening concurrently inside other warps or even other CTAs.



*Tile layout.*  
Let  

* \( B_r \) denote how many tokens belong to one query chunk,
* \( B_c \) how many tokens form one key/value chunk,
* \( T_r=\lceil N/B_r\rceil,\;\;T_c=\lceil N/B_c\rceil\)

for sequence length \(N\).  
A single CTA owns one quantum of indices \(i=0,\ldots,T_r-1\); it then needs every \(B_c\times d\) slice \( (\mathbf K_j,\mathbf V_j)\) (\(j=0,\ldots,T_c-1)\).

*Circular staging.*  
A pad called *\( s\)-stage circular buffer* resides entirely in shared memory.
Each stage holds one pair four bytes worth constituents  
\(Z_{b}^{(j)}=(K_j,V_j)\in {\rm SMEM}\) indexed by modulo arithmetic `(j % s)` –
therefore when backlog size permits,
the next block overwrites an older stream whose consumer has already drained it.



### Load Scheduling Loop

```text
for(j = 0 … T_c−1):
     wait until stage(j % s) becomes free           ← barrier sync E/U
     launch async TMA load(Kj , Vk → SMEM·stage(j % s))
     commit notify(consumer,j % s)
```

The build above efficiently overlaps **all** TMA transfers:
a release/deliver handshake guarantees no producer will overwrite idle tensors,
yet because TMAs run independently of general CUDA cores,
the situation naturally becomes *wait‑free* once enough stages were provisioned.



*A binding register budget.*  
Because Tensor Cores consume no ordinary integer registers when operating on raw SMEM pointers,
the LLVM compiler can reclaim unused registers (`setmaxnreg`), lowering pressure during long pipelines.
