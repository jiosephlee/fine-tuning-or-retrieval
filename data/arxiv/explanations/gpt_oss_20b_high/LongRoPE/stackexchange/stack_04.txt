\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "LongRoPE: Extending LLM Context Window Beyond 2 Million Tokens"}

### How were the bounds for λ_i chosen for evolutionary search ([1 to s×1.25] stepping 0.01)?
Question:
In Section 4 B Search Space λ_i ranges from 1 → s·1.25 where s=L′/L.
Common sense says PI sets λ=s exactly, so why allow larger values ↑ s·1.25?
Could you explain:
– What happens if λ exceeds s?
– Why step size δλ = 0.01 was chosen instead of coarser increments/generically finer decimals?

Is there evidence that “excess” interpolation actually improves performance above naive linear scaling?
Answer:
**Short answer**

The evolutionary search does not try only “the one‑parameter” line–interpolation used by PI ($\\lambda=s$).  
It treats $\\lambda$ as a **per–dimension** scale factor that can either shrink or swell the rotary frequencies **more or less than $s$** depending on how informative each dimension is for the target sequence length.  
The upper bound $${} s\times 1\.25$$ allows the algorithm  
to *over–compress* low–frequency dimensions when it turns out such compression leaves more room left in the high–frequency dimensions  
(which are most sensitive early on).  
The step size $${}0\.01$$ was chosen experimentally as a sweet spot  
between sufficient resolution (~hundreds of grid points per dimension)  
and tractable evaluation cost in an evolutionary scheme whose fitness function is expensive luxury inference on real data ($5$ PG19 samples).

Below I give a detailed walk–through answering each part of your question.



---

### 1 When can $\lambda$ be larger than $s$?

Section 4.B defines  

$$L'={} s\,L,\qquad   
{\rm RoPE}_{n,i}
={}\Bigl[
      \cos{}!\Bigl({}\tfrac{n}{\,{\color{Apricot}$\\lambda$\color{Apricot}\,$\,{\color{Apricot}$\\beta${}^{}^{i}}}\Bigr),
      \sin{}!\Bigl({}\tfrac{n}{\,${}\\color{Apricot}\\lamda\nwait?\color{Apricot}^{ij})}\Bigr)
{}\Bigr] .
$$  

*A value $\lambda> s$ means **additional down–scaling** (larger denominator) compared with plain PI.*  
Concretely,

| situation | implication | why it can help |
|-----------|------------|------------------------|
| $\lambda< s$ | *up–scaling*: angles grow – exacerbates “catastrophic” values for very large $n$. | Causes overflow/unstable norms → hurts models trained only up-to-$L$. |
| $\lambda=s$   | Linear POI – all dimensions shrunk uniformly.| Works reasonably well up–to familiar extensions but creates severe “crowding” when $L'\gg L$, putting many distant tokens almost indistinguishable in most high–frequency dimensions.\ |\_\._|
| $\lambda>s$    | Extra shrinkage – lower–frequency components squeezed more than needed.| Lightens crowding in those dimensional bands that dominate long–range relations while keeping key high–frequency bands relatively less compressed (thanks to later optimisation over individual $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_i)$.|

Thus letting $\lambda>s$ provides a way for the optimiser's allocate *more* compression budget where it matters most,
(e.g., lower ‎$i≈d/4–d/3→low frequency )$)
and leave other parts near unity,
which empirical searches confirm gives lower perplexity than plain linear PIE.

### 2 Why restrict the upper bound to                      ***\(s\times1\.25)*?”

The authors performed an ablation study shown in the paper’s “Finding 3”.  
When extending from $4{\rm k}$→$32{\rm k}$,
the best solutions spaced between $\![\,s,\;
          s\times1\.15]\]$.  
Going deeper does **not** bring additional gains,
the model simply starts losing discriminative power again:
all rotations become excessively similar,
yielding synthetic periodicity rather than adequate distinctness.
Therefore imposing an upper limit keeps exploration focused around
the promising region identified empirically.
Moreover it guarantees numerical stability;
when angles become too small numerically there’s little practical benefit."

### 3 What happens if ${}\boldsymbol{\lambda}> s*$？

For a fixed position $n=i j,$

```
angle_before_interpolation ∝ n · ω_i .
angle_after_interpolation ∝ n · ω_i / λ .
```


Increasing ${}\boldsymbol{\lamda}> s$

* **Sharpens** locality information carried by low‐frequency std‑dev—
dimensions – these avert “crowding”;  
* **Dilutes** phase separation among more distant records  
in those very low‐frequency bands – which isn’t detrimental because those rows carry only global context;
* Leads overall loss curves falling below plain PI results once encoded through evolution –
exactly what Fig.\Sx shows.
In practice,
the loss curve rises again when ${}\\boldsymbol{\lamda}≳≈${}${}\\color{SepiaBABE}s×u70–80⟩⟩⟩.
