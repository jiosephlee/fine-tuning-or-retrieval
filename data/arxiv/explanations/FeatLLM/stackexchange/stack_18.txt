\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: FeatLLM}

### Why average probabilities across ensembles instead of stacking or logit averaging?
Question:
At inference you average per-trial class probabilities. Did you try averaging logits (before softmax), or learning a lightweight stacking model on top of the per-trial probabilities? In few-shot regimes stacking risks overfitting, but a simple logit average might improve calibration. Any comparisons in the appendix?
Answer:
Short answer: Because in our few-shot, per-trial setup averaging class probabilities is the simplest, most robust way to combine heterogeneous trial models without needing extra calibration data or risking strong overfitting. Averaging logits or training a stacker can help in some settings, but they require either consistent logit scales or additional labeled validation data (or strong regularization) that we typically do not have in the low-shot regimes studied in the paper.

Why averaging probabilities is what we used (intuitively and technically)
- Robustness to scale differences. Each ensemble trial in FeatLLM produces a small linear model (per-class weights) trained on different bagged features / few-shot subsets and hence its unnormalized scores (logits) can lie on very different scales. Averaging softmaxed probabilities normalizes away scale differences trial-by-trial, so a single badly scaled logit vector cannot dominate the ensemble. Averaging logits naively assumes comparable logit scales and biases across trials; that assumption is weak in our setup.
- No extra data required. Probability averaging needs no extra held-out data. In the few-shot regimes we target, there is very little labeled data; training a stacking model or calibrating logits (temperature scaling) requires a validation set or cross-validation folds, which increases variance and risks overfitting. The paper explicitly emphasizes low-shot settings and shows (in the Ablation section) that components that rely on training (weight tuning) become more effective only as shots increase.
- Simplicity and interpretability. Averaging probabilities is computationally trivial, easy to implement, and aligns with our goal of keeping inference light (we already avoid per-sample LLM queries). It also preserves interpretability of per-trial likelihood estimates, which fits FeatLLM’s design of combining many weak, diverse rule-based predictors.
- Implicit calibration benefits. Softmaxed outputs from small linear models (especially constrained to non-negative weights as in Eq. (1)) are often better behaved as probabilities than raw logits across diverse trials. Without consistent calibration procedures across trials, logit averaging can produce less well-calibrated final probabilities.

Why logit averaging or stacking can help (and why we didn’t use them as the default)
- Logit averaging can improve sharpness and sometimes calibration because averaging pre-softmax scores is equivalent to aggregating unnormalized affinities before normalization. If all trials’ logits are on comparable scales and biases, averaging logits and then applying a single softmax can be better than averaging post-softmax probabilities.
- Stacking (learning a lightweight combiner on the per-trial outputs) can outperform simple averaging because it can learn to upweight more accurate trials and correct correlated errors. However, stacking requires training data for the combiner. In the few-shot regimes FeatLLM focuses on, that extra training signal is tiny and stacking will easily overfit unless you use strong regularization (e.g., heavy $L_2$, sparse weights) or careful cross-validation, which substantially complicates the pipeline and can negate the low-data advantage.

Practical considerations and recommended experiments
- If you have somewhat more labeled data (or can do robust cross-validation), try averaging logits versus probabilities and compare calibration (Brier score, expected calibration error) and discrimination (AUC). If logits differ in scale, first apply per-trial temperature scaling on a small validation fold, then average logits.
- If you want to try stacking in low-shot, use a very simple combiner (e.g., non-negative linear combination with strong $L_2$ or $L_1$ penalty) and obtain combiner hyperparameters via nested cross-validation or Bayesian priors; otherwise the combiner will overfit.
- Another low-cost option is to take the geometric mean of probabilities (equivalently, average $\log$-probabilities and renormalize), which partially blends the benefits of logit and probability averaging and is less sensitive to extreme probabilities than arithmetic mean.

Summary
We average per-trial class probabilities because it is simple, scale-robust, and does not require extra labeled data or per-trial calibration — properties that matter in the few-shot, bagged-rule setting FeatLLM targets. Logit averaging and stacking are reasonable alternatives and may improve calibration or accuracy if you have enough labeled data or if you first align/calibrate per-trial logits; otherwise they risk instability and overfitting in the low-shot regimes emphasized in the paper.
