### How exactly does the 'stabilizer state' $m_t$ prevent overflow in sLSTM without altering the final output?
Question:
In Section 3.2 regarding sLSTM, the authors introduce exponential gating ($i_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t)$ and potentially $f_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t)$) to allow revising storage decisions. They note this causes overflow risks and introduce a stabilizer state $m_t$ defined as:

$$ m_t = \max \left( \log ( f_t ) + m_{t-1} , \log ( i_t ) \right) $$

They then define stabilized gates $i'_t$ and $f'_t$ using this max value. The paper claims in Appendix B that replacing the raw gates with these stabilized versions "does neither change the output of the whole network nor the derivatives." 

I am struggling to see *why* mathematically this holds true. If I divide both $c_{t-1}$ and $z_t$ contributions by different factors derived from $m_t$, doesn't that shift the balance of the cell state update equation? Specifically, how does the division by $\exp(m_t)$ cancel out perfectly in the recursive definition of $c_t$?
Answer:
The reason the stabilizer state $m_t$ prevents overflow without altering the final output lies in the fact that it acts as a **common scaling factor** that cancels out exactly in the calculation of the hidden state $h_t$. While your intuition suggests that dividing the previous cell state contribution and the new input contribution by different factors might shift their relative balance, the algebra reveals that both terms are effectively divided by the exact same quantity, $\exp(m_t)$.

In the standard sLSTM formulation (Equation 4), the cell state is updated as $c_t = f_t c_{t-1} + i_t z_t$, and the hidden state is computed as $\tilde{h}_t = c_t / n_t$, where $n_t = f_t n_{t-1} + i_t$. When exponential gating is used, $f_t$ and $i_t$ can become extremely large, causing numerical overflow. The stabilization technique replaces them with $f'_t$ and $i'_t$, defined in Equation 5 as:
$$ i'_t = \exp(\tilde{i}_t - m_t) = \frac{i_t}{\exp(m_t)} $$
$$ f'_t = \exp(\tilde{f}_t + m_{t-1} - m_t) = \frac{f_t \exp(m_{t-1})}{\exp(m_t)} $$
Notice that $f'_t$ includes a correction term involving $\exp(m_{t-1})$. This is crucial because the previous stabilized cell state $c'_{t-1}$ and normalizer $n'_{t-1}$ were already scaled down by $\exp(m_{t-1})$ relative to their unscaled counterparts. Specifically, if we denote the unstabilized variables as superscript $(u)$ and stabilized as $(s)$, then recursively we have $c'_{t-1} = c^{(u)}_{t-1} / \exp(m_{t-1})$ and similarly for $n'_{t-1}$.
