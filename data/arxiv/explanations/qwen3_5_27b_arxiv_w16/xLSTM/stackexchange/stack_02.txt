\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "xLSTM: Extended Long Short-Term Memory"}

### What is the precise role of the normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ in mLSTM retrieval, and why is absolute value necessary?
Question:
Reading Section 3.3 on mLSTM, I'm trying to understand the mechanics of retrieving information from the matrix memory $\mathbf{C}_t$. The hidden state is calculated as:

$$ \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_t = \mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t / \max \left\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \right\} $$

Where $\mathbf{n}_t$ is updated recursively as a weighted sum of keys: $\mathbf{n}_t = f_t \mathbf{n}_{t-1} + i_t \mathbf{k}_t$. 

My confusion lies in two parts:
1. Intuitively, if $\mathbf{C}_t$ stores outer products of values and keys ($v k^T$), shouldn't multiplying by a query $q$ naturally retrieve values proportional to similarity? Why do we need to explicitly divide by a normalizer involving keys?
2. Why is taking the absolute value inside the denominator critical? If $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ becomes negative, wouldn't that just flip the sign of the retrieved vector? Does this relate to numerical stability near zero or something deeper about covariance updates?
Answer:
The normalizer state $\mathbf{n}_t$ in the mLSTM serves as a dynamic denominator that accounts for the cumulative "weight" of all stored keys, ensuring that the retrieved hidden state represents a proper weighted average of values rather than a raw accumulation of outer products. While the cell state $\mathbf{C}_t$ accumulates terms of the form $i_\tau f_{\dots} \mathbf{v}_\tau \mathbf{k}_\tau^\top$, simply multiplying this matrix by a query $\mathbf{q}_t$ yields a numerator where every stored value is scaled by its corresponding key-query similarity *and* its historical gating weights. Without division by $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$, the magnitude of the retrieved vector would grow arbitrarily large as more items are added to memory, regardless of whether those items are actually similar to the current query. By dividing by $\sum_\tau (\text{gate\_product}) (\mathbf{k}_\tau^\top \mathbf{q}_t)$, which is exactly what $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ computes, the model effectively normalizes the retrieval operation so that the output magnitude reflects the relative relevance of matching memories rather than the sheer count of stored memories. This mirrors the normalization in standard attention mechanisms, preventing saturation and maintaining stable gradients during training.

The use of the absolute value $|\cdot|$ in the denominator, specifically within $\max \left\{ |\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t|, 1 \right\}$, is primarily a safeguard for numerical stability and robustness against cancellation effects inherent in covariance-based updates. Although keys and queries often have zero mean due to layer normalization (as noted in Section 3.3), their dot product with the accumulated normalizer state can theoretically become negative or extremely close to zero if opposing signals cancel each other out or if noise dominates. If $\mathbf{n}_t^\top \mathbf{q}_t$ were allowed to cross zero or approach it without protection, the division could produce extreme spikes (division by near-zero) or invert the direction of the retrieved vector unexpectedly (sign flipping), leading to unstable training dynamics or exploding activations. The absolute value ensures that we normalize by the *magnitude* of the alignment signal regardless of its sign, treating strong anti-alignment similarly to strong alignment in terms of scaling down potentially noisy retrievals. Furthermore, hard-capping this minimum at $1.0$ guarantees that even if no meaningful correlation exists between current queries and past keys (resulting in a near-zero dot product), the model defaults to returning a scaled version of $\mathbf{C}_t \mathbf{q}_t$ without blowing up numerically, allowing gradients to flow smoothly through non-retrieved states.
