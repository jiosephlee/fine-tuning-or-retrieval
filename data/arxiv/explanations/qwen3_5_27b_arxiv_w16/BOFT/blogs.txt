# From Blocky to Butterfly: A Smarter Way to Fine-Tune AI Giants

Large foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion have revolutionized artificial intelligence, offering unprecedented capabilities in language and vision. However, their sheer scale, often comprising hundreds of billions of parameters, makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), which aims to adapt these massive models to specific downstream tasks without retraining everything. One promising approach is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which preserves the geometric relationships between neurons by applying orthogonal transformations. Yet, standard OFT faces a significant bottleneck: maintaining orthogonality across high-dimensional matrices requires a vast number of trainable parameters. To solve this, researchers have introduced a novel method called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which borrows a clever structural trick from signal processing to achieve density and flexibility with minimal cost.

## The Problem with Block-Diagonal Constraints

To understand the innovation behind BOFT, we must first look at the limitations of its predecessor, OFT. The core philosophy of OFT is to transform the neurons in a layer using an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ rather than simply adding a low-rank update like LoRA does. Mathematically, if $\bm{W}^0$ is the pretrained weight matrix, the fine-tuned weights become $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Because $\bm{R}$ is orthogonal ($\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$), this operation preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, theoretically safeguarding the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining.

However, learning a full dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d \times d$ requires approximately $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which defeats the purpose of parameter efficiency. To mitigate this, earlier versions of OFT adopted a block-diagonal structure. This approach splits the dimension $d$ into smaller chunks and applies independent orthogonal transformations to each chunk. While this drastically reduces the parameter count to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ (where $b$ is the block size), it introduces rigid constraints. By treating dimensions in isolation based on their index order, block-diagonal OFT assumes that features within one block are irrelevant to those in another. This creates an undesirable inductive bias that limits expressiveness; essentially, it forces the model to ignore cross-block interactions that might be crucial for solving complex downstream tasks. Furthermore, finding an optimal sparsity pattern for these blocks remains an open mystery.

## Viewing Matrix Factorization as Information Transmission

The authors of BOFT propose a fresh perspective to overcome these limitations: viewing the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. Imagine a grid where nodes represent dimensions and edges represent connections allowing information flow. A dense matrix implies that every node at the input level can eventually communicate with every node at the output level through a series of intermediate steps.

If we try to build this connectivity using a single step (a dense matrix), we need too many edges (parameters). Instead, BOFT decomposes the transformation into a sequence of sparse matrices multiplied together: $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \dots \bm{B}_1$. In this framework, each sparse matrix $\bm{B}_i$ acts as a stage where limited information exchange occurs between adjacent layers of nodes. The goal is to design these stages such that they collectively allow full connectivity from start to finish while minimizing the total number of edges (trainable parameters). This shifts the challenge from finding arbitrary sparse patterns to designing an efficient communication topology that satisfies two conditions: dense connectivity and minimum free edges under orthogonality constraints.

## Borrowing from Signal Processing: The Butterfly Structure

Where does one find such an efficient topology? The answer lies in signal processing and computer science history: the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm. The FFT relies on a recursive structure known as the "butterfly" graph to efficiently compute transforms by breaking down large problems into smaller ones with logarithmic complexity. In this structure, information propagates through layers where pairs of nodes interact locally before mixing globally across subsequent layers.

BOFT adapts this butterfly structure for orthogonal fine-tuning. Instead of isolating dimensions into static blocks, BOFT constructs the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse "butterfly components." Each component consists of small $2 \times 2$ (or $b \times b$) orthogonal blocks arranged in a specific pattern that allows information to mix progressively deeper into the network stack. By chaining $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ such layers together, BOFT can synthesize a fully dense orthogonal matrix using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This is a significant improvement over both full dense matrices ($\mathcal{O}(d^2)$) and block-diagonal approaches ($\mathcal{O}(bd)$).

Mathematically, if we define a butterfly factor involving diagonal vectors and arrange them recursively into larger matrices $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$, we can ensure each component remains orthogonal using techniques like Cayley parameterization or 2D rotations. The resulting composite matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ maintains strict orthogonality while providing a smooth interpolation between identity matrices (no change) and complex rotations (full expressiveness). Unlike block-diagonal OFT, which cannot approximate classic linear transforms like Fourier or Hadamard transforms due to its rigid separation of dimensions, BOFT's butterfly structure naturally recovers these operations as special cases or approximations within its hypothesis space.

## Practical Advantages and Empirical Results

Beyond theoretical elegance, BOFT offers practical advantages for modern machine learning workflows. First initialization plays a critical role; BOFT initializes all butterfly components as identity matrices (via zero-initialized skew-symmetric matrices), ensuring fine-tuning starts exactly from the pretrained model state without deviation until gradients dictate otherwise. Additionally, standard dropout techniques designed for additive updates like LoRA do not translate well to multiplicative updates in BOFT due to orthogonality constraints. To address this, BOFT introduces "multiplicative dropout," which randomly replaces selected butterfly components or internal blocks with identity matrices during training to prevent overfitting while preserving structural integrity after merging weights back into the base model at inference time—a process that incurs zero latency overhead since no extra modules remain active during deployment.

Extensive experiments demonstrate that BOFT outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods across diverse domains including Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and text-to-image diffusion models. In terms of expressivity simulations approximating random dense orthogonal matrices, BOFT consistently achieves lower approximation errors with fewer parameters compared to block-diagonal OFT configurations with equivalent budgets. For instance, a configuration denoted as BOFT(9, 2)—using 9 layers with block size 2—surpasses BOFT(1, 16) in accuracy despite having significantly fewer trainable parameters. This confirms that increasing depth ($m$) while keeping width ($b$) small yields superior parameter efficiency compared to shallow wide structures typical of traditional block-diagonal methods.

# The Secret Life of Matrices: Why Fast Fourier Transforms Are Key to Efficient AI Training

Did you know the algorithm that speeds up your music streaming, the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), is now playing a pivotal role in training artificial intelligence? Recent research reveals a surprising connection between classical signal processing and modern machine learning optimization. By reframing matrix mathematics through the lens of information transmission, researchers have developed a new method called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). This technique drastically reduces the computational cost of fine-tuning massive foundation models while maintaining, and often improving, their generalization capabilities.

### The Challenge of Fine-Tuning Massive Models

Large foundation models like LLMs and diffusion models have revolutionized AI, but their sheer size makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the field relies on efficient adaptation techniques to customize these models for specific downstream tasks. One promising approach is Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which updates model weights by multiplying them with an orthogonal matrix. This method preserves the pairwise angles between neurons, theoretically safeguarding the semantic knowledge acquired during pre-training.

However, standard OFT faces a significant bottleneck: parameter efficiency. A dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters. To mitigate this, earlier versions of OFT utilized a block-diagonal structure, breaking the large matrix into smaller independent blocks. While this reduced the parameter count, it introduced rigid inductive biases. Essentially, it forced unrelated parts of the data to remain isolated from one another during the transformation, limiting the model's ability to capture complex interactions across the entire feature space.

### Viewing Matrix Math as Data Flow

The breakthrough came from changing how we visualize matrix factorization. Instead of seeing a matrix simply as a grid of numbers, the authors propose an "information transmission" framework. In this view, constructing a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying several sparse matrices is equivalent to transmitting information across a grid-structured graph.

Imagine a network where nodes represent dimensions of your data and edges represent connections allowing information to flow between them. A dense matrix implies that every input node can eventually influence every output node after passing through several layers of transformations. The challenge is to design a graph topology that ensures full connectivity (so no information is lost) while minimizing the total number of edges (to keep parameter counts low). Traditional block-diagonal approaches fail here because they create disconnected islands; information cannot travel between different blocks regardless of how many layers you stack.

### Borrowing from Signal Processing: The Butterfly Structure

To solve this connectivity puzzle efficiently, researchers looked back at computer science history and found inspiration in the Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm. The FFT relies on a specific topology known as the "butterfly structure," which allows for extremely efficient information exchange between nodes using far fewer connections than a fully connected mesh.

By applying this butterfly topology to orthogonal matrices, BOFT constructs a dense transformation using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters instead of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. This is achieved by decomposing the target orthogonal matrix into a product of sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in butterfly stages. Each stage performs localized rotations and swaps, similar to how FFT processes signals locally before combining results globally. Crucially, unlike block-diagonal methods, this structure guarantees that information from any input dimension can propagate to any output dimension through successive layers, restoring full expressivity without exploding parameter counts.

### BOFT: A General Framework for Efficient Adaptation

The resulting method, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), serves as a generalized framework that subsumes traditional OFT as a special case. When configured with specific hyperparameters, BOFT can mimic block-diagonal OFT or even approximate arbitrary dense orthogonal matrices depending on the depth ($m$) and block size ($b$) chosen. This flexibility allows practitioners to tune the trade-off between regularization and expressivity precisely for their specific task.

Beyond just saving memory and compute resources during training, BOFT offers unique advantages in model behavior:
*   **Training Stability:** Like OFT, BOFT multiplies weights by an orthogonal matrix rather than adding noise-like perturbations (as LoRA does). This preserves the spectral norm of the original weights, leading to more stable gradients and preventing catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning.
*   **Implicit Inductive Bias:** The butterfly structure implicitly encodes patterns found in classic linear transforms like Discrete Cosine Transforms and Hadamard transforms. This acts as a beneficial prior that helps models generalize better to unseen data compared to unstructured low-rank approximations or rigid block-diagonal constraints.
*   **Zero Inference Overhead:** Once trained, the learned butterfly components can be multiplied together once and fused into the base model weights. This means there is absolutely no additional latency during inference; the adapted model runs exactly as fast as the original pre-trained version.

### Conclusion

The journey from signal processing algorithms to cutting-edge AI fine-tuning illustrates how cross-disciplinary thinking can yield powerful solutions. By interpreting matrix operations as information flow problems and adopting the elegant butterfly topology from FFT theory, BOFT resolves long-standing inefficiencies in orthogonal finetuning. Empirical studies show that BOFT outperforms state-of-the-art methods across diverse domains including natural language processing, computer vision, and text-to-image generation. As foundation models continue to grow larger in scale but harder to train efficiently looking backward at established algorithms might just be our best path forward for future innovation in machine learning efficiency



# Beyond LoRA: Preserving Neural Angles with Orthogonal Butterfly Finetuning

The rise of massive foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion has revolutionized artificial intelligence, but it has also created a significant bottleneck: training these models from scratch is prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods that adapt pre-trained giants to specific downstream tasks without retraining the entire network. While Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has become the industry standard for its simplicity and speed, recent research suggests it may not be the optimal solution for every scenario. A new technique called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) offers a compelling alternative by fundamentally changing how we modify neural weights, prioritizing the preservation of geometric relationships within the model.

At the heart of BOFT lies a philosophical shift from additive updates to multiplicative transformations. Traditional methods like LoRA update a pre-trained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by adding a low-rank matrix product, effectively shifting the weights in a specific direction. In contrast, Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT), the precursor to BOFT, multiplies the weight matrix by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. The critical advantage here is geometry: an orthogonal transformation preserves the pairwise angles between neurons. Since these angles encode semantic relationships learned during pre-training, maintaining them ensures that the foundational knowledge of the model remains intact while adapting to new tasks. This approach has been shown to yield superior training stability and generalization compared to additive methods, particularly in complex domains like text-to-image generation.

However, early implementations of OFT faced a practical hurdle: computational cost. To maintain orthogonality across high-dimensional spaces requires updating a dense $d \times d$ matrix, which involves $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters—a number that quickly becomes unmanageable for large models. Previous attempts solved this by using block-diagonal structures, breaking the matrix into smaller independent chunks. While this reduced parameter counts, it introduced rigid sparsity patterns that limited expressiveness and forced unrelated dimensions to be processed independently. The challenge was clear: how could we construct a dense orthogonal matrix that allows information to flow freely across all dimensions while remaining parameter-efficient?

The authors of BOFT solve this puzzle by reframing matrix construction as an information transmission problem. Imagine a grid where nodes represent dimensions and edges represent information flow; constructing a dense matrix is equivalent to ensuring every node in the input layer can transmit information to every node in the output layer through a series of intermediate steps. Instead of trying to create one giant dense connection map, BOFT composes the final orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse matrices. Drawing inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, they utilize "butterfly structures." These structures allow information from different nodes to be exchanged efficiently through recursive sparse operations. By chaining together $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries, BOFT constructs a fully dense orthogonal transformation using merely $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ trainable parameters.

This architectural innovation allows BOFT to subsume traditional OFT as a special case while offering significantly greater flexibility. Unlike block-diagonal approaches that arbitrarily split dimensions based on indices, the butterfly structure creates a smooth interpolation between identity matrices (preserving pre-training exactly) and full orthogonal matrices (allowing maximum adaptation). This provides an implicit inductive bias derived from classic linear transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform and Hadamard transform, which are known for their robustness and efficiency in signal processing. Furthermore, because BOFT relies on multiplicative updates rather than additive ones, standard dropout techniques do not apply directly; instead, the method employs "multiplicative dropout," which randomly replaces specific butterfly components with identity matrices during training to prevent overfitting without breaking orthogonality constraints.

Empirical evaluations demonstrate that BOFT excels across diverse modalities including large language models, vision transformers, and diffusion models for image generation. In scenarios where preserving spectral norms is crucial for stability—such as preventing gradient explosion or vanishing—theoretical guarantees show that multiplying by an orthogonal matrix leaves the singular values of the original weight matrix unchanged. This contrasts sharply with LoRA, which can alter spectral properties unpredictably depending on rank choices. Experiments indicate that BOFT often outperforms state-of-the-art methods by considerable margins in terms of generalization capability while maintaining competitive parameter efficiency. Although there is a slight increase in training runtime due to computing products of multiple matrices during backpropagation, this overhead disappears entirely at inference time once the learned components are fused into the base model weights.

Ultimately, BOFT challenges the notion that low-rank approximations are always sufficient for fine-tuning foundation models. By leveraging butterfly factorization to achieve dense orthogonal transformations with minimal parameters, it offers a principled way to respect the geometric integrity of pre-trained knowledge while enabling powerful adaptation. As researchers continue to push boundaries in multimodal AI and generative modeling, techniques like BOFT highlight that sometimes looking back at classical algorithms like FFT can provide fresh solutions for modern machine learning problems. Whether you are building specialized chatbots or customizing image generators, considering angle-preserving fine-tuning might just be the key to unlocking better performance and stability than you thought possible with standard adapters alone
