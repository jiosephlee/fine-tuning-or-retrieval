# From Blocky to Butterfly: How a Classic Algorithm is Revolutionizing AI Fine-Tuning

Large foundation models like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion have transformed the AI landscape, but their massive size makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. Consequently, the field has shifted toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), which adapts these pre-trained giants to specific downstream tasks without retraining the entire network. While methods like LoRA have gained popularity by using low-rank matrices, a newer approach called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) has shown superior generalization by preserving the geometric relationships between neurons. However, OFT traditionally suffers from a significant drawback: it requires a large number of trainable parameters because it relies on high-dimensional orthogonal matrices. A recent breakthrough addresses this bottleneck by borrowing a mathematical structure from the 1960s Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, leading to a novel method called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT).

The core challenge in making OFT efficient lies in the nature of orthogonal matrices. To preserve the pairwise angles between neurons—a key factor in retaining pre-training knowledge—OFT multiplies the pre-trained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. A dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is too many for efficient adaptation. Previous attempts to reduce this count used a block-diagonal structure, effectively splitting the matrix into smaller independent blocks. While this reduced parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ where $b$ is the block size, it introduced rigid inductive biases. This "blocky" approach assumes that dimensions of a neuron can be arbitrarily grouped and transformed independently, which limits expressiveness and prevents the model from approximating classic linear transforms effectively.

To solve this, researchers reframed the problem of constructing an efficient dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. Imagine a grid where information must flow from input nodes to output nodes through a series of layers. A dense matrix represents full connectivity, but achieving this with sparse matrices requires a specific topology that ensures every input node can eventually reach every output node with minimal connections. The solution lies in "butterfly structures," originally developed to enable fast Fourier transforms by efficiently exchanging information between nodes in a logarithmic number of steps. By applying this topology to orthogonal matrices, BOFT constructs a dense transformation using a product of sparse orthogonal matrices. Instead of requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, BOFT achieves dense connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters when using the standard butterfly configuration.

The mechanics of BOFT involve factorizing the target orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of $m$ sparse butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}_i$. Each component is itself composed of small orthogonal blocks (often $2 \times 2$ rotations) arranged in a specific pattern that allows information to mix globally across the layer while maintaining local sparsity. This structure subsumes traditional OFT as a special case; if one sets the number of layers $m=1$, BOFT reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT. However, by increasing $m$, BOFT creates a smoother interpolation between the identity matrix (no change) and full orthogonal transformations, offering a much richer hypothesis class than its block-diagonal predecessor. This flexibility allows BOFT to approximate complex linear transforms that block-diagonal structures cannot recover, providing an implicit inductive bias that benefits generalization and prevents overfitting.

Beyond parameter efficiency, BOFT retains the critical stability properties of orthogonal finetuning. Because the transformation is strictly orthogonal, it preserves the spectral norm of the pre-trained weights, ensuring that the model does not deviate too drastically from its original knowledge base during fine-tuning. The authors also introduce specific techniques like multiplicative dropout tailored for this architecture to further prevent overfitting without breaking the orthogonality constraint. Empirical studies demonstrate that BOFT outperforms state-of-the-art methods across diverse domains, including large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models. By leveraging a classic algorithmic insight from computer science history, BOFT successfully bridges the gap between parameter efficiency and expressive power, offering a robust new paradigm for adapting foundation models in resource-constrained environments.



# The Information Highway: Visualizing How AI Models Learn Without Forgetting

Large foundation models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion have revolutionized artificial intelligence, but their massive size makes training them from scratch prohibitively expensive. This has shifted the focus of the research community toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), a strategy that adapts these powerful models to specific downstream tasks without retraining the entire network. While methods like LoRA have gained popularity by using low-rank matrices, a newer approach called Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) takes a different path. OFT preserves the geometric relationships between neurons by applying orthogonal transformations, ensuring that pre-trained knowledge is not lost. However, the original OFT method struggled with parameter efficiency because dense orthogonal matrices require a quadratic number of parameters relative to their dimension. A new paper titled "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization" introduces a breakthrough solution called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), which reimagines weight updates as an information transmission problem to solve this efficiency bottleneck.

The core innovation in this research is the "information transmission" framework, which provides a fresh perspective on how to construct dense orthogonal matrices efficiently. Traditionally, creating a dense $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters, which is too costly for large models. The authors propose viewing the generation of such a matrix not just as a linear algebra problem, but as a data flow problem on a grid-structured graph. In this view, multiplying sparse matrices together is analogous to transmitting information across nodes in a network. If we want to generate a dense matrix where every input node can influence every output node, we need to design a network topology that allows information from the first layer of nodes to reach every node in the final layer using the fewest possible connections (edges). This graphical interpretation transforms the challenge of parameter efficiency into an optimization problem of finding the most efficient network topology for information exchange.

To solve this topology problem, the researchers drew inspiration from computer science history, specifically the Cooley-Tukey algorithm used for Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT). The FFT relies on a "butterfly structure," a specific pattern of connections that allows data from different parts of an array to be mixed efficiently with logarithmic complexity rather than quadratic complexity. By applying this butterfly structure to orthogonal matrices, the authors created BOFT. Instead of learning one massive dense matrix, BOFT learns a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices arranged in a butterfly pattern. This structure allows the model to generate a fully dense orthogonal transformation using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. This is a significant reduction compared to the original OFT's block-diagonal approach, which saves parameters but introduces rigid sparsity patterns that limit expressiveness and cannot approximate classic linear transforms like the Fourier transform.

The mathematical elegance of BOFT lies in how it balances expressivity and regularity through this factorization. In standard fine-tuning, there is often a trade-off between learning new task-specific features and preserving old knowledge. BOFT addresses this by initializing its butterfly components as identity matrices and then learning small deviations through skew-symmetric matrices via Cayley parameterization. This ensures that at initialization, the model behaves exactly like the pre-trained version ($\bm{R} = \bm{I}$), and as training progresses, it smoothly interpolates toward new transformations while maintaining orthogonality at every step. Because each component in the butterfly chain is orthogonal, their product remains orthogonal, guaranteeing that pairwise angles between neurons are preserved throughout training. This preservation of spectral properties leads to greater training stability and better generalization compared to methods that add low-rank noise or update weights additively without geometric constraints.

Beyond theory, BOFT has demonstrated impressive empirical results across diverse domains including large language models, vision transformers, and text-to-image diffusion models. By subsuming original OFT as a special case (where the number of butterfly stages $m=1$), BOFT offers a generalized framework that can be tuned for different levels of expressiveness and parameter budgets. The method also introduces novel techniques like multiplicative dropout tailored for its specific architecture to prevent overfitting during training. While there is a slight increase in training runtime due to the multiplication of multiple sparse matrices compared to simpler methods, this overhead disappears during inference because the learned components can be merged into the original weight matrix before deployment. Ultimately, BOFT proves that by treating neural network fine-tuning as an information highway problem, we can design architectures that are both highly expressive and incredibly parameter-efficient, allowing AI models to learn new tasks without forgetting what they already know.



# BOFT vs. LoRA: The New Contender for Efficient Large Model Adaptation

The landscape of large foundation models has shifted dramatically in recent years. With models like GPT-3 and Stable Diffusion boasting hundreds of billions of parameters, training them from scratch has become prohibitively expensive for most researchers and organizations. Consequently, the focus has moved toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods that can adapt these massive models to downstream tasks without retraining every weight. For a long time, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has been the de facto standard in this space. However, a new contender has emerged that challenges the low-rank paradigm: Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). While LoRA updates weights by adding low-rank matrices, BOFT takes a fundamentally different geometric approach by transforming neurons using orthogonal matrices, promising better stability and generalization with significantly improved parameter efficiency compared to previous orthogonal methods.

To understand why BOFT is gaining traction, we must first look at the limitations of its predecessors. The original Orthogonal Fine-tuning (OFT) method was designed to preserve the pairwise angles between neurons in a layer, ensuring that the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining remains intact. This is achieved by multiplying the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, such that the forward pass becomes $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. Because orthogonal transformations preserve norms and angles, they offer superior training stability and generalization compared to additive methods like LoRA. However, the original OFT suffered from a critical flaw: parameter inefficiency. A dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often too large for practical fine-tuning. To mitigate this, OFT previously relied on block-diagonal structures, but this introduced rigid sparsity patterns that limited expressiveness and created undesirable inductive biases.

BOFT solves this dilemma by reimagining how we construct dense orthogonal matrices. Instead of treating the matrix as a monolithic block or a collection of disjoint blocks, BOFT views the generation of an orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. Drawing inspiration from the butterfly structures found in the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm, BOFT factorizes a dense orthogonal matrix into a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. Specifically, it constructs a $d$-dimensional dense matrix using $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. This results in a total parameter count of $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$, which is a massive reduction from the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ requirement of full orthogonal matrices and significantly more efficient than the block-diagonal approaches used in earlier OFT implementations.

The theoretical advantages of this approach are compelling when compared directly to LoRA. LoRA operates by adding a low-rank update to the weights ($\bm{W} = \bm{W}^0 + \Delta \bm{W}$), which can alter the spectral properties of the original weight matrix and potentially destabilize training if not carefully regularized. In contrast, BOFT multiplies the weights by an orthogonal matrix ($\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$). Since orthogonal matrices preserve singular values, BOFT maintains the spectral norm of the pretrained weights exactly. This property ensures that the magnitude of activations does not explode or vanish during fine-tuning, leading to more stable convergence. Furthermore, while LoRA relies on low-rank structures to save parameters, BOFT leverages sparse structures within an information transmission framework to achieve density without sacrificing efficiency. This allows BOFT to approximate classic linear transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform and Hadamard transform naturally, introducing an implicit inductive bias that benefits generalization across diverse tasks ranging from computer vision to natural language processing.

Empirically, BOFT has demonstrated its versatility across various foundation model architectures. The authors applied BOFT to large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models with impressive results. In many benchmarks, BOFT outperformed state-of-the-art methods including LoRA and BitFit by a considerable margin while maintaining or reducing the number of trainable parameters compared to previous orthogonal methods. A key feature of BOFT is its ability to subsume OFT as a special case; by adjusting hyperparameters such as the number of butterfly layers $m$ and block size $b$, one can interpolate between simple block-diagonal transformations and fully dense orthogonal transformations. This flexibility allows practitioners to find an optimal trade-off between expressivity and regularity for their specific downstream task without being locked into a fixed sparsity pattern.

Despite its advantages, it is worth noting that BOFT does come with some trade-offs compared to LoRA's simplicity. Because BOFT represents the orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse matrices rather than a single dense update or additive term, there is a slight increase in training runtime overhead due to the repeated matrix multiplications required during each iteration. However, this cost is purely confined to the training phase; once fine-tuning is complete, all learned butterfly components can be multiplied together into a single dense matrix and fused with the pretrained weights. This means there is zero additional inference latency compared to standard fine-tuning or LoRA-deployed models. As research continues into more efficient network topologies for information transmission within this framework, it is likely that even these minor training costs will be further optimized.

In conclusion, while LoRA has dominated parameter-efficient fine-tuning for some time due to its simplicity and effectiveness on language tasks, Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) presents a robust alternative grounded in geometric principles and efficient signal processing theory. By replacing low-rank additive updates with structured orthogonal multiplicative updates via butterfly factorization, BOFT offers a path toward more stable training and better generalization without sacrificing parameter efficiency. As foundation models continue to grow in scale and complexity, methods like BOFT that respect the intrinsic geometry of pretrained weights may become increasingly essential for unlocking their full potential across diverse downstream applications



# One Method to Rule Them All: Fine-Tuning Text, Images, and Diffusion Models with BOFT

The era of foundation models has brought us powerful systems like ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion, yet training these massive architectures from scratch remains prohibitively expensive. The field has consequently shifted toward parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods that adapt pre-trained models to downstream tasks without retraining the entire network. While methods like LoRA have gained popularity for their simplicity, they often rely on low-rank approximations that may limit expressivity. A newer approach, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), preserves the geometric structure of the pre-trained model by using orthogonal matrices to transform neurons, ensuring pairwise angles are maintained. However, standard OFT struggles with parameter efficiency because dense orthogonal matrices require a quadratic number of parameters relative to the dimension size. To solve this, researchers have introduced Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT), a novel method that combines the stability of orthogonal transformations with the parameter efficiency of butterfly structures.

## The Challenge of Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning

The core principle behind OFT is to update a pre-trained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ by multiplying it with a learnable orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, resulting in a new weight matrix $\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. This multiplicative update preserves the spectral norm of the weights and maintains the relational information between neurons, which is crucial for retaining pre-training knowledge. While effective, a full $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is too large for efficient fine-tuning.

Previous attempts to reduce this parameter count involved using a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$. While this reduces parameters to $\mathcal{O}(bd)$ where $b$ is the block size, it introduces significant inductive biases. Specifically, block-diagonal matrices cannot approximate classic linear transforms like the Fourier transform and force dimensions within a neuron to be transformed independently based on arbitrary index groupings. This creates a dilemma: how can we construct a dense orthogonal matrix that is expressive enough to capture complex task adaptations while remaining parameter-efficient?

## An Information Transmission Perspective

The authors of BOFT propose a novel framework to address this dilemma by viewing matrix factorization as an information transmission problem. Instead of treating the orthogonal matrix as a static entity, they interpret its construction as transmitting information across a grid-structured graph. In this view, generating a dense matrix $\bm{R}$ via the product of sparse matrices $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \cdots \bm{B}_1$ is equivalent to ensuring that every node in an input layer can transmit information to every node in an output layer through a series of sparse connections.

This perspective reveals two key desiderata for an efficient design: dense connectivity (every input node must reach every output node) and minimum free edges (minimizing trainable parameters while maintaining orthogonality). A naive dense connection requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges, but by carefully designing the sparsity pattern of intermediate matrices, one can achieve dense connectivity with far fewer parameters. This insight leads directly to the adoption of butterfly structures, originally popularized by the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for fast Fourier transforms.

## The Power of Butterfly Factorization

BOFT leverages butterfly factorization to construct dense orthogonal matrices using only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters. A butterfly matrix is defined recursively as a product of sparse block-diagonal matrices, where each block allows for local mixing of information before passing it to the next stage. By ensuring each component in this product is orthogonal—often achieved via Cayley parameterization or 2D rotations—the final product remains strictly orthogonal while being highly parameter-efficient.

Mathematically, BOFT defines the transformation as:
$$ \bm{z} = (\bm{R}(m,b) \cdot \bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x} $$
where $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ butterfly components with block size $b$. When $m=1$, BOFT reduces to standard block-diagonal OFT. However, by increasing $m$ and keeping $b$ small (e.g., $b=2$), BOFT constructs a fully dense orthogonal matrix that can approximate any classic linear transform, something block-diagonal OFT cannot do. This structure provides a smoother interpolation between identity matrices (regularity) and full orthogonal matrices (expressivity), effectively expanding the hypothesis class available for fine-tuning without exploding the parameter count.

To prevent overfitting in this multiplicative setting, BOFT also introduces "Multiplicative Dropout," which randomly replaces specific butterfly components or diagonal blocks with identity matrices during training, analogous to how dropout works in additive methods like LoRA but adapted for multiplicative updates.

## Universal Performance Across Domains

The true strength of BOFT lies in its versatility as a generic fine-tuning method applicable across diverse modalities. Extensive empirical studies demonstrate that BOFT outperforms state-of-the-art methods in three major domains: Large Language Models (LLMs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion.

In natural language processing tasks involving LLMs such as LLaMA or OPT, BOFT consistently achieves higher accuracy than LoRA and standard OFT while using fewer or comparable parameters. The structured inductive bias provided by the butterfly network appears to help these models generalize better on downstream benchmarks without overfitting to small datasets. Similarly, in computer vision tasks using ViTs for image classification and segmentation, BOFT shows superior convergence stability and final performance compared to adapter-based methods and low-rank approaches.

Perhaps most notably, BOFT extends the utility of orthogonal fine-tuning beyond its original application in controllable text-to-image generation. In diffusion model adaptation tasks such as DreamBooth or style transfer, BOFT maintains high fidelity and stability where other methods might struggle with mode collapse or training instability. The preservation of spectral properties ensures that the underlying generative capabilities of the diffusion model are not disrupted during adaptation.

## Balancing Expressivity and Regularity

Why does BOFT work so well across such different architectures? The answer lies in its ability to balance expressivity and regularity more effectively than previous methods. While full fine-tuning offers universal expressivity but poor generalization due to overfitting, and low-rank methods offer efficiency but limited capacity for complex transformations, BOFT occupies a sweet spot in between. By constraining the search space to structured subsets of the orthogonal group derived from butterfly networks, it introduces an implicit inductive bias that favors solutions aligned with classic linear transforms known for their generalizability properties (e.g., Fourier transforms).

Furthermore, theoretical analysis suggests that BOFT is provably more expressive than block-diagonal OFT with the same number of parameters because it can recover dense connectivity patterns that block structures inherently miss. This allows BOFT to find optimal solutions within a smaller hypothesis class that still retains enough flexibility to adapt to diverse downstream tasks efficiently.

## Conclusion

Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) represents a significant step forward in parameter-efficient fine-tuning by unifying geometric stability with computational efficiency through butterfly factorization. By reframing matrix construction as an information transmission problem on sparse graphs, BOFT achieves dense orthogonal transformations with only logarithmic parameter overhead relative to dimension size. Its success across language models, vision transformers, and diffusion models suggests that structured sparsity inspired by signal processing algorithms may be key to unlocking more robust adaptation strategies for future foundation models. As research continues into network topologies for information transmission, we can expect even more efficient variants of this approach to emerge further solidifying its role as a universal tool for model adaptation
