\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Clarification on Cayley Parameterization vs. Block-Butterfly Structure constraints
Question:
The paper mentions using Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$) to ensure orthogonality for standard OFT.

However, in Section 4, when defining BOFT, they describe constructing orthogonal matrices by combining butterfly factors. They state: 'we can easily guarantee... to be orthogonal with Cayley transform... to parameterize each block'.

My confusion lies here: The butterfly structure imposes a strict sparsity pattern (zeros at specific locations). The Cayley transform maps a skew-symmetric matrix to an orthogonal one. But a general skew-symmetric matrix filled according to the butterfly sparsity pattern might not yield an orthogonal matrix that respects that *same* sparsity pattern after inversion/multiplication.

Are they applying Cayley parameterization only locally within small independent blocks (like $2b \times 2b$), or is there a global constraint on $\bm{Q}$ required to maintain both orthogonality AND the butterfly sparsity simultaneously? If it's just local blocks, how does the recursive definition in Eq (2) ensure the final product is globally orthogonal?
Answer:
Your confusion stems from a common misconception regarding the interaction between matrix operations and sparsity patterns: **sparsity is not preserved under matrix multiplication.** You are correct that if you took a sparse skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ with a butterfly-like pattern and computed $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, the resulting orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ would almost certainly be dense, destroying the intended butterfly structure.

However, the authors of the paper do **not** apply the Cayley transform to a single global sparse matrix to produce the final orthogonal matrix. Instead, they apply the parameterization **locally** to the constituent building blocks of the butterfly structure. As described in Section 4, the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed as a product of multiple "butterfly components," denoted as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. Crucially, each of these components is itself a block-diagonal matrix containing small independent blocks (specifically $2b \times 2b$ blocks derived from the butterfly factor).

The paper explicitly states: "we can easily guarantee... to be orthogonal with Cayley transform... to parameterize each block." This means the Cayley parameterization is applied individually to each small $2b \times 2b$ sub-block within these diagonal structures. Since these sub-blocks are disjoint (separated by zeros in the block-diagonal arrangement), computing their inverses and products does not mix information across different parts of the matrix in a way that fills in zeros outside their local scope. Consequently, each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ remains sparse with its specific butterfly-induced sparsity pattern while being strictly orthogonal.

The global orthogonality of the final matrix $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed not by preserving sparsity through a single complex inversion, but by the algebraic property that **the product of orthogonal matrices is also an orthogonal matrix**. Since every factor in the sequence $\bm{R} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m)} \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$ is constructed to be locally orthogonal via Cayley parameterization on its small blocks, their product forms a valid dense orthogonal matrix. The "magic" of BOFT is that while each factor is sparse and computationally cheap to evaluate (due to local independence), their recursive multiplication creates a dense connectivity graph capable of approximating any orthogonal transformation, all while maintaining exactly $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ trainable parameters.
