\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Mechanism of Multiplicative Dropout: Replacing blocks with Identity vs. Zeroing weights
Question:
Section 4 introduces 'Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT'. Standard dropout adds noise or zeroes out activations/weights additively. Here, because BOFT uses multiplicative updates ($\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$), standard dropout doesn't apply directly.

The paper says: 'replaces these blocks as identity matrices.'

Conceptually, replacing a learned orthogonal transformation block with an Identity matrix seems like forcing those specific neurons to remain exactly as they were during pretraining (no change).

Is this equivalent to setting the corresponding parameters in that block's skew-symmetric generator $\bm{Q}$ to zero? If so, why is this preferred over just scaling down the magnitude of the transformation? Also, since orthogonality must be maintained during training steps where dropout is active, does replacing a sub-block with Identity break the global orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ temporarily during forward passes?
Answer:
The mechanism described in Section 4 relies on the fact that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in BOFT is constructed as a product of multiple sparse butterfly components, $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Unlike standard dropout which zero-additively removes features, multiplicative dropout in this context targets the structural building blocks of $\bm{R}$. When the paper states that selected blocks are replaced by identity matrices, it refers to substituting specific $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal sub-matrices within the factorization chain with $\bm{I}_{2b}$. Conceptually, this forces those specific stages of the transformation to act as "pass-through" layers, meaning the input to that stage exits unchanged relative to that step.

This operation is **not** strictly equivalent to simply setting the corresponding parameters in the skew-symmetric generator $\bm{Q}$ to zero, although they share a similar effect of reducing transformation magnitude locally. In Cayley parameterization, $\bm{R}_{block} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, setting $\bm{Q} = \mathbf{0}$ indeed yields $\bm{I}$. However, the distinction lies in how this interacts with the global structure during training. Standard dropout scales remaining activations or weights to maintain expected values; here, replacing a block with Identity is a hard structural regularization that explicitly enforces a subset of the transformation path to revert to the pretrained state (since BOFT initializes all blocks as Identity). This is preferred over merely scaling down magnitudes because it leverages the "information transmission" framework central to the paper: by dropping entire blocks, you are effectively pruning paths in the underlying graph representation of the matrix factorization. This creates a dynamic sparsity pattern where information flow is blocked entirely for certain dimensions at certain depths, providing a stronger regularizer against overfitting than soft scaling would.

Regarding your concern about global orthogonality: replacing a sub-block with an Identity matrix **does not break** the global orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ during forward passes. The orthogonality of the composite matrix $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed algebraically because it is defined as a product of individual orthogonal matrices. Since the Identity matrix is itself an orthogonal matrix ($\bm{I}^\top\bm{I} = \bm{I}$), swapping any component $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ for $\bm{I}$ results in a new product where every term remains orthogonal. Consequently, their product remains orthogonal regardless of which specific blocks are dropped in any given forward pass. This allows BOFT to maintain its core theoretical advantage—the preservation of pairwise angles and spectral norms—while simultaneously applying stochastic regularization through structural simplification.
