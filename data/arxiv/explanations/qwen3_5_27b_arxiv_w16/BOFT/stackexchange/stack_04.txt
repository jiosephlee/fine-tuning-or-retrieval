\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why is standard Dropout incompatible with BOFT's multiplicative update rule?
Question:
In Section 4, under 'Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT', the authors state: 'The conventional Dropout naturally works for LoRA, but not for BOFT due to our multiplicative weight update.'

I understand that LoRA adds a low-rank update ($\bm{W} + \Delta\bm{W}$), so dropping out elements in $\Delta\bm{W}$ simply reduces the magnitude of specific updates. However, BOFT applies an orthogonal transformation via multiplication ($\bm{R}\bm{W}$).

Why exactly does standard dropout fail here? If I apply standard dropout by zeroing out random elements within one of the sparse butterfly factors before multiplication, wouldn't that just create a sparser transformation? Is there a theoretical reason why zeroing out entries breaks the orthogonality constraint required for BOFT, or is it simply that it destroys the specific 'butterfly' structure needed for efficient information transmission?
Answer:
The incompatibility of standard Dropout with BOFT stems from the fundamental difference between additive and multiplicative weight updates, specifically regarding how zeroing out elements affects the **orthogonality constraint** that defines the method. In LoRA, the update is additive ($\bm{W} + \Delta\bm{W}$), so applying standard Dropout (zeroing random elements in $\Delta\bm{W}$) simply reduces the magnitude of specific updates without altering the structural properties of the base weight matrix $\bm{W}$. However, BOFT relies on a multiplicative update where the pretrained weights are transformed by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ ($\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$). The core theoretical guarantee of Orthogonal Finetuning is that $\bm{R}$ must satisfy $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ to preserve pairwise angles between neurons and maintain spectral stability.

If you apply standard element-wise Dropout to the sparse butterfly factors that compose $\bm{R}$, you are effectively setting specific entries within these factors to zero. While this creates a sparser transformation as you hypothesized, it immediately breaks the orthogonality of those factors. Since $\bm{R}$ is constructed as a product of these orthogonal butterfly components ($\bm{R} = \prod \tilde{\bm{B}}$), if any individual component $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ loses its orthogonality due to random zeroing, the final product $\bm{R}$ will no longer be an orthogonal matrix. This violates the primary inductive bias of BOFT, potentially causing the model to lose its spectral norm preservation and destabilizing training. The "butterfly" structure is not just a sparsity pattern for efficiency; it is a carefully constrained topology where non-zero entries are mathematically coupled to ensure orthogonality (often via Cayley parameterization or rotation matrices). Randomly breaking these couplings destroys the geometric properties required for the method to function.

To resolve this while still providing regularization, the authors propose **Multiplicative Dropout**, which operates at a structural level rather than an element-wise level. Instead of zeroing individual scalars within a factor, this method randomly selects entire butterfly components or diagonal blocks within them and replaces them with **identity matrices**. Because an identity matrix is orthogonal ($\bm{I}^\top\bm{I} = \bm{I}$), replacing a learned orthogonal block with an identity block preserves the orthogonality of the entire product chain. This allows BOFT to "drop out" learned transformations (reverting specific parts of the network to their pre-finetuned state) without violating the mathematical constraints that make Orthogonal Finetuning effective.
