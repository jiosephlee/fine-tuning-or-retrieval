\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the 'Information Transmission' analogy mathematically enforce the requirement for a dense resulting matrix?
Question:
In Section 3, the authors reframe the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as an information transmission problem on a grid graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. They state that for $\bm{R}$ to be dense, 'every node in the first level should be able to transmit information to all the nodes in the $(m+1)$-th level.'

I am struggling to connect this topological requirement back to linear algebra. Specifically:
1. Does the existence of *at least one* path between node $j$ (level 1) and node $i$ (level $m+1$) strictly guarantee that the corresponding entry $R_{ij} \neq 0$?
2. If intermediate matrices $\bm{B}_k$ are orthogonal (and thus invertible), doesn't this imply that information is never truly 'lost'? Why isn't any sequence of full-rank matrices sufficient to create a dense matrix without needing this specific graph connectivity check?

I feel like I'm missing a nuance regarding how zero entries in sparse matrices propagate through multiplication versus how paths exist in the graph.
Answer:
The "information transmission" analogy enforces density by mapping the algebraic operation of matrix multiplication to the topological concept of reachability in a directed acyclic graph. To answer your first question: **Yes**, under the standard assumptions of real-valued arithmetic used in this framework, the existence of *at least one* valid path from node $j$ (input level) to node $i$ (output level) guarantees that the resulting entry $R_{ij}$ is non-zero, provided the edge weights along that path are non-zero. Mathematically, the entry $R_{ij}$ is computed as the sum over all possible paths $\mathcal{P}_{ji}$ connecting these nodes:
$$ R_{ij} = \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ji}} \prod_{e \in p} w_e $$
where $w_e$ represents the weight of an edge corresponding to a non-zero entry in one of the sparse factor matrices $\bm{B}_k$. In the context of orthogonal matrices constructed via Cayley parameterization or rotations, the learned parameters are continuous variables. Consequently, unless they are explicitly constrained to be exactly zero (which defines the sparsity pattern/edges), they will almost surely be non-zero. Therefore, if a path exists, there is a term in this summation that is non-zero, making $R_{ij} \neq 0$. Conversely, if no path exists ($\mathcal{P}_{ji} = \emptyset$), the sum is empty and equals zero, enforcing sparsity.

Regarding your second question about why full-rank intermediate matrices aren't sufficient to guarantee density without explicit connectivity checks: while it is true that orthogonal matrices are invertible and thus preserve information globally (they do not collapse dimensions), **invertibility does not imply denseness**. A product of full-rank sparse matrices can remain sparse if their sparsity patterns align in a way that restricts information flow to specific subsets of nodes. For instance, consider two block-diagonal orthogonal matrices; their product is also block-diagonal and full-rank, yet it contains massive amounts of zeros because information from node $j$ in one block can never reach node $i$ in a different block. The "graph connectivity" requirement specifically ensures **global mixing**: it forces every input dimension to interact with every output dimension through at least one chain of transformations. Without checking for this specific path existence across all pairs $(i, j)$, you might inadvertently construct a matrix that is full-rank but structurally sparse (like a permuted block-diagonal matrix), failing to capture the dense interactions required for maximal expressivity. The graph formulation allows the authors to visualize and verify that their butterfly factorization creates a connected topology where information propagates fully across all dimensions before reaching the final layer, distinguishing it from merely stacking independent or disjoint transformations.
