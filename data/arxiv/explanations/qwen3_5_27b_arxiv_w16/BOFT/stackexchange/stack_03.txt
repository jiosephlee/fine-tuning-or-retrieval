\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Why does BOFT claim higher expressivity than block-diagonal OFT despite both being subsets of O(d)?
Question:
Theorem 1 states: 'BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.'

Intuitively, both methods restrict the search space from the full Orthogonal Group $O(d)$ down to a subset defined by their respective structures:
- OFT uses a block-diagonal structure: $O(b)^r$ where $br=d$.
- BOFT uses a product of sparse butterfly matrices.

If we fix the number of parameters (which roughly equates to fixing degrees of freedom), why is the manifold covered by BOFT considered 'larger' or 'more expressive' than that of OFT? Is it simply because OFT enforces disjoint transformations on subspaces (dimensions don't mix across blocks), whereas BOFT allows mixing across all dimensions through depth ($m > 1$)?

Could someone clarify if this theorem implies that for any specific block-diagonal matrix achievable by OFT, there exists an equivalent configuration in BOFT, but not vice versa? Or is it about covering volume in parameter space?
Answer:
The claim that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT stems from the fact that BOFT strictly **subsumes** the block-diagonal structure as a special case while allowing for dense interactions across all dimensions through matrix composition.

As explicitly stated in the paper, when the depth parameter $m=1$, BOFT reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. In this scenario, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ consists of independent blocks $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, meaning dimensions within one block never interact with dimensions in another. However, when $m > 1$, BOFT constructs the final orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Even though each individual component $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ is sparse and locally constrained, their sequential multiplication creates a **dense** connectivity graph. This allows information to propagate from any input dimension to any output dimension across the layers of the factorization, effectively breaking the rigid subspace isolation enforced by standard OFT.

Therefore, your intuition regarding "mixing across all dimensions" is correct and aligns with Theorem 1 in the paper. The theorem implies a strict inclusion relationship: for any specific block-diagonal matrix achievable by OFT (with $m=1$), there exists an equivalent configuration in BOFT (specifically when setting $m=1$). Conversely, there exist dense orthogonal matrices generated by BOFT (with $m > 1$) that cannot be represented by any block-diagonal OFT configuration, regardless of how many parameters are allocated to the blocks. The "larger manifold" refers to this superset capability; BOFT explores a continuous path from isolated blocks ($m=1$) toward full dense orthogonality as $m$ increases, whereas OFT is stuck at the disconnected endpoint. The paper further notes that with sufficient depth ($l=d-1$ layers of specific butterfly compositions), one can theoretically approximate the entire orthogonal group $O(d)$, confirming that BOFT covers a significantly richer hypothesis space than its block-diagonal counterpart.
