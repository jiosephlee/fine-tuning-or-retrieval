\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Computational Complexity vs. Parameter Count: The hidden cost of Matrix Factorization
Question:
The abstract highlights that BOFT reduces trainable parameters from $O(d^2)$ (full) or high counts in OFT down to $O(d \log d)$.

However, Section 6 notes: 'Since the final orthogonal matrix... is the product of multiple orthogonal matrix, the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT.'

If we assume standard Matrix-Matrix multiplication costs dominate:
To compute $(\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_i) \cdot W^0$, do we compute each multiplication sequentially ($((B_m B_{m-1}) ... ) W^0$) which would be costly if done naively?
The paper claims no inference latency because weights are fused post-training. But during training gradients must flow back through all these layers.
Does maintaining orthogonality constraints via Cayley parameterization inside each sparse layer add significant Jacobian computation overhead compared to just updating dense weights directly? How does this compare computationally to LoRA's low-rank update which involves two small dense multiplications per layer?
Answer:
The computational trade-off you identified is central to the BOFT methodology: it explicitly exchanges increased training-time computation for a drastic reduction in parameter count. While LOFT achieves $O(d \log d)$ parameters by factorizing the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ into a product of $m = O(\log d)$ sparse butterfly components, computing the forward pass during training indeed requires evaluating the chain $\mathbf{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_i$ before applying it to the frozen weights $\mathbf{W}^0$.

Regarding your concern about sequential multiplication costs, the paper acknowledges this overhead in Section 6 ("Concluding Remarks"), stating that "since the final orthogonal matrix... is the product of multiple orthogonal matrix, the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT." However, this cost is mitigated by two key factors derived from the butterfly structure. First, unlike a naive dense matrix multiplication which scales as $O(d^2)$ or worse depending on implementation, each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_i$ is highly sparse (specifically block-diagonal with small blocks), meaning each step in the chain operates in $O(d)$ time rather than $O(d^2)$. Consequently, computing the full product $\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_i$ scales as $O(md) = O(d \log d)$, which is asymptotically cheaper than forming a single dense orthogonal matrix ($O(d^2)$) but more expensive than a single block-diagonal operation used in standard OFT. Second, during training, modern frameworks optimize this sequence; while gradients must flow back through all $m$ layers via the chain rule, the operations remain lightweight due to sparsity.

Your question regarding Jacobian overhead from Cayley parameterization touches on how orthogonality is enforced. BOFT utilizes Cayley parameterization ($\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$) locally within each small butterfly block (typically $2b \times 2b$) rather than globally on a massive dense matrix. This avoids computing inverses or Jacobians for huge matrices; instead, it computes them for tiny blocks where $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. The computational burden here is negligible compared to the matrix multiplications themselves. In contrast to LoRA, which adds two small dense matrices ($A$ and $B$) resulting in an additive update $\Delta W = BA$, BOFT performs a multiplicative update involving a deep stack of sparse rotations. While LoRA's forward pass involves two cheap dense GEMMs per layer, BOFT involves a sequence of sparse GEMMs. The paper argues that despite this slight increase in FLOPs per iteration compared to OFT (and potentially LoRA depending on rank settings), the memory savings from storing only $O(d \log d)$ parameters allow for larger batch sizes or models that might otherwise not fit in memory, and crucially, **there is no inference latency** because these learned components are fused into a single weight matrix post-training.

Ultimately, BOFT accepts a modest increase in training complexity—stemming from traversing the depth-$m$ butterfly graph and propagating gradients through it—to unlock a denser representation space than block-diagonal OFT while maintaining far fewer parameters than full orthogonal finetuning. The "hidden cost" is real but bounded by logarithmic growth relative to dimension $d$, whereas parameter storage grows linearly-logarithmically rather than quadratically.
