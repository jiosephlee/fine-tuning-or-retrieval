\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Connection between Butterfly Factorization and Cayley Parameterization
Question:
The paper uses Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$) to ensure orthogonality. It also mentions that butterfly components can be parameterized as orthogonal matrices because their non-zero patterns are permutations of smaller blocks.

How exactly is Cayley parameterization applied recursively or locally within a single butterfly factor? For example, if a butterfly factor consists of diagonal blocks containing vectors $\bm{d}_i$, how do we ensure that when we apply Cayley transform to these local components, their product remains globally orthogonal?

Is each small block (e.g., $2 \times 2$ or $b \times b$) treated as an independent orthogonal generator via Cayley transform, and then combined? If so, how do we handle cases where blocks overlap or interact across layers?
Answer:
The connection between Butterfly Factorization and Cayley parameterization in the paper relies on the principle that a global orthogonal matrix can be constructed as a product of sparse, locally orthogonal matrices, where each local block is independently parameterized using the Cayley transform.

To ensure global orthogonality, the method does not apply a single Cayley transform to the entire dense matrix; instead, it decomposes the target orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ into a product of $m$ sparse butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. The paper establishes that if every individual factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$ is orthogonal, their product is guaranteed to be orthogonal due to the group property of orthogonal matrices (the product of two orthogonal matrices is always orthogonal). The "local" application of Cayley parameterization occurs within these sparse factors. Specifically, each butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ is a block-diagonal matrix composed of smaller blocks (e.g., $2b \times 2b$ blocks for the base case). The paper explicitly states that to guarantee these components are orthogonal, one can parameterize each small block independently using the Cayley transform $\bm{R}_{\text{block}} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a learnable skew-symmetric matrix specific to that block.

Regarding your question about overlapping or interacting blocks, it is important to clarify that within a single butterfly layer (a specific $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$), the blocks are strictly disjoint and arranged in a block-diagonal structure; they do not overlap within that specific factor. The "interaction" or mixing of information happens sequentially across different layers in the product. For instance, while one layer might mix dimensions $(1,2)$ and another might mix $(3,4)$, subsequent layers with different sparsity patterns (permutations of the base pattern) will eventually connect all dimensions. Because each layer's non-zero pattern is simply a permutation of the base $2b \times 2b$ block structure, the same Cayley parameterization logic applies uniformly: you treat every active $2b \times 2b$ sub-block in every layer as an independent generator of an orthogonal transformation. By ensuring every constituent $2b \times 2b$ block across all layers satisfies orthogonality via its own skew-symmetric parameterization, the final composed matrix $\bm{R}$ remains globally orthogonal without requiring complex constraints between layers or handling overlapping parameters within a single step.
