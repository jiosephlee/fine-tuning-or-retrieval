\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Understanding Theorem 1: How can a structured subset be 'more expressive' than OFT?
Question:
Theorem 1 states: 'BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.' The proof sketch mentions multiplying butterfly matrices with their transposes to approximate any orthogonal matrix.

My confusion lies in comparing two constrained spaces:
1. OFT with block size $b$: A block-diagonal orthogonal matrix where blocks are independent.
2. BOFT with block size $b$: A product of sparse butterfly matrices.

Intuitively, both seem like subsets of the full orthogonal group. Why is one subset strictly larger or 'more expressive' than the other given they use similar parameter counts (or at least similar structural constraints)? Does 'more expressive' here mean they can reach points in parameter space that OFT cannot reach *for a fixed number of parameters*, or does it refer to asymptotic properties as dimensions grow?
Answer:
Theorem 1 asserts that BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size $b$ because the set of matrices representable by BOFT strictly contains the set of matrices representable by OFT, effectively subsuming OFT as a special case while allowing for dense interactions that OFT cannot achieve.

To understand this intuitively, consider the structural constraints imposed on the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in both methods. In OFT with block size $b$, $\bm{R}$ is constrained to be block-diagonal, meaning it is composed of independent $b \times b$ orthogonal blocks along the diagonal. This structure enforces a "locality" constraint where dimensions within one block cannot interact with dimensions in another block; information transmission is strictly confined within these disjoint groups. In contrast, BOFT constructs $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple sparse butterfly matrices, $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. As the paper explains using the information transmission framework, this sequential multiplication allows information to flow across all $d$ dimensions through a series of sparse connections. While each individual butterfly component is sparse and structured, their product can generate a fully dense orthogonal matrix where every input dimension can influence every output dimension.

The key to the "more expressive" claim lies in the fact that BOFT can exactly replicate any OFT configuration while also accessing configurations that OFT cannot. Specifically, if we set the number of butterfly layers $m=1$ in BOFT, it reduces exactly to the block-diagonal structure of OFT with block size $b$. However, by increasing $m$ (e.g., to $\log d$), BOFT introduces additional degrees of freedom that allow it to approximate or exactly represent dense orthogonal matrices. The proof sketch mentioned in your question highlights that by multiplying butterfly matrices with their transposes (a technique related to the kaleidoscope hierarchy), one can approximate any arbitrary orthogonal matrix in the full group $O(d)$. Since OFT is restricted to a specific subset of $O(d)$ defined by its fixed sparsity pattern (block-diagonal), and BOFT can reach that subset plus a vast region of dense matrices, BOFT's hypothesis space is strictly larger.

Therefore, "more expressive" here refers to the ability to reach points in the parameter space (specifically, dense orthogonal transformations) that are mathematically unreachable by OFT for a fixed block size $b$, even though both methods may use similar parameter counts depending on how many layers $m$ are used in BOFT. The paper argues that this expanded space allows BOFT to find a better trade-off between expressivity and regularity, as it can model complex global interactions (like those found in Fourier transforms) which are impossible for the isolated blocks of OFT to capture.
