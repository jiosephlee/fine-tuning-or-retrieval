\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### Trade-off analysis: Why not use Kaleidoscope Hierarchy for maximum expressivity?
Question:
In Section 5 ('Expressivity'), the authors mention that by stacking multiple butterfly matrices and their transposes (Kaleidoscope hierarchy), one can theoretically represent *any* orthogonal matrix in $O(d)$ if parameters allow ($l=d-1$).

They acknowledge: 'better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance... The trade-off between expressivity and regularity is key.'

Given that LOFA/LORA relies on Low-Rank approximations which drastically cut expressivity but work well, and Full Fine-tuning has max expressivity but poor generalization/stability:
Where exactly does BOFT sit on this spectrum compared to Kaleidoscope? If Kaleidoscope can reach any point in $O(d)$, why stop at simple Butterfly factorization ($m=\log d$)? Is there evidence in their ablation studies that increasing depth beyond logarithmic scales hurts generalization due to over-parameterization within the constrained manifold?
Answer:
The reason BOFT stops at the standard butterfly factorization ($m = \log d$) rather than expanding to the full Kaleidoscope hierarchy ($l = d-1$) lies precisely in the trade-off between **expressivity** and **inductive bias**, a core theme of the paper. While the authors acknowledge in Section 5 that the Kaleidoscope hierarchy can theoretically represent *any* orthogonal matrix (achieving maximum expressivity equivalent to full fine-tuning), they explicitly argue that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance."

Full fine-tuning serves as the empirical proof for this claim: although it possesses universal expressivity, it often suffers from poor generalization and instability because the hypothesis space is too vast, allowing the model to overfit noise or drift too far from the pretrained manifold. The Kaleidoscope hierarchy, by requiring $l=d-1$ stages, essentially removes the structural constraints that define BOFT's advantage. By limiting $m$ to $\log d$, BOFT restricts the search space to a specific, highly structured subset of the orthogonal group that shares topological similarities with efficient signal processing transforms like the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and Hadamard transforms. This restriction acts as a powerful **implicit inductive bias**.

Rather than viewing this limitation as a loss of capability, BOFT leverages it to enforce **regularity**. The butterfly structure ensures that information is mixed globally across dimensions (unlike block-diagonal OFT) but does so through a smooth, hierarchical interpolation between the identity matrix and a dense orthogonal matrix. This allows BOFT to explore a richer function space than block-diagonal OFT while remaining far more regularized than full fine-tuning or the fully expressive Kaleidoscope hierarchy. The ablation studies and theoretical discussion suggest that increasing depth beyond $\log d$ would dilute this beneficial bias, likely causing performance degradation due to over-parameterization within an unnecessarily large manifold, thereby negating the gains in parameter efficiency without providing corresponding improvements in downstream task accuracy.
