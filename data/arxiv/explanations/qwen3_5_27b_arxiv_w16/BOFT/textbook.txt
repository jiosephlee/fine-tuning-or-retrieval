# Introduction to Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning and Orthogonal Constraints

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence has been driven by the emergence of large foundation models, such as ChatGPT and Stable Diffusion, which exhibit remarkable generalization capabilities across a wide spectrum of tasks. These models, however, come with a prohibitive cost: the sheer number of parameters required to achieve state-of-the-art performance has grown exponentially, with models like GPT-3 containing approximately 175 billion parameters. This scale renders training a foundation model from scratch computationally infeasible for most researchers and organizations. Consequently, the field has shifted its focus from training new models to efficiently adapting existing pre-trained models to specific downstream tasks. This paradigm shift necessitates methods that can leverage the vast knowledge encoded in pre-trained weights without the need for retraining the entire network, thereby democratizing access to these powerful tools while managing computational resources effectively.

To address the challenge of adapting massive models, three primary paradigms for efficient task adaptation have emerged: model fine-tuning, adapter tuning, and prompt tuning. Model fine-tuning is distinguished by its simplicity and lack of inference latency; it involves keeping the model architecture unchanged while updating a subset of the model's parameters. In contrast, adapter tuning introduces additional trainable parameters into the original architecture, often as small modules inserted between layers, which increases memory usage during inference. Prompt tuning takes a different approach by attaching trainable prefix tokens to the input data rather than modifying the model weights directly. Among these strategies, model fine-tuning stands out as a particularly powerful approach because it maintains the original model structure and introduces no overhead during the inference phase. The fundamental principle guiding model fine-tuning is to ensure that the fine-tuned model remains sufficiently similar to the pre-trained model according to specific measurements, thereby preserving the valuable knowledge acquired during pre-training.

A critical insight in modern fine-tuning is that simply using a small learning rate is an ad hoc method to limit deviation from pre-trained weights; a more principled approach involves preserving the structural relationships within the weight matrices themselves. Specifically, researchers have identified that preserving pairwise angles between neurons is essential for maintaining semantic knowledge. If neurons in a layer are transformed such that their relative angles change drastically, the geometric structure of the learned representations may be distorted, leading to catastrophic forgetting or poor generalization. This geometric perspective motivates Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT), a framework where neurons within the same layer are transformed by a shared orthogonal matrix. By definition, an orthogonal matrix preserves vector lengths and angles; therefore, applying such a transformation ensures that pairwise angles between neurons are provably preserved throughout the fine-tuning process. This property allows OFT to update weights multiplicatively while rigorously maintaining the relational information established during pre-training.

Despite its theoretical elegance and demonstrated success in tasks like text-to-image generation, standard Orthogonal Fine-Tuning faces a significant bottleneck regarding parameter efficiency. A dense orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ trainable parameters due to its high dimensionality. For large foundation models where $d$ can be very large (e.g., thousands or tens of thousands), this parameter count becomes comparable to or even exceeds that of other efficient methods like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), which relies on additive low-rank updates with far fewer parameters. To mitigate this issue without abandoning orthogonality entirely, standard OFT often employs a block-diagonal structure. In this approach, the large orthogonal matrix is decomposed into smaller independent orthogonal blocks along the diagonal. While this reduces the number of parameters significantly—scaling linearly with $d$ rather than quadratically—it introduces severe limitations on expressivity and imposes rigid inductive biases on the learning process.

The block-diagonal compromise in OFT creates a fundamental trade-off between parameter efficiency and model expressivity that limits its general applicability. By forcing dimensions of a neuron into disjoint groups that are transformed independently based on their indices, this structure assumes that features within different blocks do not need to interact globally during adaptation. This assumption introduces an undesirable sparsity pattern where information cannot flow freely between all dimensions of a neuron vector. Consequently, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices cannot approximate classic linear transforms or dense interactions as effectively as full orthogonal matrices would allow. Furthermore, determining an optimal block size or sparsity pattern remains an open problem; choosing blocks that are too small restricts expressivity too much, while larger blocks negate parameter efficiency gains. The inability of block-diagonal structures to recover dense transformations means they may fail to capture complex dependencies required for diverse downstream tasks in vision and language processing.

Ultimately, the limitations of standard OFT highlight a critical gap in current parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods: how to construct a dense orthogonal matrix that preserves geometric properties while remaining computationally feasible in terms of trainable parameters. The ideal solution would allow for global interactions between all neuron dimensions—ensuring high expressivity—without requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters or introducing arbitrary sparsity patterns like those found in block-diagonal approaches. Addressing this challenge requires rethinking how orthogonal matrices are parameterized and constructed within neural networks. By viewing matrix factorization through an information transmission lens and leveraging structured sparse factorizations inspired by algorithms like Cooley-Tukey's Fast Fourier Transform, it becomes possible to design methods that bridge this gap. Such approaches aim to compose dense orthogonal matrices from products of sparse components, thereby achieving parameter efficiency without sacrificing the ability to perform complex global transformations necessary for robust adaptation across various foundation models and downstream tasks.

# An Information Transmission Framework for Matrix Factorization

The challenge of adapting large foundation models to downstream tasks often hinges on the ability to modify weight matrices efficiently without compromising the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. In the context of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), the goal is to learn an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ that transforms the pretrained weights while preserving the pairwise angles between neurons. While a dense orthogonal matrix offers maximal expressivity, it requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional models. Conversely, simple block-diagonal structures reduce parameter counts but introduce restrictive inductive biases that limit the model's ability to approximate general linear transforms. To bridge this gap between parameter efficiency and expressivity, we introduce a novel theoretical perspective: reframing the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem on a grid-structured graph. This framework shifts the focus from static matrix algebra to dynamic flow, allowing us to design sparse factorizations that achieve dense connectivity with significantly fewer trainable parameters.

In this view, the construction of a target dense matrix $\bm{R}$ is decomposed into a product of $m$ square matrices, expressed as $\bm{R} = \bm{B}_m \bm{B}_{m-1} \cdots \bm{B}_1$. Rather than viewing this merely as a sequence of linear algebraic operations, we interpret it as a process of transmitting information across a grid containing $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. Imagine a graph where there are $d$ nodes at each of the $m+1$ levels, representing the dimensions of the vector space at different stages of transformation. The multiplication of these matrices corresponds to the sequential exchange of information between adjacent levels in this grid. Specifically, each factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$ acts as a connectivity map between the nodes at level $i$ and level $i+1$. If we consider an input vector entering at level 1, its components propagate through the network defined by $\bm{B}_1$, then $\bm{B}_2$, and so on, until they reach level $m+1$. The final state of the system at level $m+1$ represents the result of applying the composite matrix $\bm{R}$ to the input. This graphical interpretation provides an intuitive mechanism for understanding how sparse intermediate matrices can collectively produce a dense transformation: information flows from source nodes to receiver nodes through multiple hops, allowing every node in the first level to eventually influence every node in the last level, even if direct connections between them are absent in any single factor matrix.

To make this correspondence precise, we map the elements of the factor matrices directly to directed edges in our grid graph. Consider a specific element $(j_1, j_2)$ in a factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$. This element determines whether there is a directed edge from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $i+1$. If the entry is non-zero, an edge exists, and information can flow from the source node to the destination node during that stage of transmission. If the entry is zero, no edge exists, effectively blocking any direct information flow between those specific nodes at that specific step. Consequently, determining whether the final product matrix $\bm{R}$ is dense becomes equivalent to checking if there exists a path from every node in level 1 to every node in level $m+1$. For instance, if we examine a partial product such as $\bm{R}' = \bm{B}_4 \bm{B}_3 \bm{B}_2 \bm{B}_1$, and find that information from node 1 at level 1 cannot reach node 3 at level 5 due to missing intermediate edges in any valid path sequence, then the corresponding entry $(3, 1)$ in $\bm{R}'$ must be zero. Thus, visualizing sparsity patterns through induced graphs allows us to diagnose exactly where connectivity fails and how adding or rearranging edges in specific factor matrices can restore global density without requiring every single factor to be fully dense itself.

The ultimate objective within this framework is to satisfy two competing desiderata simultaneously: dense connectivity and minimum free edges. Dense connectivity requires that every node in the first level has at least one valid path leading to every node in the last level; this ensures that the resulting composite matrix $\bm{R}$ has no zero entries and can theoretically approximate any linear transformation within its hypothesis class. Simultaneously, we seek minimum free edges to maximize parameter efficiency. In our graph formulation, "edges" correspond to non-zero entries in the factor matrices that carry trainable parameters. However, not all non-zero entries contribute equally to parameter count due to structural constraints. We distinguish between "necessary" edges required by mathematical properties like orthogonality and "free" edges that represent actual degrees of freedom for learning. A naive approach might suggest that achieving dense connectivity requires connecting every node at one level directly to every node at the next with unique weights, which would result in $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges per layer and defeat the purpose of factorization. Instead, by chaining multiple sparse layers together ($\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ total), we can achieve full connectivity with far fewer free parameters than a single dense layer would require.

A critical component of this framework is understanding how orthogonality constraints shape the allowable topology of our graph. Orthogonality imposes strict requirements on how information can be routed through each layer without loss or distortion. For each factor matrix $\bm{B}_i$ to be orthogonal (and thus full-rank), it must establish a bijection between all nodes at level $i$ and all nodes at level $i+1$. In graph terms, this means that for every node at level $i$, there must be exactly one outgoing edge (or more generally, a set of edges forming an orthogonal basis) connecting it uniquely to nodes at level $i+1$, ensuring no two source nodes map identically or lose rank during transmission. Specifically, for a standard orthogonal matrix with only non-zero entries being permutations or rotations (like identity or rotation matrices), we require exactly $d$ edges between adjacent levels just to satisfy full rank; these are often fixed structural elements (e.g., values like $\pm 1$) rather than trainable parameters. These necessary edges do not count toward our "free edge" budget because their values are constrained by orthogonality rather than learned freely from data. Furthermore, orthogonality introduces dependencies among edges: if one edge is missing or constrained in a specific way within an orthogonal block (e.g., setting one element to zero forces another element's value or position), it propagates constraints across other potential connections in that block. Therefore, designing an efficient factorization involves navigating these rigid topological constraints while strategically placing trainable "free" edges where they most effectively contribute to global connectivity.

Finally, comparing naive dense connections against our proposed factorized sparse connections reveals significant gains in efficiency under this information transmission lens. A single dense orthogonal matrix corresponds to a graph where every node at level 0 connects directly to every node at level 1 via trainable weights (excluding fixed structural constraints). For dimension $d$, this requires approximately $d^2 - d$ trainable edges (since orthogonality fixes some degrees of freedom), resulting in quadratic complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. In contrast, our framework demonstrates that by unrolling this process over multiple levels using sparse factors like butterfly structures, we can achieve equivalent global connectivity with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ total trainable parameters. For example, while connecting 4 nodes densely might require 12 trainable edges in one step (after accounting for orthogonality constraints), decomposing this into five sparse steps using specific butterfly-like topologies might require only 8 or 10 total edges across all steps while still ensuring every source reaches every destination through some path sequence. This graphical perspective not only clarifies why block-diagonal structures fail—they create disjoint subgraphs where information cannot cross between blocks—but also illuminates why butterfly structures succeed: they provide a highly efficient network topology that minimizes redundant paths while guaranteeing that information from any source eventually permeates all receivers through recursive mixing operations inspired by fast Fourier transform algorithms.

# Butterfly Structures and Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization

The challenge of adapting massive foundation models to specific downstream tasks without the prohibitive cost of full retraining has led to the development of parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods. Among these, Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) stands out by preserving the pairwise angles between neurons, thereby maintaining the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. However, the original OFT approach relies on high-dimensional orthogonal matrices that require a quadratic number of parameters, $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$, making it computationally expensive. While block-diagonal structures were introduced to reduce this count, they impose rigid sparsity patterns that limit expressiveness and introduce undesirable inductive biases. To resolve this tension between parameter efficiency and matrix density, we turn to a novel mathematical framework inspired by signal processing: the butterfly structure derived from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm. This chapter details how these structures enable the construction of dense orthogonal matrices with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, forming the backbone of the Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) method.

## The Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) Algorithm as a Source of Inspiration

The Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm represents a paradigm shift in how we compute linear transformations, offering a blueprint for efficient matrix factorization. In its standard application, the FFT computes the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) of a sequence with $N$ elements. A naive computation of the DFT requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations because every output element depends on every input element through a dense matrix multiplication. The Cooley-Tukey algorithm revolutionizes this by recursively decomposing an $N$-point DFT into two $N/2$-point DFTs, effectively breaking down a dense transformation into a sequence of sparse operations. This recursive decomposition induces a specific computational topology known as the "butterfly" structure. In this structure, information from different nodes is exchanged efficiently across multiple stages; a local change in one part of the input can propagate globally to affect all outputs, yet this is achieved through a series of localized interactions rather than a single massive matrix operation.

From an information transmission perspective, which is central to our approach for orthogonal finetuning, the butterfly structure is ideal because it achieves dense connectivity with minimal resources. In our context, generating a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be viewed as transmitting information from $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes across a grid-structured graph. A single dense matrix corresponds to a fully connected graph requiring $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges (parameters). However, just as the FFT achieves global frequency analysis through local butterfly operations, we can construct a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying multiple sparse matrices that follow a butterfly topology. The key insight is that while each individual sparse matrix in the product only connects neighboring nodes or small groups of nodes (representing local information exchange), their sequential multiplication allows information from any source node to eventually reach any receiver node. This property ensures that the final composed matrix is dense and expressive, capable of approximating general linear transforms like the DFT or Hadamard transform, while maintaining a parameter count that scales logarithmically with dimension rather than quadratically. By leveraging this established efficiency from computer science and signal processing, we can overcome the limitations of block-diagonal OFT without sacrificing the ability to learn complex transformations.

## Definition of Butterfly Factors ($\bm{B}^F(k)$) and Butterfly Components ($\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$)

To formalize this intuition mathematically, we must define the atomic building blocks of our parameterization: the butterfly factor and the butterfly component. We assume that $k \geq 2$ is a power of 2 for simplicity in defining these structures recursively. The fundamental unit is the **butterfly factor**, denoted as $\bm{B}^F(k)$, which is a $k \times k$ matrix designed to mix information between two halves of its input vector. Specifically, $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as:

$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
$$

where $\bm{d}_1, \bm{d}_2, \bm{d}_3,$ and $\bm{d}_4$ are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$. This structure creates four diagonal blocks within a $k \times k$ space. Unlike an arbitrary dense matrix where every entry could be non-zero here only entries on four specific diagonals are active within each quadrant relative to the partitioning of rows and columns. This sparsity pattern allows for efficient computation while ensuring that every element in the top half of the input interacts with elements in both halves of the output through these diagonal weightings. The vectors $\bm{d}_i$ contain the trainable parameters for this specific stage of interaction.

Building upon this atomic factor, we define the **butterfly component**, denoted as $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$, which operates on a larger dimension $d$. This component is constructed by placing multiple copies of smaller butterfly factors along the diagonal to form a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$. Formally:

$$
\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}\left(\bm{B}^F_1(k), \dots, \bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k)\right).
$$

Here, $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ consists of $d/k$ blocks along its diagonal, where each block is an independent instance of a $k \times k$ butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$. The remaining entries outside these diagonal blocks are zero. This construction effectively applies parallelized versions of the same mixing operation across different segments of the input vector simultaneously. For instance, if $k=2$, then $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ consists entirely of disjoint $2 \times 2$ blocks along the diagonal; if $k=d$, then there is only one block which is simply $\bm{B}^F(d)$. These components serve as layers in our factorization chain; by multiplying several such components with varying block sizes $k$, we can progressively mix information across increasingly larger portions of the vector space until full global connectivity is achieved in their product.

## Recursive Construction of $d$-dimensional Butterfly Matrices ($\bm{B}(d)$)

With the definitions for factors and components established, we can now construct the full **butterfly matrix** $\bm{B}(d)$ for an arbitrary dimension $d$, assuming $d=2^N$. The construction follows a recursive strategy analogous to divide-and-conquer algorithms found in computer science literature like Cooley-Tukey. The core idea is that an orthogonal transformation on dimension $d$ can be decomposed into transformations on dimension $d/2$, interleaved with mixing operations at dimension $k$. Mathematically, this recursive definition is expressed as:

$$
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

# Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT): Methodology and Implementation

The transition from theoretical insight to practical application in parameter-efficient fine-tuning requires a rigorous definition of the proposed architecture and its operational mechanics. Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT) represents a novel paradigm that addresses the parameter inefficiency of previous orthogonal fine-tuning methods by leveraging structured matrix factorization. At its core, BOFT is defined by two primary hyperparameters: $m$ and $b$. The parameter $m$ controls the depth of the factorization, representing the number of sparse orthogonal matrices multiplied together to form the final transformation matrix. The parameter $b$ dictates the block size, determining the granularity of the interactions within each sparse component. Together, these parameters define a specific instance of the method, denoted as $\text{BOFT}(m, b)$. This notation encapsulates a trade-off between expressivity and parameter count; increasing $m$ allows for more complex information mixing across dimensions, while adjusting $b$ controls the local connectivity within the butterfly components. The method is designed such that it can flexibly interpolate between sparse, block-diagonal structures and fully dense orthogonal transformations, all while maintaining a significantly reduced number of trainable parameters compared to standard dense orthogonal matrices.

The mathematical formulation of BOFT relies on constructing a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ as a product of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components. Each component, denoted as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, is itself a block-diagonal matrix composed of smaller butterfly factors. Specifically, for a linear layer with dimension $d$, the forward pass in BOFT modifies the computation from the standard $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{W}^0$ is the frozen pretrained weight matrix and $\bm{x}$ is the input vector. The transformation matrix is explicitly defined as $\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Crucially, every individual component in this product must satisfy the orthogonality constraint, meaning that for all $j \in [1, m]$, $(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)})^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)})^\top = \bm{I}_d$, where $\bm{I}_d$ is the identity matrix of size $d$. This recursive construction ensures that while each factor is sparse and parameter-efficient, their product results in a dense matrix capable of complex global transformations. The total number of effective trainable parameters required for this configuration is given by $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$, which scales linearly with both the dimension $d$ and the depth $m$, offering a substantial reduction over the quadratic scaling of full dense orthogonal matrices.

A critical feature of BOFT is its ability to subsume existing orthogonal fine-tuning methods as special cases, thereby unifying them under a single generalized framework. When the depth parameter is set to $m=1$, the method reduces to $\text{BOFT}(1, b)$, which corresponds exactly to the original block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) with block size $b$. In this scenario, no multiplicative composition occurs; instead, a single block-diagonal orthogonal matrix transforms the weights independently within disjoint groups of dimensions. Conversely, if one sets $m=1$ and allows the block size to equal the full dimension ($b=d$), we obtain $\text{BOFT}(1, d)$, which represents an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix update. This recovers the original OFT formulation where high-dimensional dense matrices are used directly, albeit at a high computational cost in terms of parameters. These special cases demonstrate that BOFT generalizes previous approaches: it retains their structural properties when desired but offers a pathway to denser connectivity without incurring prohibitive parameter costs by simply increasing $m$.

The primary innovation of BOFT lies in its capacity to generate dense orthogonal matrices efficiently using logarithmic depth. By setting $m = \log d$ and choosing a small block size such as $b=2$, we arrive at $\text{BOFT}(\log d, 2)$. In this configuration, the method utilizes a butterfly matrix structure derived from principles similar to those in fast Fourier transform algorithms. Here, a dense orthogonal matrix is constructed through a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero entries. This results in a total parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$, which is exponentially more efficient than the $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ required for a full dense orthogonal matrix. Unlike block-diagonal structures that inherently limit expressiveness by isolating dimensions into independent groups, this butterfly-based factorization ensures that information from any node in the input space can be transmitted to any node in the output space through a sequence of sparse interactions. This capability allows BOFT to approximate classic linear transforms like discrete Fourier transforms and Hadamard transforms while maintaining strict orthogonality and parameter efficiency.

Initialization strategies are paramount in fine-tuning scenarios where preserving pretrained knowledge is essential. In additive methods like LoRA, zero initialization ensures that no update occurs at step zero. For multiplicative methods like BOFT, where weights are transformed rather than added upon zero initialization would result in an identity transformation only if applied additively; however, since BOFT operates via multiplication by an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$, initializing $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix achieves the equivalent effect: $(\bm{I}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x} = (\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. To implement this practically within BOFT's Cayley parameterization framework—where an orthogonal matrix is represented as $(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}$ being skew-symmetric—all trainable skew-symmetric matrices corresponding to each butterfly component are initialized to zero matrices ($\mathbfbf0$). This ensures that at initialization ($t=0$), every component becomes an identity matrix ($(\bm{I}+\mathbfbf0)(\mathbfbf0-\mathbfbf0)^{-1}$ effectively simplifies conceptually via limits or direct definition to identity), resulting in no deviation from the pretrained model immediately upon starting training. This "zero-initialization" behavior guarantees that finetuning begins from an exact copy of the foundation model before any gradient updates occur.

Regularization techniques must also be adapted for multiplicative updates where standard dropout mechanisms may fail or behave unexpectedly. Conventional dropout randomly zeroes out elements or neurons during training to prevent overfitting; however, applying standard dropout directly to multiplicative weight updates can disrupt orthogonality or introduce instability because dropping elements does not preserve the structural constraints required for valid rotations or reflections. To address this challenge specific to BOFT's architecture, we introduce Multiplicative Dropout tailored for butterfly components. Since each butterfly component can be viewed as a collection of independent diagonal blocks (specifically $2b \times 2b$ blocks), regularization can be applied at this structural level without breaking orthogonality within active blocks. The proposed strategy involves randomly selecting $p_1$ percent of all butterfly components across all layers and replacing them entirely with identity matrices during training steps where dropout is active (or effectively skipping their update). Furthermore, within each selected component that remains active (or potentially even within those being dropped depending on implementation specifics described), one can randomly select $p_2$ percent of its internal diagonal blocks and replace those specific blocks with identity matrices as well. By replacing active transformation blocks with identity matrices rather than zeroing them out or adding noise additively, we maintain orthogonality for all non-dropped parts while effectively reducing model capacity during training epochs to prevent overfitting on downstream tasks.
