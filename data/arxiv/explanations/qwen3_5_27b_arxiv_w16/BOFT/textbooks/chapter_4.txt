Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Designing Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT)

Having established the limitations of standard Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) regarding parameter efficiency and explored the conceptual framework of viewing matrix factorization as an information transmission problem, we now turn to the formal construction of our proposed solution: Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). The central challenge addressed in this chapter is how to synthesize a dense, expressive orthogonal matrix that preserves the geometric properties of pretrained weights while drastically reducing the number of trainable parameters. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on block-diagonal structures which impose rigid sparsity patterns and limit expressiveness, BOFT leverages the topological efficiency of butterfly structures derived from the Cooley-Tukey Fast Fourier Transform algorithm. By decomposing a large orthogonal transformation into a sequence of sparse, structured operations, we achieve a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ instead of the prohibitive $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ required for a fully dense matrix, or the limited flexibility of block-diagonal approximations. This chapter details the mathematical machinery behind this construction, including the recursive definition of butterfly components, the enforcement of orthogonality constraints through Cayley parameterization, and specific implementation strategies designed to maintain training stability and prevent overfitting in a multiplicative setting.

# $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ Parameterization via Recursive Butterfly Matrices

The core innovation enabling BOFT's parameter efficiency lies in its ability to construct a dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix through the multiplication of a logarithmic number of sparse matrices. In the context of our information transmission framework, generating a dense matrix is equivalent to ensuring that information from every node at the initial level can reach every node at the final level across a grid-structured graph. A naive approach would require $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ connections (edges) per layer to ensure density immediately, resulting in quadratic parameter growth. However, inspired by network topologies used in fast linear transforms like the Discrete Fourier Transform, we can achieve full connectivity with far fewer edges by distributing connections across multiple stages. Specifically, we utilize a butterfly structure that recursively combines smaller transformations to build up global mixing capabilities.

To formalize this, let $d = 2^N$ for some integer $N$, assuming without loss of generality that dimensions are powers of two for clarity in recursion (padding can handle otherwise). We begin by defining the elementary building block known as the *butterfly factor*. For a dimension $k$ where $k \geq 2$ is a power of two, the butterfly factor $\bm{B}^F(k)$ is defined as a $k \times k$ matrix constructed from four diagonal matrices:
$$
\bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
\text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
$$
where each $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{k/2}$ represents a vector determining the scaling and interaction within that specific block. This structure allows local interactions between pairs of nodes separated by half the dimension size. Building upon this factor, we define the full $d$-dimensional *butterfly matrix* $\bm{B}(d)$ recursively. The recursion proceeds by combining two smaller butterfly matrices operating on disjoint halves of the input space and then applying a permutation-like mixing step across these halves. Mathematically, this is expressed as:
$$
\bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}
\bm{B}_1(d/2) & \bm{0} \\
\bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(d/2)
\end{bmatrix},
$$
where $\bm{B}_1(d/2)$ and $\bm{B}_2(d/2)$ are independent butterfly matrices of dimension $d/2$, and $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)$ acts as a global mixer. Expanding this recursion reveals that any $d$-dimensional butterfly matrix can be represented as a product of components corresponding to different scales:
$$
\bm{B}(d)= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d/2)\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2).
$$
Here, each term $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ represents a *butterfly component*, which is itself a block-diagonal matrix formed by repeating smaller butterfly factors across the entire dimension $d$. Specifically, $\tilde{\BM B}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\BM B^F_1(k),\dots,\BM B^F_{d/k}(k))$. This hierarchical composition ensures that information propagates from fine-grained local interactions ($k=2$) to coarse-grained global interactions ($k=d$), eventually connecting every input node to every output node with only $\mathcal{o}(d \log d)$ non-zero entries distributed across $\log d$ layers.

To generalize this structure for finetuning applications where we might want to balance granularity and computational cost further, we introduce *block butterfly components*. Instead of scalar entries in the vectors $\BM d_i$, we allow them to be $b \times b$ matrices. We denote this generalized component as $\tilde{\BM B}^b(d,k)$. The forward pass in BOFT utilizes an orthogonal matrix $\BM R(m,b)$ constructed as the product of $m$ such block butterfly components:
$$
\BM z=\big(\BM R(m,b)\cdot\BM W^0\big)^\top\BM x,\quad \text{s.t. } \Bigg{\{}\BM R(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\BM B}_{(d,i)} ~~&~~ (\tilde{\BM B}_{(d,j)})^\top\tilde{\BM B}_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\BM B}_{(d,j)}(\tilde{\BM B}_{(d,j)})^\top=\BM I_d ~~~~ \forall j \in [1,m] \Bigg{\}},
$$
where $\tilde{\BM B}_{(d,i)}$ corresponds to $\tilde{\BM B}^b(d, 2^{m-i+1})$. This formulation creates a flexible family of operators denoted as BOFT($m$, $b$). When $m=1$, BOFT collapses back to standard block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. Conversely, when $m = \log_2(2d/b)$ and appropriate settings are chosen, BOFT constructs a fully dense orthogonal matrix using only sparse intermediate steps. Crucially, the total number of effective trainable parameters for finetuning a linear layer is approximately proportional to $(b-1)d m / 2$. In the optimal regime where we use pure butterfly matrices ($m=\log d$, $b=2$), this results in exactly $\mathcal{o}(d \log d)$ parameters. This stands in stark contrast to original OFT requiring $\mathcal{o}(d^2)$ parameters for density or block-diagonal OFT requiring careful selection of block sizes that often sacrifice expressiveness for efficiency. The recursive nature ensures that even with minimal parameters per layer, the cumulative effect over $m$ layers yields high-dimensional mixing capabilities comparable to dense transformations but with significantly lower memory footprint during optimization.

# Enforcing Orthogonality via Cayley Transform on Butterfly Blocks

While constructing sparse matrices via butterfly structures solves the parameter count issue, it does not automatically satisfy the requirement that each component—and consequently their product—must be strictly orthogonal. Preserving orthogonality is paramount because it guarantees that pairwise angles between neurons remain invariant throughout finetuning, thereby preserving semantic knowledge encoded in pretrained weights without distortion caused by arbitrary scaling or shearing effects common in low-rank methods like LoRA. To enforce this constraint within our factorized framework without resorting to expensive projection techniques during every gradient descent step (such as Gram-Schmidt or SVD projections), we employ Cayley parameterization directly on the constituent blocks of our butterfly structure.

Cayley parameterization offers an elegant algebraic solution for mapping unconstrained skew-symmetric matrices onto orthogonal matrices with determinant +1 (excluding eigenvalues equal to -1). For any skew-symmetric matrix $\QM Q$ satisfying $\QM Q = -\QM Q^\top$, we can define an orthogonal matrix via:
$$
\M R = (\M I + M Q)(M I - M Q)^{-1}.
$$
In our context, instead of applying this transform globally—which would reintroduce density—we apply it locally to each irreducible unit within our butterfly components. Recall that our generalized block butterfly components are composed essentially repeated instances of small mixing units acting on subspaces determined by block size $b$. Specifically, at each stage involving scale $k$, we encounter blocks resembling expanded versions of our base factor structure where interactions occur between pairs (or groups if using blocks) indexed appropriately by binary representations or bit-reversals inherent in FFT algorithms.
