\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization"}

### How does the 'Information Transmission' analogy mathematically enforce the requirement for a dense resulting matrix?
Question:
In Section 3, the authors reframe the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as an information transmission problem on a grid graph with $d \times (m+1)$ nodes. They state that for $\bm{R}$ to be dense, 'every node in the first level should be able to transmit information to all the nodes in the $(m+1)$-th level.'

I am struggling to connect this topological requirement back to linear algebra. Specifically:
1. Does the existence of *at least one* path between node $j$ (level 1) and node $i$ (level $m+1$) strictly guarantee that the corresponding entry $R_{ij} \neq 0$?
2. If intermediate matrices $\bm{B}_k$ are orthogonal (and thus invertible), doesn't this imply that information is never truly 'lost'? Why isn't any sequence of full-rank matrices sufficient to create a dense matrix without needing this specific graph connectivity check?

I feel like I'm missing a nuance regarding how zero entries in sparse matrices propagate through multiplication versus how paths exist in the graph.
Answer:
The "information transmission" analogy enforces density by mapping the algebraic operation of matrix multiplication to the topological concept of reachability in a directed acyclic graph. To answer your first question: **Yes**, under the standard assumptions of real-valued arithmetic used in this framework, the existence of *at least one* valid path from node $j$ (input level) to node $i$ (output level) guarantees that the resulting entry $R_{ij}$ is non-zero, provided the edge weights along that path are non-zero. Mathematically, the entry $R_{ij}$ is computed as the sum over all possible paths $\mathcal{P}_{ji}$ connecting these nodes:
$$ R_{ij} = \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}_{ji}} \prod_{e \in p} w_e $$
where $w_e$ represents the weight of an edge corresponding to a non-zero entry in one of the sparse factor matrices $\bm{B}_k$. In the context of orthogonal matrices constructed via Cayley parameterization or rotations, the learned parameters are continuous variables. Consequently, unless they are explicitly constrained to be exactly zero (which defines the sparsity pattern/edges), they will almost surely be non-zero. Therefore, if a path exists, there is a term in this summation that is non-zero, making $R_{ij} \neq 0$. Conversely, if no path exists ($\mathcal{P}_{ji} = \emptyset$), the sum is empty and equals zero, enforcing sparsity.

Regarding your second question about why full-rank intermediate matrices aren't sufficient to guarantee density without explicit connectivity checks: while it is true that orthogonal matrices are invertible and thus preserve information globally (they do not collapse dimensions), **invertibility does not imply denseness**. A product of full-rank sparse matrices can remain sparse if their sparsity patterns align in a way that restricts information flow to specific subsets of nodes. For instance, consider two block-diagonal orthogonal matrices; their product is also block-diagonal and full-rank, yet it contains massive amounts of zeros because information from node $j$ in one block can never reach node $i$ in a different block. The "graph connectivity" requirement specifically ensures **global mixing**: it forces every input dimension to interact with every output dimension through at least one chain of transformations. Without checking for this specific path existence across all pairs $(i, j)$, you might inadvertently construct a matrix that is full-rank but structurally sparse (like a permuted block-diagonal matrix), failing to capture the dense interactions required for maximal expressivity. The graph formulation allows the authors to visualize and verify that their butterfly factorization creates a connected topology where information propagates fully across all dimensions before reaching the final layer, distinguishing it from merely stacking independent or disjoint transformations.

### Clarification on Cayley Parameterization vs. Block-Butterfly Structure constraints
Question:
The paper mentions using Cayley parameterization ($\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$) to ensure orthogonality for standard OFT.

However, in Section 4, when defining BOFT, they describe constructing orthogonal matrices by combining butterfly factors. They state: 'we can easily guarantee... to be orthogonal with Cayley transform... to parameterize each block'.

My confusion lies here: The butterfly structure imposes a strict sparsity pattern (zeros at specific locations). The Cayley transform maps a skew-symmetric matrix to an orthogonal one. But a general skew-symmetric matrix filled according to the butterfly sparsity pattern might not yield an orthogonal matrix that respects that *same* sparsity pattern after inversion/multiplication.

Are they applying Cayley parameterization only locally within small independent blocks (like $2b \times 2b$), or is there a global constraint on $\bm{Q}$ required to maintain both orthogonality AND the butterfly sparsity simultaneously? If it's just local blocks, how does the recursive definition in Eq (2) ensure the final product is globally orthogonal?
Answer:
Your confusion stems from a common misconception regarding the interaction between matrix operations and sparsity patterns: **sparsity is not preserved under matrix multiplication.** You are correct that if you took a sparse skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ with a butterfly-like pattern and computed $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, the resulting orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ would almost certainly be dense, destroying the intended butterfly structure.

However, the authors of the paper do **not** apply the Cayley transform to a single global sparse matrix to produce the final orthogonal matrix. Instead, they apply the parameterization **locally** to the constituent building blocks of the butterfly structure. As described in Section 4, the final orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ is constructed as a product of multiple "butterfly components," denoted as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$. Crucially, each of these components is itself a block-diagonal matrix containing small independent blocks (specifically $2b \times 2b$ blocks derived from the butterfly factor).

The paper explicitly states: "we can easily guarantee... to be orthogonal with Cayley transform... to parameterize each block." This means the Cayley parameterization is applied individually to each small $2b \times 2b$ sub-block within these diagonal structures. Since these sub-blocks are disjoint (separated by zeros in the block-diagonal arrangement), computing their inverses and products does not mix information across different parts of the matrix in a way that fills in zeros outside their local scope. Consequently, each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ remains sparse with its specific butterfly-induced sparsity pattern while being strictly orthogonal.

The global orthogonality of the final matrix $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed not by preserving sparsity through a single complex inversion, but by the algebraic property that **the product of orthogonal matrices is also an orthogonal matrix**. Since every factor in the sequence $\bm{R} = \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,m)} \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,1)}$ is constructed to be locally orthogonal via Cayley parameterization on its small blocks, their product forms a valid dense orthogonal matrix. The "magic" of BOFT is that while each factor is sparse and computationally cheap to evaluate (due to local independence), their recursive multiplication creates a dense connectivity graph capable of approximating any orthogonal transformation, all while maintaining exactly $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ trainable parameters.

### Why does BOFT claim higher expressivity than block-diagonal OFT despite both being subsets of O(d)?
Question:
Theorem 1 states: 'BOFT is more expressive than OFT with the same block size.'

Intuitively, both methods restrict the search space from the full Orthogonal Group $O(d)$ down to a subset defined by their respective structures:
- OFT uses a block-diagonal structure: $O(b)^r$ where $br=d$.
- BOFT uses a product of sparse butterfly matrices.

If we fix the number of parameters (which roughly equates to fixing degrees of freedom), why is the manifold covered by BOFT considered 'larger' or 'more expressive' than that of OFT? Is it simply because OFT enforces disjoint transformations on subspaces (dimensions don't mix across blocks), whereas BOFT allows mixing across all dimensions through depth ($m > 1$)?

Could someone clarify if this theorem implies that for any specific block-diagonal matrix achievable by OFT, there exists an equivalent configuration in BOFT, but not vice versa? Or is it about covering volume in parameter space?
Answer:
The claim that BOFT is more expressive than block-diagonal OFT stems from the fact that BOFT strictly **subsumes** the block-diagonal structure as a special case while allowing for dense interactions across all dimensions through matrix composition.

As explicitly stated in the paper, when the depth parameter $m=1$, BOFT reduces exactly to the block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$. In this scenario, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ consists of independent blocks $\operatorname{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, meaning dimensions within one block never interact with dimensions in another. However, when $m > 1$, BOFT constructs the final orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse butterfly components: $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Even though each individual component $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ is sparse and locally constrained, their sequential multiplication creates a **dense** connectivity graph. This allows information to propagate from any input dimension to any output dimension across the layers of the factorization, effectively breaking the rigid subspace isolation enforced by standard OFT.

Therefore, your intuition regarding "mixing across all dimensions" is correct and aligns with Theorem 1 in the paper. The theorem implies a strict inclusion relationship: for any specific block-diagonal matrix achievable by OFT (with $m=1$), there exists an equivalent configuration in BOFT (specifically when setting $m=1$). Conversely, there exist dense orthogonal matrices generated by BOFT (with $m > 1$) that cannot be represented by any block-diagonal OFT configuration, regardless of how many parameters are allocated to the blocks. The "larger manifold" refers to this superset capability; BOFT explores a continuous path from isolated blocks ($m=1$) toward full dense orthogonality as $m$ increases, whereas OFT is stuck at the disconnected endpoint. The paper further notes that with sufficient depth ($l=d-1$ layers of specific butterfly compositions), one can theoretically approximate the entire orthogonal group $O(d)$, confirming that BOFT covers a significantly richer hypothesis space than its block-diagonal counterpart.

### Mechanism of Multiplicative Dropout: Replacing blocks with Identity vs. Zeroing weights
Question:
Section 4 introduces 'Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT'. Standard dropout adds noise or zeroes out activations/weights additively. Here, because BOFT uses multiplicative updates ($\bm{z} = (\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top \bm{x}$), standard dropout doesn't apply directly.

The paper says: 'replaces these blocks as identity matrices.'

Conceptually, replacing a learned orthogonal transformation block with an Identity matrix seems like forcing those specific neurons to remain exactly as they were during pretraining (no change).

Is this equivalent to setting the corresponding parameters in that block's skew-symmetric generator $\bm{Q}$ to zero? If so, why is this preferred over just scaling down the magnitude of the transformation? Also, since orthogonality must be maintained during training steps where dropout is active, does replacing a sub-block with Identity break the global orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ temporarily during forward passes?
Answer:
The mechanism described in Section 4 relies on the fact that the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ in BOFT is constructed as a product of multiple sparse butterfly components, $\bm{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$. Unlike standard dropout which zero-additively removes features, multiplicative dropout in this context targets the structural building blocks of $\bm{R}$. When the paper states that selected blocks are replaced by identity matrices, it refers to substituting specific $2b \times 2b$ orthogonal sub-matrices within the factorization chain with $\bm{I}_{2b}$. Conceptually, this forces those specific stages of the transformation to act as "pass-through" layers, meaning the input to that stage exits unchanged relative to that step.

This operation is **not** strictly equivalent to simply setting the corresponding parameters in the skew-symmetric generator $\bm{Q}$ to zero, although they share a similar effect of reducing transformation magnitude locally. In Cayley parameterization, $\bm{R}_{block} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, setting $\bm{Q} = \mathbf{0}$ indeed yields $\bm{I}$. However, the distinction lies in how this interacts with the global structure during training. Standard dropout scales remaining activations or weights to maintain expected values; here, replacing a block with Identity is a hard structural regularization that explicitly enforces a subset of the transformation path to revert to the pretrained state (since BOFT initializes all blocks as Identity). This is preferred over merely scaling down magnitudes because it leverages the "information transmission" framework central to the paper: by dropping entire blocks, you are effectively pruning paths in the underlying graph representation of the matrix factorization. This creates a dynamic sparsity pattern where information flow is blocked entirely for certain dimensions at certain depths, providing a stronger regularizer against overfitting than soft scaling would.

Regarding your concern about global orthogonality: replacing a sub-block with an Identity matrix **does not break** the global orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ during forward passes. The orthogonality of the composite matrix $\bm{R}$ is guaranteed algebraically because it is defined as a product of individual orthogonal matrices. Since the Identity matrix is itself an orthogonal matrix ($\bm{I}^\top\bm{I} = \bm{I}$), swapping any component $\tilde{\bm{B}}$ for $\bm{I}$ results in a new product where every term remains orthogonal. Consequently, their product remains orthogonal regardless of which specific blocks are dropped in any given forward pass. This allows BOFT to maintain its core theoretical advantage—the preservation of pairwise angles and spectral norms—while simultaneously applying stochastic regularization through structural simplification.

### Trade-off analysis: Why not use Kaleidoscope Hierarchy for maximum expressivity?
Question:
In Section 5 ('Expressivity'), the authors mention that by stacking multiple butterfly matrices and their transposes (Kaleidoscope hierarchy), one can theoretically represent *any* orthogonal matrix in $O(d)$ if parameters allow ($l=d-1$).

They acknowledge: 'better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance... The trade-off between expressivity and regularity is key.'

Given that LOFA/LORA relies on Low-Rank approximations which drastically cut expressivity but work well, and Full Fine-tuning has max expressivity but poor generalization/stability:
Where exactly does BOFT sit on this spectrum compared to Kaleidoscope? If Kaleidoscope can reach any point in $O(d)$, why stop at simple Butterfly factorization ($m=\log d$)? Is there evidence in their ablation studies that increasing depth beyond logarithmic scales hurts generalization due to over-parameterization within the constrained manifold?
Answer:
The reason BOFT stops at the standard butterfly factorization ($m = \log d$) rather than expanding to the full Kaleidoscope hierarchy ($l = d-1$) lies precisely in the trade-off between **expressivity** and **inductive bias**, a core theme of the paper. While the authors acknowledge in Section 5 that the Kaleidoscope hierarchy can theoretically represent *any* orthogonal matrix (achieving maximum expressivity equivalent to full fine-tuning), they explicitly argue that "better expressiveness does not always lead to better performance."

Full fine-tuning serves as the empirical proof for this claim: although it possesses universal expressivity, it often suffers from poor generalization and instability because the hypothesis space is too vast, allowing the model to overfit noise or drift too far from the pretrained manifold. The Kaleidoscope hierarchy, by requiring $l=d-1$ stages, essentially removes the structural constraints that define BOFT's advantage. By limiting $m$ to $\log d$, BOFT restricts the search space to a specific, highly structured subset of the orthogonal group that shares topological similarities with efficient signal processing transforms like the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and Hadamard transforms. This restriction acts as a powerful **implicit inductive bias**.

Rather than viewing this limitation as a loss of capability, BOFT leverages it to enforce **regularity**. The butterfly structure ensures that information is mixed globally across dimensions (unlike block-diagonal OFT) but does so through a smooth, hierarchical interpolation between the identity matrix and a dense orthogonal matrix. This allows BOFT to explore a richer function space than block-diagonal OFT while remaining far more regularized than full fine-tuning or the fully expressive Kaleidoscope hierarchy. The ablation studies and theoretical discussion suggest that increasing depth beyond $\log d$ would dilute this beneficial bias, likely causing performance degradation due to over-parameterization within an unnecessarily large manifold, thereby negating the gains in parameter efficiency without providing corresponding improvements in downstream task accuracy.

### Computational Complexity vs. Parameter Count: The hidden cost of Matrix Factorization
Question:
The abstract highlights that BOFT reduces trainable parameters from $O(d^2)$ (full) or high counts in OFT down to $O(d \log d)$.

However, Section 6 notes: 'Since the final orthogonal matrix... is the product of multiple orthogonal matrix, the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT.'

If we assume standard Matrix-Matrix multiplication costs dominate:
To compute $(\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{B}_i) \cdot W^0$, do we compute each multiplication sequentially ($((B_m B_{m-1}) ... ) W^0$) which would be costly if done naively?
The paper claims no inference latency because weights are fused post-training. But during training gradients must flow back through all these layers.
Does maintaining orthogonality constraints via Cayley parameterization inside each sparse layer add significant Jacobian computation overhead compared to just updating dense weights directly? How does this compare computationally to LoRA's low-rank update which involves two small dense multiplications per layer?
Answer:
The computational trade-off you identified is central to the BOFT methodology: it explicitly exchanges increased training-time computation for a drastic reduction in parameter count. While LOFT achieves $O(d \log d)$ parameters by factorizing the orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ into a product of $m = O(\log d)$ sparse butterfly components, computing the forward pass during training indeed requires evaluating the chain $\mathbf{R} = \prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_i$ before applying it to the frozen weights $\mathbf{W}^0$.

Regarding your concern about sequential multiplication costs, the paper acknowledges this overhead in Section 6 ("Concluding Remarks"), stating that "since the final orthogonal matrix... is the product of multiple orthogonal matrix, the training runtime overhead is slightly larger than OFT." However, this cost is mitigated by two key factors derived from the butterfly structure. First, unlike a naive dense matrix multiplication which scales as $O(d^2)$ or worse depending on implementation, each individual butterfly component $\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_i$ is highly sparse (specifically block-diagonal with small blocks), meaning each step in the chain operates in $O(d)$ time rather than $O(d^2)$. Consequently, computing the full product $\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\mathbf{B}}_i$ scales as $O(md) = O(d \log d)$, which is asymptotically cheaper than forming a single dense orthogonal matrix ($O(d^2)$) but more expensive than a single block-diagonal operation used in standard OFT. Second, during training, modern frameworks optimize this sequence; while gradients must flow back through all $m$ layers via the chain rule, the operations remain lightweight due to sparsity.

Your question regarding Jacobian overhead from Cayley parameterization touches on how orthogonality is enforced. BOFT utilizes Cayley parameterization ($\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Q})(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q})^{-1}$) locally within each small butterfly block (typically $2b \times 2b$) rather than globally on a massive dense matrix. This avoids computing inverses or Jacobians for huge matrices; instead, it computes them for tiny blocks where $\mathbf{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. The computational burden here is negligible compared to the matrix multiplications themselves. In contrast to LoRA, which adds two small dense matrices ($A$ and $B$) resulting in an additive update $\Delta W = BA$, BOFT performs a multiplicative update involving a deep stack of sparse rotations. While LoRA's forward pass involves two cheap dense GEMMs per layer, BOFT involves a sequence of sparse GEMMs. The paper argues that despite this slight increase in FLOPs per iteration compared to OFT (and potentially LoRA depending on rank settings), the memory savings from storing only $O(d \log d)$ parameters allow for larger batch sizes or models that might otherwise not fit in memory, and crucially, **there is no inference latency** because these learned components are fused into a single weight matrix post-training.

Ultimately, BOFT accepts a modest increase in training complexity—stemming from traversing the depth-$m$ butterfly graph and propagating gradients through it—to unlock a denser representation space than block-diagonal OFT while maintaining far fewer parameters than full orthogonal finetuning. The "hidden cost" is real but bounded by logarithmic growth relative to dimension $d$, whereas parameter storage grows linearly-logarithmically rather than quadratically.
