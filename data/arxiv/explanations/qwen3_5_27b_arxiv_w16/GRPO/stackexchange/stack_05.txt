\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does process supervision calculate advantages across different step lengths in GRPO?
Question:
The paper discusses Process Supervision RL with GRPO in Section 4.2. It states that rewards are given at each step $index(j)$ and normalized across all steps in all outputs in a group. Then, Equation for advantage says: 
$$\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$$

My confusion arises from how this summation works when outputs have different numbers of steps ($K_i$ varies). If one output has 5 steps and another has 10 steps in a group of size $G$, how are they normalized together before summation? Specifically:
1. Does 'mean($\mathbf{R}$)' refer to averaging all step rewards across all outputs regardless of length?
2. When summing rewards for tokens after step $j$, do we assume zero reward for non-existent future steps?
3. If one model generates a very long solution with many small errors and another generates a short correct solution, how does this normalization prevent short solutions from being unfairly penalized due to having fewer positive reward terms?
Answer:
In the DeepSeekMath paper, the normalization of rewards in Process Supervision with GRPO is performed globally across the entire group of sampled outputs before any advantage calculation occurs, which directly addresses your concerns about varying step lengths. Specifically, when the paper states that rewards are normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation of $\mathbf{R}$, the set $\mathbf{R}$ is defined as the collection of **all** individual step rewards from **all** $G$ outputs in the group, regardless of how many steps ($K_i$) each specific output contains. Therefore, to answer your first question: yes, $\text{mean}(\mathbf{R})$ refers to the average of every single step reward across every output in the group, treating a 5-step solution and a 10-step solution as contributing their respective 5 and 10 data points to this global pool.

Regarding your second question about summing rewards for tokens after a certain step, the advantage calculation $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{\text{index}(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)}$ is strictly defined over the existing steps of that specific output $i$. The summation index $j$ iterates only through the steps that actually exist for output $i$ (from $1$ to $K_i$). Consequently, there are no "non-existent future steps" with zero rewards; if a token at position $t$ falls after the final step index $\text{index}(K_i)$, or if there are no steps remaining after $t$, the summation simply yields zero because there are no terms to add. The advantage for a token is effectively the sum of normalized rewards for all reasoning steps that occur at or after that token's position within that specific trajectory.

Finally, your concern about short solutions being penalized due to having fewer positive terms is mitigated by this global normalization strategy. Because every step reward is normalized against the distribution of *all* steps in the group, a short solution with high-quality steps will receive high normalized values ($\widetilde{r}$), while a long solution with many small errors will likely have its individual step rewards pulled toward or below zero after normalization (assuming errors result in lower raw scores). The advantage for a token in a short solution is not penalized by its brevity; rather, it benefits from having fewer opportunities for error accumulation. If a short solution has correct steps throughout, its tokens will accumulate a sum of high positive normalized rewards. Conversely, if a long solution contains many low-scoring intermediate steps, those negative or low-normalized values will reduce the cumulative advantage for earlier tokens in that long trajectory. Thus, the mechanism inherently favors concise correctness over verbose mediocrity without requiring length-based adjustments to the normalization formula itself.
