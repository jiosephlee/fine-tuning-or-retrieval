\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Interpretation of RL boosting Maj@K but not Pass@K: Alignment vs. Capability
Question:
In Section 5.3 ('Why RL Works?'), the authors observe that Reinforcement Learning increases Maj@K (Majority Vote accuracy) but not Pass@K significantly. They conclude: 'the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.'

Can someone elaborate on what this implies for model behavior? If Pass@K remains static, it means if you sample enough times, you still get a correct answer just as often as before SFT+RL. So RL isn't making it *more likely* to stumble upon a solution by chance. Instead, it seems RL forces all sampled trajectories towards a consensus correct path.

Does this suggest that SFT models suffer from 'mode collapse' where they know how to solve it but express it inconsistently, whereas RL acts purely as an alignment mechanism to converge modes rather than expanding knowledge boundaries?
Answer:
Your interpretation is largely correct and aligns precisely with the authors' conclusions in Section 5.3. The observation that Reinforcement Learning (RL) boosts $\text{Maj}@K$ (Majority Vote accuracy) without significantly improving $\text{Pass}@K$ indicates that the model's underlying "capability"—defined here as the probability of generating a correct solution within a fixed sample budget—has not fundamentally expanded. Instead, RL functions primarily as an **alignment mechanism** that reshapes the output distribution to favor consistency.

When $\text{Pass}@K$ remains static, it implies that if you were to sample infinitely many times ($K \to \infty$), the likelihood of stumbling upon a correct trajectory is roughly the same for both the Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) and RL-tuned models. The SFT model possesses the latent knowledge to solve the problem but suffers from what you aptly described as inconsistent expression or "mode collapse," where valid reasoning paths are scattered across a wide probability landscape alongside many hallucinated or incorrect variations. Consequently, while a correct answer exists in the tail of the distribution, it is hard to isolate via simple sampling.

In contrast, RL (specifically GRPO in this paper) does not necessarily teach the model new facts or reasoning steps it didn't already possess; rather, it suppresses incorrect modes and concentrates probability mass onto the single most robust, correct trajectory. By doing so, it ensures that when multiple samples are generated, they overwhelmingly agree on the same correct solution, thereby driving up $\text{Maj}@K$. The authors explicitly attribute this gain to "boosting the correct response from $\text{Top}K$ rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities." This suggests that for mathematical reasoning tasks at this scale, the bottleneck was not a lack of knowledge acquisition during pre-training or SFT, but rather a misalignment where the model failed to consistently prioritize its own internal correct reasoning over plausible-sounding errors. Therefore, RL acts less like an engine for expanding knowledge boundaries and more like a filter that converges multiple potential modes into a single, dominant consensus path.
