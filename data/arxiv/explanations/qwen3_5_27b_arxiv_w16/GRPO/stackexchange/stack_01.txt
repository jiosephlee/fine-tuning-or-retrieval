\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### How does GRPO calculate the advantage function without a learned value network (critic)?
Question:
I'm reading the DeepSeekMath paper and I'm confused about the mechanics of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). In standard PPO, the advantage $A_t$ is calculated using a learned value function $V_{\psi}$ via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). However, the paper states that GRPO 'foregoes the critic model' and instead estimates the baseline from group scores.

Specifically, in Equation 3, the advantage is denoted as $\hat{A}_{i,t}$. The text mentions normalizing rewards by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation. Does this mean $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is simply the normalized reward of the entire output assigned to every token in that output (for outcome supervision)? If so, how does this handle variance reduction compared to GAE, which smooths advantages over time steps? Is there a risk that assigning a single scalar reward to every token in a long chain-of-thought introduces high variance or incorrect gradients for intermediate steps?
Answer:
In Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the advantage function $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ is indeed derived directly from the normalized rewards of the sampled outputs, effectively replacing the learned value function $V_{\psi}$ used in standard PPO. For **outcome supervision**, your intuition is correct: the single scalar reward assigned to a completed output is normalized by subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation, and this resulting value $\widetilde{r}_i$ is assigned as the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ to **every token** $t$ in that specific output sequence $o_i$. Mathematically, this is expressed as $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(\mathbf{r})}{\text{std}(\mathbf{r})}$, where $r_i$ is the reward for the $i$-th output in a group of $G$ outputs generated for the same question.

This approach handles variance reduction differently than Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE). While GAE smooths advantages over time steps using a learned baseline to reduce variance within a single trajectory, GRPO leverages **group-relative normalization** to reduce variance across parallel trajectories. By comparing an output against other outputs generated for the same prompt within a single batch, GRPO creates a local baseline that adapts dynamically to the difficulty of each specific question. This eliminates the need for a separate critic model, which can be unstable or inaccurate when rewards are sparse (e.g., only available at the final token). The paper argues that this group-relative comparison aligns naturally with how reward models are trained (on pairwise comparisons) and significantly reduces memory overhead by removing the critic network entirely.

Regarding your concern about assigning a single scalar reward to every token in a long chain-of-thought: while this does propagate a uniform gradient signal across all tokens in an outcome-supervised setting, it does not necessarily introduce high variance or incorrect gradients because the normalization ensures that only outputs performing better than the group average receive positive advantages, while those below average receive negative ones. This effectively penalizes or reinforces the entire reasoning path based on its final correctness relative to peers. However, to address potential inefficiencies in supervising intermediate steps, GRPO also supports **process supervision**. In this variant, rewards are assigned at each reasoning step rather than just at the end. The advantage for a token at step $t$ is then calculated as the sum of normalized rewards from all subsequent steps ($\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$), providing finer-grained feedback that mitigates the "all-or-nothing" nature of outcome supervision while still avoiding a learned value network.
