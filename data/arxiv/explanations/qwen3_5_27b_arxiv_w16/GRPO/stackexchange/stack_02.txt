\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why does training on arXiv papers seem ineffective for mathematical reasoning according to this paper?
Question:
The paper makes a somewhat counter-intuitive claim in Section 5.2: 'ArXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning.' They tested models trained on MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama and found no notable improvements or even deterioration on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH.

This contradicts my intuition that arXiv contains high-quality mathematical proofs and derivations. The authors mention they removed preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies. Could it be that the remaining text is too dense or formal for a 1.3B or 7B model to learn from without specific scaffolding? Or is it possible that the sheer volume of non-mathematical scientific content (physics, CS theory) dilutes the signal? I'm struggling to understand why such a rich source of formal math would yield negative results compared to web-scraped data.
Answer:
The counter-intuitive finding that arXiv papers are ineffective for improving mathematical reasoning in this study stems from a mismatch between the nature of the data and the specific benchmarks used to evaluate the models, rather than a lack of mathematical content in arXiv itself. As detailed in Section 5.2, the authors conducted experiments training models (DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B) exclusively on arXiv corpora, specifically MathPile (which is over $85\%$ arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama (cleaned LaTeX files). The results showed no notable improvements, and in some cases, a deterioration in performance on benchmarks like GSM8K, MATH, and MMLU-STEM.

The primary reason for this failure is likely the structural and stylistic difference between arXiv papers and the problem-solving tasks found in these benchmarks. ArXiv papers consist largely of formal proofs, theoretical derivations, and dense academic prose intended for human experts, whereas benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH require step-by-step procedural reasoning to solve specific word problems. The paper suggests that simply exposing a model to high-level formal mathematics does not automatically translate to an ability to generate the specific chain-of-thought reasoning required for competition-style problems. Furthermore, even after removing preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies to isolate the mathematical text, the remaining content may be too dense or abstract for smaller models ($1.3$B or $7$B parameters) to distill into actionable reasoning patterns without explicit scaffolding or instruction tuning.

Additionally, the signal-to-noise ratio plays a critical role; while arXiv contains high-quality math proofs, it also includes vast amounts of non-mathematical scientific content (e.g., physics theory or computer science methodology) that may dilute the specific reasoning signals needed for arithmetic and algebraic problem solving. The authors explicitly note that their conclusion is limited by their experimental setup: they have not yet tested whether arXiv data becomes beneficial when combined with other data types (as opposed to training on it exclusively), whether it helps with different tasks like theorem informalization, or if larger model scales might eventually be able to leverage this dense formalism more effectively. Thus, within the context of this paper's specific pre-training regime and evaluation metrics, arXiv appears ineffective compared to their meticulously curated web-scraped corpus which likely contains more diverse and accessible problem-solving examples.
