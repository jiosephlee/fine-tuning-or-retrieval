\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Does Process Supervision simply sum normalized step rewards for token advantages?
Question:
In the 'Process Supervision RL with GRPO' subsection, the authors describe calculating advantages as:
$$ \hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} $$
where $index(j)$ is the end token index of step $j$. My confusion lies in how this handles variable-length steps. If one reasoning step takes 10 tokens and another takes 50 tokens, does every token within that step receive the exact same accumulated advantage value? 

If so, doesn't this dilute the signal for longer steps since they contribute more terms to the sum but spread them over more tokens? Or is there an implicit assumption that all steps are roughly equal length in their dataset?
Answer:
Yes, your interpretation is correct: in the Process Supervision setup described in the paper, every token belonging to a specific reasoning step receives the exact same accumulated advantage value, regardless of how many tokens constitute that step.

According to the "Process Supervision RL with GRPO" subsection, the advantage $\hat{A}_{i, t}$ for any token $t$ is calculated as the sum of normalized rewards $\widetilde{r}_i^{\text{index}(j)}$ for all steps $j$ whose end token index $\text{index}(j)$ occurs at or after position $t$. Since the formula sums rewards based on step completion indices rather than distributing them proportionally across tokens, all tokens generated within a single step (from its start up to its end index) share identical future reward contexts and thus receive the identical advantage value. Consequently, a short step of 10 tokens and a long step of 50 tokens will indeed assign the same scalar advantage to every token within their respective spans if they occur at equivalent positions in the solution sequence relative to subsequent rewards.

This design implies that longer steps do not inherently "dilute" the signal in terms of the magnitude of the gradient received per token; rather, they extend the duration over which that specific reward signal is applied. The intuition here is that if a reasoning step leads to a successful outcome (positive cumulative future reward), every token contributing to that logical unit should be reinforced equally, treating the entire span as a coherent semantic block. Conversely, if a step leads to failure (negative cumulative reward), every token in that span is penalized equally. The paper does not explicitly assume equal step lengths; instead, it relies on this uniform assignment to propagate credit or blame backward from verified checkpoints (the ends of steps) through the entire generation history of those steps. This approach simplifies credit assignment by assuming that within a verified reasoning unit, all intermediate tokens are equally responsible for reaching that state, avoiding the complexity of intra-step weighting which might be difficult to define without finer-grained annotations.
