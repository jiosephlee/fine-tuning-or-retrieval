\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Does RL improve fundamental reasoning capabilities or just align output distributions?
Question:
In Section 5.3 ('Why RL Works?'), the authors analyze Pass@K and Maj@K metrics. They conclude that RL enhances Maj@K performance but not Pass@K, suggesting that 'the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities.'

I'm confused about what this implies for model evaluation. If Pass@K (the probability that at least one sample out of K is correct) doesn't increase significantly, but Maj@K (the probability that the majority vote is correct) does, does this mean RL is just making the model more 'confident' in its existing knowledge rather than learning new reasoning paths? Or does it mean RL is better at suppressing hallucinations so that when you sample multiple times, you get consistent correct answers more often? How should we interpret 'fundamental capabilities' in this context?
Answer:
The distinction between Pass@$K$ and Maj@$K$ in Section 5.3 reveals that Reinforcement Learning (RL) primarily acts as a distributional alignment mechanism rather than a generator of new reasoning capabilities. In this context, "fundamental capabilities" refers to the model's intrinsic ability to discover at least one correct solution path among many attempts, which is measured by Pass@$K$ (the probability that at least one of $K$ samples is correct). Since the authors observe that Pass@$K$ does not significantly increase after RL, it implies that the model's underlying "search space" for solutions has not expanded; the model is not learning new types of reasoning steps or solving problems it previously could not solve even with multiple tries.

Instead, the improvement in Maj@$K$ (the probability that the majority vote of $K$ samples is correct) indicates that RL successfully reshapes the output probability distribution to be more robust and consistent. Before RL, a model might generate a correct solution with low probability alongside many hallucinated or incorrect paths, meaning that while a correct answer exists in the Top-$K$ samples (high Pass@$K$ potential), it is not the dominant mode of the distribution. RL effectively suppresses these incorrect or hallucinated paths and boosts the probability mass of the correct responses already present in the model's latent knowledge. Consequently, when sampling multiple times, the correct answer appears more frequently, allowing majority voting to succeed more often.

This finding suggests that for mathematical reasoning tasks, a significant portion of performance gains from RL comes from "alignment" rather than "discovery." The model learns to be more confident and consistent in its existing knowledge by penalizing deviations from high-reward trajectories (via the GRPO algorithm) and reinforcing successful patterns. Therefore, RL makes the model better at selecting and stabilizing its best known strategies rather than inventing novel ones. This interpretation aligns with the authors' conclusion that RL renders the output distribution more robust by boosting correct responses from the Top-$K$ set, addressing a "misalignment problem" where SFT models know how to solve problems but fail to consistently prioritize those solutions during generation.
