\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

### Why did arXiv data fail to improve mathematical reasoning despite being high-quality?
Question:
The paper makes a surprising claim in Section 5.2: 'arXiv papers seem ineffective in improving mathematical reasoning.' They tested models trained on MathPile (>85% arXiv) and ArXiv-RedPajama and found no notable improvements or even deterioration on GSM8K and MATH.

Intuitively, arXiv papers contain rigorous proofs and complex derivations. Why would training on these degrade performance compared to web-scraped math content (Common Crawl)? Is it possible that arXiv papers are too dense/formal (LaTeX heavy) for a model trained primarily on natural language/code mixtures to parse effectively without explicit formatting handling? Or is it an issue of data noise vs. signal density?
Answer:
The apparent failure of arXiv data to improve mathematical reasoning, despite its reputation for rigor, stems from a mismatch between the **formatting density** of academic papers and the **reasoning patterns** required by modern benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH. According to Section 5.2 of the *DeepSeekMath* paper, training on corpora heavily dominated by arXiv (such as MathPile, which is $>85\%$ arXiv, or ArXiv-RedPajama) resulted in "no notable improvements or even deterioration" across quantitative reasoning and formal mathematics tasks.

This phenomenon occurs because arXiv papers are fundamentally different from the instructional data that drives reasoning capabilities in Large Language Models (LLMs). Academic papers are typically dense with $\LaTeX$-heavy formalism, abstract definitions, and complex derivations that prioritize theoretical completeness over pedagogical clarity. In contrast, benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH consist of distinct problems requiring step-by-step logical deduction (Chain-of-Thought) to reach a solution. The authors suggest that the sheer volume of formal notation and structural overhead in arXiv documents acts as noise rather than signal for a model trying to learn *how* to solve problems sequentially. Instead of learning the causal logic of a solution path, the model may struggle to parse the dense syntax or simply memorize static proofs without internalizing the generative reasoning process.

Conversely, the DeepSeekMath Corpus was constructed by mining Common Crawl using an iterative classification pipeline that prioritized web pages containing solvable mathematical problems and tutorials rather than just research articles. This "web-scraped" data appears to contain a higher density of natural-language explanations, worked examples, and intermediate reasoning steps that align better with the Chain-of-Thought prompting strategy used during evaluation. The paper highlights that while arXiv is valuable for specific tasks like theorem autoformalization (as seen in their miniF2F results), it fails to transfer effectively to general quantitative reasoning when used as a primary pre-training source compared to curated web data. Essentially, the "signal-to-noise ratio" for learning procedural reasoning is lower in raw arXiv dumps than in the diverse, problem-oriented content found on the open web.
