\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Stop Reading ArXiv Papers? What Data Actually Makes an AI Smarter at Math

When building large language models for scientific reasoning, the prevailing wisdom has long been to feed them everything available from academic repositories. Researchers have traditionally assumed that the dense, formal knowledge found in arXiv papers is the gold standard for teaching machines how to think like mathematicians. However, a groundbreaking new study introducing **DeepSeekMath** challenges this assumption head-on. The results suggest that for mathematical reasoning, vast amounts of curated web data might actually be far more effective than the specialized academic corpora we rely on today.

The team behind DeepSeekMath discovered that by meticulously mining public web crawls, they could construct a dataset that outperforms models trained exclusively on academic literature. Their 7-billion parameter model, DeepSeekMath-7B, achieved a staggering 51.7% accuracy on the competition-level MATH benchmark without using external tools or voting techniques. This performance places it on par with massive closed-source giants like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4, despite having a fraction of the parameters and training data volume compared to competitors who relied heavily on arXiv.

## The Surprising Power of Web Data Over Academic Papers

The core revelation of this research lies in the composition of the training data. The researchers constructed the **DeepSeekMath Corpus**, a massive dataset containing 120 billion tokens derived entirely from Common Crawl. They did not simply scrape random web pages; instead, they engineered an iterative pipeline using fastText classifiers to identify and extract high-quality mathematical content from millions of web pages. Through four iterations of refinement and human annotation, they built a dataset nearly seven times larger than what was previously used by models like Minerva and nine times larger than OpenWebMath.

Contrast this with the standard approach seen in many recent studies: training on corpora dominated by arXiv papers, such as MathPile or Proof-Pile-2. In rigorous ablation studies, DeepSeekMath tested whether training on arXiv papers provided any tangible benefit to mathematical reasoning capabilities. The results were counter-intuitive and starkly negative. When models were trained solely on arXiv-derived data—whether cleaned via heuristics or stripped of metadata—they showed no notable improvement across various benchmarks ranging from grade school arithmetic (GSM8K) to college-level competitions (MATH). In some cases, performance even deteriorated compared to baselines that didn't see any arXiv data at all.

This finding suggests that while arXiv papers are invaluable for human researchers seeking deep theoretical insights, they may not be the optimal format for teaching foundation models how to perform step-by-step reasoning. The structured, verbose nature of web discussions, tutorials, and problem-solving forums appears to provide richer contextual patterns for learning logic than the condensed formalism of published papers. Consequently, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B managed to outperform Minerva 540B—a model with 77 times more parameters—solely by leveraging this high-quality web-sourced data rather than academic archives.

## Why Starting with Code Matters

Another critical insight from the paper is the foundational role of code in mathematical reasoning. Before diving into math-specific pre-training, DeepSeekMath initialized its model using **DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5**. This decision was driven by empirical evidence suggesting that code training acts as a powerful catalyst for logical thinking.

The researchers conducted experiments comparing two-stage training (code first, then math) against one-stage mixed training and general pre-training controls. They found that exposure to code significantly boosted a model's ability to solve mathematical problems both with and without tool use (like Python execution). Even before seeing any explicit math tokens, models trained on code demonstrated moderate enhancements in solving quantitative problems. When followed by math-specific training, this combination yielded superior results compared to starting from scratch or using only general natural language data. This supports the hypothesis that syntax-heavy programming languages help internalize the rigid structures necessary for mathematical deduction better than pure prose does initially.

## Introducing GRPO: Reinforcement Learning Without the Overhead

Once the base model was established through pre-training and supervised fine-tuning (SFT), the researchers turned their attention to reinforcement learning (RL) to push performance further. Traditional RL methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) are known to be computationally expensive because they require training a separate "critic" model (a value function) alongside the main policy model to estimate rewards accurately at every token step. This dual-model setup creates significant memory bottlenecks and slows down convergence.

To solve this, DeepSeek introduced **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. This novel algorithm eliminates the need for a separate critic model entirely. Instead of predicting values for individual tokens, GRPO estimates a baseline reward by generating a group of candidate answers ($G$ samples) for a single question and normalizing their scores relative to each other within that group. If an answer performs better than its peers in the group, it receives a positive advantage signal; if it lags behind, it receives a negative one.

By removing the heavy overhead of training a value function network while maintaining stable optimization through group-relative normalization, GRPO drastically reduces memory usage and computational costs despite achieving superior results. Applying GRPO to their instruction-tuned model resulted in significant jumps in performance: GSM8K accuracy rose from 82.9% to 88.2%, and MATH accuracy climbed from 46.8% to 51.7%. Remarkably, these gains occurred even though the RL phase used only a subset of English instruction-tuning data from GSM8K and MATH, proving that efficient RL can generalize well beyond its immediate training distribution without needing massive new datasets or expensive critics.

The implications of these findings extend far beyond just making better math bots first off-the-shelf calculators but versatile reasoning engines capable of handling complex logic across disciplines like physics or law where similar structural rigor is needed? As we move forward toward more capable agents understanding nuance contextually rather than just memorizing facts patterns observed here offer promising avenues exploration ahead!
