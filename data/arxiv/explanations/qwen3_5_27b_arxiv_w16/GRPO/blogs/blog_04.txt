\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# Beyond Right Answers: How RL Teaches AI to Be Consistently Good at Math

Large language models have made remarkable strides in solving mathematical problems, yet a significant gap often remains between a model's potential and its actual performance on benchmarks. The DeepSeekMath paper offers a compelling explanation for this phenomenon, suggesting that the key to unlocking a model's full potential lies not in teaching it new mathematical facts, but in aligning its probability distribution to consistently select the correct path from what it already knows. By introducing a novel reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm called Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and proposing a "Unified Paradigm" for understanding training methods, the researchers demonstrate how RL transforms an inconsistent model into a reliable problem solver.

## The Unified Paradigm: Seeing Training as One Family

To understand why RL is so effective, the DeepSeek team first establishes a unified framework that categorizes various training techniques, including Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and traditional Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In this paradigm, any training method can be viewed as optimizing a policy based on three core components: the data source, the reward function, and the algorithm that processes these signals into gradient updates.

Mathematically, the gradient update for any method can be expressed as an expectation over data samples where the magnitude of the update is determined by a gradient coefficient derived from the reward signal. The crucial distinction lies in how these components interact. Methods like SFT and DPO often rely on "offline" data sampled from an initial model, whereas RL methods like GRPO utilize "online" sampling from the current policy. This shift to online sampling allows the model to explore its own evolving capabilities rather than being constrained by static datasets. The researchers found that online methods significantly outperform offline ones in later training stages because they leverage data that reflects the model's current state of knowledge, allowing for more targeted improvements.

## Why RL Works: Robustness Over New Knowledge

One of the most fascinating findings in the DeepSeekMath study is that reinforcement learning does not necessarily expand a model's fundamental mathematical knowledge base. Instead, it addresses what the authors call a "misalignment problem." When evaluating models using metrics like Pass@K (the probability that at least one of K samples is correct) versus Maj@K (the probability that the majority of K samples are correct), they observed that RL dramatically improves Maj@K without significantly changing Pass@K.

This indicates that before RL, a model might possess the ability to solve a problem correctly but fails to do so consistently; it might generate five wrong answers and one right answer out of six attempts. After RL training, the model's output distribution shifts so that it generates mostly correct answers. In essence, RL teaches the model to be confident and consistent in retrieving its existing knowledge rather than discovering new facts. It stabilizes the distribution of outputs, ensuring that when the model knows how to solve a problem, it actually executes that solution reliably.

## Outcome vs. Process Supervision

The effectiveness of this alignment depends heavily on how rewards are assigned during training. The paper distinguishes between two primary approaches: outcome supervision and process supervision. Outcome supervision provides a single reward signal only at the very end of a solution based on whether the final answer is correct or incorrect. While useful, this approach can be inefficient for complex mathematical tasks where a single error early in the reasoning chain leads to a wrong final answer, yet provides no feedback on which specific step failed.

In contrast, process supervision breaks down reasoning into steps and assigns rewards at each stage using a specialized process reward model. This fine-grained feedback allows the gradient coefficients to penalize specific incorrect steps while reinforcing correct intermediate reasoning paths. The DeepSeekMath experiments showed that GRPO combined with process supervision outperformed outcome supervision alone. By providing step-aware signals, process supervision guides the model through complex logical chains more effectively, preventing it from "hallucinating" through errors just to reach a plausible-looking final answer.

## GRPO: Efficiency Without Sacrificing Performance

A major barrier to applying RL in large-scale language models has been computational cost and memory usage, particularly due to the need for a separate "critic" or value function model as seen in PPO. To address this, DeepSeek introduced Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Unlike PPO, which requires training an additional value network to estimate baselines for advantage calculation, GRPO eliminates this requirement entirely.

Instead of learning a value function, GRPO estimates the baseline by sampling multiple outputs for each question and using their average reward as a reference point within each group. For every question $q$, GRPO generates $G$ outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \dots, o_G\}$ and calculates advantages relative to this group mean. This approach significantly reduces memory consumption and training resources while maintaining high performance gains. In their experiments with DeepSeekMath-7B, applying GRPO boosted performance on competition-level benchmarks like MATH from 46.8% to 51.7% without external toolkits or voting techniques, approaching the capabilities of much larger closed-source models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra.

## Future Directions for Reliable AI Reasoning

The insights from DeepSeekMath point toward several promising avenues for future research in AI reliability and reasoning capabilities. The authors suggest that future improvements will likely come from refining three key areas within their unified paradigm: data sources, algorithms, and reward functions regarding uncertainty and generalization. For instance, moving beyond simple nucleus sampling to advanced tree-search strategies could help models explore more diverse solution paths during training. Additionally, developing algorithms robust against noisy reward signals—acknowledging that even human-annotated datasets contain errors—could lead to more resilient models capable of "weak-to-strong" alignment where weaker supervisors guide stronger policies effectively without being misled by noise in their feedback loops ultimately leading to more trustworthy AI systems capable of handling complex reasoning tasks with high consistency
