\title{Blogs about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

# The Secret Sauce of Math AI: Why Scraping the Web Works Better Than Reading ArXiv

In the world of Large Language Models (LLMs), there is a prevailing assumption that to teach a machine advanced mathematics, you must feed it the highest quality academic literature. Researchers have long relied on datasets like arXiv, believing that curated scientific papers provide the gold standard for mathematical reasoning. However, the recent DeepSeekMath paper challenges this intuition with a surprising finding: training on academic papers alone yields negligible improvements in mathematical reasoning capabilities. Instead, the breakthrough came from a meticulously engineered pipeline that mined 120 billion tokens of "messy" web data from Common Crawl, proving that quantity and diversity often trump curation when it comes to teaching models to solve complex equations.

## The ArXiv Myth: Why Academic Papers Failed to Deliver

DeepSeek's team conducted extensive experiments to isolate the impact of different data sources on mathematical reasoning. They trained models on corpora heavily dominated by arXiv papers, such as MathPile and ArXiv-RedPajama, which consist largely of scientific LaTeX files. Contrary to expectations, these models showed no notable improvement—and in some cases, even deterioration—across various benchmarks ranging from grade-school arithmetic (GSM8K) to competition-level problems (MATH).

This result is counter-intuitive because arXiv papers are written by experts and contain rigorous proofs. However, the paper suggests that these texts may lack the specific structure or diversity needed for an LLM to learn *how* to reason step-by-step in a problem-solving context. The data is often dense with notation and formal proofs but may not provide the conversational or procedural scaffolding necessary for a model to generalize its reasoning skills to new problems. Consequently, relying solely on arXiv appears ineffective for boosting performance on standard mathematical benchmarks.

## Mining Gold from Common Crawl: The Iterative Pipeline

If arXiv wasn't the answer, where did DeepSeek find their success? They turned to Common Crawl, a massive repository of raw web pages that typically contains a lot of noise alongside valuable information. The key was not just scraping everything, but implementing a sophisticated, iterative data selection pipeline designed to filter out the noise and keep only high-quality mathematical content.

The process began with a seed corpus of high-quality math texts from OpenWebMath. Using this seed data, the team trained a fastText classifier to distinguish between mathematical and non-mathematical web pages. This initial classifier was used to scan through 40 billion deduplicated HTML pages from Common Crawl, selecting those with high predicted scores for math content.

However, the team realized that a single pass was insufficient due to the lack of diversity in the initial seed data. They adopted an iterative approach:
1.  **Domain Identification:** After the first pass, they analyzed which domains (base URLs) had yielded significant amounts of math content (e.g., over 10% of pages).
2.  **Human Annotation:** They manually annotated URLs within these identified domains to find specific paths containing mathematical content that were missed by the initial classifier.
3.  **Model Retraining:** These newly discovered positive examples were added back into the training set to retrain and improve the fastText classifier.
4.  **Repetition:** This cycle was repeated four times until diminishing returns set in (with nearly 98% overlap between iterations three and four).

The result was the DeepSeekMath Corpus: a dataset of 120 billion tokens derived from 35.5 million web pages. This corpus is significantly larger than previous math datasets—almost seven times larger than Minerva's dataset and nine times larger than OpenWebMath—and covers multiple languages, including English and Chinese.

## Why Web Data Beats Curated Literature

The success of this approach highlights several critical advantages of using filtered web data over curated academic papers for training LLMs:

*   **Diversity and Scale:** The web contains a vast array of mathematical explanations, ranging from forum discussions and homework help sites to educational blogs and online courses. This diversity exposes the model to different ways of explaining concepts and solving problems, which is crucial for developing robust reasoning skills. In contrast, arXiv papers often follow a rigid formal structure that may not translate well into generalizable reasoning patterns for an LLM.
*   **Multilingual Capabilities:** Because Common Crawl is global, the resulting corpus naturally includes high-quality math content in languages other than English. DeepSeek observed significant improvements in Chinese mathematical benchmarks (such as CMATH), whereas English-centric corpora like those based on arXiv failed to improve performance in non-English languages and sometimes hindered it.
*   **Learning Curve Efficiency:** Experiments showed that models trained on the DeepSeekMath Corpus exhibited steeper learning curves compared to those trained on smaller baselines like Proof-Pile-2 or MathPile. While smaller datasets cause model performance to plateau quickly due to repetition during training runs, the massive scale of the 120B token corpus allowed for sustained improvements throughout training.

## Beyond Data: Code Training and GRPO

While data selection was pivotal, DeepSeekMath also leveraged two other key strategies that contributed to its state-of-the-art performance on open-source models: code training and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

The team found that initializing their model with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 (a model pre-trained on code) provided a significant boost over starting with a general language model. Their experiments confirmed that code training improves mathematical reasoning both with and without tool use, suggesting that learning programming logic enhances general algorithmic thinking required for math problems.

Furthermore, after supervised fine-tuning, they applied GRPO, a novel reinforcement learning algorithm designed specifically for LLMs unlike Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), GRPO eliminates the need for a separate critic model (value function). Instead, it estimates the baseline advantage by comparing rewards within a group of sampled outputs for the same question ($G$ samples). This approach significantly reduces memory usage while effectively optimizing policy updates using relative rewards rather than absolute values from an external critic model. By applying GRPO solely on instruction-tuning data subsets related to GSM8K and MATH, they pushed their 7B parameter model's accuracy on MATH from 46.8% to 51.7%, approaching the performance levels of much larger closed-source models like GPT-4 without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques during inference (though self-consistency over 64 samples pushes this even higher).

## Conclusion: A New Direction for Math AI

The DeepSeekMath study offers a paradigm shift in how we approach pre-training language models for specialized domains like mathematics. It demonstrates that high-quality reasoning capabilities can be cultivated not just by feeding models "perfect" academic texts, but by intelligently mining vast amounts of diverse web data through iterative filtering pipelines combined with strategic code pre-training and efficient reinforcement learning algorithms like GRPO.

For researchers and practitioners looking to build better reasoning models, the lesson is clear: do not underestimate the potential of publicly available web data when paired with rigorous engineering pipelines sometimes what looks like "noise" is actually just untapped signal waiting to be discovered
