# Chapter 1: The Challenge of Mathematical Reasoning in LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) have fundamentally transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence, particularly in the domain of mathematical reasoning. This field presents a unique set of challenges due to the complex, structured, and multi-step nature of mathematical problems, which require precise logical deduction rather than mere pattern matching. While cutting-edge closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in solving quantitative and geometric problems, a significant performance gap persists between these proprietary systems and the currently available open-source models. The latter often trail behind in their ability to handle competition-level mathematics or complex reasoning tasks without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques. This disparity highlights a critical need for domain-specific language models that can bridge the divide between general-purpose conversational abilities and rigorous mathematical problem-solving.

The DeepSeekMath project was initiated to address this specific gap by pushing the limits of mathematical reasoning within open language models. The primary goal is to develop a model that not only outperforms existing open-source counterparts but also approaches the performance levels of state-of-the-art closed models like GPT-4 on academic benchmarks. To achieve this, the project focuses on two pivotal strategies: first, harnessing the vast potential of publicly available web data through a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline to create a high-quality pre-training corpus; and second, introducing novel reinforcement learning techniques to refine reasoning capabilities. By continuing pre-training on 120 billion math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl and employing advanced optimization algorithms, DeepSeekMath aims to demonstrate that smaller models, when trained on superior data and optimized effectively, can rival much larger systems in mathematical proficiency.

## The state of mathematical reasoning in AI: From GPT-4 to Open Source

The current state of mathematical reasoning in artificial intelligence is characterized by a sharp dichotomy between closed-source giants and open-source alternatives. Models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have revolutionized the field, achieving high scores on quantitative reasoning benchmarks such as MATH and geometry reasoning benchmarks. These models have proven instrumental not only in solving complex mathematical problems but also in assisting humans with intricate derivations that were previously beyond the reach of automated systems. However, these powerful models are not publicly available, restricting their accessibility for research and broader application. Consequently, researchers and developers must rely on open-source models, which historically have considerably trailed behind their closed-source counterparts in performance.

This performance gap is particularly evident when evaluating models on competition-level datasets. While closed-source models can achieve high accuracy through sophisticated internal mechanisms and massive parameter counts, open-source models often struggle with the same tasks unless they employ external toolkits or ensemble voting techniques like self-consistency over multiple samples. For instance, without such auxiliary methods, many open-source models fail to match the reasoning depth required for high-school or college-level mathematics. The DeepSeekMath project posits that this gap is not insurmountable and that it stems largely from data quality and training methodology rather than just model size. By initializing their model with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B—a model already trained on code—and continuing pre-training with a massive corpus of math-specific tokens, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that a 7B parameter model can achieve an impressive score of 51.7% on the MATH benchmark without external tools. This achievement places it near the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4, suggesting that strategic data curation and algorithmic innovation can compensate for smaller parameter counts in domain-specific tasks.

Furthermore, the trajectory of open-source development suggests that code training plays a pivotal role in enhancing mathematical reasoning capabilities prior to specialized math training. The observation that starting from a code-trained base model yields better results than starting from a general LLM indicates an intrinsic link between programming logic and mathematical deduction. This synergy allows open-source models to leverage the structured nature of code to improve their general reasoning abilities, extending benefits beyond mathematics to other domains like natural language understanding and general knowledge benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH. Thus, while closed-source models currently lead the pack, the rapid advancements in open-source architectures like DeepSeekMath are narrowing the divide by focusing on high-quality data pipelines and efficient reinforcement learning strategies tailored specifically for mathematical domains.

## Key Benchmarks: MATH, GSM8K, and their significance

To rigorously evaluate the mathematical capabilities of language models, researchers rely on standardized benchmarks that span various levels of complexity and problem types. Two of the most prominent benchmarks utilized in this field are GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) and MATH (Merely Another Test for Human-level Reasoning). GSM8K consists of grade-school level math word problems designed to test basic quantitative reasoning skills. It serves as a foundational benchmark for assessing whether a model can understand simple arithmetic operations within natural language contexts and perform multi-step calculations correctly. High performance on GSM8K is often considered a baseline requirement for any model claiming proficiency in mathematical reasoning; however, success here does not necessarily guarantee competence in more advanced mathematics due to its relatively low complexity ceiling compared to competition-level problems.

In contrast, the MATH benchmark represents a significantly higher tier of difficulty, comprising competition-level problems drawn from sources such as AMC (American Mathematics Competitions) competitions ranging from grade school to college level complexity. These problems cover diverse fields including algebra, number theory, geometry, probability, calculus, and combinatorics. Unlike GSM8K problems which often have straightforward solution paths once understood, MATH problems require deep logical insight, creative problem-solving strategies, and rigorous step-by-step deduction without relying on brute-force computation alone. Achieving high accuracy on MATH is considered a major milestone because it indicates that a model possesses genuine reasoning capabilities rather than just memorized patterns or superficial statistical correlations within its training data. In the context of DeepSeekMath, reaching an accuracy of over 50% on MATH without external tools is highlighted as a significant breakthrough for open-source models within this parameter range.

Beyond English benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, comprehensive evaluation also extends to Chinese mathematical datasets such as CMATH (Chinese Math) and MGSM-zh (Multilingual Grade School Math), as well as formal mathematics tasks using miniF2F with Isabelle proof assistants. These additional benchmarks are crucial for assessing multilingual capabilities and formal theorem proving skills respectively. The significance of these benchmarks lies not only in providing quantitative metrics but also in revealing specific strengths and weaknesses across different types of reasoning tasks—for instance distinguishing between tool-integrated reasoning where Python execution is allowed versus self-contained text solutions where no external computation is permitted. By performing well across this spectrum—from elementary arithmetic in GSM8K to Olympiad-level challenges in MATH—models like DeepSeekMath demonstrate robustness across varying degrees of difficulty # Chapter 2: Constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus

The foundation of DeepSeekMath's superior performance lies not merely in its architectural design or reinforcement learning strategies, but in the meticulous construction of its pre-training corpus. Mathematical reasoning poses a unique challenge for language models due to its complex, structured, and often sparse nature within the general web. To address this, the researchers developed a scalable data engineering pipeline designed to extract high-quality mathematical content from the vast and unstructured Common Crawl (CC). This chapter details the methodology used to create the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a dataset comprising 120 billion math-related tokens. We will explore how a fastText-based classifier was iteratively refined to mine this data, how seed corpora were expanded through domain identification, and how rigorous decontamination techniques ensured the integrity of benchmark evaluations. Finally, we will analyze the comparative advantages of this corpus over existing datasets like MathPile and OpenWebMath, highlighting the critical role of multilingual data in enhancing reasoning capabilities across different languages.

# Data Sources: Leveraging Common Crawl for Math Content

The primary source for the DeepSeekMath Corpus is Common Crawl (CC), a massive repository of web crawl data that serves as a foundational resource for training large language models. While CC contains a vast amount of text, mathematical content is relatively sparse and often intermingled with low-quality or irrelevant material. The central hypothesis driving this work is that publicly available web data contains significant untapped potential for mathematical reasoning if one can effectively filter for high-quality instances. Unlike previous approaches that relied heavily on curated academic repositories like arXiv or limited datasets such as OpenWebMath, this approach seeks to scale up by systematically mining CC.

To manage the sheer volume of data in Common Crawl, the pipeline first employs URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication techniques. This preprocessing step reduces the original dataset to approximately 40 billion HTML web pages, creating a more manageable pool from which to extract mathematical content. The goal is not simply to increase the quantity of tokens but to ensure that every token included contributes meaningfully to the model's ability to understand and solve mathematical problems. By focusing on CC rather than restricting training to specific academic sources, the researchers aim to capture a broader diversity of mathematical expressions, explanations, and problem-solving strategies found across educational websites, forums, and technical blogs. This strategy is predicated on the belief that a larger volume of high-quality data can compensate for or even surpass the benefits of larger model parameters, as evidenced by DeepSeekMath-Base 7B outperforming Minerva 540B despite having significantly fewer parameters.

# Iterative Data Selection Pipeline using fastText Classifiers

The core mechanism for extracting mathematical content from Common Crawl is an iterative data selection pipeline driven by a fastText-based classifier. This process begins with an initial seed corpus of high-quality mathematical texts, specifically OpenWebMath, which serves as the ground truth for training the classifier. In this first iteration, 500,000 instances from OpenWebMath are selected as positive training examples. To teach the model what *not* to select, another 500,000 web pages are randomly sampled from Common Crawl to serve as negative examples. The fastText model is then trained with specific hyperparameters: a vector dimension of 256, a learning rate of 0.1, a maximum word n-gram length of 3, a minimum word occurrence threshold of 3, and three training epochs.

Once trained on this initial set, the classifier is applied to the deduplicated Common Crawl dataset to score and rank all web pages based on their likelihood of containing mathematical content. The system does not simply accept all pages above a certain threshold; instead, it ranks them by their predicted scores and preserves only the top-ranking pages. To determine the optimal volume of data for pre-training without introducing noise from lower-quality pages at the tail end of the distribution, experiments were conducted using subsets corresponding to the top 40 billion, 80 billion, 120 billion, and 160 billion tokens. In this initial phase, the top 40 billion tokens were selected for further processing. This iterative approach allows for a dynamic refinement of data quality; by starting with a conservative selection based on an initial classifier trained on limited positive examples, the system ensures that only high-confidence mathematical content enters the training set in early stages while leaving room for expansion in subsequent iterations as the classifier improves.

# Seed Corpus Construction and Domain Identification

A critical insight in this pipeline is that relying solely on an initial seed corpus like OpenWebMath limits diversity because it may not cover all types of mathematical web pages present in Common Crawl. To overcome this limitation and optimize the fastText model's recall capabilities in subsequent iterations, researchers implemented a strategy focused on domain identification and manual annotation. After collecting data in an iteration where over 10% of web pages within specific domains were identified as math-related (e.g., mathoverflow.net), these domains are flagged for deeper exploration.

The process involves organizing Common Crawl into disjoint domains defined by shared base URLs. For each domain where more than 10% of pages were collected in previous iterations, researchers manually annotate specific URLs associated with high-quality mathematical content within those domains (for example, distinguishing between general forum discussions and specific question-answer threads). Web pages linked to these newly annotated URLs that were not previously collected are then added back into the seed corpus as new positive examples. This enriched seed corpus is used to retrain and update the fastText classifier for the next iteration. This cycle was repeated four times in total. By continuously feeding new positive examples derived from diverse domains back into the training loop significantly improved its ability to recall previously missed mathematical content across different types of websites. By the fourth iteration, nearly 98% of collectible data had already been gathered in previous steps leading researchers decide cease collection at that point resulting final dataset consisting approximately $35$ million mathematical web pages totaling $12$ billion tokens which forms basis DeepSeekMath Corpus used continue pre-training models such as DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v15 $7$B alongside natural language code data sources including GitHub AlgebraicStack arXiv papers ensuring comprehensive coverage various aspects mathematics while maintaining high quality throughout entire process

# Chapter 3: Pre-Training Strategies and Base Model Development

The development of DeepSeekMath-Base represents a pivotal shift in how large language models are trained for mathematical reasoning, moving away from the assumption that sheer parameter count or the inclusion of academic papers is the primary driver of performance. Instead, this chapter details a strategy centered on high-quality, domain-specific data curation and the strategic initialization of models with code capabilities. The core hypothesis driving this work is that mathematical reasoning is not an isolated skill but one deeply intertwined with logical structuring found in programming. By initializing a model with strong coding foundations and continuing pre-training on a meticulously filtered corpus of web-derived mathematical content, DeepSeekMath-Base achieves performance that rivals or surpasses significantly larger closed-source models. This approach challenges conventional wisdom regarding the utility of arXiv papers for general reasoning tasks and establishes a new baseline for open-source mathematical models. The following sections will dissect the initialization choices, the comparative dynamics of training strategies, the surprising limitations of academic corpora, and the comprehensive evaluation of the resulting base model across diverse benchmarks.

# Initialization from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B

The architectural foundation of DeepSeekMath-Base is not built from a generic pre-trained language model but is initialized specifically from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B. This deliberate choice stems from empirical observations suggesting that starting from a model already trained on extensive code data provides a superior starting point for mathematical reasoning compared to general-purpose language models. The intuition behind this decision lies in the structural similarities between code and mathematics; both domains require rigorous logical consistency, step-by-step derivation, and precise syntax to arrive at a correct solution. Code training implicitly teaches the model to decompose complex problems into executable steps, a skill that directly translates to solving mathematical word problems where intermediate reasoning steps are crucial.

By leveraging DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B as the starting point, the researchers aim to capitalize on the "code reasoning" capabilities already embedded in the model's weights before introducing specific mathematical data. This initialization strategy serves as a control mechanism to isolate the benefits of code pre-training from other factors. If a model initialized with code outperforms one initialized with general text after identical math training, it provides strong evidence that code exposure enhances logical reasoning abilities. The DeepSeekMath-Base 7B model undergoes continued pre-training for 500 billion tokens following this initialization. The data distribution for this phase is carefully balanced: 56% consists of the newly curated DeepSeekMath Corpus, 20% is GitHub code to maintain coding proficiency, 10% is natural language data from Common Crawl in English and Chinese, 10% comes from arXiv papers, and 4% is derived from AlgebraicStack (mathematical code). This mixture ensures that while the model specializes in mathematics, it does not suffer from catastrophic forgetting regarding its coding or general language capabilities.

The decision to use a 7B parameter model initialized with code also addresses efficiency concerns without sacrificing performance. Historically, state-of-the-art performance in mathematics was often associated with massive parameter counts, such as Minerva's 540B parameters. However, by combining a smaller parameter count with high-quality domain-specific pre-training and a strong code-based initialization, DeepSeekMath-Base demonstrates that data quality and architectural suitability can compensate for scale. This approach allows for more accessible open-source development while pushing performance boundaries previously thought to be reserved for proprietary giants like GPT-4 or Gemini-Ultra. The initialization process effectively primes the neural network to treat mathematical problems as structured logic puzzles rather than mere text generation tasks, setting the stage for significant gains during subsequent fine-tuning stages.

# The role of code training in mathematical reasoning (Code vs. General tokens)

One of the most significant contributions of this research is providing empirical evidence regarding the long-standing hypothesis: does code training improve reasoning abilities? While many researchers have speculated that exposure to programming languages enhances logical deduction, DeepSeekMath offers concrete experimental validation specifically within the domain of mathematics. To investigate this, experiments were conducted comparing models trained on code tokens versus those trained on general natural language tokens prior to math-specific training. The results indicate that code training provides a distinct advantage over general text training when it comes to both tool-augmented reasoning (using Python) and self-contained text-based reasoning (Chain-of-Thought).

The mechanism behind this improvement appears to be related to how models learn to structure their internal thought processes. Code requires explicit variable definitions, function calls, and logical flow control (if-else statements), which forces the model to learn precise causal relationships between inputs and outputs. When these skills are transferred to mathematics, the model becomes better at breaking down word problems into solvable sub-problems and verifying intermediate steps logically rather than relying on pattern matching alone. In experiments where DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B was trained on 400 billion tokens of either code or general text followed by 150 billion tokens of math data, the code-initialized model consistently outperformed its general-text counterpart on benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH when using Python tools for calculation. This suggests that syntax-driven logic learned from programming creates a robust scaffold for quantitative reasoning that general text cannot provide as effectively.

Furthermore, even when external tools are not used, meaning the model must generate all reasoning steps in natural language using Chain-of-Thought prompting, prior exposure to code still yields moderate enhancements in performance during two-stage training scenarios. This implies that code training improves the fundamental "reasoning engine" of the model, not just its ability to write executable scripts. However, it is important to note that this benefit is most pronounced when code training precedes math training or when they are mixed carefully; simply adding math data without this logical foundation results in lower ceilings for performance improvement. The findings suggest that for any future large language models aiming for high-level mathematical competence, incorporating substantial amounts of high-quality code data into the pre-training phase should be considered a prerequisite rather than an optional enhancement.

# Training dynamics: Two-stage vs One-stage training approaches

To fully understand how best to integrate code and math data into a single model architecture, researchers explored two distinct training dynamics: two-stage sequential training versus one-stage mixed training. In the two-stage approach, the model undergoes an initial phase dedicated entirely to one domain (e.g., 400 billion tokens of code) followed by a second phase dedicated exclusively to another (e.g., 150 billion tokens of math). In contrast, one-stage training involves mixing tokens from both domains simultaneously throughout a single continuous training run (e.g., mixing 400 billion code tokens with 150 billion math tokens). These experiments revealed nuanced trade-offs regarding performance optimization and catastrophic forgetting across different types of tasks.

In scenarios involving program-aided mathematical reasoning (where models write Python scripts), both two-stage and one-stage approaches showed benefits from including code tokens compared to pure math training or pure general text training under two-stage settings specifically enhanced programmatic problem solving significantly more than general text did in two-stage settings). However, one-stage mixing proved particularly effective at mitigating catastrophic forgetting—a phenomenon where learning new information causes the loss of previously learned skills—in coding tasks often observed after specialized math fine-tuning in two-stage setups). # Chapter 4: Supervised Fine-Tuning for Instruction Alignment

The transition from a raw, pre-trained language model to a specialized assistant capable of solving complex mathematical problems requires a critical intermediate step known as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). While the DeepSeekMath-Base 7B model, introduced in the previous chapter, possesses strong foundational reasoning capabilities derived from its extensive pre-training on the DeepSeekMath Corpus and code data, it lacks the specific alignment required to follow user instructions and generate structured solutions. This chapter details the construction of the DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B model, focusing on the meticulous curation of instruction datasets, the adoption of diverse reasoning formats, and the evaluation of the resulting model against both proprietary giants and open-source peers. The primary objective of this stage is to teach the model not just to "know" mathematics, but to "communicate" its reasoning process effectively, thereby laying the necessary groundwork for the subsequent reinforcement learning phase.

## Curating instruction tuning datasets: English and Chinese problems

The foundation of an effective instruction-tuned model lies in the quality and diversity of its training data. For DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, the research team constructed a comprehensive mathematical instruction-tuning dataset comprising 776,000 training examples. This dataset was designed to cover a broad spectrum of mathematical fields and varying levels of complexity, ensuring that the model is exposed to problems ranging from elementary arithmetic to advanced college-level concepts. A distinctive feature of this curation process is its explicit multilingual focus, addressing both English and Chinese mathematical problems. This approach contrasts with many prior works that relied heavily on English-only data, allowing DeepSeekMath to achieve superior performance on Chinese benchmarks such as CMATH and Gaokao-MathQA.

For the English component of the dataset, the researchers annotated problems from well-known benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH with solutions that integrate tool use. Additionally, they incorporated a subset of the MathInstruct dataset and utilized the training set from Lila-OOD. These sources were selected because they provide high-quality examples where problems are solved using either Chain-of-Thought (CoT) or Program-of-Thought (PoT) reasoning strategies. The resulting English collection spans diverse mathematical domains including algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry. This breadth ensures that the model learns to handle different types of logical structures and problem-solving heuristics inherent in various branches of mathematics.

The Chinese component was curated with equal rigor to support non-English mathematical reasoning. The team collected K-12 level mathematical problems spanning 76 distinct sub-topics, such as linear equations. Crucially, these Chinese problems were annotated with solutions in two specific formats: Chain-of-Thought and Tool-Integrated Reasoning. By including these diverse formats in both languages, the dataset ensures that DeepSeekMath-Instruct learns to articulate its reasoning steps clearly in natural language while also knowing when and how to leverage computational tools for verification or complex calculation. This dual-language strategy not only broadens the model's applicability but also demonstrates that high-quality mathematical data exists beyond English-centric sources like arXiv or Western educational repositories.

## Data formats: Chain-of-Thought, Program-of-Thought, and Tool-Integrated Reasoning (ToRA)

To maximize the reasoning capabilities of DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, the instruction tuning data was structured around three distinct problem-solving paradigms: Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and Tool-Integrated Reasoning (ToRA). Each format serves a unique purpose in guiding the model toward accurate solutions while maintaining transparency in its logical process. Chain-of-Thought prompting involves generating a sequence of intermediate natural language steps that lead to a final answer. This format encourages the model to break down complex problems into manageable sub-problems, mimicking human deductive reasoning processes where intermediate conclusions are explicitly stated before reaching a final result.

Program-of-Thought represents a shift from purely linguistic reasoning to executable code generation. In this format, instead of writing out every step in natural language, the model is trained to write Python programs that solve the problem at hand. These programs can utilize standard libraries such as `math` for basic operations or `sympy` for symbolic algebra and calculus. The solution is then derived by executing this code rather than relying solely on the model's internal arithmetic capabilities. This approach leverages Python's precision for computation while allowing the model to focus on translating word problems into algorithmic logic. The inclusion of PoT data is particularly significant given that DeepSeekMath was initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5; it capitalizes on the model's inherent coding strengths to solve mathematical tasks more reliably than pure text generation might allow for intricate calculations.

Tool-Integrated Reasoning (ToRA) combines elements of natural language explanation with program execution within a single coherent response flow. In this format, models are prompted to interleave natural language reasoning steps with code snippets that perform specific calculations or verifications before proceeding to the next logical step in their explanation. This hybrid approach allows for a more dynamic interaction where the model can reason about a concept in text, execute code to verify a hypothesis or perform a heavy computation, interpret those results back into text, and then continue its reasoning chain. By training on ToRA data alongside CoT and PoT examples, DeepSeekMath-Instruct learns flexibility; it can choose whether to rely entirely on internal logic (CoT), offload computation entirely (PoT), or use tools selectively within a broader narrative (ToRA). This versatility is crucial for handling real-world mathematical queries where different strategies may be optimal depending on problem complexity.
