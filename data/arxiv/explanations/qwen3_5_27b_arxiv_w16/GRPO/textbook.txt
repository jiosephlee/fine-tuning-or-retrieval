# Chapter 1: Introduction to Mathematical Reasoning in Large Language Models

## The Challenge of Structured Mathematical Reasoning

Mathematical reasoning represents one of the most formidable frontiers for large language models (LLMs), presenting unique difficulties distinct from general natural language processing tasks. Unlike conversational dialogue or creative writing, which rely heavily on probabilistic associations and contextual fluency, mathematical problems demand rigorous logical consistency, precise symbolic manipulation, and adherence to strict structural constraints. A single error in a multi-step deduction can invalidate an entire solution path, requiring the model to maintain a coherent internal state over long sequences of generation. This inherent complexity means that simply scaling up model parameters or exposure to generic internet text is insufficient to master the discipline. The challenge lies not merely in recognizing mathematical terms but in executing the procedural logic required to transform a problem statement into a verified solution. Consequently, achieving proficiency in this domain requires specialized training regimes that go beyond standard next-token prediction, necessitating data that explicitly encodes the structure of mathematical thought and optimization algorithms capable of refining the model's decision-making boundaries.

The difficulty is compounded by the fact that mathematical knowledge is hierarchical and cumulative; understanding advanced concepts relies fundamentally on mastering foundational principles. Standard pre-training often exposes models to fragmented snippets of mathematical discourse found in forums or articles, which may lack the step-by-step rigor necessary for deep comprehension. Without explicit guidance on how intermediate steps connect to final conclusions, models tend to hallucinate plausible-sounding but logically flawed derivations. Addressing this requires moving away from passive absorption of text toward active engagement with the mechanics of problem-solving. The goal is to cultivate an internal representation where the model understands not just *what* the answer is, but *how* to derive it reliably through valid operations, mirroring the deductive processes employed by human mathematicians.

## Current State of Open vs. Closed Source Models (GPT-4, Gemini vs. Open Alternatives)

Despite rapid advancements in artificial intelligence, a significant disparity persists between closed-source proprietary models and openly available alternatives regarding mathematical capabilities. Cutting-edge systems such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have demonstrated remarkable prowess in handling complex quantitative and geometric reasoning tasks, serving as the gold standard for performance on academic benchmarks. These models benefit from massive compute budgets, access to vast amounts of private high-quality data, and sophisticated engineering pipelines that are not disclosed to the public. Their dominance highlights what is theoretically possible when resource constraints are minimized; however, their opacity prevents researchers from dissecting their methodologies or replicating their successes independently. This creates a barrier where open-source communities lag significantly behind, limiting the accessibility of state-of-the-art reasoning tools for broader scientific inquiry and educational applications.

Currently accessible open-source models generally fall short when tasked with competition-level mathematics, often struggling with problems that require multi-hop reasoning or nuanced interpretation of conditions prior attempts have yielded modest results compared to their closed counterparts. While some open models have incorporated code-generation capabilities or attempted fine-tuning on curated datasets like GSM8K or MATH, they frequently fail to generalize well outside their immediate training distributions or reach parity with giants like GPT-4 without resorting to computationally expensive techniques such as ensemble voting or external tool reliance at inference time. The DeepSeekMath project emerges directly from this observation aiming to close this gap by developing an open architecture that rivals closed-system performance through targeted innovations rather than sheer brute force scaling alone. By leveraging publicly available web data more effectively and introducing novel reinforcement learning strategies, it seeks to prove that high-performance mathematical reasoning does not exclusively belong to proprietary ecosystems but can be achieved within the open-source paradigm through meticulous design choices regarding data quality and training dynamics.

Chapter 2: Constructing High-Quality Mathematical Corpora

The advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) in mathematical reasoning has historically been constrained by the scarcity and fragmentation of high-quality training data. Early efforts relied heavily on curated datasets like Minerva, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile, which, while foundational, exhibited significant limitations in terms of scale, linguistic diversity, and data composition. Minerva, despite achieving strong performance with a massive 540-billion parameter model, relied on a mathematical web corpus that was roughly seven times smaller than what became possible with newer extraction techniques. Similarly, OpenWebMath offered a filtered view of Common Crawl totaling approximately 13.6 billion tokens, representing only a fraction of the available mathematical knowledge on the web. Proof-Pile attempted to broaden this horizon by aggregating data from textbooks, Wikipedia, and arXiv papers; however, its heavy reliance on arXiv—comprising over 85% of its content—and its specific mix of code and text resulted in a dataset that did not necessarily translate to superior reasoning capabilities for smaller models. Experimental validation revealed that simply scaling up arXiv inclusion did not yield proportional gains in mathematical reasoning benchmarks; in fact, models trained exclusively on arXiv corpora sometimes showed no improvement or even deterioration in performance compared to baselines. These observations highlighted a critical gap: the existence of vast amounts of untapped mathematical knowledge scattered across public web pages that traditional static scraping methods failed to capture effectively. Consequently, the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus was driven by the necessity to move beyond these established limits by leveraging a dynamic, iterative mining strategy capable of extracting high-quality data at an unprecedented scale.

The core innovation in constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus lies in an iterative mining pipeline designed to progressively expand the pool of known mathematical web pages through active learning principles. The process begins with a "seed corpus," initially populated by OpenWebMath, which serves as the ground truth for identifying positive examples. Using this seed set of 500,000 positive instances and an equal number of random negative instances drawn from Common Crawl, researchers trained a fastText-based binary classifier configured with specific hyperparameters such as a vector dimension of 256 and a maximum word n-gram length of 3. This initial classifier acts as a sieve applied to a deduplicated version of Common Crawl reduced to approximately 40 billion HTML pages through URL-based deduplication techniques. The classifier assigns confidence scores to each page, allowing the team to rank candidates and retain only the highest-scoring entries for the first iteration. However, recognizing that an initial classifier trained on a limited seed might miss diverse formats or niche mathematical communities due to insufficient representation in the training set, the pipeline employs an adaptive refinement loop. Instead of stopping after one pass, the system identifies domains where collection rates were surprisingly high (defined as domains where over 10% of pages were flagged as math-related) and targets them for deeper inspection. This recursive approach ensures that as more positive examples are discovered and verified, the classifier itself becomes more sophisticated and capable of retrieving increasingly obscure or specialized mathematical content in subsequent passes.

Following the initial broad sweep by the fastText classifier, the pipeline shifts toward targeted domain identification and manual annotation to resolve ambiguities and enrich diversity. Once domains with high concentrations of previously collected pages are identified—for instance, sites like mathoverflow.net—the research team performs granular analysis by organizing Common Crawl into disjoint domains based on shared base URLs. Within these promising domains, human annotators manually inspect URLs associated with specific sections known to host high-value mathematical discussions or problems (such as question threads). Pages linked to these manually vetted URLs but missed by the automated classifier are harvested and added back into the seed corpus as new positive examples. This hybrid approach combines statistical pattern recognition with human expertise to overcome the blind spots inherent in purely algorithmic filtering. Over four distinct iterations of this cycle—scanning classifying refining—the cumulative effort yielded approximately 35 million unique mathematical web pages amounting to 120 billion tokens. The diminishing returns observed in later stages confirmed the efficacy of this method; by the fourth iteration, nearly 98% of retrievable data had already been captured in previous rounds signaling saturation and justifying cessation of collection. Crucially, unlike earlier corpora dominated almost entirely by English content derived from Western academic repositories or forums like StackExchange, this iterative process naturally uncovered rich multilingual resources significantly enhancing coverage for Chinese mathematics alongside English text thereby addressing a major shortcoming in prior datasets which often degraded non-English reasoning capabilities when used for training monolingual-heavy models

# Chapter 3: Foundations of DeepSeekMath-Base

This chapter delves into the architectural and data-driven foundations of DeepSeekMath-Base, a specialized language model designed to push the boundaries of mathematical reasoning in open-source systems. Unlike generic large language models that rely solely on broad internet scraping, DeepSeekMath-Base represents a strategic synthesis of high-quality mathematical data, code proficiency, and targeted pre-training methodologies. The core premise driving this foundation is that mathematical reasoning requires a distinct type of structural understanding—one that is naturally cultivated through exposure to formal logic found in computer code. Consequently, the construction of this model begins not with a general-purpose backbone, but with a deliberate initialization strategy leveraging DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5. This decision posits that the syntactic rigor and logical flow inherent in programming languages provide a superior substrate for acquiring mathematical competencies compared to general natural language corpora alone. Throughout this chapter, we will examine the meticulous curation of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, the nuanced balance between code and mathematical tokens during training, and the surprising empirical findings regarding the utility of traditional academic repositories like arXiv. Finally, we will assess the resultant model's performance across diverse linguistic landscapes, demonstrating how a 7-billion parameter model can rival or exceed significantly larger predecessors through optimized data engineering rather than sheer scale expansion.

# Initialization Strategy: Why Start with DeepSeek-Coder?

The genesis of DeepSeekMath lies in a critical design choice regarding its initialization architecture: selecting DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B as the starting point rather than a general-purpose large language model (LLM). This decision was driven by an observation that models initially trained on vast amounts of code possess latent structures conducive to logical deduction and symbolic manipulation—skills that are paramount for solving complex mathematical problems. In the landscape of modern AI, code serves as a highly compressed representation of logic; every variable assignment, loop structure, and function call embodies a precise causal relationship devoid of the ambiguities often found in natural language discourse. By initializing with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5, researchers hypothesized that embedding this "logical grammar" into the model's weights before introducing explicit mathematical content would accelerate convergence toward robust reasoning capabilities. Empirical validation supported this hypothesis immediately; experiments revealed that starting from a code-specialized model yielded superior outcomes compared to initiating from a general LLM. The rationale extends beyond mere syntax; it touches upon the cognitive scaffolding required for multi-step problem solving. Just as programmers debug code by tracing execution paths step-by-step, mathematicians decompose problems into intermediate lemmas and calculations. A model pre-exposed to code is arguably better primed to internalize this decomposition process, treating mathematical derivations similarly to algorithmic flows. Furthermore, this initialization ensures that the model retains strong coding proficiencies even after undergoing extensive mathematical specialization, preventing what is known as catastrophic forgetting—the phenomenon where learning new tasks erases previously acquired knowledge. Thus, DeepSeekCoder acts not merely as a weight initializer but as a functional scaffold upon which advanced mathematical reasoning is built, allowing the final 7B parameter model to compete effectively with much larger architectures like Minerva 540B despite having fewer parameters by orders of magnitude.

# Curriculum Design: Mixing Math Tokens with Code and Natural Language

Once initialized with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B, the next pivotal phase involves continuing pre-training on a massive corpus tailored specifically for mathematical enrichment while maintaining necessary breadth in other modalities. The resulting curriculum is embodied in the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a dataset constructed from Common Crawl containing approximately 120 billion tokens dedicated exclusively to math-related content derived from web pages filtered via an iterative classification pipeline. However, pure isolationism in training data composition proved suboptimal; instead, a balanced mixture was employed to sustain general intelligence alongside domain expertise. The final training distribution for DeepSeekMath-Base allocates roughly 56% of tokens from this newly curated DeepSeekMath Corpus ensuring deep immersion in quantitative reasoning contexts ranging from elementary arithmetic to university-level calculus and abstract algebra interwoven with natural language explanations crucially supplemented by 4% tokens from AlgebraicStack providing rigorous symbolic computation examples followed by 10% drawn directly from arXiv papers offering theoretical depth though ultimately found less impactful than anticipated plus 20% comprised entirely of GitHub code preserving programming fluency alongside residual natural language data constituting another 10%. # Chapter 4: Supervised Fine-Tuning for Instruction Alignment

## Unified Paradigm Definition

To truly comprehend the landscape of modern Large Language Model (LLM) alignment, one must move beyond viewing techniques like Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Reinforcement Learning methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as isolated, distinct methodologies. Instead, these approaches can be understood through a unified theoretical paradigm that reveals their underlying structural similarities and subtle distinctions. This perspective posits that every training method aimed at aligning a model with human preferences or specific objectives can be mathematically decomposed into three fundamental components: the Data Source ($\mathcal{D}$), the Reward Function ($\pi_{rf}$), and the Algorithm ($\mathcal{A}$). By analyzing how these components interact to form the gradient updates applied to the model parameters, we gain profound insight into why certain methods succeed in specific contexts while others falter.

At the heart of this unified view lies a generalized expression for the gradient of the optimization objective with respect to the model parameters $\theta$. Regardless of whether one is performing simple supervised learning or complex reinforcement learning, the direction in which the model weights are adjusted is determined by an expectation over a dataset of input-output pairs $(q, o)$, weighted by a specific gradient coefficient derived from a reward signal. Formally, this relationship is expressed as:
$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right).
$$
Here, $\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})$ represents the standard log-probability gradient familiar from maximum likelihood estimation. The critical innovation in this paradigm is recognizing that all alignment techniques differ primarily in how they define and manipulate the terms surrounding this core gradient: specifically, where the data comes from ($\mathcal{D}$), what constitutes the reward signal ($\pi_{rf}$), and how that signal is transformed into a scaling factor for the gradient via the algorithm's logic ($GC_{\mathcal{A}}$). This formulation allows us to see that methods traditionally categorized differently—such as offline fine-tuning versus online reinforcement learning—are actually variations on a theme involving different configurations of these three pillars.

The first pillar, **Data Source** ($\mathcal{D}$), dictates whether the training examples are static or dynamic relative to the evolving policy. In this framework, we distinguish between "offline" and "online" sampling regimes. Offline methods rely on data generated once by an initial model before training begins; consequently, as seen in traditional SFT or standard RFT/DPO implementations where data is fixed from an earlier checkpoint. Conversely, online methods continuously sample fresh outputs from the very policy model being trained at that moment. This distinction is crucial because online sampling ensures that as the model learns and shifts its behavior toward better responses, it immediately trains on these new behaviors rather than stagnating on outdated distributions. The transition from offline to online paradigms explains why iterative reinforcement learning often outperforms static fine-tuning in later stages of training; it prevents the "data lag" where a powerful policy is forced to learn from mediocre examples generated by its own past selves or an inferior initialization.

The second pillar involves the **Reward Function** ($\pi_{rf}$), which serves as the ultimate arbiter of quality for any given output sequence. In this unified view, reward functions generally fall into two categories: rule-based systems and model-based systems. A rule-based reward function relies on explicit heuristics or ground-truth verification—for instance, checking if a mathematical solution ends with a correct numerical answer—to determine if an output is acceptable. In contrast, a model-based reward function employs a separate neural network (the Reward Model) trained to predict human preference scores for arbitrary outputs. While rule-based rewards are precise within narrow domains like arithmetic verification, they struggle with nuanced reasoning steps or open-ended generation where binary correctness is insufficiently descriptive. Model-based rewards offer granularity but introduce risks of noise and misalignment if not perfectly calibrated. The choice here fundamentally alters how gradients are shaped; a binary rule might simply reject bad paths entirely without guiding good ones proportionally to their quality unless sophisticated weighting schemes are applied within the algorithm itself.

Finally, the **Algorithm** ($\mathcal{A}$) acts as the engine that synthesizes data and rewards into actionable updates by defining the Gradient Coefficient ($GC$). This coefficient determines not just *whether* to reinforce a path but *how strongly*. Traditional SFT essentially treats all labeled data equally with uniform coefficients implicitly assuming perfect labels. Methods like RFT refine this by discarding incorrect samples entirely (effectively setting their coefficient to zero) but treating all retained correct samples identically regardless of their nuance or difficulty. Advanced algorithms like PPO and GRPO take this further by calculating advantage estimates that allow for differential reinforcement; they amplify probabilities for highly rewarded sequences more aggressively than moderately rewarded ones while actively suppressing poorly scored trajectories based on their relative standing within a group or against a baseline value function. This granular control over $GC$ allows RL methods to navigate complex optimization landscapes more effectively than simpler rejection-sampling approaches which lack mechanisms for graded feedback between success states.

By mapping established techniques onto this tripartite structure—the origin of data ($\mathcal{D}$), the nature of evaluation ($\pi_{rf}$), and the mechanics of update calculation ($GC$)—we uncover deep connections previously obscured by disparate terminologies.
