Chapter 4: # Chapter 4: Supervised Fine-Tuning for Instruction Alignment

## Unified Paradigm Definition

To truly comprehend the landscape of modern Large Language Model (LLM) alignment, one must move beyond viewing techniques like Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Reinforcement Learning methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) or Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) as isolated, distinct methodologies. Instead, these approaches can be understood through a unified theoretical paradigm that reveals their underlying structural similarities and subtle distinctions. This perspective posits that every training method aimed at aligning a model with human preferences or specific objectives can be mathematically decomposed into three fundamental components: the Data Source ($\mathcal{D}$), the Reward Function ($\pi_{rf}$), and the Algorithm ($\mathcal{A}$). By analyzing how these components interact to form the gradient updates applied to the model parameters, we gain profound insight into why certain methods succeed in specific contexts while others falter.

At the heart of this unified view lies a generalized expression for the gradient of the optimization objective with respect to the model parameters $\theta$. Regardless of whether one is performing simple supervised learning or complex reinforcement learning, the direction in which the model weights are adjusted is determined by an expectation over a dataset of input-output pairs $(q, o)$, weighted by a specific gradient coefficient derived from a reward signal. Formally, this relationship is expressed as:
$$
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{A}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[(q,o) \sim \mathcal{D}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} GC_{\mathcal{A}}(q, o, t, \pi_{rf}) \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right).
$$
Here, $\nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})$ represents the standard log-probability gradient familiar from maximum likelihood estimation. The critical innovation in this paradigm is recognizing that all alignment techniques differ primarily in how they define and manipulate the terms surrounding this core gradient: specifically, where the data comes from ($\mathcal{D}$), what constitutes the reward signal ($\pi_{rf}$), and how that signal is transformed into a scaling factor for the gradient via the algorithm's logic ($GC_{\mathcal{A}}$). This formulation allows us to see that methods traditionally categorized differently—such as offline fine-tuning versus online reinforcement learning—are actually variations on a theme involving different configurations of these three pillars.

The first pillar, **Data Source** ($\mathcal{D}$), dictates whether the training examples are static or dynamic relative to the evolving policy. In this framework, we distinguish between "offline" and "online" sampling regimes. Offline methods rely on data generated once by an initial model before training begins; consequently, as seen in traditional SFT or standard RFT/DPO implementations where data is fixed from an earlier checkpoint. Conversely, online methods continuously sample fresh outputs from the very policy model being trained at that moment. This distinction is crucial because online sampling ensures that as the model learns and shifts its behavior toward better responses, it immediately trains on these new behaviors rather than stagnating on outdated distributions. The transition from offline to online paradigms explains why iterative reinforcement learning often outperforms static fine-tuning in later stages of training; it prevents the "data lag" where a powerful policy is forced to learn from mediocre examples generated by its own past selves or an inferior initialization.

The second pillar involves the **Reward Function** ($\pi_{rf}$), which serves as the ultimate arbiter of quality for any given output sequence. In this unified view, reward functions generally fall into two categories: rule-based systems and model-based systems. A rule-based reward function relies on explicit heuristics or ground-truth verification—for instance, checking if a mathematical solution ends with a correct numerical answer—to determine if an output is acceptable. In contrast, a model-based reward function employs a separate neural network (the Reward Model) trained to predict human preference scores for arbitrary outputs. While rule-based rewards are precise within narrow domains like arithmetic verification, they struggle with nuanced reasoning steps or open-ended generation where binary correctness is insufficiently descriptive. Model-based rewards offer granularity but introduce risks of noise and misalignment if not perfectly calibrated. The choice here fundamentally alters how gradients are shaped; a binary rule might simply reject bad paths entirely without guiding good ones proportionally to their quality unless sophisticated weighting schemes are applied within the algorithm itself.

Finally, the **Algorithm** ($\mathcal{A}$) acts as the engine that synthesizes data and rewards into actionable updates by defining the Gradient Coefficient ($GC$). This coefficient determines not just *whether* to reinforce a path but *how strongly*. Traditional SFT essentially treats all labeled data equally with uniform coefficients implicitly assuming perfect labels. Methods like RFT refine this by discarding incorrect samples entirely (effectively setting their coefficient to zero) but treating all retained correct samples identically regardless of their nuance or difficulty. Advanced algorithms like PPO and GRPO take this further by calculating advantage estimates that allow for differential reinforcement; they amplify probabilities for highly rewarded sequences more aggressively than moderately rewarded ones while actively suppressing poorly scored trajectories based on their relative standing within a group or against a baseline value function. This granular control over $GC$ allows RL methods to navigate complex optimization landscapes more effectively than simpler rejection-sampling approaches which lack mechanisms for graded feedback between success states.

By mapping established techniques onto this tripartite structure—the origin of data ($\mathcal{D}$), the nature of evaluation ($\pi_{rf}$), and the mechanics of update calculation ($GC$)—we uncover deep connections previously obscured by disparate terminologies.
