\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models"}

Chapter 2: Chapter 2: Constructing High-Quality Mathematical Corpora

The advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) in mathematical reasoning has historically been constrained by the scarcity and fragmentation of high-quality training data. Early efforts relied heavily on curated datasets like Minerva, OpenWebMath, and Proof-Pile, which, while foundational, exhibited significant limitations in terms of scale, linguistic diversity, and data composition. Minerva, despite achieving strong performance with a massive 540-billion parameter model, relied on a mathematical web corpus that was roughly seven times smaller than what became possible with newer extraction techniques. Similarly, OpenWebMath offered a filtered view of Common Crawl totaling approximately 13.6 billion tokens, representing only a fraction of the available mathematical knowledge on the web. Proof-Pile attempted to broaden this horizon by aggregating data from textbooks, Wikipedia, and arXiv papers; however, its heavy reliance on arXiv—comprising over 85% of its content—and its specific mix of code and text resulted in a dataset that did not necessarily translate to superior reasoning capabilities for smaller models. Experimental validation revealed that simply scaling up arXiv inclusion did not yield proportional gains in mathematical reasoning benchmarks; in fact, models trained exclusively on arXiv corpora sometimes showed no improvement or even deterioration in performance compared to baselines. These observations highlighted a critical gap: the existence of vast amounts of untapped mathematical knowledge scattered across public web pages that traditional static scraping methods failed to capture effectively. Consequently, the construction of the DeepSeekMath Corpus was driven by the necessity to move beyond these established limits by leveraging a dynamic, iterative mining strategy capable of extracting high-quality data at an unprecedented scale.

The core innovation in constructing the DeepSeekMath Corpus lies in an iterative mining pipeline designed to progressively expand the pool of known mathematical web pages through active learning principles. The process begins with a "seed corpus," initially populated by OpenWebMath, which serves as the ground truth for identifying positive examples. Using this seed set of 500,000 positive instances and an equal number of random negative instances drawn from Common Crawl, researchers trained a fastText-based binary classifier configured with specific hyperparameters such as a vector dimension of 256 and a maximum word n-gram length of 3. This initial classifier acts as a sieve applied to a deduplicated version of Common Crawl reduced to approximately 40 billion HTML pages through URL-based deduplication techniques. The classifier assigns confidence scores to each page, allowing the team to rank candidates and retain only the highest-scoring entries for the first iteration. However, recognizing that an initial classifier trained on a limited seed might miss diverse formats or niche mathematical communities due to insufficient representation in the training set, the pipeline employs an adaptive refinement loop. Instead of stopping after one pass, the system identifies domains where collection rates were surprisingly high (defined as domains where over 10% of pages were flagged as math-related) and targets them for deeper inspection. This recursive approach ensures that as more positive examples are discovered and verified, the classifier itself becomes more sophisticated and capable of retrieving increasingly obscure or specialized mathematical content in subsequent passes.

Following the initial broad sweep by the fastText classifier, the pipeline shifts toward targeted domain identification and manual annotation to resolve ambiguities and enrich diversity. Once domains with high concentrations of previously collected pages are identified—for instance, sites like mathoverflow.net—the research team performs granular analysis by organizing Common Crawl into disjoint domains based on shared base URLs. Within these promising domains, human annotators manually inspect URLs associated with specific sections known to host high-value mathematical discussions or problems (such as question threads). Pages linked to these manually vetted URLs but missed by the automated classifier are harvested and added back into the seed corpus as new positive examples. This hybrid approach combines statistical pattern recognition with human expertise to overcome the blind spots inherent in purely algorithmic filtering. Over four distinct iterations of this cycle—scanning classifying refining—the cumulative effort yielded approximately 35 million unique mathematical web pages amounting to 120 billion tokens. The diminishing returns observed in later stages confirmed the efficacy of this method; by the fourth iteration, nearly 98% of retrievable data had already been captured in previous rounds signaling saturation and justifying cessation of collection. Crucially, unlike earlier corpora dominated almost entirely by English content derived from Western academic repositories or forums like StackExchange, this iterative process naturally uncovered rich multilingual resources significantly enhancing coverage for Chinese mathematics alongside English text thereby addressing a major shortcoming in prior datasets which often degraded non-English reasoning capabilities when used for training monolingual-heavy models
