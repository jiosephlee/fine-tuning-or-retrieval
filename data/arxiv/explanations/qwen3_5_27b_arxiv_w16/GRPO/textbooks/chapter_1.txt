Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: The Challenge of Mathematical Reasoning in LLMs

Large Language Models (LLMs) have fundamentally transformed the landscape of artificial intelligence, particularly in the domain of mathematical reasoning. This field presents a unique set of challenges due to the complex, structured, and multi-step nature of mathematical problems, which require precise logical deduction rather than mere pattern matching. While cutting-edge closed-source models such as GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in solving quantitative and geometric problems, a significant performance gap persists between these proprietary systems and the currently available open-source models. The latter often trail behind in their ability to handle competition-level mathematics or complex reasoning tasks without relying on external toolkits or voting techniques. This disparity highlights a critical need for domain-specific language models that can bridge the divide between general-purpose conversational abilities and rigorous mathematical problem-solving.

The DeepSeekMath project was initiated to address this specific gap by pushing the limits of mathematical reasoning within open language models. The primary goal is to develop a model that not only outperforms existing open-source counterparts but also approaches the performance levels of state-of-the-art closed models like GPT-4 on academic benchmarks. To achieve this, the project focuses on two pivotal strategies: first, harnessing the vast potential of publicly available web data through a meticulously engineered data selection pipeline to create a high-quality pre-training corpus; and second, introducing novel reinforcement learning techniques to refine reasoning capabilities. By continuing pre-training on 120 billion math-related tokens sourced from Common Crawl and employing advanced optimization algorithms, DeepSeekMath aims to demonstrate that smaller models, when trained on superior data and optimized effectively, can rival much larger systems in mathematical proficiency.

## The state of mathematical reasoning in AI: From GPT-4 to Open Source

The current state of mathematical reasoning in artificial intelligence is characterized by a sharp dichotomy between closed-source giants and open-source alternatives. Models like GPT-4 and Gemini-Ultra have revolutionized the field, achieving high scores on quantitative reasoning benchmarks such as MATH and geometry reasoning benchmarks. These models have proven instrumental not only in solving complex mathematical problems but also in assisting humans with intricate derivations that were previously beyond the reach of automated systems. However, these powerful models are not publicly available, restricting their accessibility for research and broader application. Consequently, researchers and developers must rely on open-source models, which historically have considerably trailed behind their closed-source counterparts in performance.

This performance gap is particularly evident when evaluating models on competition-level datasets. While closed-source models can achieve high accuracy through sophisticated internal mechanisms and massive parameter counts, open-source models often struggle with the same tasks unless they employ external toolkits or ensemble voting techniques like self-consistency over multiple samples. For instance, without such auxiliary methods, many open-source models fail to match the reasoning depth required for high-school or college-level mathematics. The DeepSeekMath project posits that this gap is not insurmountable and that it stems largely from data quality and training methodology rather than just model size. By initializing their model with DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B—a model already trained on code—and continuing pre-training with a massive corpus of math-specific tokens, DeepSeekMath demonstrates that a 7B parameter model can achieve an impressive score of 51.7% on the MATH benchmark without external tools. This achievement places it near the performance level of Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4, suggesting that strategic data curation and algorithmic innovation can compensate for smaller parameter counts in domain-specific tasks.

Furthermore, the trajectory of open-source development suggests that code training plays a pivotal role in enhancing mathematical reasoning capabilities prior to specialized math training. The observation that starting from a code-trained base model yields better results than starting from a general LLM indicates an intrinsic link between programming logic and mathematical deduction. This synergy allows open-source models to leverage the structured nature of code to improve their general reasoning abilities, extending benefits beyond mathematics to other domains like natural language understanding and general knowledge benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH. Thus, while closed-source models currently lead the pack, the rapid advancements in open-source architectures like DeepSeekMath are narrowing the divide by focusing on high-quality data pipelines and efficient reinforcement learning strategies tailored specifically for mathematical domains.

## Key Benchmarks: MATH, GSM8K, and their significance

To rigorously evaluate the mathematical capabilities of language models, researchers rely on standardized benchmarks that span various levels of complexity and problem types. Two of the most prominent benchmarks utilized in this field are GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) and MATH (Merely Another Test for Human-level Reasoning). GSM8K consists of grade-school level math word problems designed to test basic quantitative reasoning skills. It serves as a foundational benchmark for assessing whether a model can understand simple arithmetic operations within natural language contexts and perform multi-step calculations correctly. High performance on GSM8K is often considered a baseline requirement for any model claiming proficiency in mathematical reasoning; however, success here does not necessarily guarantee competence in more advanced mathematics due to its relatively low complexity ceiling compared to competition-level problems.

In contrast, the MATH benchmark represents a significantly higher tier of difficulty, comprising competition-level problems drawn from sources such as AMC (American Mathematics Competitions) competitions ranging from grade school to college level complexity. These problems cover diverse fields including algebra, number theory, geometry, probability, calculus, and combinatorics. Unlike GSM8K problems which often have straightforward solution paths once understood, MATH problems require deep logical insight, creative problem-solving strategies, and rigorous step-by-step deduction without relying on brute-force computation alone. Achieving high accuracy on MATH is considered a major milestone because it indicates that a model possesses genuine reasoning capabilities rather than just memorized patterns or superficial statistical correlations within its training data. In the context of DeepSeekMath, reaching an accuracy of over 50% on MATH without external tools is highlighted as a significant breakthrough for open-source models within this parameter range.

Beyond English benchmarks like GSM8K and MATH, comprehensive evaluation also extends to Chinese mathematical datasets such as CMATH (Chinese Math) and MGSM-zh (Multilingual Grade School Math), as well as formal mathematics tasks using miniF2F with Isabelle proof assistants. These additional benchmarks are crucial for assessing multilingual capabilities and formal theorem proving skills respectively. The significance of these benchmarks lies not only in providing quantitative metrics but also in revealing specific strengths and weaknesses across different types of reasoning tasks—for instance distinguishing between tool-integrated reasoning where Python execution is allowed versus self-contained text solutions where no external computation is permitted. By performing well across this spectrum—from elementary arithmetic in GSM8K to Olympiad-level challenges in MATH—models like DeepSeekMath demonstrate robustness across varying degrees of difficulty
