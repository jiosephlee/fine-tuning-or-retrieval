Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: The Challenge of Aligning Large Language Models

Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, acquiring broad world knowledge and reasoning skills through training on massive datasets of human-generated text. Models trained in this manner can perform complex tasks such as answering questions, writing code, and engaging in conversation with surprising fluency. However, the unsupervised nature of this pre-training presents a fundamental challenge: while these models possess a vast array of abilities and knowledge, they lack precise control over their behavior. Because the training data encompasses a wide variety of human goals, priorities, and skillsets—some of which may be undesirable—the model inevitably learns to mimic patterns that are not always safe or helpful. For instance, a coding assistant might learn to recognize common programming errors to correct them, yet without intervention, it may default to generating code that reflects frequent mistakes rather than the rare instances of high-quality engineering present in its training data. Similarly, a model might become aware of a widespread misconception held by half the population; without alignment, it risks asserting this falsehood as truth in fifty percent of relevant queries. Consequently, selecting the desired responses and behaviors from the model's extensive but indiscriminate knowledge base is critical for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable.

The core difficulty lies in distinguishing between what a model *knows* and what it should *do*. Unsupervised pre-training optimizes for predicting the next token in a sequence based on statistical likelihoods found in the internet corpus. This process does not inherently prioritize safety, honesty, or helpfulness as defined by modern ethical standards or specific user intents. Instead, it creates a distribution over outputs that mirrors the aggregate behavior of humanity as recorded in text data. To transform such a general-purpose engine into a specialized tool that adheres to specific human preferences—such as avoiding harmful content or providing concise summaries—we must intervene after pre-training. This intervention requires moving beyond simple pattern matching to active steering mechanisms that can guide the model away from statistically probable but undesirable outputs toward those that align with human judgment. Without such alignment procedures, even highly capable models remain unpredictable agents that cannot reliably serve specific user needs or operate safely in sensitive domains.

To address these limitations, researchers have developed frameworks designed to steer language models using curated sets of human preferences. The prevailing methodology for achieving this alignment is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which attempts to teach the model to maximize a reward signal derived from human choices between different model generations. This approach acknowledges that while collecting absolute scores for every possible output is difficult and subjective, humans are remarkably good at judging relative quality—that is, deciding whether response A is better than response B given a specific prompt. By leveraging these comparative judgments, RLHF seeks to shape the model's policy so that it consistently generates responses that humans prefer over alternatives. However, despite its success in creating conversational assistants with impressive abilities, the RLHF pipeline introduces significant complexity compared to standard supervised learning methods. It involves multiple distinct stages of training separate neural networks and requires intricate loops where the model samples its own outputs during training to calculate gradients for improvement.

The standard RLHF pipeline typically consists of three sequential phases: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reward Modeling, and RL Optimization. The process begins with SFT, where a pre-trained language model is fine-tuned using supervised learning on high-quality human demonstrations for specific downstream tasks like dialogue or summarization. This step produces an initial policy, often denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, which serves as a starting point closer to human-like behavior than the raw pre-trained model but still lacks nuanced alignment with preferences. In the second phase, known as Reward Modeling or Preference Sampling and Learning, this SFT model generates pairs of candidate responses $(y_1, y_2)$ for various prompts $x$. Human labelers then review these pairs and indicate their preference for one response over the other ($y_w \succ y_l$). These labeled comparisons are used to train a separate neural network reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$ via maximum likelihood estimation under a probabilistic preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model. This reward model acts as a surrogate for human judgment, assigning scalar scores to text sequences intended to reflect their desirability relative to others seen during training.

Once an explicit reward function has been learned from human preferences enters the third phase: RL Optimization. Here, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated using reinforcement learning algorithms to maximize the expected reward provided by $r_\phi$. Crucially, this optimization is constrained by a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty against a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (usually the SFT model). The objective function seeks to balance maximizing reward with staying close enough to the original distribution to prevent "mode collapse," where the model degenerates into repeating short phrases or nonsensical tokens that happen to score high on the imperfect reward function but fail semantically. Mathematically, this involves solving an optimization problem where one maximizes $\mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta || \pi_\text{ref}]$. Because language generation is discrete and non-differentiable regarding token selection probabilities directly affecting long-term outcomes without sampling gradients cannot flow back through standard backpropagation easily necessitates complex actor-critic architectures like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
