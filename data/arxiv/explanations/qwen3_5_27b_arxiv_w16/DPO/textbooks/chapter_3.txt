Chapter 3: # Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

The derivation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) begins with a fundamental re-examination of the standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline. In traditional RLHF, the process is decoupled into two distinct stages: first, a reward model is trained to predict human preferences, and second, a language model policy is optimized via reinforcement learning to maximize this learned reward while staying close to a reference policy. This separation introduces significant complexity, including the instability of reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and the computational overhead of sampling from the policy during training. The core insight of DPO is that this separation is unnecessary. By recognizing that the optimal policy for a KL-constrained reward maximization problem has a specific analytical form, we can invert this relationship. Instead of treating the reward function as an independent entity to be learned, we can express the reward function directly in terms of the ratio between the target policy and a reference policy. This change-of-variables technique allows us to bypass the explicit reward modeling phase entirely, transforming the complex RL objective into a simple, stable classification loss that can be optimized directly on preference data.

The mathematical foundation for this transformation lies in the solution to the constrained optimization problem defined in standard RLHF. The goal is to find a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that maximizes the expected reward $r(x, y)$ while minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The theoretical solution to this problem is known to take a specific exponential form relative to the reference policy. Specifically, for any given reward function $r(x, y)$ and a temperature parameter $\beta$ controlling the strength of the KL constraint, the optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ is proportional to the reference policy scaled by an exponential factor of the reward. Mathematically, this is expressed as $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$, where $Z(x)$ is a partition function that ensures the probabilities sum to one over all possible completions $y$. While this equation describes how an optimal policy looks given a reward, it also implies that if we have an optimal policy and a reference policy, we can mathematically recover a corresponding reward function. By taking the logarithm of both sides and rearranging terms, we derive an expression for the reward: $r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. This equation reveals that any valid optimal policy implicitly defines a reward function up to an additive term that depends only on the prompt $x$, represented by $\beta \log Z(x)$.

This reparameterization becomes powerful when applied within the context of human preference modeling, specifically using the Bradley-Terry model. The Bradley-Terry model posits that human preferences between two responses $y_1$ and $y_2$ given a prompt $x$ are determined by the difference in their underlying rewards. The probability that humans prefer $y_1$ over $y_2$ is given by $p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma(r^*(x, y_1)- r^*(x, y_2))$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $r^*$ represents the ground-truth latent reward function. When we substitute our derived expression for $r^*$ into this preference probability equation, something remarkable happens: we substitute $\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ for both $r^*(x, y_1)$ and $r^*(x, y_2)$. Because the preference probability depends only on the *difference* between these two rewards, and because both terms share the identical partition function component $\beta \log Z(x)$ (which depends only on $x$), this term cancels out perfectly in subtraction. Consequently, we are left with an expression for human preference probabilities that depends solely on the ratio of probabilities between pairs of completions under the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The partition function $Z(x)$, which was previously considered intractable and difficult to estimate without sampling over all possible completions, vanishes from our objective entirely.

With the partition function eliminated and preferences expressed purely in terms of policy ratios, we can now formulate a direct training objective for our language model parameters $\theta$. We treat our parametrized language model $\pi_\theta$ as an approximation of this optimal policy $\pi^*$. By substituting $\pi_\theta$ into our simplified preference equation, we arrive at a likelihood function that measures how well our model's implicit reward ordering matches human preferences. Maximizing this likelihood is equivalent to minimizing its negative log-likelihood. This results in what we define as Direct Preference Optimization loss: $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$. Here, $(x, y_w, y_l)$ represents a sample from our dataset where $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$. This loss function takes on exactly the form of binary cross-entropy used in standard classification tasks. It does not require sampling from $\pi_\theta$ during training loops (as RL methods do), nor does it require training a separate network to estimate rewards. Instead, it simply adjusts $\pi_\theta$ so that it assigns higher relative probability (compared to $\pi_\text{ref}$) to preferred responses than to dispreferred ones.

Finally, interpreting DPO through this lens reveals that "your language model is secretly a reward model." In traditional approaches, one network learns rewards and another learns policies; in DPO, these roles are unified within a single network architecture optimized via gradient descent on preference pairs. The gradient of this loss function provides further intuition: it pushes up log-probabilities for preferred completions and pulls them down for dispreferred ones relative to their baseline under $\pi_\text{ref}$. Crucially, these updates are weighted by how much current implicit rewards disagree with human labels; if the model already correctly ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$, its implicit reward difference will be positive and large enough such that its contribution to gradient updates diminishes via properties of logistic regression gradients.
