Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations of Preference Learning

The transition from unsupervised language models to systems that exhibit specific, human-aligned behaviors relies fundamentally on a rigorous mathematical framework for interpreting and optimizing preferences. While large-scale unsupervised models acquire vast amounts of world knowledge, they lack the intrinsic mechanism to distinguish between desirable and undesirable outputs without external guidance. To bridge this gap, researchers have developed a pipeline that translates subjective human judgments into quantifiable signals that can drive model updates. This chapter establishes the theoretical bedrock for this process, moving from the statistical modeling of pairwise comparisons to the formulation of constrained optimization problems that balance performance with behavioral stability. By understanding these foundations, one gains the necessary intuition to grasp why traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is complex and how newer methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) reframe these equations to simplify the training process.

## The Bradley-Terry model for pairwise preferences

At the heart of modern preference learning lies the need to formalize how humans judge the relative quality of two different model outputs. Unlike supervised learning, where a single correct label exists for an input, preference learning deals with comparative data: given a prompt $x$ and two candidate responses $y_1$ and $y_2$, a human annotator indicates which response they prefer, denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the "winner" (preferred) and $y_l$ is the "loser" (dispreferred). To convert these binary choices into a probabilistic framework suitable for machine learning, we rely on the Bradley-Terry model. This statistical model posits that there exists an underlying latent utility or score associated with every possible response, and the probability of preferring one response over another is determined solely by the relative magnitudes of these scores.

Mathematically, let us assume there is a true, albeit hidden, reward function $r^*(x, y)$ that assigns a scalar value representing the quality of response $y$ given prompt $x$. The Bradley-Terry model asserts that the probability of observing a preference for $y_1$ over $y_2$ follows a sigmoidal distribution based on the difference in their rewards. Specifically, if we denote this probability as $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$, it is defined as:

$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
$$

This equation reveals an intuitive property: if $r^*(x, y_1)$ is significantly larger than $r^*(x, y_2)$, the numerator dominates the denominator, driving the probability close to 1. Conversely, if the rewards are equal, the probability settles at 0.5. By utilizing exponentials in both terms, the model ensures that probabilities remain bounded between 0 and 1 regardless of how large or small the raw reward values become. This formulation effectively transforms ordinal preference data—who won against whom—into a continuous space where distances between items correspond to differences in perceived quality. While more complex ranking models like Plackett-Luce exist for scenarios involving lists of more than two items, the Bradley-Terry model serves as the canonical foundation for pairwise comparison tasks in language model alignment due to its simplicity and sufficiency for capturing relative human judgment.

## Formulating preference probabilities as a function of latent rewards

Once we accept that human preferences can be modeled through latent rewards via the Bradley-Terry framework, we must address how to operationalize this concept within a machine learning architecture. The core challenge is that we never observe the actual reward values $r^*(x, y)$; we only observe outcomes where one response was chosen over another. Therefore, our goal becomes estimating a parametric approximation of this reward function that best explains the observed preference data. To facilitate this estimation within standard deep learning frameworks, it is beneficial to rewrite the Bradley-Terry probability expression using properties of logarithms and exponential functions to isolate the difference between rewards.

By dividing both the numerator and denominator of Equation (1) by $\exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)$ and recognizing that $\frac{\exp(a)}{\exp(b)} = \exp(a-b)$, we can reformulate the preference probability entirely in terms of reward differences:

$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left[r^*(x, y_w) - r^*(x, y_l)\right]\right)}.
$$

Recognizing that $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ is the standard logistic sigmoid function used extensively in binary classification tasks allows us to express this relationship compactly as:

$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\left(r^*(x, y_w) - r^*(x, y_l)\right).
$$

This transformation is pivotal because it recasts preference learning as a binary classification problem where the target variable is whether $y_w$ was preferred over $y_l$, and features are derived from a learned reward network evaluating both candidates independently before taking their difference. The term inside the sigmoid function acts as a margin; if our learned reward model correctly assigns a higher score to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, then $r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$ will be positive large numbers pushing $\sigma(\cdot)$ towards 1. If it fails or reverses them predictably incorrectly predicting preferences—the output will be closer to 0 or below 0 depending on severity—indicating poor calibration with human intent.
