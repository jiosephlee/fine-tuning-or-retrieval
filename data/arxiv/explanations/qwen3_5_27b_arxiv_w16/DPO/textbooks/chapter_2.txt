Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations: Preference Models and Constrained Optimization

The Bradley-Terry model for pairwise preference probabilities

To mathematically formalize the concept of human preference in the context of language model alignment, we must first establish a statistical framework that translates subjective human judgments into a probabilistic distribution. The standard approach adopted in this field is the Bradley-Terry model, a statistical method originally designed for ranking items based on pairwise comparisons. In the setting of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), we assume that human preferences are not arbitrary but are generated by an underlying, latent reward function, denoted as $r^*(x, y)$. Here, $x$ represents the input prompt or context, and $y$ represents a specific completion or response generated by a language model. The core hypothesis of the Bradley-Terry model is that the probability of a human preferring one response $y_1$ over another response $y_2$, given a prompt $x$, is determined solely by the difference in their latent rewards. Specifically, the probability that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$, written as $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$, is modeled as a sigmoid function of the reward difference. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

This formulation implies that if the reward assigned to $y_1$ is significantly higher than that of $y_2$, the probability of preferring $y_1$ approaches 1. Conversely, if the rewards are equal, the probability is exactly 0.5. This probabilistic structure allows us to treat preference learning as a binary classification problem where we attempt to learn a parameterized reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ that maximizes the likelihood of observing the human-labeled preferences in our dataset $\mathcal{D}$. By framing the problem this way, we can utilize standard maximum likelihood estimation techniques to fit a neural network to approximate the latent reward function. The loss function derived from this model is essentially a binary cross-entropy loss over the log-odds of the preference pairs. It is important to note that while this model assumes preferences are driven by a scalar reward value, it does not require us to know the absolute values of these rewards; rather, it relies on their relative differences. This property is crucial because it means that any transformation of the reward function that preserves these differences will result in identical preference predictions. This observation lays the groundwork for understanding why we do not need to recover a unique reward function to achieve optimal alignment.

Formulating the RLHF objective: Maximizing reward with KL-divergence constraints

Once we have established how to model preferences via a reward function, the next step in traditional RLHF pipelines is to optimize a language model policy $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ to maximize this learned reward. However, simply maximizing expected reward leads to pathological behaviors known as "reward hacking" or mode collapse, where the model learns to generate repetitive or nonsensical text that tricks the reward model into assigning high scores without actually being helpful or safe. To prevent this deviation from desirable behavior and maintain generation diversity, we must constrain how far our optimized policy can drift from an initial reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$. This reference policy is typically a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model trained on high-quality data before any preference optimization occurs. The constraint is formalized using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, which measures the information loss when $\pi_\text{ref}$ is used to approximate $\pi_\theta$. The resulting optimization problem seeks to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected reward while penalizing large deviations from $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The mathematical formulation of this objective combines these two competing goals into a single equation:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

In this equation, $\beta$ acts as an inverse temperature parameter or regularization strength that controls the trade-off between maximizing reward and staying close to the reference distribution. A larger $\beta$ enforces stricter adherence to $\pi_\text{ref}$, potentially limiting performance gains but ensuring safety and stability. A smaller $\beta$ allows for greater deviation in pursuit of higher rewards but increases the risk of generating low-quality or unsafe content if the reward model is imperfect. The term $\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}]$ expands to $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta} [\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)}]$, representing the expected log-ratio between the new policy and the reference policy under samples drawn from the new policy itself. This constrained optimization problem defines what it means for a language model to be "aligned": it must be good at generating responses that humans prefer (high expected reward) while remaining faithful to its original training distribution (low KL divergence). This specific formulation is critical because it provides a well-defined target for optimization algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which are traditionally used because they can handle non-differentiable objectives involving sampling from discrete action spaces like text generation.

Deriving the closed-form optimal policy ($\pi^*$) from a reward function

A profound theoretical insight underpinning Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is that for any given reward function and reference policy satisfying specific regularity conditions, there exists an analytical solution for the optimal policy that maximizes our constrained objective. We do not need iterative reinforcement learning algorithms like PPO or REINFORCE to find this solution; instead, we can derive it directly using calculus and Lagrange multipliers. Starting with our objective function which balances expected reward against KL divergence, we can treat this as an optimization problem over probability distributions for every prompt $x$. By introducing Lagrange multipliers for constraints such as normalization (probabilities must sum to 1), one can show that the optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ corresponding to any arbitrary reward function $r(x,y)$ takes an exponential form relative to both the reference policy and the exponential of scaled rewards.

The closed-form solution for this optimal policy is given by:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

In this expression, $\frac{1}{Z(x)}$ acts as a normalization constant ensuring that $\sum_y \pi_r(y|x) = 1$. The structure of this equation reveals an intuitive relationship: optimal behavior involves taking our prior beliefs about good responses (encoded in $\pi_\text{ref}$) and re-weighting them by how much better they score under our current understanding of human preferences (encoded in $r(x,y)$), scaled by our tolerance for deviation ($\beta$). Responses with higher rewards receive exponentially higher probabilities relative to those with lower rewards compared to their baseline probability in $\pi_\text{ref}$. This derivation holds regardless of whether $r(x,y)$ is learned via maximum likelihood on preference data or defined by some other means; it simply describes what *would* be done if one could perfectly optimize against that specific reward signal under KL constraints. Consequently, if we knew exactly what human preferences were (i.e., if we knew $r^*$), we could theoretically construct an optimal policy directly using this formula without ever needing an RL loop involving sampling and gradient estimation through discrete actions. This analytical mapping suggests that reinforcement learning might be an unnecessary intermediate step if we can parameterize our search space differently.
