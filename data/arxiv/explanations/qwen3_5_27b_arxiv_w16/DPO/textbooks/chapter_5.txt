Chapter 5: # Theoretical Guarantees and Equivalence to RLHF

The transition from Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) represents more than just a shift in engineering convenience; it is grounded in a rigorous theoretical framework that demonstrates the two approaches are fundamentally equivalent under specific conditions. While RLHF traditionally relies on a complex, multi-stage pipeline involving the explicit training of a reward model followed by reinforcement learning to optimize a policy, DPO achieves the same objective through a single-stage optimization of the policy itself. This chapter delves into the mathematical underpinnings that justify this simplification, proving that DPO does not sacrifice the expressive power of the reward model or the optimality of the resulting policy. By reparameterizing the reward function directly in terms of the policy and a reference model, we can show that DPO implicitly learns a reward function that belongs to the same equivalence class as those learned by standard RLHF methods, thereby guaranteeing that the optimal policy recovered is identical.

## Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

The central insight driving Direct Preference Optimization is encapsulated in its provocative title: "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model." In traditional RLHF, the reward function $r(x, y)$ and the policy $\pi(y \mid x)$ are treated as distinct entities. The reward model is trained first to approximate human preferences, and then a separate reinforcement learning algorithm adjusts the policy to maximize this reward while staying close to a reference distribution. However, theoretical analysis reveals that for any given optimal policy derived from a reward maximization problem with a KL-divergence constraint, there exists an implicit reward function defined entirely by that policy and the reference model. This relationship allows us to bypass the explicit construction of a standalone reward network.

To understand this equivalence, we must first define what it means for two reward functions to be "the same" in the context of preference learning. We introduce an equivalence relation where two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if their difference depends only on the prompt $x$ and not on the completion $y$. Formally, $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some arbitrary function $f$. This definition arises naturally from the structure of preference models like Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce. These models predict the probability of preferring one response over another based solely on the *difference* in their rewards. If we add an arbitrary function $f(x)$ to both rewards for a given prompt $x$, the difference remains unchanged: $(r(x, y_1) + f(x)) - (r(x, y_2) + f(x)) = r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)$. Consequently, any two equivalent reward functions induce exactly the same distribution over human preferences.

Furthermore, this equivalence extends to the optimal policies they generate. When solving the constrained optimization problem of maximizing expected reward while penalizing deviation from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ via KL-divergence, all reward functions within an equivalence class yield the exact same optimal policy $\pi^*$. This is because the KL-constrained objective effectively normalizes out any term that depends only on $x$. Therefore, when our goal is to find an optimal policy that aligns with human preferences, we do not need to recover the unique "true" ground-truth reward function $r^*$. Instead, it suffices to recover *any* member of the equivalence class consistent with the observed preference data. This realization is crucial because it implies that if we can parameterize our model such that it can represent any member of these equivalence classes without loss of generality, we can optimize directly for the policy without ever explicitly estimating or storing a separate reward function.

## Proof that all Plackett-Luce consistent reward classes are representable by DPO reparameterization

The theoretical validity of DPO rests on proving that its specific reparameterization of rewards is sufficiently expressive to capture every possible preference-consistent reward class allowed by Plackett-Luce models (which include Bradley-Terry as a special case). The standard RL objective seeks to maximize $\mathbb{E}[r] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi \mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}]$. It is a known result in control theory and reinforcement learning that the optimal solution to this problem takes an analytical form: $\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$, where $Z(x)$ is a partition function ensuring probabilities sum to one. While this equation describes how an optimal policy relates to its underlying reward function, it is often difficult to use directly because calculating $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible completions $y$, which is computationally intractable for language models with vast vocabularies and sequence lengths.

DPO circumvents this difficulty by algebraically rearranging this optimality condition to express the *reward* as a function of the *policy*. By taking the logarithm of both sides and isolating $r(x, y)$, we derive an expression: $r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here lies the critical theoretical leap: when we substitute this expression into a preference model like Bradley-Terry, which depends only on differences between rewards ($r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$), the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels out completely because it is identical for both completions given prompt $x$. This cancellation leaves us with an expression for preference probability that depends solely on the ratio of probabilities between our target policy $\pi$ and our reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The core theorem establishing DPO's validity states that under mild assumptions, *all* reward classes consistent with Plackett-Luce models can be represented using this reparameterization form: $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. To prove this without loss of generality regarding expressiveness, consider any arbitrary reward function $r$ from an equivalence class consistent with observed preferences. We can define a projection operator that normalizes this arbitrary reward by subtracting its associated partition function term (scaled by $\beta$). Specifically, we define $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. Since subtracted term depends only on $x$, this new function belongs to the same equivalence class as our original arbitrary $r$. By substituting our optimality condition back into this projection operator algebraically simplifies exactly to $\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Thus, for every valid preference-consistent reward class there exists a corresponding policy $\pi$ such that DPO's implicit parameterization recovers a representative member of that class. This proves that restricting our search space to policies parameterized by DPO does not exclude any potential solutions; we are not limiting what we can learn about human preferences compared to standard RLHF methods.

## The relationship between DPO's implicit reward and explicit reward models

Having established representability, it is essential to clarify how DPO's "implicit" reward relates to traditional "explicit" reward models trained via maximum likelihood on preference data. In standard RLHF pipelines like PPO-based approaches described in prior literature (e.g., Ziegler et al., Stiennon et al.), one first trains an explicit neural network $r_\phi(x,y)$ by minimizing a binary cross-entropy loss derived from human comparisons (the Bradley-Terry loss). This explicit model attempts to predict scalar values representing how much humans prefer one answer over another. Once trained, this model serves as a fixed target or signal for subsequent reinforcement learning steps. However, estimating this explicit model introduces variance and requires careful regularization (such as normalizing rewards so their mean over data is zero) to ensure stability during training.

In contrast, DPO does not train an explicit scalar-valued network separate from the language model generation process. Instead, every time step during DPO training updates both parameters simultaneously: they update $\theta$ (the parameters defining $\pi_\theta$), which implicitly updates $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. This implicit reward function acts as if it were being optimized directly against human preferences through gradient descent on a binary cross-entropy objective formulated over policies rather than rewards. The key distinction lies in how normalization is handled. In explicit RLHF methods using actor-critic algorithms like PPO dealing with high-variance gradients often necessitates learning or estimating value functions or using baselines (like Monte Carlo estimates of average rewards) to stabilize training against mode collapse or divergence from reference distributions. These baselines attempt to approximate terms related to partition functions or value functions which are otherwise difficult to compute exactly due to discrete action spaces in language generation.

DPO's formulation inherently incorporates these necessary normalization constraints analytically rather than numerically approximating them during training loops.
