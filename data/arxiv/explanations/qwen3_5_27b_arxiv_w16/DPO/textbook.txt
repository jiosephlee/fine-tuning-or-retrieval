# Introduction to Language Model Alignment and the RLHF Paradigm

The rapid advancement of large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) has demonstrated that training on massive text corpora allows these systems to acquire broad world knowledge and surprising reasoning capabilities. However, a fundamental disconnect exists between the capabilities learned during this unsupervised pre-training phase and the specific, safe, and helpful behaviors desired in deployed AI systems. Pre-training data is generated by humans with a vast array of goals, priorities, and skill levels, meaning the model learns to imitate a wide distribution of behaviors, many of which may be undesirable for a specific application. For instance, while an AI coding assistant must understand common programming errors to correct them, it should not generate code that replicates those errors simply because they are frequent in its training data. Similarly, a model might be aware of a common misconception held by a significant portion of the population, yet it is crucial that the model does not propagate this misconception as fact. Consequently, the primary challenge in modern AI development is not merely endowing models with knowledge, but rather selecting and steering their desired responses from this broad spectrum of abilities to ensure safety, performance, and controllability.

To bridge this gap between raw capability and aligned behavior, researchers have turned to preference learning. While straightforward supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human demonstrations can improve instruction following, it often fails to capture the nuanced relative quality judgments that humans make between different model outputs. Relative human judgments—deciding which of two responses is better—are often easier to collect than expert-written demonstrations for every possible query. Therefore, the most successful class of methods for aligning language models relies on datasets of human preferences rather than absolute scores or perfect demonstrations. These methods aim to teach the model not just what a good response looks like in isolation, but how to rank responses relative to one another based on human values such as helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness. This shift from absolute supervision to relative preference learning forms the bedrock of current alignment strategies, necessitating a mechanism that can translate these comparative judgments into a policy that consistently generates preferred outputs.

The standard approach for leveraging these preferences has been Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), a complex multi-stage pipeline designed to optimize a language model's policy against human values. This process typically begins with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), where a pre-trained LM is fine-tuned on high-quality data for specific downstream tasks like dialogue or summarization to establish a baseline policy. The second phase involves reward modeling: the SFT model generates pairs of responses for various prompts, which are then presented to human labelers who indicate their preference for one response over the other ($y_w \succ y_l$). These preferences are assumed to be generated by an underlying latent reward function $r^*(x,y)$, which is unknown but can be approximated by training a separate neural network reward model $r_\phi(x,y)$. This reward model is trained via maximum likelihood estimation using a preference framework such as the Bradley-Terry model, which posits that the probability of preferring one response over another depends on the exponential difference in their rewards. Once this reward function is learned, it serves as a proxy for human judgment in the final stage.

The final stage of the RLHF pipeline involves Reinforcement Learning fine-tuning, where the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is optimized to maximize the expected reward predicted by $r_\phi$ while simultaneously staying close to an initial reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (usually the SFT model). This optimization problem is formulated as maximizing $\mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta \mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}]$, where $\beta$ controls the strength of the constraint preventing the model from drifting too far from its original distribution. The KL-divergence term is critical; without it, models tend to "mode collapse," generating repetitive or degenerate text that maximizes reward but lacks diversity or coherence. Because language generation is discrete and non-differentiable with respect to token probabilities in this context, this objective cannot be solved with standard gradient descent alone. Instead, practitioners typically employ complex reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which require sampling from the policy during training loops and maintaining separate critic networks to estimate value functions. This creates a computationally intensive workflow involving multiple models running in concert: one for generating samples (the actor), one for evaluating them (the critic/reward model), and one serving as a static reference point.

Despite its success in producing capable conversational agents and coding assistants, the RLHF paradigm suffers from significant practical drawbacks that hinder its widespread adoption and stability. The pipeline is notoriously complex to implement and tune, requiring careful orchestration of multiple training stages and hyperparameters across different components. The reinforcement learning loop itself is often unstable; small changes in hyperparameters or sampling strategies can lead to catastrophic failures where the model's performance degrades rapidly or it begins generating nonsensical text. Furthermore, RLHF incurs substantial computational costs due to the necessity of sampling from the language model repeatedly during every step of training to estimate gradients and value functions. This sampling requirement makes training slower and more resource-intensive compared to standard supervised learning methods. Additionally, fitting an explicit reward model introduces its own set of challenges; if the reward model overfits or fails to generalize well outside its training distribution, optimizing against it can lead to "reward hacking," where the policy exploits flaws in the reward estimator rather than genuinely improving alignment with human intent. These instabilities and complexities motivate the search for alternative approaches that can achieve similar alignment goals without relying on explicit reinforcement learning loops or separate reward modeling stages.

The limitations inherent in standard RLHF have driven research toward more direct optimization methods that can bypass these complexities while retaining theoretical rigor. The central insight motivating these new approaches is that explicit reward modeling may be unnecessary if one can directly parameterize the relationship between preferences and policies. By re-examining the mathematical structure of RLHF objectives under common preference models like Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce, it becomes possible to derive an analytical mapping between an optimal policy and its corresponding implicit reward function. This realization suggests that instead of first learning a reward function $r$ and then using reinforcement learning to find $\pi$, one could optimize for $\pi$ directly using a simple classification loss derived from preference data alone. Such an approach would eliminate the need for sampling during fine-tuning, remove the instability associated with actor-critic algorithms like PPO, and significantly reduce computational overhead while theoretically optimizing for the same objective as RLHF: maximizing expected reward subject to KL-divergence constraints relative to a reference policy. This shift toward direct preference optimization represents a paradigm change in how we align large language models with human values.

Mathematical Foundations: Preference Models and Constrained Optimization

The Bradley-Terry model for pairwise preference probabilities

To mathematically formalize the concept of human preference in the context of language model alignment, we must first establish a statistical framework that translates subjective human judgments into a probabilistic distribution. The standard approach adopted in this field is the Bradley-Terry model, a statistical method originally designed for ranking items based on pairwise comparisons. In the setting of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), we assume that human preferences are not arbitrary but are generated by an underlying, latent reward function, denoted as $r^*(x, y)$. Here, $x$ represents the input prompt or context, and $y$ represents a specific completion or response generated by a language model. The core hypothesis of the Bradley-Terry model is that the probability of a human preferring one response $y_1$ over another response $y_2$, given a prompt $x$, is determined solely by the difference in their latent rewards. Specifically, the probability that $y_1$ is preferred to $y_2$, written as $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$, is modeled as a sigmoid function of the reward difference. Mathematically, this is expressed as:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}$$

This formulation implies that if the reward assigned to $y_1$ is significantly higher than that of $y_2$, the probability of preferring $y_1$ approaches 1. Conversely, if the rewards are equal, the probability is exactly 0.5. This probabilistic structure allows us to treat preference learning as a binary classification problem where we attempt to learn a parameterized reward model $r_\phi(x, y)$ that maximizes the likelihood of observing the human-labeled preferences in our dataset $\mathcal{D}$. By framing the problem this way, we can utilize standard maximum likelihood estimation techniques to fit a neural network to approximate the latent reward function. The loss function derived from this model is essentially a binary cross-entropy loss over the log-odds of the preference pairs. It is important to note that while this model assumes preferences are driven by a scalar reward value, it does not require us to know the absolute values of these rewards; rather, it relies on their relative differences. This property is crucial because it means that any transformation of the reward function that preserves these differences will result in identical preference predictions. This observation lays the groundwork for understanding why we do not need to recover a unique reward function to achieve optimal alignment.

Formulating the RLHF objective: Maximizing reward with KL-divergence constraints

Once we have established how to model preferences via a reward function, the next step in traditional RLHF pipelines is to optimize a language model policy $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ to maximize this learned reward. However, simply maximizing expected reward leads to pathological behaviors known as "reward hacking" or mode collapse, where the model learns to generate repetitive or nonsensical text that tricks the reward model into assigning high scores without actually being helpful or safe. To prevent this deviation from desirable behavior and maintain generation diversity, we must constrain how far our optimized policy can drift from an initial reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$. This reference policy is typically a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model trained on high-quality data before any preference optimization occurs. The constraint is formalized using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, which measures the information loss when $\pi_\text{ref}$ is used to approximate $\pi_\theta$. The resulting optimization problem seeks to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that maximizes the expected reward while penalizing large deviations from $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The mathematical formulation of this objective combines these two competing goals into a single equation:

$$\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]$$

In this equation, $\beta$ acts as an inverse temperature parameter or regularization strength that controls the trade-off between maximizing reward and staying close to the reference distribution. A larger $\beta$ enforces stricter adherence to $\pi_\text{ref}$, potentially limiting performance gains but ensuring safety and stability. A smaller $\beta$ allows for greater deviation in pursuit of higher rewards but increases the risk of generating low-quality or unsafe content if the reward model is imperfect. The term $\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}]$ expands to $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta} [\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y|x)}]$, representing the expected log-ratio between the new policy and the reference policy under samples drawn from the new policy itself. This constrained optimization problem defines what it means for a language model to be "aligned": it must be good at generating responses that humans prefer (high expected reward) while remaining faithful to its original training distribution (low KL divergence). This specific formulation is critical because it provides a well-defined target for optimization algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which are traditionally used because they can handle non-differentiable objectives involving sampling from discrete action spaces like text generation.

Deriving the closed-form optimal policy ($\pi^*$) from a reward function

A profound theoretical insight underpinning Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is that for any given reward function and reference policy satisfying specific regularity conditions, there exists an analytical solution for the optimal policy that maximizes our constrained objective. We do not need iterative reinforcement learning algorithms like PPO or REINFORCE to find this solution; instead, we can derive it directly using calculus and Lagrange multipliers. Starting with our objective function which balances expected reward against KL divergence, we can treat this as an optimization problem over probability distributions for every prompt $x$. By introducing Lagrange multipliers for constraints such as normalization (probabilities must sum to 1), one can show that the optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ corresponding to any arbitrary reward function $r(x,y)$ takes an exponential form relative to both the reference policy and the exponential of scaled rewards.

The closed-form solution for this optimal policy is given by:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

In this expression, $\frac{1}{Z(x)}$ acts as a normalization constant ensuring that $\sum_y \pi_r(y|x) = 1$. The structure of this equation reveals an intuitive relationship: optimal behavior involves taking our prior beliefs about good responses (encoded in $\pi_\text{ref}$) and re-weighting them by how much better they score under our current understanding of human preferences (encoded in $r(x,y)$), scaled by our tolerance for deviation ($\beta$). Responses with higher rewards receive exponentially higher probabilities relative to those with lower rewards compared to their baseline probability in $\pi_\text{ref}$. This derivation holds regardless of whether $r(x,y)$ is learned via maximum likelihood on preference data or defined by some other means; it simply describes what *would* be done if one could perfectly optimize against that specific reward signal under KL constraints. Consequently, if we knew exactly what human preferences were (i.e., if we knew $r^*$), we could theoretically construct an optimal policy directly using this formula without ever needing an RL loop involving sampling and gradient estimation through discrete actions. This analytical mapping suggests that reinforcement learning might be an unnecessary intermediate step if we can parameterize our search space differently.

# Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

The derivation of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) begins with a fundamental re-examination of the standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) pipeline. In traditional RLHF, the process is decoupled into two distinct stages: first, a reward model is trained to predict human preferences, and second, a language model policy is optimized via reinforcement learning to maximize this learned reward while staying close to a reference policy. This separation introduces significant complexity, including the instability of reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and the computational overhead of sampling from the policy during training. The core insight of DPO is that this separation is unnecessary. By recognizing that the optimal policy for a KL-constrained reward maximization problem has a specific analytical form, we can invert this relationship. Instead of treating the reward function as an independent entity to be learned, we can express the reward function directly in terms of the ratio between the target policy and a reference policy. This change-of-variables technique allows us to bypass the explicit reward modeling phase entirely, transforming the complex RL objective into a simple, stable classification loss that can be optimized directly on preference data.

The mathematical foundation for this transformation lies in the solution to the constrained optimization problem defined in standard RLHF. The goal is to find a policy $\pi_{\theta}$ that maximizes the expected reward $r(x, y)$ while minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The theoretical solution to this problem is known to take a specific exponential form relative to the reference policy. Specifically, for any given reward function $r(x, y)$ and a temperature parameter $\beta$ controlling the strength of the KL constraint, the optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ is proportional to the reference policy scaled by an exponential factor of the reward. Mathematically, this is expressed as $\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$, where $Z(x)$ is a partition function that ensures the probabilities sum to one over all possible completions $y$. While this equation describes how an optimal policy looks given a reward, it also implies that if we have an optimal policy and a reference policy, we can mathematically recover a corresponding reward function. By taking the logarithm of both sides and rearranging terms, we derive an expression for the reward: $r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. This equation reveals that any valid optimal policy implicitly defines a reward function up to an additive term that depends only on the prompt $x$, represented by $\beta \log Z(x)$.

This reparameterization becomes powerful when applied within the context of human preference modeling, specifically using the Bradley-Terry model. The Bradley-Terry model posits that human preferences between two responses $y_1$ and $y_2$ given a prompt $x$ are determined by the difference in their underlying rewards. The probability that humans prefer $y_1$ over $y_2$ is given by $p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\sigma(r^*(x, y_1)- r^*(x, y_2))$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic function and $r^*$ represents the ground-truth latent reward function. When we substitute our derived expression for $r^*$ into this preference probability equation, something remarkable happens: we substitute $\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$ for both $r^*(x, y_1)$ and $r^*(x, y_2)$. Because the preference probability depends only on the *difference* between these two rewards, and because both terms share the identical partition function component $\beta \log Z(x)$ (which depends only on $x$), this term cancels out perfectly in subtraction. Consequently, we are left with an expression for human preference probabilities that depends solely on the ratio of probabilities between pairs of completions under the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. The partition function $Z(x)$, which was previously considered intractable and difficult to estimate without sampling over all possible completions, vanishes from our objective entirely.

With the partition function eliminated and preferences expressed purely in terms of policy ratios, we can now formulate a direct training objective for our language model parameters $\theta$. We treat our parametrized language model $\pi_\theta$ as an approximation of this optimal policy $\pi^*$. By substituting $\pi_\theta$ into our simplified preference equation, we arrive at a likelihood function that measures how well our model's implicit reward ordering matches human preferences. Maximizing this likelihood is equivalent to minimizing its negative log-likelihood. This results in what we define as Direct Preference Optimization loss: $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$. Here, $(x, y_w, y_l)$ represents a sample from our dataset where $y_w$ is preferred over $y_l$. This loss function takes on exactly the form of binary cross-entropy used in standard classification tasks. It does not require sampling from $\pi_\theta$ during training loops (as RL methods do), nor does it require training a separate network to estimate rewards. Instead, it simply adjusts $\pi_\theta$ so that it assigns higher relative probability (compared to $\pi_\text{ref}$) to preferred responses than to dispreferred ones.

Finally, interpreting DPO through this lens reveals that "your language model is secretly a reward model." In traditional approaches, one network learns rewards and another learns policies; in DPO, these roles are unified within a single network architecture optimized via gradient descent on preference pairs. The gradient of this loss function provides further intuition: it pushes up log-probabilities for preferred completions and pulls them down for dispreferred ones relative to their baseline under $\pi_\text{ref}$. Crucially, these updates are weighted by how much current implicit rewards disagree with human labels; if the model already correctly ranks $y_w$ above $y_l$, its implicit reward difference will be positive and large enough such that its contribution to gradient updates diminishes via properties of logistic regression gradients. # Mechanistic Understanding: Gradients and Stability in DPO

## Analyzing the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{DPO}$ with respect to model parameters

To truly understand why Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) succeeds where other methods struggle, we must look beneath the surface of its loss function and examine the gradient flow that drives parameter updates. The DPO objective, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref})$, is formulated as a binary cross-entropy loss that operates on the log probability ratios of preferred and dispreferred responses relative to a reference policy. When we compute the gradient of this loss with respect to the model parameters $\theta$, we uncover a precise mechanism for how the model learns to align with human preferences. The gradient can be decomposed into three distinct components that work in concert: a weighting term, a positive update for preferred responses, and a negative update for dispreferred responses.

Mathematically, the gradient takes the form of an expectation over the dataset of preference pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$. The core of the update involves the difference between the gradients of the log probabilities of the winning response $y_w$ and the losing response $y_l$. Specifically, the term $\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)$ acts to increase the likelihood of generating the preferred completion, while $-\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)$ acts to decrease the likelihood of generating the dispreferred completion. This structure mirrors standard supervised learning objectives where we push probability mass toward correct answers and away from incorrect ones. However, in DPO, these updates are not applied uniformly; they are modulated by a sophisticated weighting factor derived from an implicit reward function. This implicit reward, denoted as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, represents how much more likely the current policy $\pi_\theta$ is to generate a response compared to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, scaled by a temperature parameter $\beta$.

The interaction between these components reveals that DPO is not merely maximizing a static reward but is dynamically adjusting its learning trajectory based on its current performance. The gradient effectively tells the model: "Increase your probability for $y_w$ and decrease it for $y_l$, but do so with an intensity proportional to how wrong your current implicit reward model is about their relative quality." If the model already correctly ranks $y_w$ higher than $y_l$ according to its implicit reward function, the weighting term diminishes, slowing down further updates. Conversely, if the model currently ranks $y_l$ higher than $y_w$, or if it fails to distinguish them sufficiently given the KL constraint strength $\beta$, the weighting term increases, forcing a stronger correction. This mechanism ensures that every update step is informed by both the direction of improvement (preferred vs. dispreferred) and the magnitude of error in the current policy's implicit valuation of those responses.

## The role of dynamic importance weighting in preventing model degeneration

A critical insight provided by analyzing the DPO gradient is the necessity of its dynamic importance weighting term to prevent model degeneration. In many naive approaches to preference learning, one might be tempted to simply maximize the log probability ratio between preferred and dispreferred responses without any modulation. Such an approach would essentially try to push $\pi_\theta(y_w|x)$ toward 1 and $\pi_\theta(y_l|x)$ toward 0 as aggressively as possible at every step. While this seems intuitive for maximizing preference alignment, it often leads to catastrophic failure modes known as mode collapse or degeneration. In these scenarios, the model becomes overly confident in specific token sequences that happen to be slightly favored in training data but are not robustly aligned with human intent across diverse contexts. It may also completely suppress valid but less frequent variations of high-quality responses, leading to repetitive and unnatural generations.

The dynamic importance weight in DPO acts as a stabilizer against this degeneration by scaling updates based on how "surprising" or "incorrect" a preference pair is under the current policy's implicit reward estimate. The weight is defined by $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))$, which represents a sigmoid function applied to the difference between the implicit rewards of losing and winning responses. When this difference is large (meaning $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) > \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w)$), indicating that the model currently values a bad response more than a good one relative to their reference probabilities, sigmoid approaches 1, applying maximum force to correct this error. However, as soon as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)$ exceeds $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l)$ sufficiently such that their difference becomes negative and large in magnitude (meaning $y_w$ is already highly favored), sigmoid approaches 0. This effectively stops further aggressive updates once a sufficient margin has been established between preferred and dispreferred responses relative to their baseline probabilities in $\pi_\text{ref}$.

This behavior prevents mode collapse because it respects an implicit boundary defined by reference policy probabilities scaled by $\beta$. The model does not simply drive all probability mass into a single "best" answer regardless of cost; rather, it seeks an equilibrium where preferred answers are favored *relative* to what was already possible under $\pi_\text{ref}$. If pushing probability too far toward $y_w$ would require deviating excessively from $\pi_\text{ref}$ (which would make $\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}$ very large), doing so would eventually make it difficult for other examples in different contexts to be satisfied without violating this balance across datasets. Empirical evidence suggests that removing this weighting coefficient leads directly to degenerate generations where models produce nonsensical or repetitive text optimized only for local preference signals without regard for global coherence or diversity. Thus, this weighting is not merely an optimization trick but a fundamental component that enforces stability by coupling preference alignment with distributional constraints inherent in KL-divergence minimization.

## Comparison with naive 'unlikelihood' training methods

To appreciate DPO's design choices fully, it is instructive to compare it against simpler baseline methods like 'unlikelihood' training or naive probability ratio maximization. Unlikelihood training typically involves constructing an objective that explicitly maximizes $\log \pi(y_w|x)$ while simultaneously minimizing $\log \pi(y_l|x)$ (or maximizing $-\log \pi(y_l|x)$). Often implemented with an optional coefficient $\alpha$ controlling how strongly dispreferred responses are penalized ($-\alpha \log \pi(y_l|x)$), this approach attempts to directly shape both sides of preference pairs through separate loss terms. While conceptually straightforward—pushing up good things and pushing down bad things—this method lacks any mechanism for balancing these forces against each other or against prior knowledge encoded in a reference distribution during training steps involving only preference data pairs $(x,y)$.

# Theoretical Guarantees and Equivalence to RLHF

The transition from Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) represents more than just a shift in engineering convenience; it is grounded in a rigorous theoretical framework that demonstrates the two approaches are fundamentally equivalent under specific conditions. While RLHF traditionally relies on a complex, multi-stage pipeline involving the explicit training of a reward model followed by reinforcement learning to optimize a policy, DPO achieves the same objective through a single-stage optimization of the policy itself. This chapter delves into the mathematical underpinnings that justify this simplification, proving that DPO does not sacrifice the expressive power of the reward model or the optimality of the resulting policy. By reparameterizing the reward function directly in terms of the policy and a reference model, we can show that DPO implicitly learns a reward function that belongs to the same equivalence class as those learned by standard RLHF methods, thereby guaranteeing that the optimal policy recovered is identical.

## Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

The central insight driving Direct Preference Optimization is encapsulated in its provocative title: "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model." In traditional RLHF, the reward function $r(x, y)$ and the policy $\pi(y \mid x)$ are treated as distinct entities. The reward model is trained first to approximate human preferences, and then a separate reinforcement learning algorithm adjusts the policy to maximize this reward while staying close to a reference distribution. However, theoretical analysis reveals that for any given optimal policy derived from a reward maximization problem with a KL-divergence constraint, there exists an implicit reward function defined entirely by that policy and the reference model. This relationship allows us to bypass the explicit construction of a standalone reward network.

To understand this equivalence, we must first define what it means for two reward functions to be "the same" in the context of preference learning. We introduce an equivalence relation where two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if their difference depends only on the prompt $x$ and not on the completion $y$. Formally, $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some arbitrary function $f$. This definition arises naturally from the structure of preference models like Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce. These models predict the probability of preferring one response over another based solely on the *difference* in their rewards. If we add an arbitrary function $f(x)$ to both rewards for a given prompt $x$, the difference remains unchanged: $(r(x, y_1) + f(x)) - (r(x, y_2) + f(x)) = r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)$. Consequently, any two equivalent reward functions induce exactly the same distribution over human preferences.

Furthermore, this equivalence extends to the optimal policies they generate. When solving the constrained optimization problem of maximizing expected reward while penalizing deviation from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ via KL-divergence, all reward functions within an equivalence class yield the exact same optimal policy $\pi^*$. This is because the KL-constrained objective effectively normalizes out any term that depends only on $x$. Therefore, when our goal is to find an optimal policy that aligns with human preferences, we do not need to recover the unique "true" ground-truth reward function $r^*$. Instead, it suffices to recover *any* member of the equivalence class consistent with the observed preference data. This realization is crucial because it implies that if we can parameterize our model such that it can represent any member of these equivalence classes without loss of generality, we can optimize directly for the policy without ever explicitly estimating or storing a separate reward function.

## Proof that all Plackett-Luce consistent reward classes are representable by DPO reparameterization

The theoretical validity of DPO rests on proving that its specific reparameterization of rewards is sufficiently expressive to capture every possible preference-consistent reward class allowed by Plackett-Luce models (which include Bradley-Terry as a special case). The standard RL objective seeks to maximize $\mathbb{E}[r] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi \mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}]$. It is a known result in control theory and reinforcement learning that the optimal solution to this problem takes an analytical form: $\pi_r(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$, where $Z(x)$ is a partition function ensuring probabilities sum to one. While this equation describes how an optimal policy relates to its underlying reward function, it is often difficult to use directly because calculating $Z(x)$ requires summing over all possible completions $y$, which is computationally intractable for language models with vast vocabularies and sequence lengths.

DPO circumvents this difficulty by algebraically rearranging this optimality condition to express the *reward* as a function of the *policy*. By taking the logarithm of both sides and isolating $r(x, y)$, we derive an expression: $r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here lies the critical theoretical leap: when we substitute this expression into a preference model like Bradley-Terry, which depends only on differences between rewards ($r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$), the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ cancels out completely because it is identical for both completions given prompt $x$. This cancellation leaves us with an expression for preference probability that depends solely on the ratio of probabilities between our target policy $\pi$ and our reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$.

The core theorem establishing DPO's validity states that under mild assumptions, *all* reward classes consistent with Plackett-Luce models can be represented using this reparameterization form: $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. To prove this without loss of generality regarding expressiveness, consider any arbitrary reward function $r$ from an equivalence class consistent with observed preferences. We can define a projection operator that normalizes this arbitrary reward by subtracting its associated partition function term (scaled by $\beta$). Specifically, we define $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. Since subtracted term depends only on $x$, this new function belongs to the same equivalence class as our original arbitrary $r$. By substituting our optimality condition back into this projection operator algebraically simplifies exactly to $\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Thus, for every valid preference-consistent reward class there exists a corresponding policy $\pi$ such that DPO's implicit parameterization recovers a representative member of that class. This proves that restricting our search space to policies parameterized by DPO does not exclude any potential solutions; we are not limiting what we can learn about human preferences compared to standard RLHF methods.

## The relationship between DPO's implicit reward and explicit reward models

Having established representability, it is essential to clarify how DPO's "implicit" reward relates to traditional "explicit" reward models trained via maximum likelihood on preference data. In standard RLHF pipelines like PPO-based approaches described in prior literature (e.g., Ziegler et al., Stiennon et al.), one first trains an explicit neural network $r_\phi(x,y)$ by minimizing a binary cross-entropy loss derived from human comparisons (the Bradley-Terry loss). This explicit model attempts to predict scalar values representing how much humans prefer one answer over another. Once trained, this model serves as a fixed target or signal for subsequent reinforcement learning steps. However, estimating this explicit model introduces variance and requires careful regularization (such as normalizing rewards so their mean over data is zero) to ensure stability during training.

In contrast, DPO does not train an explicit scalar-valued network separate from the language model generation process. Instead, every time step during DPO training updates both parameters simultaneously: they update $\theta$ (the parameters defining $\pi_\theta$), which implicitly updates $\hat{r}_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. This implicit reward function acts as if it were being optimized directly against human preferences through gradient descent on a binary cross-entropy objective formulated over policies rather than rewards. The key distinction lies in how normalization is handled. In explicit RLHF methods using actor-critic algorithms like PPO dealing with high-variance gradients often necessitates learning or estimating value functions or using baselines (like Monte Carlo estimates of average rewards) to stabilize training against mode collapse or divergence from reference distributions. These baselines attempt to approximate terms related to partition functions or value functions which are otherwise difficult to compute exactly due to discrete action spaces in language generation.
