# Chapter 1: The Challenge of Aligning Large Language Models

Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, acquiring broad world knowledge and reasoning skills through training on massive datasets of human-generated text. Models trained in this manner can perform complex tasks such as answering questions, writing code, and engaging in conversation with surprising fluency. However, the unsupervised nature of this pre-training presents a fundamental challenge: while these models possess a vast array of abilities and knowledge, they lack precise control over their behavior. Because the training data encompasses a wide variety of human goals, priorities, and skillsets—some of which may be undesirable—the model inevitably learns to mimic patterns that are not always safe or helpful. For instance, a coding assistant might learn to recognize common programming errors to correct them, yet without intervention, it may default to generating code that reflects frequent mistakes rather than the rare instances of high-quality engineering present in its training data. Similarly, a model might become aware of a widespread misconception held by half the population; without alignment, it risks asserting this falsehood as truth in fifty percent of relevant queries. Consequently, selecting the desired responses and behaviors from the model's extensive but indiscriminate knowledge base is critical for building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable.

The core difficulty lies in distinguishing between what a model *knows* and what it should *do*. Unsupervised pre-training optimizes for predicting the next token in a sequence based on statistical likelihoods found in the internet corpus. This process does not inherently prioritize safety, honesty, or helpfulness as defined by modern ethical standards or specific user intents. Instead, it creates a distribution over outputs that mirrors the aggregate behavior of humanity as recorded in text data. To transform such a general-purpose engine into a specialized tool that adheres to specific human preferences—such as avoiding harmful content or providing concise summaries—we must intervene after pre-training. This intervention requires moving beyond simple pattern matching to active steering mechanisms that can guide the model away from statistically probable but undesirable outputs toward those that align with human judgment. Without such alignment procedures, even highly capable models remain unpredictable agents that cannot reliably serve specific user needs or operate safely in sensitive domains.

To address these limitations, researchers have developed frameworks designed to steer language models using curated sets of human preferences. The prevailing methodology for achieving this alignment is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which attempts to teach the model to maximize a reward signal derived from human choices between different model generations. This approach acknowledges that while collecting absolute scores for every possible output is difficult and subjective, humans are remarkably good at judging relative quality—that is, deciding whether response A is better than response B given a specific prompt. By leveraging these comparative judgments, RLHF seeks to shape the model's policy so that it consistently generates responses that humans prefer over alternatives. However, despite its success in creating conversational assistants with impressive abilities, the RLHF pipeline introduces significant complexity compared to standard supervised learning methods. It involves multiple distinct stages of training separate neural networks and requires intricate loops where the model samples its own outputs during training to calculate gradients for improvement.

The standard RLHF pipeline typically consists of three sequential phases: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), Reward Modeling, and RL Optimization. The process begins with SFT, where a pre-trained language model is fine-tuned using supervised learning on high-quality human demonstrations for specific downstream tasks like dialogue or summarization. This step produces an initial policy, often denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$, which serves as a starting point closer to human-like behavior than the raw pre-trained model but still lacks nuanced alignment with preferences. In the second phase, known as Reward Modeling or Preference Sampling and Learning, this SFT model generates pairs of candidate responses $(y_1, y_2)$ for various prompts $x$. Human labelers then review these pairs and indicate their preference for one response over the other ($y_w \succ y_l$). These labeled comparisons are used to train a separate neural network reward function $r_\phi(x,y)$ via maximum likelihood estimation under a probabilistic preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model. This reward model acts as a surrogate for human judgment, assigning scalar scores to text sequences intended to reflect their desirability relative to others seen during training.

Once an explicit reward function has been learned from human preferences enters the third phase: RL Optimization. Here, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is updated using reinforcement learning algorithms to maximize the expected reward provided by $r_\phi$. Crucially, this optimization is constrained by a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty against a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ (usually the SFT model). The objective function seeks to balance maximizing reward with staying close enough to the original distribution to prevent "mode collapse," where the model degenerates into repeating short phrases or nonsensical tokens that happen to score high on the imperfect reward function but fail semantically. Mathematically, this involves solving an optimization problem where one maximizes $\mathbb{E}[r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta || \pi_\text{ref}]$. Because language generation is discrete and non-differentiable regarding token selection probabilities directly affecting long-term outcomes without sampling gradients cannot flow back through standard backpropagation easily necessitates complex actor-critic architectures like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). # Chapter 2: Mathematical Foundations of Preference Learning

The transition from unsupervised language models to systems that exhibit specific, human-aligned behaviors relies fundamentally on a rigorous mathematical framework for interpreting and optimizing preferences. While large-scale unsupervised models acquire vast amounts of world knowledge, they lack the intrinsic mechanism to distinguish between desirable and undesirable outputs without external guidance. To bridge this gap, researchers have developed a pipeline that translates subjective human judgments into quantifiable signals that can drive model updates. This chapter establishes the theoretical bedrock for this process, moving from the statistical modeling of pairwise comparisons to the formulation of constrained optimization problems that balance performance with behavioral stability. By understanding these foundations, one gains the necessary intuition to grasp why traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is complex and how newer methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) reframe these equations to simplify the training process.

## The Bradley-Terry model for pairwise preferences

At the heart of modern preference learning lies the need to formalize how humans judge the relative quality of two different model outputs. Unlike supervised learning, where a single correct label exists for an input, preference learning deals with comparative data: given a prompt $x$ and two candidate responses $y_1$ and $y_2$, a human annotator indicates which response they prefer, denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the "winner" (preferred) and $y_l$ is the "loser" (dispreferred). To convert these binary choices into a probabilistic framework suitable for machine learning, we rely on the Bradley-Terry model. This statistical model posits that there exists an underlying latent utility or score associated with every possible response, and the probability of preferring one response over another is determined solely by the relative magnitudes of these scores.

Mathematically, let us assume there is a true, albeit hidden, reward function $r^*(x, y)$ that assigns a scalar value representing the quality of response $y$ given prompt $x$. The Bradley-Terry model asserts that the probability of observing a preference for $y_1$ over $y_2$ follows a sigmoidal distribution based on the difference in their rewards. Specifically, if we denote this probability as $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x)$, it is defined as:

$$
p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
$$

This equation reveals an intuitive property: if $r^*(x, y_1)$ is significantly larger than $r^*(x, y_2)$, the numerator dominates the denominator, driving the probability close to 1. Conversely, if the rewards are equal, the probability settles at 0.5. By utilizing exponentials in both terms, the model ensures that probabilities remain bounded between 0 and 1 regardless of how large or small the raw reward values become. This formulation effectively transforms ordinal preference data—who won against whom—into a continuous space where distances between items correspond to differences in perceived quality. While more complex ranking models like Plackett-Luce exist for scenarios involving lists of more than two items, the Bradley-Terry model serves as the canonical foundation for pairwise comparison tasks in language model alignment due to its simplicity and sufficiency for capturing relative human judgment.

## Formulating preference probabilities as a function of latent rewards

Once we accept that human preferences can be modeled through latent rewards via the Bradley-Terry framework, we must address how to operationalize this concept within a machine learning architecture. The core challenge is that we never observe the actual reward values $r^*(x, y)$; we only observe outcomes where one response was chosen over another. Therefore, our goal becomes estimating a parametric approximation of this reward function that best explains the observed preference data. To facilitate this estimation within standard deep learning frameworks, it is beneficial to rewrite the Bradley-Terry probability expression using properties of logarithms and exponential functions to isolate the difference between rewards.

By dividing both the numerator and denominator of Equation (1) by $\exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)$ and recognizing that $\frac{\exp(a)}{\exp(b)} = \exp(a-b)$, we can reformulate the preference probability entirely in terms of reward differences:

$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left[r^*(x, y_w) - r^*(x, y_l)\right]\right)}.
$$

Recognizing that $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$ is the standard logistic sigmoid function used extensively in binary classification tasks allows us to express this relationship compactly as:

$$
p^*(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\left(r^*(x, y_w) - r^*(x, y_l)\right).
$$

This transformation is pivotal because it recasts preference learning as a binary classification problem where the target variable is whether $y_w$ was preferred over $y_l$, and features are derived from a learned reward network evaluating both candidates independently before taking their difference. The term inside the sigmoid function acts as a margin; if our learned reward model correctly assigns a higher score to $y_w$ than to $y_l$, then $r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$ will be positive large numbers pushing $\sigma(\cdot)$ towards 1. If it fails or reverses them predictably incorrectly predicting preferences—the output will be closer to 0 or below 0 depending on severity—indicating poor calibration with human intent. # Chapter 3: Deriving Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

The fundamental challenge in aligning large language models with human values lies in the complexity of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). Traditional pipelines require a multi-stage process: first, training a separate reward model to predict human preferences, and second, employing complex reinforcement learning algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to update the language model policy to maximize this reward while staying close to a reference distribution. This process is computationally expensive, unstable, and prone to failure modes such as reward hacking or mode collapse. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) offers a radical simplification by demonstrating that the language model itself contains all the necessary information to act as a reward model. By leveraging an analytical solution for the optimal policy under KL-constrained reward maximization, we can bypass the explicit training of a reward model entirely. Instead of treating the reward as an external signal to be maximized via sampling, DPO treats the policy parameters themselves as the direct variables of optimization, transforming the problem into a standard supervised learning task solvable with binary cross-entropy loss. This chapter details the mathematical derivation of this transformation, explaining how a change of variables allows us to recover the optimal policy in closed form and why this approach is theoretically sound and practically superior.

# Analytical Solution for Optimal Policy under KL-Constrained Reward Maximization

To understand how DPO eliminates the need for reinforcement learning loops, we must first revisit the standard objective function used in RLHF. The goal of alignment is to find a policy $\pi_\theta$ that generates responses $y$ given prompts $x$ which maximize an expected reward derived from human preferences, subject to a constraint that prevents the model from drifting too far from its original capabilities. Mathematically, this is formulated as maximizing the expected reward minus a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty relative to a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, typically a Supervised Fine-Tuned (SFT) model. The optimization problem is expressed as:

$$
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr]
$$

Here, $r(x, y)$ represents the latent reward function reflecting human preferences, $\beta$ is a temperature-like hyperparameter controlling the strength of the regularization term, and $\mathcal{D}$ is the dataset of prompts. The KL divergence term ensures that while we seek high-reward outputs, we maintain fidelity to the reference distribution $\pi_\text{ref}$, thereby preserving linguistic fluency and avoiding catastrophic forgetting or degenerate outputs like repeating tokens indefinitely. In traditional RLHF setups, solving this optimization problem requires iterative sampling because the expectation involves summing over all possible sequences $y$, which is computationally intractable for large vocabulary spaces and long contexts. Consequently, practitioners rely on approximate gradient ascent methods like PPO that require extensive hyperparameter tuning and stable actor-critic architectures.

However, if we treat this as a variational optimization problem over probability distributions rather than relying on stochastic approximation via sampling, an exact analytical solution exists. By applying Lagrange multipliers to enforce the probability simplex constraint (that probabilities must sum to one), it can be proven that for any given reward function $r(x,y)$ and reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, there exists a unique optimal policy $\pi_r$ that solves this maximization problem exactly. This optimal policy takes a specific Boltzmann-like form:

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
$$

In this equation, $Z(x)$ acts as a normalization constant or partition function required to ensure that $\sum_y \pi_r(y|x) = 1$. Explicitly defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$, this term sums over all possible continuations $y$ weighted by their exponential rewards relative to the reference policy. While this formula provides a perfect theoretical characterization of what an ideal aligned agent looks like for any fixed reward function $r$, it presents two immediate practical hurdles for implementation. First, calculating $Z(x)$ requires summing over an exponentially large space of possible token sequences, rendering it impossible to compute exactly during training. Second, traditional RLHF methods attempt to learn $r$ separately and then try to approximate this target distribution through trial-and-error sampling. DPO sidesteps both issues by recognizing that we do not actually need to know $r$ explicitly if our ultimate goal is simply to find $\pi_r$. If we can invert this relationship—expressing $r$ in terms of $\pi_r$—we can substitute it directly into our preference modeling equations before ever needing to calculate difficult expectations or partition functions numerically during training iterations.

# Reparameterization Technique: Expressing Reward as a Function of Policy Ratios

The central innovation enabling DPO is a change of variables that re-expresses the latent reward function not as an independent entity learned by a neural network head attached to the transformer architecture but as an implicit quantity defined entirely by the ratio between the current policy and the reference policy. Starting from the analytical solution derived previously:

$$
\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
$$

We can algebraically manipulate this expression to isolate $r(x,y)$. Taking the natural logarithm of both sides yields:

$$
\log \pi_r(y\mid x) = -\log Z(x) + \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x) + \frac{1}{β}r(x,y)
$$

By multiplying through by $\beta$ and rearranging terms to solve for $r(x,y)$, we arrive at a critical identity:

$$
r(x,y) = β \log \frac{\pi_r(y| x)}{\pi_\text_ref(y| x)} + β \log Z(x)
$$

This equation reveals that any optimal policy $\pi_r$ implies an underlying reward structure where the "true" value assigned to a response $(x,y)$ consists of two components: a term dependent on how much more likely $\pi_r$ finds $(x,y)$ compared to $\pi_\text{ref}$ (the log-ratio), scaled by $\beta$, plus an additive offset determined solely by $Z(x)$ which depends only on the prompt $x$. Crucially, in many preference learning frameworks such as Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce models used in RLHF predictions depend only on *differences* in rewards between candidate responses conditioned on the same prompt $x$. Because both candidates share identical context $x$, they also share identical partition functions $Z(x)$. When taking differences between rewards for two different responses $y_1$ and $y_2$, these additive constants cancel out perfectly:

$$
r^*(x,y_1) - r^*(x,y_2) = β \left( \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_\text_ref(y_1|x)} - \log \frac{\π^*(y_2|x)}{\π_\text_ref(y_2|x)} \right)
$$

This cancellation property implies that we never actually need to estimate or compute $Z(x)$ explicitly if our objective relies only on pairwise comparisons or relative rankings. The "secret" revealed here is profound: every language model parameterized by weights $\theta$, when viewed alongside its frozen reference counterpart $\pi_\text{ref}$ implicitly defines its own unique reward landscape via this log-ratio formulation. We do not need a separate network outputting scalar scores; the logits produced by decoding tokens already encode these relative valuations once normalized against their pre-alignment counterparts. By adopting this perspective—that our trainable parameters define both generation probabilities and implicit rewards simultaneously—we transform an ill-conditioned joint optimization problem into one # Chapter 4: Theoretical Properties and Stability Analysis

## Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

The central theoretical breakthrough of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) lies in recognizing that a language model policy and a reward function are not merely distinct components of a pipeline, but are fundamentally interchangeable representations under specific conditions. In traditional Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), the process is bifurcated: first, a separate neural network is trained to approximate a latent reward function $r(x, y)$ based on human preferences, and second, an agent (the language model) is trained via reinforcement learning to maximize this reward while staying close to a reference policy. DPO collapses these two stages into one by leveraging an analytical mapping that reveals the language model itself contains all the necessary information to act as the reward model. This insight stems from the observation that for any reward function $r(x, y)$ subject to a KL-divergence constraint against a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, there exists a unique optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ derived in closed form. By reversing this relationship, we can express the reward function entirely in terms of the ratio between the current policy and the reference policy. Consequently, optimizing the policy directly using preference data is mathematically equivalent to optimizing a re-parameterized reward function. This duality implies that we do not need to explicitly construct or store a separate reward model; the parameters of the language model $\pi_\theta$ implicitly encode the reward landscape required to satisfy human preferences.

To formalize this relationship, we must first establish what it means for two different mathematical objects to represent the "same" underlying preference structure. In the context of preference learning using models like Bradley-Terry or Plackett-Luce, absolute reward values are less important than relative differences. If we add an arbitrary constant or a function dependent solely on the prompt $x$ to every reward associated with that prompt, the relative ordering of responses—and thus the predicted probability of preferring one response over another—remains unchanged. Formally, we define an equivalence relation between two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ such that they are considered equivalent if their difference depends only on the input prompt $x$, expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for some arbitrary function $f$. This definition partitions the infinite space of possible reward functions into disjoint equivalence classes. Within any single class, every member induces identical preference distributions over pairs of outputs. Furthermore, when solving the constrained RL problem where we maximize expected reward minus a KL penalty against $\pi_\text{ref}$, all members of an equivalence class lead to exactly the same optimal policy. This redundancy suggests that attempting to recover a specific "true" ground-truth reward function is unnecessary; instead, our goal is simply to identify *any* representative from the correct equivalence class that generates human-consistent preferences and yields an optimal policy.

The power of DPO emerges when we realize that our proposed reparameterization naturally selects a canonical representative from each equivalence class without requiring external constraints or auxiliary networks. By defining the implicit reward as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, we effectively normalize out the dependency on $x$ that causes non-uniqueness in standard formulations. When substituted back into the Bradley-Terry equation for preference probabilities, any additive term depending only on $x$ cancels out perfectly in the numerator and denominator of the softmax-like expression used to calculate win probabilities. Therefore, whether we optimize an explicit neural network for rewards or optimize $\pi_\theta$ directly via DPO's binary cross-entropy loss, we are traversing the same landscape of preference consistency. The language model $\pi_\theta$ is not just approximating a policy; through its logits relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$, it is simultaneously acting as a fully functional reward model capable of distinguishing preferred from dispreferred outputs with perfect fidelity provided sufficient capacity and data.

## Lemma: Identifiability Issues in Plackett-Luce/Brunell-Terry Models and Normalization

A critical challenge in learning from pairwise preferences arises from the inherent under-specification of models like Bradley-Terry and Plackett-Luce. Because these models rely exclusively on ratios or differences between scores (rewards), they cannot uniquely determine absolute values without imposing artificial constraints. For instance, if $r(x, y)$ fits a dataset perfectly, then adding any constant vector dependent only on $x$, say $c(x)$, creates a new function $r'(x, y) = r(x, y) + c(x)$ that predicts exactly identical probabilities for every comparison in history yet assigns completely different absolute scores to individual tokens or sequences. This phenomenon creates an identifiability problem: without fixing these degrees of freedom via regularization or manual normalization (such as forcing mean rewards to zero across contexts), maximum likelihood estimation may converge slowly or fail to settle on meaningful parameters because infinitely many solutions exist with equal likelihood scores. Traditional RLHF pipelines address this by manually normalizing rewards during training or relying on heuristics within reinforcement learning algorithms like PPO to stabilize value estimates indirectly.

However, DPO resolves this identifiability issue elegantly through its structural design rather than ad-hoc constraints. The reparameterization formula derived earlier links any valid optimal policy directly to its corresponding implicit reward via $\beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. This formulation inherently enforces a specific normalization condition tied to probabilistic validity. Recall that for $\pi(y \mid x)$ to be a valid probability distribution over all possible completions $y$, it must sum to one: $\sum_y \pi(y \mid x) = 1$. Substituting our reparameterized form into this summation constraint reveals that it forces the partition function—the normalizing term usually required in Boltzmann-style distributions—to equal unity when viewed through the lens of this specific parameterization. Mathematically, this imposes that $\sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right) = 1$. This condition acts as an intrinsic anchor for every equivalence class of rewards; among all possible functions differing by an arbitrary offset $f(x)$ within an equivalence class, only one satisfies this strict normalization requirement imposed by probability axioms. Thus, DPO automatically selects this unique representative without needing external normalization procedures or manual intervention during training iterations.
