\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Intuition behind why the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out in the DPO derivation
Question:
I am trying to follow the derivation in Section 4 of 'Direct Preference Optimization'. The authors start with the optimal policy equation:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

They then rearrange this to express the reward as:

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

When substituting this back into the Bradley-Terry preference probability $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ disappears. 

My confusion is twofold: 
1. Why does $Z(x)$ depend only on $x$ and not on $y$? If it depended on $y$, wouldn't it remain in the subtraction?
2. Conceptually, does this mean that the absolute magnitude of the reward doesn't matter for preferences, only the relative difference? Does this imply that any constant offset added to a reward function (dependent on $x$) is invisible to the preference model?
Answer:
The cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ occurs precisely because it acts as a **normalization constant** that depends exclusively on the prompt $x$ and is independent of the specific completion $y$. Mathematically, $Z(x)$ is defined as the sum over all possible outputs weighted by the reference policy and the exponential reward: $Z(x) = \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y')\right)$. Since this summation aggregates probabilities across *all* potential responses for a fixed context $x$, the resulting value is invariant to which specific candidate $y_1$ or $y_2$ you are currently evaluating. When you substitute the reward expression into the Bradley-Terry preference probability, you calculate the difference between two rewards: $r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)$. Because both terms contain the identical additive component $\beta \log Z(x)$, they subtract perfectly to zero ($\beta \log Z(x) - \beta \log Z(x) = 0$), leaving only the terms dependent on the policy ratios.

Conceptually, this confirms your intuition that **absolute reward magnitudes are irrelevant for pairwise preferences**; only relative differences matter. The Bradley-Terry model (and its generalizations like Plackett-Luce) operates on rank ordering rather than absolute scores. Adding any arbitrary function of the context alone, such as a constant offset or a term dependent solely on $x$, to a reward function constitutes an "equivalence transformation." As formalized in Definition 1 and Lemma 1 of the paper, two reward functions differing only by a term $f(x)$ belong to the same equivalence class and induce identical preference distributions. Consequently, any constant offset added to a reward function that varies with $x$ but not with $y$ is indeed invisible to the preference model. This property is what allows DPO to bypass the notoriously difficult estimation of the partition function entirely: by optimizing directly for the policy ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, DPO implicitly learns a representative from this equivalence class where the normalization term effectively vanishes from the objective function.
