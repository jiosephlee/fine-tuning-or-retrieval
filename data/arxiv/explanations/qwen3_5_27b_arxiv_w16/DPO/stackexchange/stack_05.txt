\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Interpretation of 'Implicit Reward' degeneracy mentioned in Appendix Table references
Question:
In Section 4 ('What does the DPO update do?'), there is a discussion about how removing the weighting coefficient (related to how much worse $\\hat{r}_	heta$ ranks $y_l$ compared to $y_w$) causes model degeneration. The text refers to Appendix Table regarding 'unlikelihood generations'.

Specifically, if we just tried to maximize $\\\sigma(	ext{something proportional to } \\\log \\\frac{\\\pi_	heta(y_w)}{\\\pi_	heta(y_l)})$, why would this fail compared to including $\\\log \\\frac{\\\pi_	heta}{\\\pi_{ref}}$? Does omitting $\\pi_{ref}$ remove any concept of 'cost' for changing probabilities, leading to mode collapse where $\\theta$ assigns probability 1 to any token sequence seen once as preferred?
Answer:
The degeneracy described in the paper arises because omitting the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ removes the essential regularization mechanism that prevents **mode collapse**, effectively turning the optimization into an unconstrained maximization of likelihood for specific tokens.

In the DPO formulation, the implicit reward is defined as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$. This structure inherently penalizes changes to the policy that deviate too far from the reference distribution, acting as a "cost" for shifting probability mass. If you were to simplify the objective to merely maximize $\sigma(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_\theta(y_l)})$—effectively treating it as a standard binary classification on logits without the denominator—the model loses this constraint. Without $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, there is no penalty for assigning arbitrarily high probability to $y_w$ and driving $y_l$ (and potentially all other sequences) to zero probability.

As noted in Section 4 ("What does the DPO update do?"), the full DPO gradient includes a weighting term dependent on how much worse the model currently ranks $y_l$ compared to $y_w$. More critically, the presence of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ensures that the model cannot simply assign probability 1 to any sequence it sees labeled as preferred, regardless of whether that sequence was likely under the original training distribution. If you remove $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the optimizer finds it easiest to satisfy the preference condition by collapsing the entire output distribution onto a single token sequence or a very narrow set of high-probability outputs seen in the training data, ignoring fluency or diversity. This phenomenon is what leads to "unlikelihood generations" mentioned in Appendix Table 1, where models trained without this regularization produce nonsensical or repetitive text because they have collapsed into modes that technically satisfy the pairwise comparison but lack semantic coherence. The reference model acts as an anchor, ensuring that improvements in preference alignment come from refining existing capabilities rather than inventing new, degenerate distributions.
