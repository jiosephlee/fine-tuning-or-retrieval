\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Clarification on the role of $\beta$ in DPO vs. standard RLHF KL penalty
Question:
In standard RLHF (Eq. 3 in the paper), there is a hyperparameter $\beta$ multiplying the KL divergence term: $- \beta D_{KL}[...|...]$. In the derived DPO loss (Eq. 7), $\beta$ appears inside the sigmoid argument as a multiplier for the log-prob ratios.

I am confused about how these two roles connect. In RLHF, if I increase $\beta$, I penalize deviation from the reference model more heavily. In DPO, increasing $\beta$ scales up the log-ratio differences before passing them to the sigmoid.

Does a higher $\beta$ in DPO effectively act as a stronger regularization towards $\pi_{ref}$? Or does it just make the model more confident in distinguishing between winning and losing examples? The paper mentions that a naive version without proper weighting causes degeneration; is this weighting mechanism solely responsible for mimicking the KL constraint?
Answer:
The hyperparameter $\beta$ serves the exact same conceptual purpose in both the standard RLHF formulation and the derived DPO loss: it controls the strictness of the regularization constraint that keeps the learned policy $\pi_\theta$ close to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In standard RLHF (Equation 3), $\beta$ acts as a Lagrange multiplier scaling the KL divergence penalty, meaning a higher $\beta$ imposes a heavier cost for deviating from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, thereby forcing the model to stay closer to its original distribution.

In DPO, this role is preserved but manifests differently due to the change of variables that eliminates the explicit reward model. By substituting the optimal policy form (Equation 5) into the Bradley-Terry preference model, the authors derive Equation 7, where $\beta$ scales the log-probability ratios inside the sigmoid function. Mathematically, increasing $\beta$ in DPO sharpens the decision boundary required for a preference pair to be satisfied. If $\beta$ is large, even small deviations in the log-ratio difference between preferred ($y_w$) and dispreferred ($y_l$) responses result in a saturated sigmoid output, effectively punishing any configuration where the model does not assign significantly higher probability to $y_w$ relative to $y_l$ *after* accounting for their probabilities under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Consequently, a higher $\beta$ in DPO indeed enforces stronger regularization towards $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, preventing the model from overfitting to specific preference examples by arbitrarily inflating probabilities (a phenomenon known as "mode collapse" or degeneration mentioned in Section 4).

The intuition connecting these two views lies in Equation 6, which defines an implicit reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here, $\beta$ dictates how much "reward credit" is granted for shifting probability mass away from the reference model. When you increase $\beta$, you are effectively saying that moving away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is costly unless it yields a massive improvement in satisfying human preferences. The weighting mechanism inside DPO's sigmoid is not merely making the model more confident; it is dynamically adjusting how aggressively the model updates its parameters based on how far it has already drifted from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Without this specific scaling by $\beta$, as noted in Section 4 regarding "naïve versions," the optimization would lack this stabilizing force and likely lead to degenerate outputs where the model assigns near-zero probability to everything except a narrow set of memorized high-reward tokens. Thus, whether viewed as a KL penalty coefficient or a log-ratio scaler, $\beta$ fundamentally governs the trade-off between maximizing preference satisfaction and maintaining proximity to the reference distribution.
