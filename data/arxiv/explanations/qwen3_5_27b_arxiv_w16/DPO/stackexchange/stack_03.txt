\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Understanding 'Equivalence Classes' of Reward Functions in Theorem 1
Question:
Section 5 introduces an equivalence relation where two rewards are equivalent if their difference is a function of only $x$: $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$.
Theorem 1 claims that *all* reward classes consistent with Bradley-Terry can be represented by the reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$.

I don't quite grasp why this reparameterization covers *every* possible equivalence class. It seems like we are forcing every reward function to look like a log-prob ratio plus some constant (the partition function part). Are there theoretical cases where a human preference structure exists that cannot be mapped onto *any* policy $\\pi$ via this specific formula? What happens if the underlying human preferences violate the assumptions required for this mapping to hold?
Answer:
The reason the reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ covers every possible equivalence class lies in the specific definition of equivalence used in the paper and the solvability of the optimal policy equation. You are correct that adding an arbitrary function $f(x)$ to a reward does not change the resulting preferences or the optimal policy under the Bradley-Terry model; this creates an equivalence class where all members behave identically regarding human choices. Theorem 1 asserts that within *any* such class, there exists at least one specific reward function that can be expressed exactly as a log-ratio of probabilities relative to a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

The intuition comes from reversing the standard RLHF derivation. We know from Equation 3 in the paper that for *any* reward function $r$, the optimal policy $\pi_r$ that maximizes expected reward subject to a KL constraint is given by $\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$. If we treat this equation as a system where we want to solve for $r$, we can take the logarithm and rearrange terms to get $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here, $\beta \log Z(x)$ acts precisely as the function $f(x)$ mentioned in your question. Since two rewards are equivalent if they differ only by a function of $x$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ places this derived reward in the same equivalence class as any other reward that generates the same optimal policy $\pi_r$. By choosing to drop this $x$-dependent term (effectively setting it to zero or absorbing it into the definition), we select a canonical representative for that entire class: specifically, the one where the partition function is normalized such that it disappears from the relative comparison.

Therefore, there are no theoretical cases where a human preference structure consistent with Bradley-Terry cannot be mapped onto this formula, provided an optimal policy exists for those preferences. The mapping does not force human preferences to look like log-prob ratios; rather, it proves that *if* human preferences follow Bradley-Terry, then there must exist a latent reward structure—and consequently an optimal policy—that fits this exact form. If underlying human preferences violated the Bradley-Terry assumptions (e.g., exhibiting cyclic intransitivity like Rock-Paper-Scissors), then no global scalar reward function $r(x,y)$ would exist to begin with, and neither traditional RLHF nor DPO could perfectly model them. However, within the scope of models where a scalar reward explains preferences (the domain of Theorem 1), DPO's parameterization is fully expressive because it effectively solves for the unique member of each equivalence class that satisfies the normalization condition implied by being a valid probability distribution derived from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
