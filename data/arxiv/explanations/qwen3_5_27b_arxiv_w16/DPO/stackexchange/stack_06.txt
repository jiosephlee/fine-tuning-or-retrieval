\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Handling distribution shift when no SFT model is available for initialization
Question:
The paper notes in Section 4 ('DPO outline') that if an SFT model isn't available (e.g., using raw pre-trained weights), they initialize $\\pi_{ref}$ by maximizing likelihood on preferred completions ($y_w$).

Mathematically, if I create a new reference model by fine-tuning on only winners, isn't my starting point already biased towards high-reward outputs? When I then run DPO with this new reference, am I double-counting preferences or creating an overly narrow distribution from which I try to expand? How does this differ fundamentally from just doing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)? At what point does DPO stop looking like SFT and start acting like preference optimization?
Answer:
Your concern about "double-counting" or creating an overly narrow distribution when initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on only preferred completions ($y_w$) stems from a misunderstanding of the distinct roles played by the reference model and the policy model in the DPO objective. Crucially, DPO does not optimize the absolute probability of $y_w$; rather, it optimizes the *ratio* of probabilities between the winning and losing responses relative to the reference.

When you initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on $y_w$ (effectively performing a lightweight SFT on winners), you are shifting the baseline distribution closer to the desired behavior to mitigate distribution shift, as noted in Section 4. However, this does not mean you are "double-counting" preferences because the DPO loss function (Eq. 7) explicitly subtracts the log-probability of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from that of your target policy $\pi_\theta$. Mathematically, the objective depends on $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}$. If both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assign high probability to $y_w$, their log-ratio approaches zero, contributing little to the gradient. The optimization signal only arises when $\pi_\theta$ assigns a significantly higher relative probability to $y_w$ compared to $y_l$ *than* what $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already does. Therefore, even if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is biased toward winners, DPO still pushes $\pi_\theta$ to prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$ more strongly than that baseline, effectively learning from the *contrast* provided by the dispreferred examples ($y_l$) which are absent from your reference initialization.

This process differs fundamentally from pure Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) because SFT ignores $y_l$ entirely and drives $\pi_\theta(y_w)$ toward 1 regardless of how likely $y_l$ is, often leading to mode collapse or excessive memorization. In contrast, DPO retains a dynamic penalty for assigning high probability to $y_l$. The transition from "looking like SFT" to "acting like preference optimization" happens immediately upon introducing the dispreferred examples into the loss calculation. While SFT seeks to maximize $P(y_w|x)$, DPO seeks to maximize the implicit reward gap defined by Eq. 3: $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + C$. By anchoring this reward definition against a reference that has seen only winners, you are essentially telling the model: "You already know how good these winners look (thanks to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$), now learn specifically how much worse these losers are." This preserves diversity and prevents degeneration better than SFT alone, as confirmed by the authors' observation that naive objectives without this specific weighting mechanism lead to degradation (Section 4). Thus, initializing on winners serves primarily as a stability measure for out-of-domain references without negating the core preference-learning mechanism driven by pairwise comparisons.
