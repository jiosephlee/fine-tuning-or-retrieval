\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Practical implication: Why do we need $\\pi_{ref}$ frozen during DPO training?
Question:
The paper defines DPO as optimizing $\\pi_	heta$ while keeping $\\pi_{ref}$ fixed (usually initialized as $\\pi^{SFT}$). The loss function explicitly calculates ratios like $\\frac{\\pi_	heta(y_w|x)}{\\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)}$.

If I were to treat $\\pi_{ref}$ as trainable alongside $\\pi_	heta$, wouldn't that just revert to training a standard reward model and policy simultaneously? More importantly, why is it critical that $\\pi_{ref}$ remains static? If $\\pi_	heta$ updates and moves away from $\\pi_{ref}$, does that mathematically correspond to accumulating 'reward'? Is there a risk of circular logic here where moving away from reference automatically inflates the implicit reward regardless of actual quality?
Answer:
Freezing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is fundamentally required because it serves as the immutable anchor for the implicit reward function, ensuring that the optimization problem remains well-defined and mathematically equivalent to the standard RLHF objective. In Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), the reward is not an independent neural network but is derived implicitly via the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. If you were to allow $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to update alongside $\pi_\theta$, you would break this specific mapping. The DPO loss function, which is a binary cross-entropy over preference pairs, relies on the cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ when taking the difference between rewards for winning ($y_w$) and losing ($y_l$) responses. This cancellation only holds if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is treated as a constant baseline; if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ changes, the term $\log Z(x)$ would become dependent on the evolving policy in a way that invalidates the closed-form solution derived from the Bradley-Terry model.

Regarding your concern about circular logic or artificially inflating rewards: freezing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ actually prevents this degeneracy rather than causing it. The quantity $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$ represents the *change* in log-probability relative to a fixed starting point. If both models moved freely, they could potentially converge to identical distributions or drift together in a way that minimizes the loss trivially without actually learning to distinguish quality, effectively collapsing the KL-divergence constraint that keeps the model close to human-aligned behavior. By fixing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically initialized as $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$), DPO ensures that any increase in the implicit reward score corresponds strictly to $\pi_\theta$ assigning higher probability to preferred responses *relative to what was originally plausible*, thereby enforcing a meaningful distance metric from the base model. Treating $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as trainable would indeed revert parts of the process toward joint training of a policy and reward estimator, but it would lose the theoretical guarantee that DPO solves exactly: finding the optimal policy for a reward function consistent with preferences under a strict KL constraint without needing an explicit RL loop or separate reward model training phase.
