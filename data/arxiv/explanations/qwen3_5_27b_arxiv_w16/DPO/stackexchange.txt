\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Why does the partition function $Z(x)$ cancel out in the DPO derivation?
Question:
I'm trying to follow the derivation in Section 4 where the authors move from the optimal policy equation (Eq. 3) to the DPO loss (Eq. 6). 

We start with the optimal policy:
$$ \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right) $$

Rearranging this gives the reward in terms of the policy:
$$ r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) $$

The paper then states that when substituting this into the Bradley-Terry model, which depends on the difference $r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2)$, the partition function cancels out. I understand that $Z(x)$ is a function of $x$ only, but I am struggling to see exactly how the algebra works when plugging this back into the logistic function for preferences. Can someone walk through the substitution step-by-step to show explicitly why $Z(x)$ disappears?
Answer:
The partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out because the Bradley-Terry preference model depends exclusively on the **difference** between the rewards of two responses, and $Z(x)$ is a normalization constant that depends only on the prompt $x$, not on the specific response $y$.

To see this explicitly, consider the reward reparameterization derived in Eq. 4 of the paper:
$$ r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) $$
When you calculate the reward difference between a preferred response $y_w$ and a dispreferred response $y_l$ for the same prompt $x$, you substitute this expression for both terms:
$$ r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) = \left[ \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right] - \left[ \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x) \right] $$
Since both responses are conditioned on the identical prompt $x$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ appears identically in both parts of the subtraction. Algebraically, these terms subtract to zero:
$$ r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)} $$
The Bradley-Terry model (Eq. 1) defines the probability of preference as a logistic function of this difference: $\sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l))$. Because the difference no longer contains $Z(x)$, the final DPO loss (Eq. 6) can be expressed entirely in terms of the policy ratios without ever needing to compute or estimate the intractable partition function. This cancellation is possible specifically because reward functions that differ only by a constant dependent on $x$ (like $\beta \log Z(x)$) belong to the same equivalence class and induce identical preference distributions and optimal policies.

### What is the exact relationship between DPO and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)?
Question:
The paper mentions that DPO optimizes a policy using a binary cross-entropy objective, similar to how we train classifiers. However, it also mentions a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ which is usually an SFT model.

If I set $\beta$ to be very large (approaching infinity), does DPO converge to simply maximizing the likelihood of the preferred responses (i.e., standard SFT on $y_w$)? Or does it behave differently because of the log-ratio term involving $y_l$? 

Specifically, if I have a dataset of $(x, y_w, y_l)$ and I run DPO with a very high $\beta$, will my model eventually just memorize $y_w$ and ignore $y_l$, or does the presence of $y_l$ in the loss function fundamentally change the optimization landscape compared to standard SFT?
Answer:
The short answer is that even as $\beta \to \infty$, DPO does not converge to standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) because the presence of the dispreferred response $y_l$ fundamentally alters the optimization landscape, preventing the model from simply memorizing $y_w$ without regard for $y_l$.

While it is true that a large $\beta$ increases the weight of the log-ratio term, the DPO objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is derived from a KL-constrained reward maximization problem where the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$ acts as a hard anchor. In standard SFT, the objective is simply to maximize $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)$, which encourages the model to assign probability 1 to $y_w$ regardless of other completions. In contrast, DPO optimizes a binary cross-entropy loss based on the implicit reward difference: $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l)}$. As shown in Equation 6 of the paper, this formulation forces the model to increase the probability of $y_w$ *relative* to $y_l$, while simultaneously penalizing any deviation from $\pi_\text{ref}$ that is not justified by this preference gap.

If you were to set $\beta$ to infinity in DPO, you are effectively demanding an infinite margin between the implicit rewards of $y_w$ and $y_l$. However, unlike SFT which ignores $y_l$ entirely, DPO's gradient (Equation 7) explicitly contains a term that decreases the likelihood of $y_l$. This means the optimization trajectory is constrained by the need to suppress $y_l$ relative to the reference distribution, not just boost $y_w$. If you simply ran SFT on $(x, y_w)$ pairs, you would ignore $y_l$, potentially leading to mode collapse or overfitting where the model generates high-probability sequences that are technically valid but fail to distinguish itself from specific undesirable patterns present in $y_l$. DPO avoids this by using $y_l$ as a dynamic regularizer; even with high $\beta$, the model must learn a policy where $p(y_w) \gg p(y_l)$ specifically within the context of staying close to $\pi_\text{ref}$. Therefore, DPO with high $\beta$ behaves more like a "hard-margin" preference classifier than a maximum likelihood estimator for $y_w$, preserving a distinct optimization landscape where dispreferred responses actively shape the final policy.

### How does DPO avoid 'reward hacking' if it doesn't explicitly train a reward model?
Question:
One of the main motivations for RLHF is that explicit reward models can be gamed by the policy (reward hacking), where the model finds loopholes to maximize score without actually satisfying human intent.

The title claims 'Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model.' In DPO, we define an implicit reward as:
$$ \hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)} $$

Since we are optimizing this implicit reward directly via gradient descent on $\pi_\theta$, isn't it possible for $\pi_\theta$ to find a distribution where this implicit reward is high but the actual generation quality is poor? How does DPO prevent this if there isn't an external 'judge' (the explicit reward model) that could theoretically be decoupled from true quality?
Answer:
DPO does not eliminate the risk of reward hacking; rather, it shifts the mechanism of optimization such that the "hacking" behavior is constrained by the same theoretical guarantees that prevent mode collapse in standard RLHF, while removing the instability that often exacerbates these issues in actor-critic methods. The core insight from the paper is that DPO and standard RLHF (like PPO) are optimizing the exact same objective: maximizing a reward function subject to a KL-divergence constraint from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. In RLHF, this is done in two stages (learning a reward model $r_\phi$, then optimizing $\pi_\theta$), whereas DPO performs this optimization in a single stage by reparameterizing the reward function directly in terms of the policy.

The implicit reward defined in DPO, $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, is not an arbitrary score that can be maximized without consequence. It is mathematically derived from the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem. Specifically, the paper proves that for any reward function $r$ consistent with human preferences (modeled via Bradley-Terry), there exists an equivalent reward class where the optimal policy $\pi^*$ satisfies this specific log-ratio form. Consequently, when DPO optimizes this implicit reward via a binary cross-entropy loss on preference pairs $(y_w, y_l)$, it is effectively performing maximum likelihood estimation for the optimal policy under the implicit reward model. The "hacking" you fear—where $\pi_\theta$ assigns high probability to low-quality text—is prevented by two factors inherent to this formulation. First, the optimization is strictly supervised by human preference data; if a generated sequence $y$ is not preferred by humans over a dispreferred alternative $y_l$, the gradient will decrease its probability relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$. Second, and crucially, the term $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\text{ref}}$ inherently penalizes deviations from the reference model. If $\pi_\theta$ tries to assign extremely high probability to a "hacky" sequence that was not seen as preferred in the training data, it must do so at the cost of increasing its KL-divergence from $\pi_\text{ref}$, which acts as a regularizer preventing degeneration into nonsense or repetitive loops.

The paper explicitly addresses why this single-stage approach avoids the pitfalls of explicit reward models without losing safety. In traditional RLHF, an explicit reward model $r_\phi$ can be gamed because it is trained on a finite dataset and may assign high scores to out-of-distribution or adversarial inputs that do not reflect true human intent (the "reward hacking" problem). Furthermore, when optimizing against such a model using PPO, instabilities in value estimation or sampling can cause the policy to drift far from regions where $r_\phi$ is accurate. DPO mitigates this by removing the intermediate explicit reward model entirely. Instead of learning a separate function $r_\phi$ that could be decoupled from quality and then optimized against it with noisy gradients, DPO directly fits $\pi_\theta$ to match human preferences while maintaining proximity to $\pi_\text{ref}$. The "implicit" nature of the reward means there is no standalone function for an adversary (or even an over-optimizing optimizer) to exploit independently of the policy's actual output distribution. The policy cannot assign high implicit reward to itself unless it also assigns high probability relative to $\pi_\text{ref}$ on sequences that humans actually prefer over their alternatives in the training set.

Therefore, DPO prevents "reward hacking" not by introducing a new external judge, but by ensuring that any increase in implicit reward is strictly coupled with an increase in likelihood on human-preferred data and a penalty for deviating from the safe reference distribution. The paper demonstrates empirically that this approach yields policies that are at least as robust as PPO-based RLHF across tasks like summarization and dialogue, often achieving better performance with significantly less hyperparameter tuning and no sampling-based instability. The theoretical equivalence ensures that if RLHF were perfectly stable and converged to its optimum without hacking issues due to optimization noise, DPO would reach that same optimum; however, by bypassing the unstable RL loop and explicit reward fitting, DPO avoids many practical failure modes where hacking typically manifests in real-world training scenarios.

### Clarification on the 'equivalence class' of reward functions in Theorem 1
Question:
In Section 5.1, Theorem 1 states that all reward classes consistent with Plackett-Luce models can be represented by the reparameterization used in DPO.

The definition of equivalence given is: two rewards are equivalent if their difference depends only on $x$, i.e., $r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)$.

I am confused about why this specific equivalence class matters. If two rewards differ by a function of $x$, they induce different absolute probabilities for any specific token sequence. However, they induce the same *preference* probabilities between two sequences.

Does this mean that DPO cannot recover the *absolute* ground truth reward values (e.g., knowing exactly how much better response A is than B in absolute terms), only their relative ordering? And if so, does this limitation affect tasks where absolute scoring might be useful?
Answer:
The equivalence class defined in Theorem 1 is crucial because the Bradley-Terry and Plackett-Luce preference models are inherently **under-specified** regarding absolute reward values; they only provide information about the *relative* difference between rewards, not their absolute magnitudes. As you correctly noted, if two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ differ by a function of the prompt $f(x)$ (i.e., $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$), they produce identical preference probabilities because the term $f(x)$ cancels out when computing the difference $r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$.

This implies that DPO cannot recover a unique "ground truth" absolute reward function from preference data alone. Any function within that equivalence class is mathematically consistent with the observed human preferences. However, this limitation does not hinder the primary goal of alignment: finding the optimal policy. The paper demonstrates in Lemma 2 that all reward functions within the same equivalence class induce the **exact same optimal policy** under the KL-constrained RL objective. Since DPO's goal is to optimize the policy $\pi_\theta$ rather than to estimate a specific scalar reward value for every sequence, recovering any member of this equivalence class is sufficient.

Regarding your concern about absolute scoring tasks: DPO indeed does not provide a unique absolute scale for rewards (e.g., it cannot tell you if response A is "10 points" better than B in an absolute sense, only that it is preferred). If a downstream application strictly requires calibrated absolute scores (such as ranking items against a fixed threshold rather than just comparing pairs), one would need to impose additional identifiability constraints or normalization steps outside of the standard DPO objective. However, for the vast majority of alignment tasks where the objective is to maximize human preference win-rates or generate high-quality responses relative to a reference model, this relative nature is not a limitation but a feature that allows DPO to bypass the unstable estimation of absolute rewards entirely. The reparameterization used in DPO effectively selects a specific representative from this equivalence class—one where the partition function is normalized such that $\sum_y \pi_\text{ref}(y|x) \exp(r(x,y)/\beta) = 1$—which makes the optimal policy analytically tractable without needing to estimate an unknown partition function during training.

### Why is Unlikelihood training considered unstable compared to DPO?
Question:
In Section 4, under 'What does the DPO update do?', there is a discussion about a 'naive version' without weighting causing degeneration. This seems related to Unlikelihood training mentioned in Section 6.

Unlikelihood training typically minimizes:

Answer:
The instability and degeneration observed in Unlikelihood training compared to DPO stem primarily from the absence of a dynamic, data-dependent weighting mechanism and the lack of an explicit reference policy constraint in the gradient update.

In Unlikelihood training, the objective typically involves maximizing the probability of the preferred response $y_w$ while simultaneously minimizing the probability of the dispreferred response $y_l$ with a fixed coefficient $\alpha$. Mathematically, this applies a constant force to push down $\log \pi(y_l|x)$ regardless of the model's current state. The critical flaw is that this "push" does not account for how well the model is already distinguishing between $y_w$ and $y_l$. If the model already assigns a very low probability to $y_l$, Unlikelihood training continues to aggressively penalize it. This can cause the model to overfit to specific tokens or sequences, effectively "memorizing" what *not* to say rather than learning a robust distribution. As noted in Section 4 of the paper, a naive version of this approach without weighting causes the language model to degenerate, often leading to repetitive or nonsensical generations because the optimization landscape becomes unstable when forcing probabilities toward zero without regard for their relative magnitude.

DPO solves this by deriving its update rule directly from the optimal policy of a KL-constrained reward maximization problem. As shown in Equation (7) of the paper, the DPO gradient includes a crucial weighting term: $\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))$. This term represents the probability that the model currently ranks the dispreferred response higher than the preferred one. Intuitively, this acts as an adaptive learning rate: if the model correctly identifies $y_w$ as better than $y_l$ (i.e., $\hat{r}(y_w) > \hat{r}(y_l)$), this weight approaches zero, effectively stopping further updates on that example. Conversely, if the model is confused or ranks them incorrectly, the weight is large, driving a strong correction. This prevents the "over-penalization" seen in Unlikelihood training, ensuring that once a preference is learned, it is not destabilized by excessive gradient pressure.

Furthermore, your intuition regarding reference normalization is correct and central to DPO's stability. DPO implicitly enforces a KL-divergence constraint against a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ through its parameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$. This reference term acts as an anchor, preventing the policy from drifting arbitrarily far from valid language distributions or collapsing into mode-collapse (where only high-reward but low-diversity outputs are generated). In contrast, standard Unlikelihood training lacks this explicit regularization term; it pushes probabilities down without a bound on how much they can deviate from natural language priors. Without this anchor and without the dynamic weighting that scales updates based on current performance errors, Unlikelihood training lacks the theoretical guarantees that keep DPO stable and prevent degeneration.
