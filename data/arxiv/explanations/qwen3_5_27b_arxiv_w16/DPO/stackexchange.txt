\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model"}

### Intuition behind why the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out in the DPO derivation
Question:
I am trying to follow the derivation in Section 4 of 'Direct Preference Optimization'. The authors start with the optimal policy equation:

$$\pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$$

They then rearrange this to express the reward as:

$$r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$$

When substituting this back into the Bradley-Terry preference probability $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 | x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ disappears. 

My confusion is twofold: 
1. Why does $Z(x)$ depend only on $x$ and not on $y$? If it depended on $y$, wouldn't it remain in the subtraction?
2. Conceptually, does this mean that the absolute magnitude of the reward doesn't matter for preferences, only the relative difference? Does this imply that any constant offset added to a reward function (dependent on $x$) is invisible to the preference model?
Answer:
The cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ occurs precisely because it acts as a **normalization constant** that depends exclusively on the prompt $x$ and is independent of the specific completion $y$. Mathematically, $Z(x)$ is defined as the sum over all possible outputs weighted by the reference policy and the exponential reward: $Z(x) = \sum_{y'} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y')\right)$. Since this summation aggregates probabilities across *all* potential responses for a fixed context $x$, the resulting value is invariant to which specific candidate $y_1$ or $y_2$ you are currently evaluating. When you substitute the reward expression into the Bradley-Terry preference probability, you calculate the difference between two rewards: $r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2)$. Because both terms contain the identical additive component $\beta \log Z(x)$, they subtract perfectly to zero ($\beta \log Z(x) - \beta \log Z(x) = 0$), leaving only the terms dependent on the policy ratios.

Conceptually, this confirms your intuition that **absolute reward magnitudes are irrelevant for pairwise preferences**; only relative differences matter. The Bradley-Terry model (and its generalizations like Plackett-Luce) operates on rank ordering rather than absolute scores. Adding any arbitrary function of the context alone, such as a constant offset or a term dependent solely on $x$, to a reward function constitutes an "equivalence transformation." As formalized in Definition 1 and Lemma 1 of the paper, two reward functions differing only by a term $f(x)$ belong to the same equivalence class and induce identical preference distributions. Consequently, any constant offset added to a reward function that varies with $x$ but not with $y$ is indeed invisible to the preference model. This property is what allows DPO to bypass the notoriously difficult estimation of the partition function entirely: by optimizing directly for the policy ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$, DPO implicitly learns a representative from this equivalence class where the normalization term effectively vanishes from the objective function.

### Clarification on the role of $\beta$ in DPO vs. standard RLHF KL penalty
Question:
In standard RLHF (Eq. 3 in the paper), there is a hyperparameter $\beta$ multiplying the KL divergence term: $- \beta D_{KL}[...|...]$. In the derived DPO loss (Eq. 7), $\beta$ appears inside the sigmoid argument as a multiplier for the log-prob ratios.

I am confused about how these two roles connect. In RLHF, if I increase $\beta$, I penalize deviation from the reference model more heavily. In DPO, increasing $\beta$ scales up the log-ratio differences before passing them to the sigmoid.

Does a higher $\beta$ in DPO effectively act as a stronger regularization towards $\pi_{ref}$? Or does it just make the model more confident in distinguishing between winning and losing examples? The paper mentions that a naive version without proper weighting causes degeneration; is this weighting mechanism solely responsible for mimicking the KL constraint?
Answer:
The hyperparameter $\beta$ serves the exact same conceptual purpose in both the standard RLHF formulation and the derived DPO loss: it controls the strictness of the regularization constraint that keeps the learned policy $\pi_\theta$ close to the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. In standard RLHF (Equation 3), $\beta$ acts as a Lagrange multiplier scaling the KL divergence penalty, meaning a higher $\beta$ imposes a heavier cost for deviating from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, thereby forcing the model to stay closer to its original distribution.

In DPO, this role is preserved but manifests differently due to the change of variables that eliminates the explicit reward model. By substituting the optimal policy form (Equation 5) into the Bradley-Terry preference model, the authors derive Equation 7, where $\beta$ scales the log-probability ratios inside the sigmoid function. Mathematically, increasing $\beta$ in DPO sharpens the decision boundary required for a preference pair to be satisfied. If $\beta$ is large, even small deviations in the log-ratio difference between preferred ($y_w$) and dispreferred ($y_l$) responses result in a saturated sigmoid output, effectively punishing any configuration where the model does not assign significantly higher probability to $y_w$ relative to $y_l$ *after* accounting for their probabilities under $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Consequently, a higher $\beta$ in DPO indeed enforces stronger regularization towards $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, preventing the model from overfitting to specific preference examples by arbitrarily inflating probabilities (a phenomenon known as "mode collapse" or degeneration mentioned in Section 4).

The intuition connecting these two views lies in Equation 6, which defines an implicit reward $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here, $\beta$ dictates how much "reward credit" is granted for shifting probability mass away from the reference model. When you increase $\beta$, you are effectively saying that moving away from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is costly unless it yields a massive improvement in satisfying human preferences. The weighting mechanism inside DPO's sigmoid is not merely making the model more confident; it is dynamically adjusting how aggressively the model updates its parameters based on how far it has already drifted from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. Without this specific scaling by $\beta$, as noted in Section 4 regarding "naïve versions," the optimization would lack this stabilizing force and likely lead to degenerate outputs where the model assigns near-zero probability to everything except a narrow set of memorized high-reward tokens. Thus, whether viewed as a KL penalty coefficient or a log-ratio scaler, $\beta$ fundamentally governs the trade-off between maximizing preference satisfaction and maintaining proximity to the reference distribution.

### Understanding 'Equivalence Classes' of Reward Functions in Theorem 1
Question:
Section 5 introduces an equivalence relation where two rewards are equivalent if their difference is a function of only $x$: $r(x,y) - r'(x,y) = f(x)$.
Theorem 1 claims that *all* reward classes consistent with Bradley-Terry can be represented by the reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)}$.

I don't quite grasp why this reparameterization covers *every* possible equivalence class. It seems like we are forcing every reward function to look like a log-prob ratio plus some constant (the partition function part). Are there theoretical cases where a human preference structure exists that cannot be mapped onto *any* policy $\\pi$ via this specific formula? What happens if the underlying human preferences violate the assumptions required for this mapping to hold?
Answer:
The reason the reparameterization $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ covers every possible equivalence class lies in the specific definition of equivalence used in the paper and the solvability of the optimal policy equation. You are correct that adding an arbitrary function $f(x)$ to a reward does not change the resulting preferences or the optimal policy under the Bradley-Terry model; this creates an equivalence class where all members behave identically regarding human choices. Theorem 1 asserts that within *any* such class, there exists at least one specific reward function that can be expressed exactly as a log-ratio of probabilities relative to a reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

The intuition comes from reversing the standard RLHF derivation. We know from Equation 3 in the paper that for *any* reward function $r$, the optimal policy $\pi_r$ that maximizes expected reward subject to a KL constraint is given by $\pi_r(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$. If we treat this equation as a system where we want to solve for $r$, we can take the logarithm and rearrange terms to get $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. Here, $\beta \log Z(x)$ acts precisely as the function $f(x)$ mentioned in your question. Since two rewards are equivalent if they differ only by a function of $x$, the term $\beta \log Z(x)$ places this derived reward in the same equivalence class as any other reward that generates the same optimal policy $\pi_r$. By choosing to drop this $x$-dependent term (effectively setting it to zero or absorbing it into the definition), we select a canonical representative for that entire class: specifically, the one where the partition function is normalized such that it disappears from the relative comparison.

Therefore, there are no theoretical cases where a human preference structure consistent with Bradley-Terry cannot be mapped onto this formula, provided an optimal policy exists for those preferences. The mapping does not force human preferences to look like log-prob ratios; rather, it proves that *if* human preferences follow Bradley-Terry, then there must exist a latent reward structure—and consequently an optimal policy—that fits this exact form. If underlying human preferences violated the Bradley-Terry assumptions (e.g., exhibiting cyclic intransitivity like Rock-Paper-Scissors), then no global scalar reward function $r(x,y)$ would exist to begin with, and neither traditional RLHF nor DPO could perfectly model them. However, within the scope of models where a scalar reward explains preferences (the domain of Theorem 1), DPO's parameterization is fully expressive because it effectively solves for the unique member of each equivalence class that satisfies the normalization condition implied by being a valid probability distribution derived from $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

### Practical implication: Why do we need $\\pi_{ref}$ frozen during DPO training?
Question:
The paper defines DPO as optimizing $\\pi_	heta$ while keeping $\\pi_{ref}$ fixed (usually initialized as $\\pi^{SFT}$). The loss function explicitly calculates ratios like $\\frac{\\pi_	heta(y_w|x)}{\\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)}$.

If I were to treat $\\pi_{ref}$ as trainable alongside $\\pi_	heta$, wouldn't that just revert to training a standard reward model and policy simultaneously? More importantly, why is it critical that $\\pi_{ref}$ remains static? If $\\pi_	heta$ updates and moves away from $\\pi_{ref}$, does that mathematically correspond to accumulating 'reward'? Is there a risk of circular logic here where moving away from reference automatically inflates the implicit reward regardless of actual quality?
Answer:
Freezing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is fundamentally required because it serves as the immutable anchor for the implicit reward function, ensuring that the optimization problem remains well-defined and mathematically equivalent to the standard RLHF objective. In Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), the reward is not an independent neural network but is derived implicitly via the reparameterization $r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)$. If you were to allow $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to update alongside $\pi_\theta$, you would break this specific mapping. The DPO loss function, which is a binary cross-entropy over preference pairs, relies on the cancellation of the partition function $Z(x)$ when taking the difference between rewards for winning ($y_w$) and losing ($y_l$) responses. This cancellation only holds if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is treated as a constant baseline; if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ changes, the term $\log Z(x)$ would become dependent on the evolving policy in a way that invalidates the closed-form solution derived from the Bradley-Terry model.

Regarding your concern about circular logic or artificially inflating rewards: freezing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ actually prevents this degeneracy rather than causing it. The quantity $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$ represents the *change* in log-probability relative to a fixed starting point. If both models moved freely, they could potentially converge to identical distributions or drift together in a way that minimizes the loss trivially without actually learning to distinguish quality, effectively collapsing the KL-divergence constraint that keeps the model close to human-aligned behavior. By fixing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (typically initialized as $\pi^{\text{SFT}}$), DPO ensures that any increase in the implicit reward score corresponds strictly to $\pi_\theta$ assigning higher probability to preferred responses *relative to what was originally plausible*, thereby enforcing a meaningful distance metric from the base model. Treating $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as trainable would indeed revert parts of the process toward joint training of a policy and reward estimator, but it would lose the theoretical guarantee that DPO solves exactly: finding the optimal policy for a reward function consistent with preferences under a strict KL constraint without needing an explicit RL loop or separate reward model training phase.

### Interpretation of 'Implicit Reward' degeneracy mentioned in Appendix Table references
Question:
In Section 4 ('What does the DPO update do?'), there is a discussion about how removing the weighting coefficient (related to how much worse $\\hat{r}_	heta$ ranks $y_l$ compared to $y_w$) causes model degeneration. The text refers to Appendix Table regarding 'unlikelihood generations'.

Specifically, if we just tried to maximize $\\\sigma(	ext{something proportional to } \\\log \\\frac{\\\pi_	heta(y_w)}{\\\pi_	heta(y_l)})$, why would this fail compared to including $\\\log \\\frac{\\\pi_	heta}{\\\pi_{ref}}$? Does omitting $\\pi_{ref}$ remove any concept of 'cost' for changing probabilities, leading to mode collapse where $\\theta$ assigns probability 1 to any token sequence seen once as preferred?
Answer:
The degeneracy described in the paper arises because omitting the reference model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ removes the essential regularization mechanism that prevents **mode collapse**, effectively turning the optimization into an unconstrained maximization of likelihood for specific tokens.

In the DPO formulation, the implicit reward is defined as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)}$. This structure inherently penalizes changes to the policy that deviate too far from the reference distribution, acting as a "cost" for shifting probability mass. If you were to simplify the objective to merely maximize $\sigma(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_\theta(y_l)})$—effectively treating it as a standard binary classification on logits without the denominator—the model loses this constraint. Without $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, there is no penalty for assigning arbitrarily high probability to $y_w$ and driving $y_l$ (and potentially all other sequences) to zero probability.

As noted in Section 4 ("What does the DPO update do?"), the full DPO gradient includes a weighting term dependent on how much worse the model currently ranks $y_l$ compared to $y_w$. More critically, the presence of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ ensures that the model cannot simply assign probability 1 to any sequence it sees labeled as preferred, regardless of whether that sequence was likely under the original training distribution. If you remove $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, the optimizer finds it easiest to satisfy the preference condition by collapsing the entire output distribution onto a single token sequence or a very narrow set of high-probability outputs seen in the training data, ignoring fluency or diversity. This phenomenon is what leads to "unlikelihood generations" mentioned in Appendix Table 1, where models trained without this regularization produce nonsensical or repetitive text because they have collapsed into modes that technically satisfy the pairwise comparison but lack semantic coherence. The reference model acts as an anchor, ensuring that improvements in preference alignment come from refining existing capabilities rather than inventing new, degenerate distributions.

### Handling distribution shift when no SFT model is available for initialization
Question:
The paper notes in Section 4 ('DPO outline') that if an SFT model isn't available (e.g., using raw pre-trained weights), they initialize $\\pi_{ref}$ by maximizing likelihood on preferred completions ($y_w$).

Mathematically, if I create a new reference model by fine-tuning on only winners, isn't my starting point already biased towards high-reward outputs? When I then run DPO with this new reference, am I double-counting preferences or creating an overly narrow distribution from which I try to expand? How does this differ fundamentally from just doing Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)? At what point does DPO stop looking like SFT and start acting like preference optimization?
Answer:
Your concern about "double-counting" or creating an overly narrow distribution when initializing $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ on only preferred completions ($y_w$) stems from a misunderstanding of the distinct roles played by the reference model and the policy model in the DPO objective. Crucially, DPO does not optimize the absolute probability of $y_w$; rather, it optimizes the *ratio* of probabilities between the winning and losing responses relative to the reference.

When you initialize $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by maximizing likelihood on $y_w$ (effectively performing a lightweight SFT on winners), you are shifting the baseline distribution closer to the desired behavior to mitigate distribution shift, as noted in Section 4. However, this does not mean you are "double-counting" preferences because the DPO loss function (Eq. 7) explicitly subtracts the log-probability of $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ from that of your target policy $\pi_\theta$. Mathematically, the objective depends on $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}$. If both $\pi_\theta$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ assign high probability to $y_w$, their log-ratio approaches zero, contributing little to the gradient. The optimization signal only arises when $\pi_\theta$ assigns a significantly higher relative probability to $y_w$ compared to $y_l$ *than* what $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ already does. Therefore, even if $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is biased toward winners, DPO still pushes $\pi_\theta$ to prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$ more strongly than that baseline, effectively learning from the *contrast* provided by the dispreferred examples ($y_l$) which are absent from your reference initialization.

This process differs fundamentally from pure Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) because SFT ignores $y_l$ entirely and drives $\pi_\theta(y_w)$ toward 1 regardless of how likely $y_l$ is, often leading to mode collapse or excessive memorization. In contrast, DPO retains a dynamic penalty for assigning high probability to $y_l$. The transition from "looking like SFT" to "acting like preference optimization" happens immediately upon introducing the dispreferred examples into the loss calculation. While SFT seeks to maximize $P(y_w|x)$, DPO seeks to maximize the implicit reward gap defined by Eq. 3: $r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + C$. By anchoring this reward definition against a reference that has seen only winners, you are essentially telling the model: "You already know how good these winners look (thanks to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$), now learn specifically how much worse these losers are." This preserves diversity and prevents degeneration better than SFT alone, as confirmed by the authors' observation that naive objectives without this specific weighting mechanism lead to degradation (Section 4). Thus, initializing on winners serves primarily as a stability measure for out-of-domain references without negating the core preference-learning mechanism driven by pairwise comparisons.
