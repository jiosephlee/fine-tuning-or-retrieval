\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Does GSPO implicitly assume independence between token errors when aggregating rewards at the sequence level?
Question:
The paper argues that 'the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward.' Since rewards are given per sequence ($r(x,y)$), they optimize at the sequence level.

But consider a scenario where a response is mostly perfect except for one critical error early on that ruins everything downstream. In PPO/GRPO, we might clip or penalize specific tokens based on local advantage estimates (if available). In GSPO, every single token in that bad response gets multiplied by a low global advantage $\\widehat{A}_i$ and potentially clipped entirely.

Is there a risk that GSPO treats all tokens in a failed response equally guilty? Conversely, if a response succeeds only because of luck on one specific difficult token but was otherwise mediocre, does GSPO over-reward all preceding 'easy' tokens just because they belong to that lucky sequence? How does this compare conceptually to credit assignment methods used in other RL domains?
Answer:
Yes, GSPO explicitly adopts a coarse-grained credit assignment strategy where the unit of optimization matches the unit of reward, meaning it does treat all tokens in a failed sequence as equally responsible for the outcome, and conversely rewards all tokens in a successful sequence uniformly. As stated in the paper, the core motivation for GSPO is that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward"; since the verifier provides a scalar reward $r(x, y)$ for the entire sequence $y$, applying off-policy corrections at the token level (as done in GRPO) is considered an "ill-posed" application of importance sampling that introduces high-variance noise. Consequently, GSPO calculates a single sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and a single normalized advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ for every token in response $y_i$. When deriving the gradient (Equation 6), it becomes evident that every token's log-probability gradient $\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$ is scaled by exactly the same factor: $\frac{1}{|y_i|} s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i$. This ensures that if a sequence receives a negative advantage due to one critical early error ruining the rest of the output, every token in that chain receives an identical penalty signal, preventing the unstable accumulation of variance seen in token-level weighting schemes. While this approach sacrifices fine-grained attribution—meaning easy tokens in a lucky success are rewarded just as heavily as hard ones—it is intentionally designed to stabilize training dynamics, particularly for Mixture-of-Experts models where token-level probability fluctuations can cause catastrophic collapse. The authors argue that this uniform treatment is preferable to the noisy, unstable gradients produced by attempting to assign differential blame or credit at the token level when only sequence-level feedback exists. For scenarios requiring finer granularity, such as multi-turn interactions, the paper introduces a variant called GSPO-token which allows for customizing advantages per token ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}$), though even then, the importance ratio remains anchored to the stable sequence-level likelihood to preserve training robustness.
