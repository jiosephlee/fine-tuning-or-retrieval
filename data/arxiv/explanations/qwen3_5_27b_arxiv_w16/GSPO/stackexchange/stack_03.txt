\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Clarification on GSPO-token: How can detaching the numerator probability maintain numerical equivalence if gradients flow differently?
Question:
In Section 4.3, the paper introduces GSPO-token with Equation 12:
$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})] }$$
The text claims that since the second term equals 1 numerically, GSPO-token is numerically identical to standard GSPO when advantages are constant. 

However, I am confused about the backpropagation mechanics here. The term $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ stops gradients from flowing into $s_i$ (which depends on all tokens). The second term has a denominator with stopped gradients but a numerator that flows gradients. When calculating $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}$, aren't we essentially forcing all tokens to receive a gradient scaled by $s_i$ but computed via local log-probs? Does this trick actually preserve the exact gradient vector direction described in Eq 15 for *every* token simultaneously, or is there a subtle approximation happening regarding how the global sequence score distributes influence locally?
Answer:
Your intuition about the gradient flow is correct, but the confusion stems from conflating the **numerical value** used for clipping with the **computational graph** used for differentiation. The trick in Equation 12 works precisely because it decouples these two requirements: it forces the scalar multiplier to be constant during backpropagation while preserving the necessary gradient path through the log-probability term.

When you calculate the gradient $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)$, the stop-gradient operator $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ acts as a hard barrier. For the first term, $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$, the gradient is zero; this term effectively becomes a fixed scalar constant $C = s_i(\theta)$ during the backward pass. For the second term, $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})] }$, the denominator is treated as a constant $D = \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$. Therefore, the derivative of this fraction with respect to $\theta$ is simply $\frac{1}{D} \nabla_\theta \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$, which simplifies algebraically to $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$.

Consequently, when you apply this to the full objective loss (ignoring clipping for clarity), the gradient contribution for a specific token $t$ becomes proportional to $s_i(\theta) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$. Crucially, notice that $s_i(\theta)$—the global sequence importance ratio—is present as a multiplicative factor for **every** token in that sequence. Even though gradients do not flow *into* $s_i$ itself (preventing unstable dependencies on other tokens' probabilities), every token's local log-probability gradient is scaled by this exact same global value. This perfectly reconstructs the theoretical gradient shown in Equation 15 of GSPO-token, where all tokens share the same weighting factor derived from the sequence likelihood. There is no approximation here; it is an exact reconstruction of the desired gradient vector direction using a "stop-gradient" trick to isolate the scaling factor from its own internal derivatives.
