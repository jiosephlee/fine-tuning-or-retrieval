\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Mechanism behind MoE stability: Why does fixing routing replay help GRPO but become unnecessary for GSPO?
Question:
Section 5.3 discusses 'Expert-Activation Volatility' in MoE models. It states that for GRPO, small parameter updates cause different experts to activate for subsequent steps ($\\pi_\\theta$ vs $\\pi_\\text{old}$), causing wild fluctuations in token-level ratios $w_{i,t}$. They solve this with 'Routing Replay'.

The paper claims GSPO avoids this because it looks at total sequence likelihood $\\pi(y|x)$. My intuition says: if experts change activation patterns significantly between $\\pi_\\text{old}$ and $\\pi_\\theta$, shouldn't that still cause massive swings in $\\pi(y|x)$ too?

Is it true that while individual token probabilities fluctuate wildly due to expert swaps, their product (the total sequence likelihood) remains relatively smooth? Or is there something about how MoE routing works such that while local paths change globally consistent outputs are maintained unless trained explicitly otherwise?
Answer:
Your intuition correctly identifies that changing expert activations *does* affect the total sequence likelihood $\pi(y|x)$, but the critical distinction lies in **variance** and **how the optimization objective aggregates these changes**. In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ acts as a multiplicative weight for every single token's gradient contribution. When expert routing shifts between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, the probability assigned to a specific token $y_t$ can swing wildly (e.g., dropping from 0.9 to 0.1 if the wrong expert activates), causing $w_{i,t}$ to explode or vanish locally. Because GRPO sums these volatile, independent token-level weights across the sequence, the resulting gradient becomes extremely noisy and unstable, necessitating "Routing Replay" to artificially force consistency in which experts are active.

GSPO avoids this instability not because the sequence likelihood is immune to expert changes, but because it aggregates the log-probabilities first before exponentiating them into a single sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Mathematically, GSPO computes $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_t \log w_{i,t}(\theta) \right)$. By summing the log-importance ratios (which correspond to additive differences in log-probability) and then dividing by the sequence length, GSPO effectively averages out the local volatility caused by individual expert switches. Unless *every* token in a sequence simultaneously suffers a catastrophic routing shift—which is statistically unlikely—the aggregate score remains relatively smooth and bounded. Consequently, GSPO treats the entire response as a single unit of optimization; even if internal expert paths diverge slightly between policies, the global signal regarding whether the *whole* sequence is better or worse than before remains coherent. This inherent smoothing allows GSPO to tolerate natural expert-activation volatility without needing to freeze routing decisions via Replay, enabling stable training while allowing the model to utilize its full dynamic capacity.
