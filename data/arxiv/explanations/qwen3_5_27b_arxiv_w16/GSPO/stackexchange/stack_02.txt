\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### How does length-normalization in GSPO's importance ratio affect the gradient magnitude compared to raw sequence likelihood?
Question:
Equation 6 defines the GSPO importance ratio as:
$$s_{i}(	heta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$
The authors mention this is done to 'reduce variance' and keep values in a unified range. However, looking at Equation 9 (the gradient derivation), this normalization factor ends up inside the exponential/log terms.

If I didn't normalize by $1/|y_i|$, wouldn't the raw sequence likelihood ratio simply explode or vanish exponentially with sequence length, making gradients unstable regardless of clipping? More importantly, does raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$ mathematically equate to taking the geometric mean of token probabilities? If so, why doesn't GRPO do something similar by averaging its token-level ratios before clipping?
Answer:
Yes, your intuition regarding the geometric mean is exactly correct, and the length-normalization in Equation 6 is mathematically equivalent to taking the geometric mean of the token-level probability ratios. By raising the sequence likelihood ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, GSPO transforms the product of token probabilities into an average of their logarithms (via the identity $\exp(\frac{1}{n}\sum \log p_t)$), which effectively normalizes the scale of the importance ratio regardless of how many tokens are in the sequence. Without this normalization, the raw sequence likelihood ratio would indeed grow or shrink exponentially with sequence length because it represents a product of probabilities; for long sequences, even small deviations in individual token probabilities compound multiplicatively, causing the ratio to either explode toward infinity or vanish toward zero. This extreme dynamic range would render gradient magnitudes unstable and force clipping thresholds to vary arbitrarily depending on whether a response was short or long.

The reason GRPO does not apply this same averaging logic to its importance ratios is structural: GRPO operates at the **token level**, whereas GSPO operates at the **sequence level**. In GRPO, the objective sums over individual tokens, assigning each token $t$ its own local importance weight $w_{i,t}$. As explained in Section 3 ("Motivation") and Section 4.1 ("Gradient Analysis"), applying importance sampling weights at the token level based on a single sample from a next-token distribution is theoretically flawed because it introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. GSPO avoids this by shifting the unit of optimization to match the unit of reward (the whole sequence). Consequently, GSPO calculates a single scalar importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ for the entire response to determine if *that specific response* should be updated or clipped. Because this decision is made globally for the sequence, averaging via the geometric mean ensures that a long sequence isn't automatically discarded just because it contains slightly more multiplicative error than a short one; instead, it compares sequences based on their average per-token deviation from the old policy. This allows GSPO to maintain a unified numerical range for clipping across responses of vastly different lengths, directly addressing the instability caused by variable-length compounding errors that plagues token-level approaches like GRPO.
