Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Introducing Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO)

## Core Philosophy: Aligning Optimization Units with Reward Units

The foundational motivation behind Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) arises from a critical realization regarding the structural integrity of reinforcement learning objectives for large language models. In traditional approaches like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and its derivative, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), the optimization process operates at the granularity of individual tokens. These algorithms calculate importance ratios—the weights used to adjust the probability of generating specific words—based on the likelihood of each token given its preceding context. However, a fundamental disconnect exists in this design: while the optimization steps are taken token-by-token, the reward signal provided by the environment is almost exclusively assigned to the entire completed sequence. A model receives a score only after it has finished generating a full response to a query, yet it attempts to learn from this global feedback by adjusting probabilities at every intermediate step independently.

This misalignment creates what researchers have identified as an "ill-posed" objective, particularly when training massive models or dealing with long-generation tasks. The principle of importance sampling dictates that to correctly estimate an expectation under a target distribution using samples from a behavior distribution, one must account for the distributional shift across the entire sample space. When importance weights are calculated at the token level based on single-sample draws, they fail to effectively correct for these distributional mismatches. Instead, they introduce high-variance noise into the training gradients. As the length of the generated response increases, this noise accumulates progressively. Furthermore, standard clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize training inadvertently amplify this accumulated noise rather than suppressing it, often leading to catastrophic and irreversible model collapse where the model's capabilities degrade completely and cannot be recovered even by reverting to earlier checkpoints.

Consequently, GSPO proposes a paradigm shift rooted in a simple yet profound heuristic: **the unit of optimization must match the unit of reward**. Since rewards are inherently sequence-level phenomena in language modeling tasks, the corrective mechanisms for off-policy learning should also operate at the sequence level. By abandoning the token-level objective and moving toward a sequence-level framework, GSPO ensures that the alignment between how we measure success (the reward) and how we update our parameters (the optimization) is coherent. This philosophical pivot eliminates the instability caused by aggregating noisy token-level corrections over long horizons. It treats each complete response as an atomic unit of learning, allowing the algorithm to assess whether an entire trajectory was good or bad before determining how much to adjust the policy towards reproducing that trajectory. This approach not only adheres strictly to the theoretical principles of importance sampling but also provides a robust foundation for scaling reinforcement learning to increasingly complex and lengthy reasoning tasks without succumbing to training instability.

## Mathematical Formulation of Sequence Likelihood Importance Ratios ($s_i(\theta)$)

To operationalize this philosophy, GSPO redefines the concept of an importance ratio by shifting from conditional token probabilities to joint sequence probabilities. In previous algorithms like GRPO, the importance ratio for a specific token $y_{i,t}$ within response $i$ is defined as $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. This ratio compares how likely the current policy $\pi_\theta$ is to generate that specific next word compared to how likely it was under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. While intuitive locally, this metric lacks global coherence because it ignores how earlier deviations might compound later ones. GSPO replaces this with a sequence-level importance ratio, denoted as $s_i(\theta)$, which evaluates the likelihood of generating the *entire* response $y_i$ given query $x$. Mathematically, this is expressed as $s_i(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)}$. Here, $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ represents the product of all conditional token probabilities along the sequence: $\prod_{t=1}^{|y|} \pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$. This formulation captures exactly how much more probable—or improbable—the entire path taken by old policy is under the new policy compared to before.

By defining $s_i(\theta)$ in terms of sequence likelihoods, GSPO restores validity to importance sampling within off-policy settings common in large-scale RL training. When data is rolled out using an older version of the model ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) but optimized using parameters updated via mini-batch processing ($\pi_\theta$), there is an inherent distributional gap. The sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ acts as a precise correction factor that quantifies this deviation for whole trajectories rather than fragmented tokens. If a response was highly unlikely under both policies but slightly less so under $\pi_\theta$, or if it became exponentially more likely due to recent updates, $s_i(\theta)$ encapsulates this total shift accurately. This stands in stark contrast to token-level ratios where individual fluctuations might cancel out or exaggerate errors depending on local context variations that do not reflect global quality changes induced by expert activation shifts in Mixture-of-Experts architectures or other architectural complexities.
