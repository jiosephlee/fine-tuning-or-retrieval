Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Diagnosing the Instability of Token-Level Optimization

As the field of large language models scales toward greater complexity, sparsity, and response length, the stability of Reinforcement Learning (RL) training has become the primary bottleneck preventing further advancement. Current state-of-the-art approaches, most notably Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), have demonstrated success in smaller settings but exhibit severe fragility when applied to gigantic models or long-horizon reasoning tasks. This chapter dissects the theoretical foundations of these failures, revealing that the instability is not merely a matter of hyperparameter tuning but stems from a fundamental misalignment in how importance sampling is applied. By examining the mechanics of token-level optimization, we uncover how the standard application of importance weights introduces high-variance noise that compounds over time, interacts destructively with clipping mechanisms, and ultimately precipitates catastrophic model collapse. Understanding these dynamics is a prerequisite for appreciating the shift toward sequence-level optimization required to stabilize modern RL training.

# The Ill-Posed Nature of Token-Level Importance Ratios

At the heart of many modern RL algorithms for language models lies an objective function designed to update a policy $\pi_\theta$ using samples generated by an older version of itself, $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. In algorithms like GRPO, this update relies heavily on an importance ratio calculated at every single token position within a generated response. Specifically, for a token $y_{i,t}$ at position $t$ in response $i$, the algorithm computes a weight $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ defined as the ratio of the probability assigned by the new policy to that specific token versus the probability assigned by the old policy: $w_{i,t}(\theta)=\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})}$. While this formulation appears mathematically sound at first glance because it mirrors standard off-policy correction techniques used in robotics or Atari games, it suffers from being ill-posed in the context of autoregressive text generation.

The fundamental flaw arises from a mismatch between the granularity of the reward signal and the granularity of the optimization objective. In language model RL, rewards are almost exclusively granted at the sequence level; a verifier evaluates whether an entire solution to a math problem or piece of code is correct before assigning a scalar score $r(x, y)$. Consequently, all tokens within that successful or failed sequence share a common fate regarding their contribution to learning. However, by applying importance weighting and clipping at the token level, GRPO attempts to optimize individual steps based on local probabilities while attributing global rewards to them. This creates an ill-posed situation where the unit of optimization (the token) does not align with the unit of feedback (the sequence). When the goal is to adjust a trajectory based on its final outcome, correcting for distribution shifts step-by-step introduces unnecessary degrees of freedom and statistical inefficiency that destabilize the learning process.

Furthermore, this misalignment becomes critical when considering how these weights influence gradient updates. Because each token's weight is computed independently based solely on its immediate conditional probability distribution, slight variations in how $\pi_\theta$ assigns mass to different tokens can lead to wildly divergent importance ratios across adjacent positions in a sentence. Unlike environments where actions are discrete and independent steps in Markov Decision Processes often have well-defined transition dynamics conditioned only on states, language generation involves highly correlated dependencies where changing one token affects all subsequent ones. Treating each token as an isolated entity requiring its own off-policy correction ignores these deep sequential dependencies and results in an objective function that struggles to converge reliably as model size increases.

# Why Single-Sample Importance Weights Fail Distribution Correction

To understand why token-level weighting fails specifically under off-policy conditions, one must revisit the core principle of importance sampling. Theoretically, importance sampling allows us to estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ by re-weighting samples drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$. The formula $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} \left[ f(z) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} f(z) \right]$ holds true provided that we average over a sufficiently large number of samples ($N \gg 1$) drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$. Under these conditions, Law of Large Numbers ensures that rare events under $\pi_\text{beh}$ but likely under $\pi_\text{tar}$ are appropriately up-weighted on average across many trials, effectively correcting for the distributional mismatch between what was sampled and what we wish to learn about.

However, in practical RL training with large batches partitioned into mini-batches for efficiency, we operate in an off-policy setting where we rely on finite samples generated by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The critical error in GRPO's design is that it applies this re-weighting logic using only a *single* sample realization for each token position. For any given prompt $x$ and history $y_{<t}$, there is exactly one next token $y_t$ observed from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. There are no other alternative next tokens sampled from this specific conditional distribution within that mini-batch instance to provide statistical grounding for estimating expectations locally at step $t$. As such, calculating $w_{t}(\theta)$ based on this single draw does not constitute valid distribution correction; instead, it acts as a noisy estimator whose variance depends entirely on how unlikely that specific token was under either policy relative to others that were never seen.

This single-sample limitation means that if $\pi_{\theta}$ happens to assign slightly higher probability to a specific token than $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ did—perhaps due to random initialization drift or early exploration—the resulting ratio can explode arbitrarily high if $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ had assigned near-zero probability to it initially. Conversely, if $\pi_{\theta}$ downweights a common token significantly compared to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, the ratio collapses toward zero. Without averaging over multiple realizations of next-token choices at every step—which would require generating exponential numbers of trajectories—the single-sample weight cannot effectively cancel out biases introduced by sampling from an outdated policy. Instead of smoothing out differences between distributions as intended by theory applied correctly over ensembles, these weights amplify stochastic fluctuations inherent in small sample sizes into massive errors in gradient direction and magnitude.
