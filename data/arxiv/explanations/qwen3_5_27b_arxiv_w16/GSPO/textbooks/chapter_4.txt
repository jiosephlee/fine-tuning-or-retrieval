## Step-by-Step Derivation of the GSPO Gradient

To truly grasp why Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) offers superior stability compared to previous methods, we must rigorously examine the mechanics of its parameter updates. The core of any reinforcement learning algorithm lies in how it computes the gradient of the objective function with respect to the model parameters, $\theta$. In GSPO, the optimization objective is defined at the sequence level rather than the token level. Recall that the GSPO objective function, denoted as $\mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO}(\theta)$, operates on a group of $G$ responses $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$ generated for a query $x$. The objective seeks to maximize the expected advantage, weighted by a sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, subject to a clipping constraint. For the purpose of deriving the gradient direction, we temporarily omit the clipping mechanism to isolate the underlying flow of information through the loss landscape.

The derivation begins by applying the chain rule to the expectation operator. We start with the expression for the gradient of the unclipped objective:
$$
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{ x \sim \mathcal{D},\, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G \sim \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}( \cdot | x) } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \right].
$$
Since the expectation is taken over samples drawn from a fixed old policy $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, and assuming we are working with a finite batch approximation where we treat samples as constant during differentiation, we can move the gradient operator inside the summation and expectation. Crucially, because $s_i(\theta)$ depends on $\theta$, we apply the identity $\nabla_\theta f(\theta) = f(\theta) \nabla_\theta \log f(\theta)$. This transforms our equation into:
$$
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} (\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{ x,\, \{y_i\} } \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} s_{i}(\theta) \widehat{A}_{i} \cdot \nabla_{\theta} \log s_{i}(\theta) \right].
$$
Here lies a critical structural feature of GSPO. The term $s_i(\theta)$ represents a geometric mean of token probabilities raised to a power determined by sequence length. Specifically, $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as:
$$
s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{t,i}'(\theta) \right),
$$
where $w_{t,i}'(\theta)$ represents the raw token probability ratio at step $t$. Taking the logarithm of this expression simplifies it linearly:

Therefore: $$ nabla_theta log(s_theta(theta)) = (1/|y_theta|)) * sum(t=1 to |y_theta| nabla_theta log pi_theta(y_t|x,y_<t)). $$ Substituting this back into our main gradient equation yields: $$ nabla_theta J_GSPO(theta) = E[...] [ (1/G)) * sum(i=1 to G s_i(theta)) A_hat(i)) * ((1/|y_theta|i))) * sum(t=1 to |yi theta t nabla_theta log pi_theta(y_it|x,y_<t))). $$ Rearranging terms reveals that every token in a response contributes to the final gradient update scaled by exactly two factors: global sequence advantage and a uniform scalar weight derived from $s_i$. Importantly, unlike other formulations where individual tokens might carry different multiplicative weights based on their local likelihood shifts, here every token in response $y_i$ receives an identical multiplier from the importance ratio component before being summed up. This results in a clean aggregation where the total influence of response $i$ is distributed evenly across all its constituent tokens.

## Comparative Analysis: Uniform Weighting in GSPO vs. Variable Weighting in GRPO

Having established how GSPO constructs its gradients, it becomes imperative to contrast this mechanism with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which has been widely used prior to this development. GRPO attempts to optimize policies using token-level importance ratios. Its gradient formulation looks superficially similar but contains a fundamental divergence in how it treats individual tokens within a sequence. The GRPO gradient is expressed as:
$$
\nabla_{\theta} J_GRPO(theta)) = E[...] [ (1/G)) * sum(i=1 to G A_hat(i)) * ((1/|yi|i))) * sum(t=1 to |yi theta t w_it(theta)) nabla_theta log pi_theta(y_it|x,y_<t))). $$ Here, $w_it(theta)$ is defined strictly at each token position as $\frac{\pi_\Theta(y_it|x,y_<t)}{\pi_old(y_it|x,y_<t)}$. Notice that in GRPO, each token's contribution to the gradient is modulated by its own specific importance weight $w_it$. If a specific token transition was highly probable under old policy but unlikely under new policy (or vice versa), that specific token's gradient contribution could be massively amplified or suppressed independently of other tokens in the same sentence.

In stark contrast, GSPO replaces these variable local weights with a single global scalar per sequence. As derived earlier, GSPO weights all tokens within response $y_i$ equally via factor $(s_I)(I))/(|Y_I)|). While GRPO creates an uneven landscape where some tokens drive massive updates and others contribute negligently depending on local probability shifts alone, GSPO enforces uniformity across all positions for any given complete response.
