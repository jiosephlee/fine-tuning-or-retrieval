Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Foundations of Reinforcement Learning for Large Language Models

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for scaling the capabilities of large language models (LLMs), moving beyond static pre-training to dynamic, goal-oriented refinement. As the demand for AI systems capable of tackling sophisticated problems grows, RL provides the mechanism for models to develop deeper and longer reasoning processes. This shift is critical for domains requiring high levels of logical deduction, such as competition-level mathematics and advanced programming tasks. By engaging in iterative interaction with environments or verifiers, LLMs can learn to navigate complex solution spaces that were previously inaccessible. The ultimate goal of this scaling effort is to push the boundaries of what language models can achieve, enabling them to solve novel and intricate challenges through extended chains of thought. However, realizing this potential requires not just computational resources but also algorithms that remain stable and robust as model sizes and task complexities increase. Without a solid foundation in the mechanics of RL and an understanding of why current methods struggle at scale, it is impossible to appreciate the innovations required to sustain progress in this field.

The transition from smaller models to gigantic architectures introduces unique challenges that traditional RL algorithms must overcome. As models grow larger and incorporate sparse architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), the dynamics of training become increasingly fragile. The ability to maintain stable training trajectories is the foremost prerequisite for successful scaling; if an algorithm cannot handle the noise inherent in large-scale updates, it risks catastrophic failure. Therefore, before delving into specific solutions like Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), one must first grasp the fundamental motivations behind RL in LLMs and review the established baselines that currently dominate the landscape. Understanding how algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) function—and where they falter—is essential for comprehending the necessity of newer approaches designed specifically for stability and efficiency in massive scale settings.

# The Role of RL in Scaling LLM Capabilities

The integration of reinforcement learning into the lifecycle of large language models represents a significant evolution in how these systems acquire intelligence. Historically, language models were trained primarily via supervised learning on vast corpora of text, optimizing for next-token prediction accuracy. While effective for generating fluent text, this approach often falls short when tasked with solving problems that require multi-step reasoning or adherence to strict external criteria. Reinforcement learning addresses this gap by allowing models to optimize directly against a reward signal provided by a verifier or an environment. This process transforms the model from a passive predictor into an active agent capable of refining its behavior based on feedback about the quality of its outputs rather than just their statistical likelihood in a dataset.

Through large-scale RL training, language models have demonstrated remarkable advancements in their ability to handle complex cognitive tasks. For instance, in competitive mathematics, models can now engage in deep reasoning processes that involve formulating hypotheses, testing intermediate steps, and correcting errors autonomously before arriving at a final solution. Similarly, in programming contexts, RL enables models to generate code that not only compiles but also passes rigorous test suites and adheres to best practices learned through trial and error. These capabilities are not merely incremental improvements but represent qualitative leaps in performance that allow LLMs to operate at levels comparable to human experts in specialized fields. The key driver behind these achievements is the capacity for RL to guide the model toward behaviors that maximize cumulative reward over long horizons, fostering skills that are difficult to encode through simple imitation learning alone.

However, unlocking these capabilities requires scaling RL training with substantial computational investment. As researchers attempt to train ever-larger models on more diverse and difficult datasets, the complexity of the optimization landscape increases dramatically. The sheer volume of parameters and the length of generated responses mean that small instabilities in the training loop can compound rapidly, leading to degraded performance or complete system failure. Consequently, the pursuit of higher capabilities is tightly coupled with the need for more robust training methodologies. If an algorithm cannot maintain stability as compute scales up or as response lengths extend into thousands of tokens, it becomes a bottleneck preventing further advancement. Thus, understanding RL's role involves recognizing it not just as a tool for improvement but as a critical infrastructure component whose reliability determines whether we can successfully push LLMs toward new frontiers of intelligence.

# Basics of Off-Policy Learning and Importance Sampling Principles

To understand modern RL algorithms used in LLMs, one must first grasp the concept of off-policy learning and its relationship with importance sampling. In many practical scenarios involving large batches of data generation—often referred to as rollouts—it is computationally prohibitive to sample every single trajectory directly from the current policy being optimized ($\pi_\theta$). Instead, practitioners often collect data using an older version of the policy ($\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) and then use this historical data to update the current policy parameters $\theta$. This creates an off-policy setting where samples are drawn from a behavior distribution $\pi_\text{beh}$ (which corresponds here to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$) rather than from the target distribution $\pi_\text{tar}$ (corresponding to $\pi_\theta$). To ensure that updates based on this off-policy data correctly reflect expectations under the target distribution without biasing toward past behaviors or diverging uncontrollably from them, we rely on importance sampling techniques.

Importance sampling provides a mathematical framework for estimating expectations under one distribution using samples from another by applying corrective weights known as importance ratios or importance weights. Formally, if we wish to estimate the expectation $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [ f(z) ]$, we can rewrite it using samples $z$ drawn from $\pi_\text{beh}$ as:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{tar}} [ f(z) ] = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\text{beh}} \left[ \frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)} f(z) \right].
$$
Here, the term $\frac{ \pi_\text{tar} (z) }{ \pi_\text{beh} (z)}$ acts as a weighting factor that adjusts each sample's contribution based on how likely it would be under the target policy versus how likely it was under the behavior policy from which it was actually sampled. Crucially, this principle relies on averaging over multiple independent samples ($N \gg 1$) from $\pi_\text{beh}$; only through sufficient aggregation does this weighting scheme effectively correct for distributional mismatches and yield low-variance estimates close to true expectations under $\pi_\text{tar}$. Without enough samples or proper application across relevant units (such as sequences vs tokens), these weights fail their corrective purpose and instead inject high-variance noise into gradient estimations—a pitfall central to recent failures in scaling LLMs via RL.
