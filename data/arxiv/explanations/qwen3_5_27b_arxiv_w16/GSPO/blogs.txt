# Why Your AI Model Crashes: The Hidden Flaw in Current Reinforcement Learning

Training massive language models to reason deeply through complex math or code requires Reinforcement Learning (RL). Recently, algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have become the standard for scaling these models because they avoid the heavy computational cost of training separate value networks. However, as researchers push these models to handle longer responses and larger architectures, a critical instability emerges. Models frequently suffer from "catastrophic collapse," where performance degrades irreversibly no matter how much the hyperparameters are tuned. New research from the team behind Qwen3 reveals that this crash isn't just a bug; it is a fundamental flaw in how current algorithms calculate importance weights at the token level.

The root cause of this instability lies in a misapplication of importance sampling principles. In RL, we generate responses using an "old" version of the model and then try to update the "new" model based on how good those responses were. To do this correctly, we must adjust for the fact that the new model might assign different probabilities to those same tokens than the old one did. Standard theory dictates that this correction works best when averaging over many samples from a distribution. However, GRPO calculates an importance ratio for every single token individually based on just one sample path. When you chain thousands of these noisy, single-sample corrections together in a long response, the errors accumulate rapidly. This creates high-variance noise in the training gradients that eventually overwhelms the learning signal, causing the model to forget what it knows and collapse into gibberish.

To solve this, researchers introduced Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO). The core insight driving GSPO is simple yet profound: if your reward is given for an entire sequence of text, your optimization logic should operate at the sequence level, not the token level. Instead of calculating volatile importance ratios for each word independently, GSPO calculates a single importance ratio based on the total likelihood of the entire response $y$ given a prompt $x$. Mathematically, this shifts from weighting individual tokens with $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t})}$ to using a sequence-level ratio defined as $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. By aggregating probabilities before applying corrections and performing clipping on whole responses rather than individual tokens, GSPO eliminates the accumulation of noise that plagues token-level methods.

The practical benefits of shifting to sequence-level optimization are immediate and dramatic. Most notably, GSPO has solved a notorious headache associated with training Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models. In MoE architectures, small changes in model parameters can cause completely different sets of specialized "experts" to activate for a given input. Under GRPO's token-level view, this causes wild fluctuations in probability estimates that break training stability unless engineers implement complex workarounds like "Routing Replay." GSPO bypasses this entirely because it looks at the final sequence likelihood rather than intermediate token probabilities; since the overall language modeling capability remains stable even if specific experts swap out, training proceeds smoothly without extra engineering overhead.

Beyond stability, GSPO offers surprising gains in efficiency and infrastructure simplicity. Counter-intuitively, GSPO clips a significantly larger fraction of tokens—sometimes two orders of magnitude more than GRPO—yet it learns faster and reaches higher performance ceilings. This happens because GRPO's clipped tokens still contribute noisy gradients due to their flawed calculation method, whereas GSPO cleanly discards unreliable samples early on. Furthermore, because GSPO relies on sequence-level likelihoods which are less sensitive to floating-point precision differences between training and inference engines, developers can skip expensive recomputation steps during training loops. This streamlines RL infrastructure and paves the way for more scalable experiments with massive models and multi-turn interactions.

Ultimately, the transition from GRPO to GSPO represents a necessary evolution in how we train intelligent systems at scale. By aligning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward and adhering strictly to statistical principles of importance sampling, we can stop fighting against algorithmic noise and start pushing true capability boundaries. As demonstrated by its successful deployment in recent Qwen3 models, fixing these foundational flaws doesn't just prevent crashes; it unlocks continuous improvement as we increase compute budgets and task complexity.



# From Tokens to Stories: How GSPO Fixes AI Training Stability

Training massive language models with Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become the gold standard for unlocking advanced reasoning capabilities in math and coding. However, as models grow larger and generate longer responses, a critical bottleneck has emerged: training instability. State-of-the-art algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) often suffer from catastrophic model collapse when pushed to scale. This instability frequently renders continued training impossible, halting progress before the model reaches its full potential. The solution lies in a fundamental shift in perspective, moving away from analyzing individual words to evaluating the story as a whole. Enter Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), the new algorithm powering the latest Qwen3 models that finally solves this dilemma.

The root cause of instability in previous methods like GRPO lies in how they handle "importance sampling." In RL, we want to learn from data generated by an older version of the model to update the current one. To do this correctly, we must weigh the samples based on how likely they were under the old policy versus the new one. GRPO attempts this at the token level, calculating an importance ratio for every single word generated. Mathematically, it looks at $w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t}) }$. The problem is that this calculation relies on a single sample from a probability distribution. When you chain thousands of these noisy token-level calculations together in a long response, the errors accumulate rapidly. This creates high-variance noise in the training gradients, which is then amplified by clipping mechanisms designed to stabilize learning, eventually causing the model to collapse irreversibly.

GSPO addresses this by recognizing a simple but profound truth: if the reward is given for the entire response (the story), the optimization logic should also operate at the sequence level. Instead of trying to correct every token individually, GSPO calculates a single importance ratio for the whole sequence based on sequence likelihood. The formula becomes $s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$. By raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, GSPO normalizes for length and ensures that all tokens within a specific response contribute equally to the gradient update. This eliminates the compounding noise inherent in token-level weighting and aligns perfectly with how rewards are actually distributed in RL tasks.

This architectural change yields immediate benefits for stability and efficiency, particularly for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures like those used in Qwen3. MoE models activate different subsets of parameters ("experts") depending on the input, which can lead to volatility where experts change drastically between training steps. Previously, engineers had to implement complex workarounds like "Routing Replay" to force consistency in which experts were active just to keep GRPO from diverging. GSPO naturally avoids this issue because it focuses on overall sequence likelihood rather than fragile token probabilities. Consequently, GSPO stabilizes MoE training without needing these expensive memory-heavy tricks, allowing models to utilize their full capacity freely while converging smoothly even with extended generation lengths and updated query sets.

Beyond raw stability, GSPO offers surprising gains in training efficiency despite appearing more restrictive on paper. Because GSPO clips entire sequences rather than individual tokens, it rejects a significantly larger fraction of data points—sometimes two orders of magnitude more than GRPO—when they deviate too far from expected distributions. Counter-intuitively, discarding more data leads to faster learning because it removes high-noise signals entirely rather than diluting them with corrections that don't quite fit. Empirical results show that GSPO achieves higher benchmark scores on tasks like AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench with less compute compared to GRPO baselines. Furthermore, by relying only on sequence-level likelihoods, GSPO is more tolerant of precision differences between inference engines and training engines. This simplifies infrastructure requirements by potentially removing the need for costly recomputation steps during training pipelines.

The transition from token-centric correction to sequence-centric optimization represents a maturation in how we train intelligent systems. By treating generated text as coherent units rather than disjointed streams of probabilities, GSPO provides a robust foundation for scaling RL indefinitely. As demonstrated by its success in developing Qwen3, this approach not only prevents model collapse but accelerates performance gains across complex reasoning domains. As we continue to push the boundaries of what language models can achieve, algorithms like GSPO will be essential for navigating the treacherous waters of large-scale reinforcement learning with confidence and stability.



# Goodbye Workarounds: Why GSPO Makes Complex AI Architectures Easier to Train

Training massive language models using Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a critical step in developing systems capable of deep reasoning and complex problem-solving. However, as models grow larger and adopt sophisticated architectures like Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), the training process has become increasingly fragile. Historically, researchers have relied on complex engineering workarounds to maintain stability, often adding significant overhead to the training pipeline. A recent breakthrough, however, suggests that these complications might stem from the algorithms themselves rather than the models. Enter Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO), a new algorithm designed to stabilize training by fundamentally changing how importance sampling is applied, effectively removing the need for intricate fixes like "Routing Replay."

## The Instability Problem in Current RL Algorithms

To understand why GSPO is necessary, we must first look at the limitations of the current state-of-the-art method, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO improved upon earlier methods by eliminating the need for a separate value model, calculating advantages based on groups of responses to a single query. Despite its success, GRPO suffers from severe stability issues when scaled to gigantic models or long-generation tasks. In extreme cases, this instability leads to catastrophic model collapse, where the model's performance degrades irreversibly.

The root cause of this instability lies in how GRPO calculates importance ratios. Importance sampling is a statistical technique used to correct for differences between the distribution used to generate data (the old policy) and the distribution being optimized (the new policy). Mathematically, this requires weighting samples by the ratio of their probabilities under both distributions. GRPO attempts this at the **token level**, calculating an importance ratio $w_{t}(\theta)$ for every single token $y_t$ in a response:

$$ w_{t}(\theta) = \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{t} | x, y_{<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{t} | x,y_{<t}) } $$

The problem arises because this calculation relies on a single sample from each next-token distribution. When dealing with long sequences or sparse MoE models, small fluctuations in token probabilities create high-variance noise in these weights. As these noisy weights accumulate over hundreds or thousands of tokens and are processed through clipping mechanisms, they introduce erratic gradients that destabilize training. Essentially, trying to apply off-policy corrections at the granular token level creates more noise than it removes when rewards are assigned at the sequence level.

## Introducing GSPO: Shifting Focus to Sequences

GSPO addresses this fundamental flaw by realigning the unit of optimization with the unit of reward. Since rewards are typically calculated for an entire response sequence rather than individual tokens, GSPO argues that importance sampling should also occur at the **sequence level**. Instead of tracking token-by-token probability shifts, GSPO defines a sequence-level importance ratio $s_{i}(\theta)$ based on the total likelihood of generating response $y_i$:

$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$

By aggregating probabilities across the whole sequence before applying corrections, GSPO smooths out local fluctuations that plague token-level approaches. This formulation adheres strictly to the principles of importance sampling by treating the entire response as a single sample event. Consequently, GSPO applies clipping and optimization decisions to whole responses rather than individual tokens. This shift results in significantly lower variance in gradient estimates and prevents the accumulation of noise that causes model collapse in other algorithms. Empirically, this approach has demonstrated superior training efficiency and performance compared to GRPO while maintaining robustness even as generation lengths increase.

## Solving MoE Volatility Without Routing Replay

One of the most compelling benefits of GSPO is its ability to simplify training for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models without requiring complex architectural hacks. MoE models activate only a subset of parameters for each input to save computation costs during inference and training. However, this sparsity introduces a unique challenge known as "expert-activation volatility." During RL training with GRPO, slight changes in model parameters can cause different experts to be activated for subsequent steps within the same response trajectory compared to what was active during data collection.

Because GRPO relies on precise token-level likelihoods ($\pi(y_t|x...)$), swapping experts mid-sequence causes drastic jumps in probability values between the old and new policies. These jumps render token-level importance ratios meaningless and break training convergence. To combat this prior research introduced "Routing Replay," a workaround where engineers force the new policy to reuse exact expert routes from the old policy during loss calculation. While effective for stability, Routing Replay adds substantial memory overhead and communication costs between devices, limiting throughput and preventing full utilization of available experts during updates.

GSPO renders Routing Replay obsolete by design. Because GSPO operates on sequence-level likelihoods ($\pi(y|x)$), it is insensitive to which specific experts were activated at intermediate token positions; it only cares about whether the final sequence was likely under both policies. Even if expert activation patterns shift slightly between updates—a common occurrence—the overall sequence likelihood remains relatively stable because MoE models generally preserve their language modeling capabilities regardless of minor routing variations. By focusing on global sequence consistency rather than local token mechanics, GSPO naturally absorbs expert volatility without needing external constraints or caching mechanisms. This allows developers to train massive MoE models using standard pipelines while leveraging their full capacity freely.

## Simplifying Infrastructure and Future Scaling

Beyond algorithmic stability and MoE compatibility, GSPO offers practical advantages for RL infrastructure deployment. In many production environments, there exists a precision gap between high-performance inference engines (like vLLM or SGLang) used for generating rollouts and specialized training engines used for backpropagation. Traditionally, systems had to re-compute likelihoods using heavy training engines because token-level precision discrepancies could corrupt GRPO's delicate importance ratios.

Since GSPI utilizes sequence-level metrics which are inherently more tolerant of minor floating-point discrepancies between engines, it opens up possibilities for streamlining workflows entirely within inference engines or disaggregated frameworks where training and inference run separately.
