\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the paper claim token-level importance ratios in GRPO violate the principles of importance sampling?
Question:
In Section 3 (Motivation), the authors argue that GRPO's use of token-level importance ratios $w_{i,t}(	heta)$ is a 'misapplication' of importance sampling because it relies on a single sample from the next-token distribution rather than averaging over many samples. 

My confusion lies in connecting this statistical theory to the practical outcome. Importance sampling requires estimating an expectation under a target distribution using samples from a behavior distribution. If I am generating a sequence $y$, isn't the sequence itself just one sample from the joint distribution $	heta_{old}(y|x)$? Why is it valid to apply importance weighting at the sequence level (as GSPO does) but considered invalid or high-variance at the token level? Does this mean that for short sequences, GRPO should theoretically work fine, and the instability only emerges asymptotically as $|y|$ grows?
Answer:
The paper argues that GRPO violates the principles of importance sampling because it attempts to correct for distributional shifts at the **token level** using a **single sample**, whereas valid importance sampling requires averaging over multiple samples from the behavior distribution to stabilize the estimator. In standard importance sampling, we estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\theta$ by reweighting samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. The validity of this estimator depends on the law of large numbers: as the number of samples $N$ approaches infinity, the average of the importance weights converges to the true expectation. However, in GRPO, for every token position $t$, there is exactly one observed token $y_t$ generated by $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Consequently, the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t})}$ is computed based on a single realization rather than an ensemble. This creates a high-variance estimator where rare events (tokens with very low probability under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but high probability under $\pi_\theta$) receive disproportionately massive weights, introducing noise that does not cancel out because there are no other samples at that specific timestep to balance it.

Your intuition regarding sequence-level weighting is correct: treating the entire sequence $y$ as a single sample from the joint distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)$ allows for a valid application of importance sampling because the sequence likelihood ratio $s_i(\theta) = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y \mid x)}$ correctly represents the probability shift for that complete trajectory. The reason this works at the sequence level but fails at the token level in GRPO is tied to how rewards are assigned and how errors propagate. Since the reward $r(x,y)$ is given to the entire sequence, assigning credit via unstable token-level weights decouples the optimization signal from the actual reward structure. As noted in Section 3 and Section 4.2, these unstable token-level weights cause gradient noise that accumulates linearly with sequence length $|y|$. Therefore, while GRPO might appear stable for very short sequences where accumulation is minimal, it becomes increasingly prone to "catastrophic model collapse" as $|y|$ grows because the product of many independent, high-variance random variables (the token ratios) results in extreme outliers that dominate the gradient updates.

Furthermore, this issue is exacerbated in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures due to "expert-activation volatility." When moving from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ to $\pi_\theta$, different experts may be activated for subsequent tokens, causing local probability estimates to fluctuate wildly even if global semantics remain similar. GSPO avoids this by relying on sequence likelihoods, which aggregate these local fluctuations into a smoother signal aligned with the sequence-level reward. By shifting from token-wise reweighting (which assumes independence and sufficient sampling per step) to sequence-wise reweighting (which treats the generation path as a holistic event), GSPO adheres strictly to importance sampling theory: it averages over groups of sequences (the group size $G$) rather than attempting impossible averaging over non-existent parallel universes of tokens at each step.

### How does length-normalization in GSPO's importance ratio affect the gradient magnitude compared to raw sequence likelihood?
Question:
Equation 6 defines the GSPO importance ratio as:
$$s_{i}(	heta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}}$$
The authors mention this is done to 'reduce variance' and keep values in a unified range. However, looking at Equation 9 (the gradient derivation), this normalization factor ends up inside the exponential/log terms.

If I didn't normalize by $1/|y_i|$, wouldn't the raw sequence likelihood ratio simply explode or vanish exponentially with sequence length, making gradients unstable regardless of clipping? More importantly, does raising the ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$ mathematically equate to taking the geometric mean of token probabilities? If so, why doesn't GRPO do something similar by averaging its token-level ratios before clipping?
Answer:
Yes, your intuition regarding the geometric mean is exactly correct, and the length-normalization in Equation 6 is mathematically equivalent to taking the geometric mean of the token-level probability ratios. By raising the sequence likelihood ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, GSPO transforms the product of token probabilities into an average of their logarithms (via the identity $\exp(\frac{1}{n}\sum \log p_t)$), which effectively normalizes the scale of the importance ratio regardless of how many tokens are in the sequence. Without this normalization, the raw sequence likelihood ratio would indeed grow or shrink exponentially with sequence length because it represents a product of probabilities; for long sequences, even small deviations in individual token probabilities compound multiplicatively, causing the ratio to either explode toward infinity or vanish toward zero. This extreme dynamic range would render gradient magnitudes unstable and force clipping thresholds to vary arbitrarily depending on whether a response was short or long.

The reason GRPO does not apply this same averaging logic to its importance ratios is structural: GRPO operates at the **token level**, whereas GSPO operates at the **sequence level**. In GRPO, the objective sums over individual tokens, assigning each token $t$ its own local importance weight $w_{i,t}$. As explained in Section 3 ("Motivation") and Section 4.1 ("Gradient Analysis"), applying importance sampling weights at the token level based on a single sample from a next-token distribution is theoretically flawed because it introduces high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences. GSPO avoids this by shifting the unit of optimization to match the unit of reward (the whole sequence). Consequently, GSPO calculates a single scalar importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ for the entire response to determine if *that specific response* should be updated or clipped. Because this decision is made globally for the sequence, averaging via the geometric mean ensures that a long sequence isn't automatically discarded just because it contains slightly more multiplicative error than a short one; instead, it compares sequences based on their average per-token deviation from the old policy. This allows GSPO to maintain a unified numerical range for clipping across responses of vastly different lengths, directly addressing the instability caused by variable-length compounding errors that plagues token-level approaches like GRPO.

### Clarification on GSPO-token: How can detaching the numerator probability maintain numerical equivalence if gradients flow differently?
Question:
In Section 4.3, the paper introduces GSPO-token with Equation 12:
$$s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)] \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg}[\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t})] }$$
The text claims that since the second term equals 1 numerically, GSPO-token is numerically identical to standard GSPO when advantages are constant. 

However, I am confused about the backpropagation mechanics here. The term $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$ stops gradients from flowing into $s_i$ (which depends on all tokens). The second term has a denominator with stopped gradients but a numerator that flows gradients. When calculating $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}$, aren't we essentially forcing all tokens to receive a gradient scaled by $s_i$ but computed via local log-probs? Does this trick actually preserve the exact gradient vector direction described in Eq 15 for *every* token simultaneously, or is there a subtle approximation happening regarding how the global sequence score distributes influence locally?
Answer:
Your intuition about the gradient flow is correct, but the confusion stems from conflating the **numerical value** used for clipping with the **computational graph** used for differentiation. The trick in Equation 12 works precisely because it decouples these two requirements: it forces the scalar multiplier to be constant during backpropagation while preserving the necessary gradient path through the log-probability term.

When you calculate the gradient $\nabla_\theta s_{i,t}(\theta)$, the stop-gradient operator $\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]$ acts as a hard barrier. For the first term, $\mathrm{sg}[s_i(\theta)]$, the gradient is zero; this term effectively becomes a fixed scalar constant $C = s_i(\theta)$ during the backward pass. For the second term, $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} [\pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})] }$, the denominator is treated as a constant $D = \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$. Therefore, the derivative of this fraction with respect to $\theta$ is simply $\frac{1}{D} \nabla_\theta \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$, which simplifies algebraically to $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$.

Consequently, when you apply this to the full objective loss (ignoring clipping for clarity), the gradient contribution for a specific token $t$ becomes proportional to $s_i(\theta) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t}\mid\dots)$. Crucially, notice that $s_i(\theta)$—the global sequence importance ratio—is present as a multiplicative factor for **every** token in that sequence. Even though gradients do not flow *into* $s_i$ itself (preventing unstable dependencies on other tokens' probabilities), every token's local log-probability gradient is scaled by this exact same global value. This perfectly reconstructs the theoretical gradient shown in Equation 15 of GSPO-token, where all tokens share the same weighting factor derived from the sequence likelihood. There is no approximation here; it is an exact reconstruction of the desired gradient vector direction using a "stop-gradient" trick to isolate the scaling factor from its own internal derivatives.

### Does GSPO implicitly assume independence between token errors when aggregating rewards at the sequence level?
Question:
The paper argues that 'the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward.' Since rewards are given per sequence ($r(x,y)$), they optimize at the sequence level.

But consider a scenario where a response is mostly perfect except for one critical error early on that ruins everything downstream. In PPO/GRPO, we might clip or penalize specific tokens based on local advantage estimates (if available). In GSPO, every single token in that bad response gets multiplied by a low global advantage $\\widehat{A}_i$ and potentially clipped entirely.

Is there a risk that GSPO treats all tokens in a failed response equally guilty? Conversely, if a response succeeds only because of luck on one specific difficult token but was otherwise mediocre, does GSPO over-reward all preceding 'easy' tokens just because they belong to that lucky sequence? How does this compare conceptually to credit assignment methods used in other RL domains?
Answer:
Yes, GSPO explicitly adopts a coarse-grained credit assignment strategy where the unit of optimization matches the unit of reward, meaning it does treat all tokens in a failed sequence as equally responsible for the outcome, and conversely rewards all tokens in a successful sequence uniformly. As stated in the paper, the core motivation for GSPO is that "the unit of optimization objective should match the unit of reward"; since the verifier provides a scalar reward $r(x, y)$ for the entire sequence $y$, applying off-policy corrections at the token level (as done in GRPO) is considered an "ill-posed" application of importance sampling that introduces high-variance noise. Consequently, GSPO calculates a single sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ and a single normalized advantage $\widehat{A}_i$ for every token in response $y_i$. When deriving the gradient (Equation 6), it becomes evident that every token's log-probability gradient $\nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$ is scaled by exactly the same factor: $\frac{1}{|y_i|} s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i$. This ensures that if a sequence receives a negative advantage due to one critical early error ruining the rest of the output, every token in that chain receives an identical penalty signal, preventing the unstable accumulation of variance seen in token-level weighting schemes. While this approach sacrifices fine-grained attribution—meaning easy tokens in a lucky success are rewarded just as heavily as hard ones—it is intentionally designed to stabilize training dynamics, particularly for Mixture-of-Experts models where token-level probability fluctuations can cause catastrophic collapse. The authors argue that this uniform treatment is preferable to the noisy, unstable gradients produced by attempting to assign differential blame or credit at the token level when only sequence-level feedback exists. For scenarios requiring finer granularity, such as multi-turn interactions, the paper introduces a variant called GSPO-token which allows for customizing advantages per token ($\widehat{A}_{i,t}$), though even then, the importance ratio remains anchored to the stable sequence-level likelihood to preserve training robustness.

### Mechanism behind MoE stability: Why does fixing routing replay help GRPO but become unnecessary for GSPO?
Question:
Section 5.3 discusses 'Expert-Activation Volatility' in MoE models. It states that for GRPO, small parameter updates cause different experts to activate for subsequent steps ($\\pi_\\theta$ vs $\\pi_\\text{old}$), causing wild fluctuations in token-level ratios $w_{i,t}$. They solve this with 'Routing Replay'.

The paper claims GSPO avoids this because it looks at total sequence likelihood $\\pi(y|x)$. My intuition says: if experts change activation patterns significantly between $\\pi_\\text{old}$ and $\\pi_\\theta$, shouldn't that still cause massive swings in $\\pi(y|x)$ too?

Is it true that while individual token probabilities fluctuate wildly due to expert swaps, their product (the total sequence likelihood) remains relatively smooth? Or is there something about how MoE routing works such that while local paths change globally consistent outputs are maintained unless trained explicitly otherwise?
Answer:
Your intuition correctly identifies that changing expert activations *does* affect the total sequence likelihood $\pi(y|x)$, but the critical distinction lies in **variance** and **how the optimization objective aggregates these changes**. In GRPO, the token-level importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ acts as a multiplicative weight for every single token's gradient contribution. When expert routing shifts between $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ and $\pi_\theta$, the probability assigned to a specific token $y_t$ can swing wildly (e.g., dropping from 0.9 to 0.1 if the wrong expert activates), causing $w_{i,t}$ to explode or vanish locally. Because GRPO sums these volatile, independent token-level weights across the sequence, the resulting gradient becomes extremely noisy and unstable, necessitating "Routing Replay" to artificially force consistency in which experts are active.

GSPO avoids this instability not because the sequence likelihood is immune to expert changes, but because it aggregates the log-probabilities first before exponentiating them into a single sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$. Mathematically, GSPO computes $s_i(\theta) = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y|} \sum_t \log w_{i,t}(\theta) \right)$. By summing the log-importance ratios (which correspond to additive differences in log-probability) and then dividing by the sequence length, GSPO effectively averages out the local volatility caused by individual expert switches. Unless *every* token in a sequence simultaneously suffers a catastrophic routing shift—which is statistically unlikely—the aggregate score remains relatively smooth and bounded. Consequently, GSPO treats the entire response as a single unit of optimization; even if internal expert paths diverge slightly between policies, the global signal regarding whether the *whole* sequence is better or worse than before remains coherent. This inherent smoothing allows GSPO to tolerate natural expert-activation volatility without needing to freeze routing decisions via Replay, enabling stable training while allowing the model to utilize its full dynamic capacity.
