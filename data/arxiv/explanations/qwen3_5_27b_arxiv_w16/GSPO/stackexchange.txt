\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Group Sequence Policy Optimization"}

### Why does the paper claim token-level importance sampling is 'ill-posed' for sequence rewards?
Question:
In the Motivation section, the authors state that GRPO's objective is 'ill-posed' because it applies importance sampling weights at the token level ($w_{i,t}$) while the reward $r(x, y)$ is granted to the entire sequence. 

I understand that importance sampling requires re-weighting samples to correct for distribution mismatch. However, since the policy gradient theorem typically operates on token-level log-likelihoods ($\nabla \log \pi(y_t|\dots)$), why is it theoretically invalid to use token-level weights if we are just summing them up over the sequence? Specifically, how does using a single sample $y_{i,t}$ from the next-token distribution fail to correct the distributional mismatch compared to using the full sequence likelihood?
Answer:
The paper argues that token-level importance sampling is "ill-posed" in GRPO because it violates the fundamental statistical requirement of importance sampling: the correction weight must correspond to the probability of the **entire sample** being drawn from the behavior distribution, not just a single step within it.

In standard importance sampling, to estimate an expectation under a target distribution $\pi_\theta$ using samples from a behavior distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$, one must re-weight the full sample $z$ by the ratio $\frac{\pi_\theta(z)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(z)}$. In the context of language generation, the "sample" is the complete sequence $y$, and the reward $r(x, y)$ is assigned to this entire sequence. GRPO, however, attempts to correct for off-policy data by applying weights $w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | \dots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_{i,t} | \dots)}$ at each individual token position $t$. The authors contend that this is theoretically invalid because a single token $y_{i,t}$ represents only one realization from the next-token distribution $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(\cdot | x, y_{<t})$, not a full sample from which an expectation can be reliably estimated. Since importance sampling relies on averaging over many samples to correct for distributional mismatch, applying a weight derived from a single token instance introduces high-variance noise rather than a valid correction.

This mismatch becomes catastrophic as sequence length increases because these noisy token-level weights are summed or averaged over time. In GRPO, every token in a sequence receives its own independent importance ratio, which can fluctuate wildly (especially in Mixture-of-Experts models where expert activation changes between updates). These fluctuations accumulate, creating unstable gradients that lead to model collapse. The paper emphasizes that the "unit of optimization" must match the "unit of reward." Since the reward is sequence-level, the importance correction must also be sequence-level. GSPO resolves this by defining a single importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ based on the full sequence likelihood $\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}$, ensuring that the re-weighting correctly reflects how likely the entire generated response is under the new policy versus the old one. This approach eliminates the accumulation of token-level noise and aligns with the theoretical principles of off-policy learning.

### How does length normalization in GSPO's importance ratio prevent variance explosion?
Question:
The paper defines the GSPO importance ratio as:
$$ s_{i}(\theta) = \left( \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_	ext{old}} (y_i | x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} $$
The authors mention this length normalization is used to 'reduce variance' and keep ratios in a unified range. 

Mathematically, if we didn't take the $|y_i|$-th root, wouldn't we just be dealing with a product of many probabilities? I'm confused about why this specific root transformation stabilizes training. If a long sequence has a very low probability under both policies, doesn't the ratio still fluctuate wildly? How does raising it to the power of $1/|y|$ specifically mitigate the accumulation of noise over long sequences that causes model collapse in GRPO?
Answer:
The length normalization in GSPO's importance ratio, specifically raising the sequence likelihood ratio to the power of $1/|y_i|$, prevents variance explosion by transforming the metric from a multiplicative accumulation of token-level probabilities into an average log-likelihood ratio, thereby decoupling the magnitude of the importance weight from the sequence length.

In standard importance sampling without this normalization, the raw ratio $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_i | x) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_i | x)}$ is the product of individual token ratios across the entire sequence. Since language model probabilities are typically small numbers, multiplying them over a long sequence causes the resulting value to either vanish towards zero or explode towards infinity exponentially with respect to $|y_i|$. This creates a scenario where long sequences inherently possess extreme importance weights regardless of their semantic quality, introducing massive variance into the gradient estimates. As noted in the paper, this accumulation of noise is exacerbated by the clipping mechanism in algorithms like GRPO, leading to unstable training and model collapse.

By applying the $1/|y_i|$ exponent, GSPO effectively computes the geometric mean of the token-level ratios, which is mathematically equivalent to exponentiating the average of the log-ratios: $\exp \left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} (y_{i,t} | x,y_{i,<t})} \right)$. This transformation ensures that a single token with a drastically shifted probability does not dominate the entire sequence's weight; instead, its impact is diluted by averaging over all tokens. Consequently, importance ratios for sequences of varying lengths are brought into a unified numerical range where they can be meaningfully compared and clipped using a consistent threshold. This stabilizes the optimization landscape by ensuring that the "off-policy" correction reflects the average deviation per token rather than an accumulated product that scales uncontrollably with generation length.

### Clarification on GSPO-token: Why detach the current policy probability in the numerator?
Question:
In Section 4.3, the paper introduces GSPO-token with a modified importance ratio:
$$ s_{i,t}(\theta) = \mathrm{sg} \left[ s_{i}(\theta) \right]  \cdot \frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} | x, y_{i,<t}) \right] } $$
The text notes that this term numerically equals 1 but stops gradients through the denominator.

I am struggling to understand the purpose of this construction. If we are computing gradients with respect to $\theta$, why do we need to detach $\pi_\theta$ in both places? 

1. If we want token-level advantages, why not just use standard PPO/GRPO token weights?
2. How does this specific formulation ensure that $s_{i,t}$ remains numerically equal to $s_i$ while allowing different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ per token? 
3. Does this mean GSPO-token effectively uses a 'fixed' sequence weight for all tokens in a response during a single update step?
Answer:
The construction of $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ in GSPO-token is a deliberate mechanism to decouple the **magnitude of the importance weight** from the **source of the gradient signal**, thereby preserving the stability of sequence-level optimization while enabling token-level advantage customization.

The core reason for detaching $\pi_\theta$ in both the numerator and denominator of the fraction $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) }{ \mathrm{sg} \left[ \pi_{\theta} (y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t}) \right] }$ is to ensure that this specific term acts as a numerical identity (value of 1) without contributing any gradient. If you were to compute gradients through this fraction, you would recover the standard token-level importance ratio $\frac{ \pi_{\theta} }{ \pi_{\theta_\text{old}} }$, which GSPO explicitly avoids due to its high variance and instability, particularly in Mixture-of-Experts models. By using `sg` (stop gradient) on both parts, the fraction vanishes from the gradient computation entirely, leaving only the gradient from $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$ scaled by the sequence-level weight $s_i(\theta)$.

This design directly answers your questions:
1.  **Why not standard PPO/GRPO weights?** Standard token-level weights introduce high-variance noise because they attempt to correct distributional mismatch at every single token step based on a single sample. GSPO argues that since rewards are sequence-level, the correction should also be sequence-level. The GSPO-token formulation allows you to assign different advantages $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ per token (e.g., for multi-turn RL or specific token rewards) without reintroducing the unstable token-level importance ratios that cause model collapse in GRPO.
2.  **How does it ensure equality while allowing different advantages?** The term $s_{i,t}(\theta)$ is numerically identical to $s_i(\theta)$ because the fraction equals 1. However, in the gradient derivation (Equation 4 in the paper), this term disappears from the differentiation chain rule for $\theta$. The resulting gradient becomes a sum over tokens where each token's log-probability gradient is weighted by the *same* stable sequence-level factor $s_i(\theta)$ and its *own* specific advantage $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$. This effectively gives you "token-wise advantage" but "sequence-wise weighting."
3.  **Is it a fixed sequence weight?** Yes, for a single update step on a specific response $y_i$, every token within that response shares the exact same importance scaling factor $s_i(\theta)$. This ensures that no single token can disproportionately influence the update due to an outlier importance ratio, maintaining the robustness of GSPO while providing the flexibility to penalize or reward specific tokens via $\widehat{A}_{i,t}$ if needed.

In essence, this trick allows GSPO-token to function as a hybrid: it uses the stable, low-variance sequence likelihood ratio for clipping and scaling (preventing collapse), but distributes that stable signal across tokens using potentially varied advantages, avoiding the pitfalls of computing unstable per-token importance ratios.

### How does GSPO resolve MoE expert-activation volatility without Routing Replay?
Question:
The paper argues that GRPO fails on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models because small changes in parameters cause different experts to activate for the same token under $\pi_\theta$ vs $\pi_{\theta_	ext{old}}$, making token-level ratios $w_{i,t}$ unstable. They previously solved this with 'Routing Replay'.

However, they claim GSPO solves this naturally because it uses sequence likelihoods which are 'not sensitive' to individual token likelihood fluctuations.

Intuitively, if an expert switch happens at token $t$, shouldn't that drastically change $\pi_\theta(y_t | x, y_{<t})$ and thus change the total sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x)$? Why is the *product* of probabilities (sequence likelihood) more stable than individual terms when expert routing changes? Is there an assumption here about how expert switches affect the overall log-probability sum versus individual log-probabilities?
Answer:
The intuition that a single expert switch should drastically alter the total sequence likelihood is mathematically correct for the raw product of probabilities, but GSPO resolves this instability through **length-normalized geometric averaging** and the **law of large numbers** applied to the log-probability sum.

In a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, a parameter update can indeed cause a specific token $y_t$ to be routed to a different expert, causing the token-level probability ratio $w_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_t|\dots)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t|\dots)}$ to fluctuate wildly (e.g., from $0.1$ to $10.0$). In GRPO, which applies importance sampling at the token level, this single volatile ratio directly scales the gradient for that token, introducing high-variance noise that accumulates and destabilizes training. This is why "Routing Replay" was previously required: it artificially forced the new policy $\pi_\theta$ to use the same experts as $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ just for the sake of calculating a stable ratio.

GSPO avoids this by shifting the unit of importance sampling from the token to the entire sequence. The GSPO importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ is defined as:
$$ s_i(\theta) = \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)} \right)^{\frac{1}{|y_i|}} = \exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log w_{i,t}(\theta) \right) $$
This formulation effectively computes the **geometric mean** of all token-level ratios in the sequence. While an individual expert switch at token $t$ might cause $\log w_{i,t}$ to spike or drop significantly, this single term is divided by the sequence length $|y_i|$ in the exponent. In long sequences typical of RL training (e.g., hundreds or thousands of tokens), a single outlier term has negligible impact on the average sum $\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum \log w_{i,t}$. The fluctuations from different tokens tend to average out, resulting in a stable sequence-level ratio $s_i(\theta)$ even if individual routing decisions change.

Furthermore, GSPO aligns with the fundamental principle of importance sampling: since the reward $r(x, y)$ is assigned to the **entire sequence**, the correction factor should also be computed at the sequence level. By using $s_i(\theta)$ to weight the entire gradient update for response $y_i$, GSPO treats all tokens in that response equally (as seen in Equation 6 of the paper), rather than weighting them individually by their volatile local ratios. This means that even if an expert switch changes $\pi_\theta(y_t|\dots)$ locally, it does not create a disproportionate gradient spike for that specific token; instead, it contributes slightly to a stable aggregate signal for the whole response. Consequently, GSPO naturally absorbs expert-activation volatility without needing to freeze routing decisions via Replay, allowing the model to fully utilize its dynamic MoE capacity while maintaining training stability.

### Why does clipping more tokens in GSPO lead to better efficiency than clipping fewer in GRPO?
Question:
In Section 5.2 ('Curious Observation on Clipping Fractions'), Figure 2 shows that GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO (because it clips entire sequences if *any* part deviates too much?), yet achieves higher training efficiency.

My intuition suggests that clipping removes data from gradient updates. If GSPO discards far more data points (tokens) than GRPO, shouldn't its effective sample size be smaller and convergence slower? The authors claim GRPO's gradients are 'noisy and inefficient'. Can someone explain how discarding more data via sequence-level clipping results in a *cleaner* or *more effective* gradient signal compared to keeping noisy token-level gradients?
Answer:
The counter-intuitive observation that GSPO achieves higher efficiency despite clipping significantly more tokens stems from the fundamental difference in how GRPO and GSPO handle gradient noise and the validity of their importance sampling weights. In GRPO, the clipping mechanism operates at the token level, meaning that even if a single token within a sequence has an importance ratio $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ that deviates too far from 1, only that specific token is clipped while the rest of the sequence remains active in the gradient update. The paper argues that this token-level weighting is theoretically flawed because it applies importance sampling weights derived from a single sample per token distribution, which introduces high-variance noise rather than correcting for distributional mismatch. This noise accumulates over long sequences, particularly in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models where expert activation volatility causes these ratios to fluctuate drastically, leading to unstable and inefficient gradients.

In contrast, GSPO performs clipping at the sequence level using the importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$, which is based on the full sequence likelihood $\pi_\theta(y|x) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y|x)$. If any part of a sequence causes the overall likelihood ratio to exceed the clipping threshold, the entire sequence is discarded from the gradient update. While this results in a much higher fraction of clipped tokens (two orders of magnitude more than GRPO), it effectively filters out "noisy" or "off-policy" sequences entirely rather than allowing their corrupted token-level gradients to pollute the update. By discarding these problematic sequences, GSPO ensures that every remaining gradient contribution comes from a sample where the policy shift is controlled and consistent across all tokens. Consequently, although GSPO uses fewer data points (tokens) per batch, the signal-to-noise ratio of its gradients is significantly higher. The paper demonstrates that GRPO's "noisy" gradients require more compute to converge because they are fighting against instability and variance, whereas GSPO's "cleaner" sequence-level signal allows for faster and more stable convergence despite the apparent loss of data volume.

### Derivation check: How does GSPO's gradient weight all tokens equally?
Question:
In Section 4.2, Equation 6 shows the gradient for GSPO:
$$ \nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{J}_	ext{GSPO} = \\mathbb{E} [ \\dots s_i(\\theta) \\widehat{A}_i \\cdot \\frac{1}{|y_i|} \\sum_t \\nabla_\\theta \\log \\pi_\\theta(y_t|\\dots) ] $$
The authors state: 'GSPO weights all the tokens in a response equally.'

However, looking at Equation 7 for GRPO:
$$ \\nabla_\\theta \\mathcal{J}_	ext{GRPO} = \\mathbb{E} [ \\dots \\widehat{A}_i \\cdot \\frac{1}{|y_i|} \\sum_t w_{i,t}(\\theta) \\nabla_\\theta \\log \\pi_\\theta(y_t|\\dots) ] $$
The difference seems to be that GRPO multiplies each term by its own specific weight $w_{i,t}$ inside the sum, whereas GSPO pulls $s_i(\\theta)$ outside.

Is my understanding correct that in GRPO, if one token has a high importance ratio (e.g., it was very unlikely under old policy but likely under new), its gradient contribution is amplified relative to other tokens in the same sentence? And in GSPO, even if one token drives a huge change in sequence probability, its gradient contribution is averaged out by dividing by length and multiplying by a single global scalar? If so, doesn't this risk ignoring critical 'correction' steps for specific bad tokens?
Answer:
Your understanding of the mathematical distinction is correct, but your concern about GSPO "ignoring" critical correction steps stems from a slight misinterpretation of how the gradient flow operates in this specific formulation. While it is true that GSPO applies a single scalar weight $s_i(\theta)$ to the sum of token gradients, this does not mean individual tokens are treated identically in terms of their *direction* or *magnitude* of update; rather, they are treated identically in terms of their *importance sampling correction*.

In GRPO, the term $w_{i,t}(\theta)$ inside the summation acts as a per-token importance weight. As you noted, if a specific token has a high probability ratio (e.g., the new policy finds it much more likely than the old one), its gradient contribution is amplified relative to other tokens in that sequence. The authors argue this is problematic because $w_{i,t}$ is computed from a single sample at that token position, leading to high-variance noise that accumulates over long sequences and causes instability, particularly in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models where expert routing can change drastically between updates.

In contrast, GSPO moves the importance ratio to the sequence level via $s_i(\theta) = \left(\frac{\pi_\theta(y_i|x)}{\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_i|x)}\right)^{1/|y_i|}$. When deriving the gradient (Equation 9), this results in:
$$ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{J}_\text{GSPO} \propto s_i(\theta) \widehat{A}_i \cdot \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t|\dots) $$
Here, every token's gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t|\dots)$ is indeed multiplied by the same global scalar $s_i(\theta)$. However, this scalar represents the *average* geometric mean of all token-level importance ratios in that sequence. The key insight from the paper is that because the reward $\widehat{A}_i$ is assigned to the entire sequence, the optimization unit should match the reward unit. By using a sequence-level weight, GSPO ensures that if a sequence is "off-policy" (i.e., highly unlikely under the old policy), the entire sequence's update is clipped or scaled uniformly, preventing any single noisy token ratio from dominating or destabilizing the update.

Regarding your worry about ignoring "bad tokens": GSPO does not ignore them; it simply corrects for off-policy distribution shifts at the sequence level rather than the token level. If a specific token was generated with very low probability under $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ but high probability under $\pi_\theta$, this will contribute significantly to increasing $s_i(\theta)$. Consequently, *all* tokens in that sequence receive a boosted gradient signal proportional to this global shift. The paper argues that this uniform scaling is more stable because it avoids the "ill-posed" nature of estimating distribution corrections from single-token samples. If fine-grained control over specific tokens is desired (e.g., for multi-turn RL), the authors explicitly introduce **GSPO-token** (Section 4.3), which allows for per-token advantages while maintaining the stable sequence-level importance ratio $s_i(\theta)$ as a stop-gradient term. Thus, GSPO trades per-token variance reduction for global stability, which empirically prevents model collapse and simplifies infrastructure by removing dependencies like Routing Replay.
