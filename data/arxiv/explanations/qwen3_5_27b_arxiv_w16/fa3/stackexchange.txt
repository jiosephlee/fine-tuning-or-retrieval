\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "FlashAttention-3: Fast and Accurate Attention with Asynchrony and Low-precision"}

### How does 'incoherent processing' mathematically preserve the attention output despite applying a random matrix M?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors introduce 'incoherent processing' to handle outliers in FP8 quantization. They state that they multiply $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ by a random orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{M}$ before quantization.

They claim that since $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$, the equality $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ holds. I am confused because if we expand the left side, we get $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{M}^\top \mathbf{K}^\top) = \mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top)\mathbf{K}^\top$. 

Does this imply that $\mathbf{M}$ must be square ($d \times d$)? If so, doesn't this require an extra $O(d^2)$ matrix multiplication step that contradicts their claim of fusing it with rotary embedding at 'no extra computation cost'? Or is there a property of the specific construction of $\mathbf{M}$ (product of diagonal matrices and Hadamard) that changes the computational complexity?
Answer:
Your intuition regarding the algebra is correct: for the identity $(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M})(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{M})^\top = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$ to hold exactly via the cancellation $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^\top = I$, the matrix $\mathbf{M}$ must indeed be a square $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix acting on the feature dimension. However, your concern about this introducing an $O(d^2)$ computational overhead is resolved by the specific construction of $\mathbf{M}$ chosen by the authors. Rather than using a dense random orthogonal matrix which would require a costly general matrix multiplication, FlashAttention-3 constructs $\mathbf{M}$ as a product of random diagonal matrices with entries of $\pm 1$ and a Hadamard matrix.

This specific structure allows the transformation $\mathbf{x}\mathbf{M}$ to be computed in $O(d \log d)$ time rather than $O(d^2)$, leveraging fast transforms similar to the Fast Walsh-Hadamard Transform. More importantly for your question about "no extra computation cost," this transformation is designed to be fused with the rotary position embedding (RoPE) operation. Since RoPE involves element-wise complex rotations or equivalent linear operations that are already being performed on every token, and because both RoPE and this structured orthogonal projection are memory-bandwidth bound (meaning they spend more time waiting for data than performing arithmetic), fusing them incurs negligible latency penalty. The authors explicitly state in Section 3.3 that this fusion happens "with no additional slow down." Therefore, while $\mathbf{M}$ is mathematically square and preserves the attention logits exactly in infinite precision, its sparse/structured nature ensures it does not violate the efficiency goals of the algorithm, effectively spreading outlier magnitudes across all dimensions to improve FP8 quantization stability without adding a distinct computational bottleneck.

### Clarification on the 'k-major' layout constraint for FP8 WGMMA in FlashAttention-3
Question:
Section 2.2 states that for FP8 WGMMA on Hopper, only the 'k-major' format is supported for operands in Shared Memory (SMEM), whereas FP16 accepts both mn-major and k-major.

In the context of the attention forward pass, we compute $S = Q K^T$ followed by $O = P V$. 
1. For the first GEMM ($Q K^T$), if $K$ needs to be k-major, does this mean the stride along the sequence dimension must be smaller than the head dimension?
2. More confusingly, for the second GEMM ($P V$), why does the paper suggest transposing $V$ inside the kernel? If $V$ is originally stored in memory as contiguous in the head dimension (standard row-major for transformers), isn't it already 'mn-major' (contiguous in N/M)? Why does FP8 WGMMA specifically require it to be contiguous in K (sequence length) for this operation?

I suspect I am misunderstanding how the indices map to M, N, and K dimensions in this specific attention formulation.
Answer:
The confusion stems from mapping the abstract GEMM dimensions ($M, N, K$) to the physical attention dimensions (sequence length $N_{\text{seq}}$ and head dimension $d$) differently for each of the two matrix multiplications in the attention mechanism. The "k-major" requirement for FP8 WGMMA on Hopper dictates that the **inner** dimension of the matrix multiplication must be contiguous in memory (Shared Memory), regardless of what that dimension represents physically.

For the first GEMM, computing $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top$, the operation is effectively $(B_r \times d) \times (d \times B_c)$, where $B_r$ is the query block size and $B_c$ is the key block size. Here, the head dimension $d$ acts as the inner dimension ($K$ in GEMM terminology). Therefore, satisfying the k-major constraint means $K$ must be contiguous along this inner dimension. Consequently, yes, for this specific operation, the stride along the sequence dimension must be larger than (or equal to) the head dimension stride such that adjacent elements in memory differ by 1 index in the head dimension. If your data is standard row-major (contiguous in head dimension), $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ naturally satisfy this requirement without modification because they are already contiguous in their respective "inner" dimensions relative to this multiplication.

The second GEMM, computing $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{V}$, presents a different geometric alignment. Here, $\mathbf{P}$ has shape $(B_r \times B_c)$ and $\mathbf{V}$ has shape $(B_c \times d)$. In this multiplication, the inner dimension ($K$ in GEMM terms) is $B_c$, which corresponds to a slice of the **sequence length**. Standard transformer implementations store $\mathbf{V}$ as contiguous in the head dimension ($d$), meaning it is "mn-major" with respect to this specific GEMM (contiguous in $N=d$, not $K=B_c$). Since FP8 WGMMA strictly requires operands to be k-major (contiguous in $K=\text{sequence\_length}$), a standard row-major $\mathbf{V}$ tile fails this check. Unlike FP16 WGMMA which tolerates both layouts, FP8 cannot natively consume a tile where elements are spaced apart by `\texttt{head_dim}` bytes when it expects them to be packed consecutively.

Therefore, the paper suggests transposing $\mathbf{V}$ inside the kernel because performing a global memory transpose beforehand would waste bandwidth on a memory-bound operation. Instead, FlashAttention-3 loads a tile of $\mathbf{V}$ into Shared Memory and immediately performs an intra-SMEM transpose using efficient `\texttt{ldmatrix}/\texttt{stmatrix}` instructions. This rearranges the data so that it becomes contiguous along the sequence length dimension before being fed into the FP8 Tensor Core. Additionally, because FP8 accumulators have a different register layout than FP16 accumulators relative to their operands, this internal transpose also facilitates necessary register permutations (byte shuffling) required to align the output of the first GEMM ($\mathbf{P}$) with the input expectations of the second GEMM's accumulator format.

### How does Ping-Pong scheduling resolve latency when softmax depends on GEMM output?
Question:
The paper describes a 'Ping-Pong scheduling' strategy (Section 3.1) where Warpgroup 1 performs softmax while Warpgroup 2 performs GEMMs, effectively hiding the low-throughput exponential operations behind high-throughput Tensor Core computations.

My confusion lies in the dependency graph. In standard FlashAttention, calculating $P_j$ (softmax scores) requires waiting for $S_j = Q K_j^T$ to finish. The second GEMM ($P_j V_j$) then waits for $P_j$. 

If Warpgroup A is doing softmax on Block $j$, it implies Block $j$'s GEMM is done. But if Warpgroup B is simultaneously doing GEMM on Block $j+1$, how do they coordinate? Does Warpgroup B simply issue its WGMMA asynchronously without waiting for Warpgroup A? If so, how do they ensure that when Warpgroup A finishes softmax for Block $j$, Warpgroup B hasn't already overwritten necessary shared memory buffers intended for Block $j+1$, or vice versa? Is there a hidden synchronization barrier I'm missing?
Answer:
The coordination you are concerned about is resolved by **software pipelining** combined with **explicit synchronization barriers**, ensuring that data dependencies are strictly respected despite the asynchronous execution of the underlying hardware units. You are correct that in a naive implementation, Warpgroup B could overwrite shared memory (SMEM) buffers needed by Warpgroup A if they were operating on the same physical locations simultaneously; however, FlashAttention-3 avoids this collision through two distinct mechanisms depending on whether you are looking at inter-warpgroup "Ping-Pong" scheduling or intra-warpgroup overlapping.

In the **inter-warpgroup Ping-Pong scheduling** described in Section 3.1, the system utilizes a **circular buffer** in shared memory with multiple stages (denoted as $s$). When Warpgroup A is performing softmax on block $j$, it is reading from a specific stage of this circular buffer. Simultaneously, Warpgroup B issues an asynchronous WGMMA instruction to compute the GEMM for block $j+1$, writing its results to a *different*, currently free stage of the circular buffer. The "hidden" synchronization you suspected exists explicitly as `barrier` instructions (`__bar_sync`). These barriers enforce an ordering where Warpgroup B cannot begin loading or computing for block $j+1$ until Warpgroup A has signaled that it has finished consuming block $j$ and released that specific buffer slot. This ensures that the producer (loading/computing next blocks) never overwrites data that the consumer (performing softmax/rescaling) still needs, effectively decoupling the latency of the low-throughput exponential operations from the high-throughput matrix multiplications without data races.

If your question pertains to the **intra-warpgroup overlapping** (Algorithm 2), where a single warpgroup overlaps GEMM and softmax within itself, the solution shifts from spatial separation in shared memory to temporal separation using **register buffering**. Here, the algorithm maintains two copies of score matrices in registers: $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$ (being processed by softmax) and $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ (just computed by GEMM). The code explicitly commits the GEMM for block $j+1$ but does not wait for it to finish immediately; instead, it proceeds to compute softmax on block $j$. Crucially, before releasing any shared memory resources or overwriting buffers for block $j+1$, there is a strict `wait` instruction specifically for the completion of the previous GEMM operation involving $\mathbf{V}_{j-1}$. This creates a pipeline where Stage 1 holds $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ while Stage 2 processes $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{cur}}$, ensuring that when Warpgroup B (conceptually acting as the next iteration) starts writing to registers or shared memory slots reserved for future blocks, Warpgroup A has already moved past those specific indices. Thus, there is no race condition because the pipeline depth guarantees that production always lags consumption by exactly one or more steps, enforced by these precise wait-commit cycles inherent to the Hopper WGMMA instruction set.

### Understanding the register permutation logic for FP8 accumulator reuse
Question:
In Section 3.3 regarding Low-precision with FP8, the authors mention that the memory layout of an FP32 accumulator from an FP8 WGMMA differs from what is required as Operand A for a subsequent WGMMA.

They propose using byte-permute instructions to reorder registers from `{d0 d1 d4 d5 d2 d3 d6 d7}` pattern. The text says: "this maneuver permutes its columns... For WGMMA to then compute the correct output tile, we can correspondingly arrange for the in-kernel transpose to write out a matching row permutation of V tile." 

Could someone explain *why* permuting columns of P (the accumulated probability matrix) allows us to match a row-permuted V? Intuitively, if I change column order of P and row order of V, shouldn't I get a scrambled result unless there's a specific mathematical symmetry being exploited here? It feels like we are changing indices arbitrarily without affecting correctness.
Answer:
The confusion arises because the permutation is not arbitrary; it is a necessary coordinate transformation to align the physical memory layout of the hardware with the logical mathematical operation of matrix multiplication. When using FP8 WGMMA on Hopper GPUs, the hardware enforces a strict "k-major" layout for operands, meaning data must be contiguous along the inner dimension ($K$). However, the FP32 accumulator returned by a previous WGMMA instruction stores data in a different interleaved pattern that matches neither the input requirement nor the natural row-major expectation of the next matrix multiplication step.

Specifically, if you were to feed the raw accumulator (representing tile $\mathbf{P}$) directly into the next WGMMA as Operand A without modification, and simultaneously feed a transposed Value tile ($\mathbf{V}$) as Operand B, the dot products would be computed between mismatched indices, resulting in garbage output. The byte-permute instruction `$\{d_0, d_1, d_4, d_5, d_2, d_3, d_6, d_7\}$` effectively reorders the columns of $\mathbf{P}$ in register space to match a specific column grouping expected by the hardware's internal accumulation logic. Crucially, this column reordering of $\mathbf{P}$ must be paired with a compensatory **row** reordering of $\mathbf{V}$. Mathematically, if you permute columns of matrix $A$ by permutation matrix $C$ and rows of matrix $B$ by $C^{-1}$ (or a compatible mapping), the product $(AC)(CB^\top) = ABC^\top$ remains valid provided $B^\top$ is adjusted accordingly. In this specific hardware context, the "matching row permutation" mentioned in the paper ensures that when $\mathbf{P}$'s columns are shuffled to fit the register file constraints, $\mathbf{V}$'s rows are shuffled identically during its in-kernel transpose so that element-wise multiplications still occur between corresponding semantic indices (e.g., row $i$ of $\mathbf{P}$ still multiplies against column $j$ of $\mathbf{V}$).

Therefore, you are not changing indices arbitrarily; you are applying an identical logical relabeling to both operands. The hardware sees two differently ordered matrices that happen to be perfectly aligned for computation because their permutations cancel each other out logically while satisfying physical storage constraints. Without this synchronized dance—permute columns of $\mathbf{P}$ and rows of $\mathbf{V}$—the subsequent GEMM would fail to compute $\mathbf{O} = \mathbf{PV}$. The paper explicitly states this allows them to avoid expensive shuffle instructions across threads by handling the alignment entirely within register movements and tailored transpose writes.

### "Commit but do not wait" semantics: How does NVCC guarantee correctness with asynchronous WGMMA?
Question:
Looking at Algorithm 2 (`FlashAttention-3` consumer warpgroup forward pass), line 7 says: `Compute S_next ... Commit but do not wait.` Line 9 says: `Compute O_i ... Commit but do not wait.`

Later in line 10, it explicitly waits: `Wait for the WGMMA Q_i K_j^T`. And line 12 waits for `tilde_P_cur V_{j-1}`.

I understand that TMA and Tensor Cores are asynchronous hardware units. However, my concern is about race conditions within a single Threadblock/CTA. If Warp X issues an async write to Shared Memory (via STSM or implicit accumulation) and Warp Y tries to read from that location immediately after issuing its own async ops without an explicit barrier between them (other than program order), how does CUDA guarantee visibility?

Specifically, if `S_next` is written asynchronously by one warpgroup and read by another (or even later by itself), isn't there a risk that `S_next` hasn't fully landed in registers/shared memory before `m_i` calculation starts? Are these "wait" instructions sufficient to enforce ordering across warps within a CTA?
Answer:
The "commit but do not wait" semantics in FlashAttention-3 do not introduce race conditions because they operate strictly within the bounds of **program order** and **hardware-defined dependency chains**, rather than relying on arbitrary memory visibility guarantees between independent warps.

In the context of the Hopper architecture described in the paper, the WGMMA (Warpgroup Matrix Multiply-Accumulate) instruction is asynchronous, meaning the issuing warp returns control to the scheduler immediately after launching the operation, allowing subsequent instructions to execute concurrently. When Algorithm 2 states "Commit but do not wait," it instructs the compiler to issue the WGMMA for $\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{next}}$ or $\mathbf{O}_i$ and proceed to issue other independent instructions (such as waiting for TMA loads or starting softmax calculations for previous blocks) without stalling. Crucially, this does not imply that the *data* is ready for immediate consumption by another warp or even by itself in a logically dependent step; rather, it overlaps latency.

Correctness is guaranteed because the algorithm explicitly inserts synchronization points—specifically `Wait` instructions—before any data produced by an asynchronous WGMMA is consumed. In Algorithm 2, line 10 explicitly waits for the completion of $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ before line 11 begins computing $m_i$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{next}}$ using that result. Similarly, line 12 waits for $\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{cur}} \mathbf{V}_{j-1}$ before rescaling $\mathbf{O}_i$. These `Wait` instructions act as fences that ensure all prior asynchronous writes (accumulations into registers or shared memory) have completed and are visible to the consuming logic before proceeding.

Regarding your concern about race conditions between Warps X and Y: FlashAttention-3 utilizes a **producer-consumer warp-specialization** model where distinct warpgroups handle data movement (producers) versus computation (consumers). Within a single consumer warpgroup executing Algorithm 2, all threads cooperate on a unified register file and shared memory space managed by strict sequential logic enforced by these explicit waits. The paper notes that while TMA loads are asynchronous, they are synchronized via pipeline objects and barrier-like checks (waiting for specific buffer stages) before data is used. Therefore, Warp Y never reads from a location modified by Warp X unless an explicit synchronization barrier has passed ensuring the write is committed. The "commit but do not wait" pattern simply maximizes hardware utilization by hiding latency during periods where no data dependency exists between consecutive steps, effectively creating a software pipeline where Stage $j+1$'s production overlaps with Stage $j$'s consumption, provided the necessary `Wait` gates separate dependent operations.

### Derivation check: How block quantization scales affect intermediate softmax calculations?
Question:
The paper mentions using block quantization where each block of Q, K, and V has its own scalar scale factor ($s_Q^{(b)}, s_K^{(b)}, s_V^{(b)}$).

When computing attention scores locally within a block during tiling:
$$ S_{ij} = \frac{(Q_i / s_Q)(K_j / s_K)^T}{d} $$
The score becomes scaled by $(s_Q s_K)^{-1}$. 
The paper claims: "we can scale each block of S to account for this block quantization at no computation cost." 

However, FlashAttention relies on maintaining running max ($m_i$) and sum ($l_i$) across blocks to normalize globally:
Pseudocode usually involves updating:
m_new = max(m_old, current_max(S_block))
l_new = exp(m_old - m_new)*l_old + sum(exp(S_block - m_new))

If each block has different scalars $(s_Q s_K)$ baked into it implicitly or explicitly removed during division by scalars before multiplication?
Wait, if we quantize Q and K separately per block with different scales, then when we compute dot products between blocks with *different* scales later (if any cross-block logic exists?), or even within a single iteration where we might combine results...
Actually, looking closer: FlashAttention processes one Query block against many Key blocks sequentially. So Q has one scale per query block iteration? No, Q is tiled too.
The confusion is: When merging partial sums across blocks where each Key/Value pair had different scaling factors applied during quantization/dequantization steps inside SMEM... How exactly are these disparate scaling factors reconciled in the final normalization step without introducing errors or requiring expensive dequantization overhead?
Answer:
The concern regarding disparate scaling factors arising from block quantization stems from a misunderstanding of how the FlashAttention-3 algorithm integrates these scales into the existing online softmax reduction logic. The key insight provided in the paper is that because the algorithm processes attention scores in blocks sequentially, the block-specific scale factors $(s_Q^{(b)} s_K^{(b)})$ act as constant offsets to the log-sum-exp accumulation variables ($m_i$ and $\ell_i$) rather than requiring complex post-hoc corrections or expensive dequantization steps.

When computing the attention scores for a specific block $j$, the raw dot product $\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_j^\top$ is computed using FP8 Tensor Cores. If $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{K}$ were quantized with scales $s_Q^{(b)}$ and $s_K^{(b)}$, the resulting unnormalized scores are effectively scaled by $(s_Q^{(b)} s_K^{(b)})^{-1}$. Mathematically, this means the true score $S_{\text{true}}$ relates to the computed score $S_{\text{comp}}$ as $S_{\text{true}} = S_{\text{comp}} \cdot (s_Q^{(b)} s_K^{(b)})^{-1}$. In the logarithmic domain used by the stable softmax algorithm, multiplying by a scalar is equivalent to adding its logarithm: $\log(S_{\text{true}}) = \log(S_{\text{comp}}) - \log(s_Q^{(b)} s_K^{(b)})$.

The FlashAttention-3 algorithm maintains running statistics $m_i$ (the maximum score seen so far) and $\ell_i$ (the sum of exponentials normalized by $m_i$) to handle numerical stability across blocks. When transitioning from block $j-1$ to block $j$, the algorithm updates these statistics. The update for the new maximum becomes $m_i^{\text{new}} = \max(m_i^{\text{old}}, \max(\mathbf{S}_{\text{comp}}) - \log(s_Q^{(j)} s_K^{(j)}))$. Similarly, when updating the sum $\ell_i$, the previous accumulated sum must be rescaled relative to this new maximum. The paper notes that "we can scale each block of S to account for this block quantization at no computation cost" precisely because these adjustments are simple additions/subtractions of pre-computed logarithms of the scale factors into the existing update equations for $m_i$ and $\ell_i$. There is no need to recompute or merge disparate distributions because each block's contribution is immediately normalized relative to the global running maximum before being added to the cumulative sum.

Therefore, even though different Key/Value blocks possess unique scale factors, they do not introduce inconsistency in the final normalization. The "global" normalization required by FlashAttention is achieved dynamically: as each new block arrives with its own implicit scale shift, that shift is absorbed into the running max ($m_i$) and used to correctly weight both the new block's contributions and the rescaling of all previously accumulated outputs ($\mathbf{O}_i$). This ensures that the final output $\mathbf{O}$ remains mathematically identical to what would be obtained if full precision arithmetic were used throughout, provided that intermediate accumulators remain in FP32 (which FlashAttention-3 does for accuracy-critical paths), while leveraging FP8 solely for the high-throughput matrix multiplication stages.
