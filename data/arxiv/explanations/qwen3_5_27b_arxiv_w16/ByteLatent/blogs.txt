# Goodbye Tokens, Hello Patches: How the Byte Latent Transformer Changes AI Efficiency

For years, the standard recipe for building a Large Language Model (LLM) has remained largely unchanged: take raw text, chop it into fixed pieces called tokens using a heuristic vocabulary, and feed those tokens into a Transformer. This tokenization step is a necessary evil that introduces biases, struggles with rare languages, and fails to capture the nuances of raw character data. However, a new architecture called the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) is challenging this status quo. By replacing static tokens with dynamic "patches" derived directly from raw bytes, BLT achieves performance on par with industry giants like Llama 3 while potentially cutting inference costs by up to 50%.

The core innovation of BLT lies in how it groups data. Traditional models treat every token as an equal unit of computation, regardless of how easy or difficult it is to predict. If a model encounters a common word ending or a predictable sequence of characters, it still expends the same amount of computational power as it would for a complex, high-entropy decision. BLT flips this logic by dynamically allocating compute based on the complexity of the data. Instead of fixed tokens, BLT segments raw bytes into variable-sized patches. These patches serve as the primary units of computation, allowing the model to spend more resources where uncertainty is high and fewer resources where the data is predictable.

## The Problem with Fixed Tokens

To understand why BLT is significant, we must first look at the limitations of current tokenization methods like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). Tokenizers rely on a fixed vocabulary determined before training begins. This creates several issues. First, it introduces domain sensitivity; a tokenizer trained on English text might struggle with code or low-resource languages because those byte sequences do not align well with the pre-defined vocabulary. Second, tokenization breaks orthographic knowledge. Because tokens are atomic units to the model, it often lacks an understanding of individual characters within a word, making it brittle when faced with noisy inputs like misspellings or character-level noise.

Furthermore, tokenization creates an efficiency bottleneck. In standard Transformers, the computational cost is dominated by feed-forward network layers that run for every single token step. Since tokens are created via compression heuristics that do not always correlate with prediction difficulty, models often waste compute on easy predictions while under-allocating resources for hard ones. Previous attempts to train models directly on bytes failed at scale because processing every single byte individually resulted in prohibitively long sequence lengths and excessive computational costs. BLT solves this by introducing a middle ground: patches that are larger than bytes but smaller than sentences, sized dynamically based on information density.

## How Dynamic Patching Works

The heart of BLT's efficiency is its patching mechanism. Unlike static tokenizers that group bytes based on frequency or fixed rules like whitespace, BLT uses entropy to decide where one patch ends and another begins. Entropy, in this context, measures the uncertainty or unpredictability of the next byte in a sequence. The model identifies points where the entropy spikes—indicating that a new piece of information or a difficult prediction is required—and starts a new patch there. Conversely, when the entropy is low and the sequence is highly predictable (like completing a common suffix), BLT extends the current patch to include more bytes without invoking expensive global computations.

This process relies on three distinct components working in harmony:
*   **A Local Encoder:** A lightweight transformer that processes raw bytes within a patch and compresses them into a latent representation (the patch vector).
*   **A Global Latent Transformer:** A large-scale model that operates on these patch representations rather than individual bytes or tokens. This is where the bulk of the reasoning happens.
*   **A Local Decoder:** Another lightweight module that expands the patch representations back into raw byte predictions for generation.

By using entropy-based boundaries, BLT ensures that patches contain relatively uniform information density. This means that when the Global Transformer runs one step for one patch, it is effectively processing roughly the same amount of "information" regardless of whether that patch contained 4 bytes or 10 bytes. This dynamic allocation allows the model to skip over predictable content efficiently while focusing its massive capacity on complex decisions.

## Scaling Efficiency and Performance

The most striking result from the research is how well this architecture scales when compared to traditional token-based models like Llama 3. In standard scaling studies where training compute (flops) is held constant, BLT matches or even outperforms Llama 3 across various parameter sizes up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes. This proves that removing fixed-vocabulary tokenization does not inherently hurt performance; in fact, it can improve it by allowing more flexible data modeling.

However, where BLT truly shines is in inference efficiency because it decouples model size from inference cost in a way tokenizers cannot. In traditional models, increasing vocabulary size to get larger average tokens comes at the cost of massive embedding tables and limited flexibility in token size distribution. With BLT, researchers can simultaneously increase both the model size and the average patch size while maintaining a fixed inference budget. Since larger patches mean fewer steps for the expensive Global Transformer to execute per byte of output, increasing patch size saves compute that can be reallocated to make the Global Transformer itself larger and more capable.

Experiments show that for fixed inference costs, BLT demonstrates significantly better scaling trends than tokenization-based architectures. By utilizing an average patch size of roughly 8 bytes compared to Llama 3's average token size of roughly 4 bytes, BLT can achieve similar performance while using up to 50% fewer floating-point operations during inference. This efficiency gain opens up new possibilities for deploying powerful models in resource-constrained environments without sacrificing intelligence or reasoning capabilities.

# Why AI Struggles with Typos (and How Reading Raw Bytes Fixes It)

If you have ever tried to chat with a large language model using all caps, intentional misspellings, or a mix of languages, you might have noticed the conversation suddenly breaks down. This fragility is a direct consequence of how most modern AI models process text. They rely on a pre-processing step called tokenization, which chops raw text into a fixed set of sub-word units before the model ever sees it. While efficient, this approach creates a "black box" where the model loses direct access to the underlying characters. A simple typo or a change in capitalization can map to a completely different token or an out-of-vocabulary error, confusing the model. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) proposes a radical solution: stop tokenizing and let the AI read raw bytes directly. By processing data at the byte level, BLT achieves a level of robustness and character-level understanding that traditional models simply cannot match.

## The Tokenization Trap

To understand why standard models struggle with noise, we must look at how they handle input. Most Large Language Models (LLMs) use algorithms like Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) to convert text into tokens from a static vocabulary. For example, the word "unbelievable" might be split into tokens like "un", "believ", and "able". If you type "unbelieveable" with an extra 'e', the tokenizer might fail to recognize the word as a whole, splitting it into unfamiliar fragments that the model has never seen together during training.

This reliance on fixed tokens introduces several critical weaknesses. First, it creates domain and modality sensitivity; if your input doesn't match the statistical patterns used to build the vocabulary, performance drops. Second, it leads to a lack of orthographic knowledge. Token-based models often know that "apple" is a fruit but struggle to understand that "pplae" is just an anagram of "apple" because they don't see the individual letters as manipulatable units; they only see abstract tokens. Finally, this approach creates multilingual inequity, where languages with complex morphologies or rare scripts are poorly represented in fixed vocabularies compared to English-centric datasets.

## How BLT Reads Raw Bytes

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) eliminates the fixed vocabulary entirely. Instead of mapping bytes to pre-defined tokens, BLT encodes raw bytes into dynamically sized groups called "patches." These patches are not drawn from a static list; they are created on the fly based on the complexity of the data. The model uses a lightweight entropy-based mechanism to decide where one patch ends and another begins. If the next byte is highly predictable (low entropy), such as continuing a common suffix like "-ing", BLT groups many bytes into a single large patch. If the next byte is uncertain (high entropy), such as starting a new sentence or an unusual word, it creates smaller patches to allocate more computational power to that specific decision.

This architecture allows BLT to maintain direct access to byte-level features throughout its processing pipeline. It consists of three main components: two small local models that handle byte-to-patch encoding and patch-to-byte decoding, and one large global transformer that operates on these latent patch representations. Because the global model only processes patches rather than every single byte individually when possible, it achieves significant efficiency gains while retaining the granular understanding of raw characters that tokenizers discard.

## Superpowers in Noisy Environments

The most immediate benefit of reading raw bytes is robustness against input noise. In experiments designed to test this capability, researchers subjected models to various forms of corruption: converting text to all uppercase with spaces between every letter ("AntSpeak"), randomly dropping 10% of characters, mixing upper and lower case randomly, repeating characters up to four times, or simply forcing everything to uppercase.

When tested on these noisy versions of standard benchmarks like HellaSwag, BLT significantly outperformed tokenization-based models trained on similar amounts of data. In fact, BLT showed an average advantage of 8 points over comparable models and even surpassed Llama 3.1 on some noisy tasks despite being trained on significantly less data (1 trillion tokens versus 15 trillion for Llama 3.1). This happens because BLT understands that 'A' and 'a' are related variations of the same character conceptually and structurally, whereas a token-based model might treat them as entirely different entities depending on how its vocabulary was constructed. The model's ability to see the underlying byte sequence allows it to reconstruct meaning even when surface-level formatting is scrambled beyond recognition for standard tokenizers.

## Mastering Character Manipulation and Phonology

Beyond just surviving typos, BLT demonstrates genuine understanding of character composition and phonology—skills that are notoriously difficult for token-based models. Researchers evaluated this using the CUTE benchmark, which tests abilities like spelling manipulation, understanding orthographic similarity, and sequence manipulation (e.g., deleting or inserting specific letters). Token-based models often fail here because they lack knowledge of their own tokens' internal spellings; they treat tokens as atomic symbols rather than sequences of characters.

BLT excelled in these tasks, outperforming BPE-based Llama 3 models by more than 25 points on average and achieving near-perfect scores (99.9%) on spelling tasks where other models struggled significantly. Furthermore, in grapheme-to-phoneme tasks—mapping written characters to their pronunciation—BLT showed superior performance compared to baselines trained on much larger datasets. This indicates that by modeling bytes directly, BLT learns intrinsic linguistic structures like phonology and orthography naturally during pre-training without needing explicit supervision or massive amounts of additional data to learn these sub-word relationships implicitly through token collisions.

Because BLT operates on raw bytes without a fixed vocabulary limit, it treats every language equally at the fundamental level of data representation.

# The New Scaling Law: How to Make Bigger Models Without Spending More Money

For years, the scaling of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been a linear game of trade-offs. To build a bigger, smarter model, you needed more compute, more data, and more time. The standard architecture relied on tokenization, a process that groups raw bytes into a fixed vocabulary of tokens. While effective, this approach imposes a rigid constraint: the model allocates the same amount of computational power to every token, regardless of whether it is predicting a common word like "the" or a complex mathematical symbol. This inefficiency has long been accepted as the cost of doing business in AI. However, a new architecture called the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) challenges this assumption, revealing a counter-intuitive path to scaling that allows us to build larger models without increasing our inference budget.

The core innovation of BLT is the replacement of static tokens with dynamic "patches." Unlike tokens, which are drawn from a fixed dictionary and have an average size that is difficult to change without exploding the model's memory requirements, patches are variable-sized groups of bytes created on the fly. The model decides how large a patch should be based on the complexity of the data. If the text is predictable and repetitive, such as standard English prose or code comments, BLT groups many bytes into a single large patch. If the text is complex and uncertain, it breaks it down into smaller patches. This dynamic allocation means the model spends its most expensive computational resources only where they are truly needed.

This mechanism unlocks a new dimension for scaling that token-based models simply cannot access. In traditional architectures, increasing the average token size requires expanding the vocabulary table exponentially, which creates a bottleneck in memory and computation. With BLT, we can arbitrarily increase the average patch size without such penalties. Because larger patches mean fewer steps for the main global transformer to process, we save significant computational costs during inference. Crucially, these saved compute resources can be reallocated to increase the size of the global latent transformer itself. This creates a virtuous cycle: by making patches larger and more efficient, we can afford to make the underlying model bigger and more capable while staying within the exact same fixed inference flop budget.

The implications of this "patch scaling" are profound for both training and deployment efficiency. In recent experiments scaling up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes, BLT demonstrated that it could match or even outperform state-of-the-art tokenization-based models like Llama 3 when compared under strict flop-controlled conditions. The study showed that while token-based models hit a ceiling where increasing vocabulary size yields diminishing returns due to embedding table costs, BLT continues to scale effectively by simultaneously growing both its model parameters and its patch sizes. For instance, using an average patch size of 8 bytes instead of 4 can lead to nearly 50% savings in inference flops. These savings allow researchers to train significantly larger global transformers for free in terms of inference cost, leading to better performance on reasoning tasks and long-tail generalization where larger models typically excel.

Beyond raw efficiency, this shift from tokens to patches also enhances robustness and understanding at the character level. Because BLT operates directly on raw bytes without losing information through fixed vocabulary compression, it exhibits superior performance on tasks involving noisy inputs, orthographic manipulation, and low-resource languages where standard tokenizers often fail due to out-of-vocabulary issues or inconsistent splitting rules. The ability to dynamically adjust granularity allows the model to maintain high fidelity in complex regions while remaining efficient in predictable ones. As we look toward the future of AI development, BLT suggests that the next leap in capability may not come from simply throwing more data at existing architectures, but from fundamentally rethinking how we group information to optimize our compute budget for maximum intelligence per dollar spent.



# Smart Computing: How AI Decides When to Think Hard and When to Skim

For years, the standard approach to building Large Language Models (LLMs) has relied on a rigid pre-processing step called tokenization. In this system, text is chopped into fixed chunks called tokens before the model ever sees it. Whether the text is a simple, predictable word like "the" or a complex, rare scientific term, the model spends the exact same amount of computational energy processing each token. This "one-size-fits-all" approach is inefficient because it forces the model to work just as hard on easy predictions as it does on difficult ones. A new architecture called the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) challenges this status quo by introducing a mechanism that allows AI to dynamically decide when to think hard and when to skim, much like a human reader does.

The core innovation behind this efficiency is a process known as Entropy Patching. Instead of using a fixed vocabulary or static rules to group characters, BLT analyzes the uncertainty of the next byte in a sequence. In information theory, entropy is a measure of unpredictability; high entropy means the next piece of data is hard to guess, while low entropy means it is highly predictable. BLT uses a small, lightweight language model to estimate this entropy in real-time. When the model encounters a section of text with low entropy—such as the predictable ending of a common word or a repetitive string of code—it groups many bytes together into a single large "patch." Conversely, when it hits a high-entropy region where the next character is uncertain, it creates smaller patches.

This dynamic grouping fundamentally changes how compute is allocated within the architecture. BLT consists of three main components: two small local models that handle individual bytes and one large global "Latent Transformer" that processes the patches. The global transformer is computationally expensive and represents the model's heavy lifting capability. By grouping predictable bytes into large patches, BLT reduces the number of times this expensive global transformer needs to run. For example, if a model can predict that 10 bytes will follow in a specific pattern with high confidence, it can process those 10 bytes as one unit rather than ten separate steps. This allows the system to "skim" over easy sections rapidly, saving significant floating-point operations (flops).

The analogy here is similar to how an expert reader processes text. When reading a familiar sentence like "The cat sat on the mat," your brain does not analyze every single letter with deep scrutiny; you recognize patterns and skip ahead quickly because the outcome is obvious. However, if you encounter a complex mathematical formula or an unfamiliar foreign word, you slow down and focus your attention intensely on every detail. Traditional token-based models lack this ability; they treat every token with equal intensity regardless of context. BLT mimics this human efficiency by reserving its most powerful processing layers for high-uncertainty moments where deep reasoning is actually required.

The results of this approach are striking in terms of both efficiency and performance. In scaling studies up to 8 billion parameters and 4 trillion training bytes, BLT matched the performance of state-of-the-art tokenized models like Llama 3 while using up to 50% fewer flops during inference for fixed costs. Furthermore, because the model can dynamically increase patch sizes for predictable data, researchers found they could simultaneously grow both the size of the model and the size of its patches within a fixed inference budget. This creates a new scaling dimension where larger models can be run more cheaply than ever before without sacrificing intelligence on complex tasks.

Beyond raw efficiency, working directly with raw bytes rather than pre-defined tokens also grants BLT superior robustness and understanding of sub-word structures. Because it doesn't rely on a fixed vocabulary that might miss rare characters or specific orthographic patterns, BLT handles noisy inputs better and demonstrates enhanced capabilities in tasks requiring character-level manipulation, such as phonology and low-resource machine translation. By letting uncertainty dictate computation rather than arbitrary token boundaries, BLT proves that smart computing isn't just about having more power; it's about knowing exactly when and where to apply it.
