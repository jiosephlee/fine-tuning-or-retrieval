# Introduction: The Limits of Tokenization and the Rise of Byte-Level Modeling

The architecture of modern Large Language Models (LLMs) is fundamentally built upon a heuristic pre-processing step known as tokenization. In nearly all existing end-to-end training pipelines, raw text data, which consists of a sequence of bytes, is first converted into a static set of discrete units called tokens. This process groups bytes into a fixed vocabulary determined prior to training, effectively compressing the input string before the model ever sees it. While this approach has been essential for making training feasible given the constraints of sequence length and computational cost, it introduces a rigid inductive bias that dictates how information is compressed and processed. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) challenges this paradigm by proposing an architecture that operates directly on raw byte data without a fixed vocabulary, aiming to match the performance of tokenization-based models while significantly improving inference efficiency and robustness.

The reliance on static tokenization creates several critical limitations that hinder the generalizability and fairness of LLMs. Because tokens are induced by compression heuristics that are not always correlated with the actual complexity of predictions, models trained on them exhibit domain and modality sensitivity. For instance, a tokenizer optimized for English text may perform poorly or introduce artifacts when processing code, mathematical notation, or low-resource languages. Furthermore, this static grouping leads to a lack of orthographic knowledge; models often fail to understand the constituent characters of words they have memorized as single tokens. This results in vulnerabilities to input noise, where minor perturbations like character case changes or spacing errors can drastically alter the token sequence and confuse the model. Additionally, fixed vocabularies contribute to multilingual inequity, as languages with different morphological structures or writing systems are forced into an arbitrary grid defined by the training data's dominant languages, often leading to suboptimal performance for long-tail languages.

Historically, attempts to bypass tokenization and train models directly on bytes have been deemed prohibitively costly at scale due to the resulting long sequence lengths. Processing every single byte requires the model to execute a step for each character, which traditionally dominated the computational budget through expensive self-attention mechanisms. Previous works attempted to mitigate this by employing more efficient attention variants or attention-free architectures like Mamba, but these approaches primarily succeeded only for small models. At the scale required for state-of-the-art performance (billions of parameters), the computational cost is dominated not by attention, but by large feed-forward network layers that must run on every input unit. Consequently, without a mechanism to dynamically group bytes into larger units of computation based on context, byte-level models remained computationally inefficient compared to their tokenized counterparts.

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) addresses these challenges by introducing a dynamic, learnable method for grouping bytes into "patches" rather than relying on a fixed vocabulary. Unlike tokens, which are drawn from a pre-defined set and lose direct access to underlying byte features once encoded, BLT patches are arbitrary groups of bytes mapped to latent representations via lightweight learned encoder and decoder modules. The core philosophy of BLT is that computational resources should be allocated dynamically based on data complexity rather than uniformly across all input units. By segmenting data based on the entropy of next-byte predictions, BLT allocates more compute and model capacity where information density is high—such as at the beginning of a sentence or in complex mathematical expressions—and fewer resources where predictions are predictable and low-entropy. This allows BLT to achieve training flop-controlled parity with models like Llama 3 while utilizing up to 50% fewer flops during inference by simultaneously growing both patch size and model size within a fixed budget.

In summary, this chapter establishes the theoretical and practical motivations for moving beyond fixed-vocabulary tokenization toward byte-level modeling with dynamic patching. We will explore how BLT redefines the trade-off between vocabulary size and compute efficiency, demonstrating that patches scale better than tokens when compute is allocated intelligently. By eliminating the biases inherent in static tokenizers and leveraging raw byte information directly, BLT offers a more robust framework capable of handling noisy inputs, understanding sub-word structures like phonology and orthography, and generalizing effectively across diverse languages and domains. The following sections will delve deeper into the specific mechanisms of patching, the architectural innovations that enable efficient byte processing, and the empirical evidence supporting BLT's superior scaling trends compared to traditional approaches.

# Dynamic Patching Strategies: From Bytes to Latent Units

The fundamental departure of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) from traditional large language models lies in its rejection of fixed-vocabulary tokenization in favor of a dynamic, learnable grouping mechanism known as patching. In standard architectures, a heuristic pre-processing step groups raw bytes into a static set of tokens before the model ever sees the data. This process biases how strings are compressed and often leads to domain sensitivity, noise fragility, and inequity across languages. BLT addresses these issues by operating directly on raw byte data, encoding them into dynamically sized patches that serve as the primary units of computation. Unlike tokens, which are drawn from a finite vocabulary determined prior to training, patches are arbitrary groups of bytes mapped to latent representations through lightweight learned encoder and decoder modules. This distinction is critical: tokens represent a fixed abstraction layer where the model loses direct access to underlying byte features, whereas patches allow the model to dynamically allocate computational resources based on the specific complexity of the data at hand. By treating patches as variable-length units without a fixed vocabulary, BLT can adapt its granularity, ensuring that compute is not wasted on predictable sequences nor insufficiently allocated to complex ones.

The computational cost of processing data in both token-based and patch-based models is primarily determined by the number of steps executed by the main Transformer. In BLT, this cost is directly proportional to the number of patches required to encode a sequence. Consequently, the average patch size becomes the dominant factor in determining efficiency during both training and inference. A patching function $f_p$ formally segments a sequence of bytes $\pmb{x}=\{x_i| i=1,\ldots n\}$ of length $n$ into a sequence of $m < n$ patches $\pmb{p}=\{p_j| j=1,\ldots,m\}$. This function maps each byte $x_i$ to a binary indicator $\{0,1\}$, where 1 signifies the start of a new patch. The goal of this segmentation is not merely to reduce sequence length, but to create contextualized groupings where information density is relatively uniform within each patch. This allows the model to invoke its most expensive components—the large global latent transformer—only when necessary, while relying on smaller local models for simpler byte-level operations within a patch.

## Static Patching Methods: Strided and Space-Based Approaches

To understand the evolution toward dynamic patching, one must first examine simpler, rule-based strategies that attempt to group bytes without relying on learned uncertainty estimates. The most straightforward approach is strided patching, which groups bytes into fixed-size chunks of length $k$. This method was utilized in earlier works like MegaByte and offers significant implementation simplicity; it provides a direct mechanism for controlling average patch size and thus flop costs during training and inference. However, this rigidity introduces severe inefficiencies because it fails to dynamically allocate compute where it is most needed. For instance, in code generation tasks containing long sequences of whitespace or repetitive characters, strided patching might waste an entire transformer step predicting trivial content. Conversely, in domains dense with information such as mathematical expressions or complex logical structures, a fixed stride might split high-entropy sequences arbitrarily or fail to provide sufficient context for prediction within a single step. Furthermore, strided patching leads to inconsistent representations; identical words or byte sequences can be split differently depending on their position relative to the stride boundary, preventing the model from learning stable contextual embeddings for recurring patterns.

A more linguistically aware improvement over strided patching is space-based patching, which creates new patches after any "space-like" byte. Space-like bytes are defined as any byte that is not a Latin character, digit, or UTF-8 continuation byte, with the additional constraint that every patch must contain at least one non-space-like byte. This heuristic leverages natural boundaries found in many languages, effectively ensuring that words are patched consistently across different sequences. By allocating a latent transformer step (and thus more floating-point operations) to every word boundary, space patching aligns compute with linguistic units where predictions are typically hardest; predicting the first character of a word following a space is significantly more difficult than predicting subsequent characters once the word's identity has been established. For example, answering "Who composed the Magic Flute?" requires high uncertainty resolution at the start of "Mozart," whereas completing "Mozart" after "M" is comparatively easy due to reduced choice space. While space patching improves upon strided methods by respecting word boundaries and allocating compute more intelligently than fixed strides have done previously in static contexts like Megabyte or SpaceByte models show promise at smaller scales but face limitations when scaling up or handling diverse domains that do not rely on whitespace delimiters or languages with different orthographic structures.

## Limitations of Rule-Based Heuristics in Diverse Domains

While static and space-based patching offer improvements over naive byte-level processing or simple tokenization in specific contexts, they ultimately fail to provide a robust solution for scaling large language models across diverse domains and languages because they cannot vary patch size based on local data complexity. Rule-based heuristics like whitespace detection assume that linguistic boundaries are universal markers for computational difficulty, an assumption that breaks down in non-textual data such as code with complex indentation schemes or mathematical notation where spaces may be irrelevant or misleadingly frequent. Moreover, these methods lack the flexibility to adapt their granularity; they cannot decide that a particular sequence requires finer resolution (smaller patches) due to high unpredictability or coarser resolution (larger patches) due to high predictability beyond what simple spacing dictates.

The inability to vary patch size gracefully limits the potential efficiency gains at scale. In tokenization-based models like Llama 3, increasing vocabulary size leads to larger average token sizes but necessitates larger embedding tables and output projection layers, creating a trade-off that restricts how much inference cost can be reduced without sacrificing performance capabilities similarly constrained by fixed rules cannot break free from this trade-off because they do not learn from data distribution properties but rather apply rigid logic regardless of context entropy Consequently space-patching models may underperform compared to dynamic approaches when evaluated on downstream tasks because they cannot optimize their internal representation density for every specific input segment This limitation becomes particularly pronounced when attempting to scale models beyond 1B parameters where subtle inefficiencies compound significantly across trillions of training bytes preventing rule-based systems from matching state-of-the-art performance achieved by adaptive architectures

## Entropy-Based Patching: Using Next-Byte Prediction Uncertainty

To overcome the rigidity of rule-based heuristics BLT introduces entropy-based patching which takes a fully data-driven approach by identifying high-uncertainty next-byte predictions using estimates derived from a small auxiliary language model The core insight driving this method is that prediction difficulty varies continuously throughout text rather than occurring only at discrete linguistic boundaries like spaces By training a small byte-level autoregressive language model on the same distribution as BLT we can compute next-byte entropies under its distribution $p_e$ over the byte vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ The entropy $H(x_i)$ for a specific byte position $i$ given previous context $\pmb{x}_{<i}$ is calculated as:
$$H(x_i) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i}) \log p_{e}(x_i=v|\pmb{x}_{<i})$$
This metric quantifies the uncertainty associated with predicting each subsequent byte High entropy indicates regions where many outcomes are plausible requiring more computational resources while low entropy signifies predictable sequences where fewer resources suffice Entropy values are computed during a lightweight preprocessing step executed during dataloading rather than requiring an end-to-end trained classifier which distinguishes our approach from prior work This allows us to segment data into contextualized groupings where information density remains relatively uniform within each patch ensuring that every invocation of the expensive global latent transformer corresponds roughly equally valuable information gain regardless of whether we are processing code text or mathematical formulas

## Global Threshold vs Approximate Monotonic Constraints for Boundary Detection

Once entropy values are computed we must determine exactly where new patches begin We experiment with two distinct methods for identifying these boundaries based on entropy profiles The first method utilizes a global constraint finding points where entropy exceeds an absolute threshold $\theta_g$:
$$H(x_t) > \theta_g$$
This approach identifies locations inherently difficult for any predictor regardless of local context However we also explore an approximate monotonic constraint which identifies points where entropy increases significantly relative to the immediately preceding value:
$$H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$$
This second approach can be interpreted as detecting breaks in approximately monotonically decreasing entropy trends within a sequence Intuitively if we assume that within predictable segments entropy should naturally decay as context accumulates then any sharp rise suggests we have entered new territory requiring fresh computation These constraints allow us flexibly tune average patch sizes by adjusting thresholds enabling precise control over computational budgets Our experiments compare these methods demonstrating their effectiveness in distinguishing between low and high entropy bytes thereby optimizing resource allocation dynamically Unlike static rules these constraints adapt automatically if underlying data distributions shift making them robust across different training phases and domain compositions

## Incremental Patching: Ensuring Autoregressive Generation Without Future Context

A critical requirement for any autoregressive generation system is incremental compatibility meaning decisions about segmentation must be made solely based on past information without access to future tokens Many modern tokenizers including Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) violate this property because they rely on looking ahead at subsequent characters before deciding how group current ones For example BPE might tokenize "unhappy" differently depending on whether it appears alone versus followed by "ness" since different continuations trigger different merge operations Formally an incremental patching scheme $f_p$ must satisfy:
$$f_p(\pmb{x}_{<i}) = f_p(\pmb{x})_{<i}$$
This ensures that when generating text token-by-token or byte-by-byte BLT knows exactly when it has completed one latent unit and must invoke its global transformer versus continuing locally within current unit If future context were required generation would become impossible without speculative decoding mechanisms adding significant latency Entropy-based patching naturally satisfies this property since next-byte entropy depends only on history up point $t$ allowing real-time decision making during inference In contrast BPE requires special delimiter tokens if forced into incremental mode increasing overall sequence length negating efficiency gains Thus our dynamic approach redefines tradeoffs between vocabulary size and compute offering unprecedented flexibility unavailable in traditional tokenization frameworks

The BLT Architecture: A Hierarchical Transformer Design

The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) represents a paradigm shift in how large language models process raw data, moving away from the rigid, fixed-vocabulary tokenization that has dominated the field. At its core, the BLT architecture is designed to dynamically allocate computational resources based on the complexity of the input data. Instead of treating every unit of text with equal computational weight, BLT segments raw byte sequences into variable-sized groups called patches. This segmentation allows the model to spend more compute on high-entropy, information-dense regions while efficiently skipping over predictable, low-entropy sequences. The architecture achieves this through a hierarchical design comprising three distinct but interconnected components: a lightweight Local Encoder that maps bytes to patch representations, a large Global Latent Transformer that performs the heavy autoregressive modeling on these patches, and a Local Decoder that expands the patch representations back into raw byte sequences. This three-stage flow ensures that the model can operate end-to-end on raw bytes without a fixed vocabulary while maintaining efficiency and robustness comparable to, and in some cases exceeding, state-of-the-art tokenization-based models.

The fundamental workflow of BLT begins with a sequence of raw bytes, denoted as $\pmb{x}=\{x_i | i=1,\ldots,n\}$. Unlike traditional models that immediately map these bytes to a discrete set of tokens from a pre-defined vocabulary, BLT first groups these bytes into patches $\pmb{p}=\{p_j | j=1,\ldots,m\}$ where $m < n$. The size of these patches is not fixed; rather, it is determined dynamically based on the entropy of next-byte predictions derived from a small auxiliary language model. This creates contextualized groupings where patches with lower information density (predictable text) are longer, and patches with higher information density (unpredictable text) are shorter. The Local Encoder processes these byte sequences to create expressive latent representations for each patch. These representations are then fed into the Global Latent Transformer, which operates autoregressively over the sequence of patches. Finally, once the Global Transformer generates an output patch representation, the Local Decoder takes this representation and conditions it on previously decoded bytes to predict the specific sequence of raw bytes that constitute that patch. This separation of concerns allows the computationally expensive global attention mechanism to run less frequently on longer patches, significantly reducing inference costs while preserving the model's ability to understand fine-grained byte-level details.

# The Local Encoder ($\mathcal{E}$): Mapping Bytes to Patch Representations with Lightweight Transformers

The Local Encoder, denoted as $\mathcal{E}$, serves as the bridge between the raw byte stream and the high-level latent space where global reasoning occurs. It is designed as a lightweight transformer-based model with significantly fewer layers ($l_{\mathcal{E}}$) than the global model ($l_{\mathcal{G}}$), ensuring that it does not become a computational bottleneck despite processing every single byte in the input sequence. Its primary function is to map a sequence of input bytes $b_i$ into expressive patch representations $p_j$. A critical architectural departure in $\mathcal{E}$ compared to standard transformers is its attention mechanism and its role in pooling information. While standard transformers operate on a fixed sequence length or require complex masking for variable lengths, $\mathcal{E}$ utilizes a local block causal attention mask. This mask restricts each byte's attention window to a fixed number $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ of preceding bytes. Crucially, this window can cross dynamic patch boundaries but cannot cross document boundaries. This design ensures that each byte representation captures sufficient local context from its immediate neighbors before being aggregated into a patch-level summary.

The encoder processes bytes through a series of alternating transformer layers and cross-attention layers. The transformer layers within $\mathcal{E}$ refine the individual byte embeddings using self-attention within their local windows and feed-forward networks. However, these refined byte representations are not yet ready for the global model; they must be condensed into single vectors representing entire patches. This condensation is achieved through specialized cross-attention modules placed after each transformer layer in the encoder stack. These modules act as pooling mechanisms that aggregate information from all bytes within a specific patch $p_j$ into a single query vector representing that patch. By interleaving self-attention (for local context) and cross-attention (for pooling), $\mathcal{E}$ effectively compresses variable-length byte sequences into fixed-dimensional latent vectors without losing critical information about the internal structure of those sequences. The result is a sequence of patch representations $P \in \mathbb{R}^{n_p \times h_{\mathcal{G}}}$ that are ready to be processed by the much larger Global Latent Transformer, where $n_p$ is the number of patches and $h_{\mathcal{G}}$ is the hidden dimensionality required by the global model.

# Hash n-gram Embeddings: Enhancing Local Context Awareness without Large Vocabularies

A key challenge in processing raw bytes directly is capturing local patterns and orthographic regularities without resorting to massive embedding tables or fixed vocabularies that might miss rare character combinations or fail in low-resource languages. To address this within the Local Encoder, BLT introduces hash n-gram embeddings as an augmentation to standard byte embeddings. This technique allows the model to incorporate information about preceding bytes efficiently by modeling both individual bytes and their local n-gram contexts simultaneously. For every step $i$ in the sequence, BLT constructs byte n-grams $g_{i,n}=\{b_{i-n + 1},\ldots,b_i\}$ for various lengths $n$, typically ranging from 3 to 8 characters. These n-grams capture short-term dependencies such as common letter digraphs or trigrams that are essential for understanding spelling and morphology but are often lost when tokens are split arbitrarily by standard tokenizers.

Instead of maintaining an explicit dictionary for every possible n-gram—which would be prohibitively large given there are $256^n$ possibilities—BLT employs hash embeddings with collision handling via modulo arithmetic. Each constructed n-gram $g_{i,n}$ is passed through a hash function (specifically RollPolyHash) which maps it to an index in a fixed-size embedding table $E_{n}^{hash}$. The resulting embedding vector is then added to the base embedding of the current byte $x_i$. Mathematically, this augmented embedding $e_i$ is calculated as:
$$e_i = x_i + \sum_{n = 3,...8} E_{n}^{hash}(\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}))$$
where $\text{Hash}(g_{i,n}) = \text{RollPolyHash}(g_{i,n}) \% |E_{n}^{hash}|$. This summation allows multiple n-gram features to contribute simultaneously to a single position's representation without exploding memory usage or parameter count relative to vocabulary size variations found in token-based models. After aggregation, these augmented embeddings are normalized by dividing by the number of n-gram sizes plus one before being fed into the first layer of $\mathcal{E}$. Empirical results indicate that these hash embeddings are vital for matching tokenizer-based performance, particularly on domains like Wikipedia and code repositories where specific character sequences carry significant semantic weight. They provide a mechanism for "orthographic knowledge" at scale without requiring explicit tokenization rules or large lookup tables for rare subwords.

# Scaling Laws and Efficiency Analysis

The computational efficiency of Large Language Models (LLMs) is traditionally governed by the trade-off between model size, training data volume, and the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs) required. In standard tokenization-based architectures, this relationship is rigidly constrained by a fixed vocabulary, which dictates a static average token size and consequently a fixed computational cost per step of generation. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) fundamentally alters this landscape by introducing a dynamic patching mechanism that decouples the unit of computation from a static vocabulary. This chapter explores the rigorous methodology used to estimate FLOPs in byte-level architectures, establishes Bits-Per-Byte as the appropriate metric for cross-architecture comparison, and presents empirical evidence demonstrating that BLT achieves superior scaling trends. By dynamically allocating compute based on data complexity, BLT not only matches the performance of state-of-the-art tokenized models like Llama 3 but also unlocks a new dimension for scaling where model capacity can be increased without increasing inference costs.

# FLOPs Estimation for Byte-Level Architectures vs. Token-Based Architectures

Accurately estimating the computational cost of a language model is critical for fair comparison across different architectural paradigms. In traditional token-based models, the FLOP count is dominated by the feed-forward network layers and self-attention mechanisms applied to every token in the sequence. However, in byte-level architectures like BLT, the computation is distributed across three distinct components: a lightweight local encoder that processes raw bytes into patches, a large global latent transformer that operates on these patches, and a lightweight local decoder that reconstructs bytes from patch representations. Consequently, the FLOP calculation for BLT must account for the varying sequence lengths at each stage of processing. The total FLOPs per byte for BLT is derived by summing the operations required for the local encoder transformer layers, which operate on a fixed window of bytes; the global latent transformer layers, which operate on the sequence of patches; and the local decoder transformer layers, which operate on the output byte sequence. Additionally, significant computational cost arises from the cross-attention blocks that facilitate information flow between bytes and patches in both the encoder and decoder.

The mathematical formulation for BLT's FLOP estimation reflects this hierarchical structure. Let $n_{ctx}$ represent the total sequence length in bytes and $n_p$ represent the average patch size. The number of patches $m$ is therefore $n_{ctx} / n_p$. The FLOP count includes terms for the global transformer operating on $m$ steps with hidden dimension $h_{\mathcal{G}}$, and terms for the local encoders and decoders operating on window sizes $w_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $w_{\mathcal{D}}$ respectively with hidden dimensions $h_{\mathcal{E}}$ and $h_{\mathcal{D}}$. Crucially, unlike token-based models where every step incurs the full cost of a large transformer layer, BLT invokes its most expensive component—the global latent transformer—only once per patch. This means that as the average patch size increases, the frequency of invoking the global model decreases linearly, leading to substantial savings in inference FLOPs. The cross-attention operations are also weighted by their respective ratios of queries to keys/values and are normalized by the patch size to yield a per-byte cost. This granular accounting reveals that while byte-level models historically suffered from prohibitive costs due to long sequences, BLT's architecture effectively amortizes these costs by grouping predictable bytes into larger patches where fewer global steps are required.

# Bits-Per-Byte (BPB) as a Tokenizer-Independent Metric for Performance Evaluation

When evaluating language models that operate on different granularities of input data—such as tokens versus raw bytes—the standard metric of perplexity becomes ambiguous because it is inherently tied to a specific tokenizer's vocabulary size and distribution. Perplexity measures uncertainty per token; therefore, comparing perplexity between two models using different tokenizers does not yield an apples-to-apples comparison of their actual predictive capability over raw data sequences. To address this limitation in byte-level research, we utilize Bits-Per-Byte (BPB), a tokenizer-independent metric that normalizes uncertainty over individual bytes rather than tokens or subword units. BPB provides a direct measure of how many bits are required to encode each byte of text given the model's probability distribution, allowing for fair comparisons between BLT and tokenization-based baselines like Llama 3 regardless of their internal representation strategies.

The calculation of BPB is derived directly from the cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ computed over a sequence $\pmb{x}$. Specifically, BPB is defined as $\text{BPB}(x) = \frac{\mathcal{L}_{CE}(\pmb{x})}{\ln(2)\cdot n_{\text{bytes}}}$, where $n_{\text{bytes}}$ is the total number of bytes in the sequence $\pmb{x}$. By dividing by $\ln(2)$, we convert natural logarithms used in cross-entropy into base-2 logarithms (bits), providing an intuitive interpretation: lower BPB indicates that the model requires fewer bits to describe each byte on average, signifying better compression and higher predictive accuracy at the fundamental level of data representation. This metric is particularly vital for assessing BLT because it isolates model performance from artifacts introduced by heuristic tokenization schemes. For instance, if a tokenizer groups common characters into single tokens while splitting rare characters into multiple tokens, perplexity might artificially improve or degrade based on these arbitrary groupings rather than true linguistic understanding. BPB eliminates this bias by treating every byte equally in its contribution to loss normalization, ensuring that improvements in scaling trends reflect genuine gains in modeling efficiency rather than artifacts of vocabulary design.

# Compute-Optimal Scaling Trends: Matching Llama 3 Performance with Fewer FLOPs

A central contribution of this research is demonstrating that byte-level models can achieve parity with state-of-the-art tokenized models when trained under compute-optimal regimes. Compute-optimal training refers to balancing model parameters and training data volume such that performance maximizes within a fixed budget of total training FLOPs. Previous attempts at scaling byte-level models often failed to match tokenized counterparts because they did not account for dynamic compute allocation or relied on inefficient attention mechanisms over every single byte. In our experiments using datasets ranging from 1 trillion tokens up to 4 trillion bytes with models up to 8 billion parameters, we trained both Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) based models following Llama 3's optimal ratios and corresponding BLT models with matched parameter counts in their global latent transformers. The results indicate that BLT matches or even outperforms BPE-based models across various scales when evaluated under these strict compute constraints.

The success of BLT in matching Llama 3's scaling trends validates two key architectural hypotheses: first, that dynamic patching effectively allocates compute where it is needed most; second, that removing fixed-vocabulary constraints does not inherently penalize scaling efficiency if compensated by appropriate architectural design like hash n-gram embeddings and cross-attention mechanisms. While earlier byte-level approaches like SpaceByte or static strided patching lagged significantly behind Llama 3 due to rigid grouping strategies or lack of sophisticated encoding/decoding modules, BLT bridges this gap entirely through entropy-based dynamic patching. Interestingly, we observed that increasing average patch sizes beyond those typical in BPE tokenizers (e.g., moving from ~4 bytes per token to 6 or even 8 bytes per patch) did not degrade performance at scale; instead, larger patch sizes appeared to perform better as model size increased from 1 billion to 7 billion parameters. This suggests that as models grow larger and training compute expands, there may be even greater potential for efficiency gains by utilizing larger patches without sacrificing accuracy at train time
