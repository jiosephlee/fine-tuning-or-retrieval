\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

# Chapter 1: Introduction to Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The advent of large-scale text-to-image diffusion models has revolutionized the field of computer graphics and creative AI, enabling the synthesis of photorealistic images from natural language descriptions. Models such as Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and DALL-E 2 have demonstrated unprecedented capabilities in translating abstract textual concepts into coherent visual representations. These systems leverage vast amounts of paired text-image data to learn complex distributions of visual features, allowing users to generate high-fidelity imagery with remarkable ease. However, despite their impressive generative power, relying solely on text prompts presents significant limitations when precise control over specific attributes or identities is required. The inherent ambiguity of natural language often leads to outputs that fail to capture fine-grained details or adhere strictly to user intent, creating a pressing need for advanced mechanisms to guide these powerful models toward specialized downstream tasks.

To bridge the gap between broad generative capabilities and specific user requirements, researchers have identified two critical application domains: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Subject-driven generation focuses on the ability to insert a specific entity—such as a particular pet, person, or object—into various contexts while maintaining its unique identity. This task requires the model to learn from only a handful of reference images, a scenario known as few-shot learning, ensuring that the generated subject remains recognizable regardless of the surrounding environment described by the text prompt. Conversely, controllable generation addresses the need for structural precision by incorporating external conditioning signals beyond text. By integrating inputs such as Canny edge maps, segmentation masks, or facial landmarks, these methods allow users to dictate the exact layout, geometry, or spatial arrangement of the generated content. Both tasks fundamentally rely on the ability to adapt pre-trained diffusion models without degrading their original high-quality generative performance.

The pursuit of effective adaptation strategies reveals a profound tension between adaptability and stability in deep learning systems. When finetuning massive pre-trained models on narrow downstream tasks, there is a persistent risk of "catastrophic forgetting," where the model loses its general knowledge and ability to generate diverse, high-fidelity images in favor of overfitting to the specific training data. Traditional finetuning approaches often attempt to mitigate this by using extremely small learning rates or freezing most of the network parameters, hoping that minimal weight updates will preserve the pre-training distribution. However, these heuristic methods lack a principled theoretical foundation and offer no guarantee that the semantic structure encoded in the original model will remain intact. The challenge lies in developing a finetuning methodology that allows for sufficient flexibility to learn new tasks while rigorously preserving the intricate geometric relationships among neurons that define the model's pre-existing generative competence.

Addressing these challenges necessitates establishing clear criteria for what constitutes an effective finetuning method. Ideally, such a method must satisfy two primary desiderata: training efficiency and generalizability preservation. Training efficiency demands that the adaptation process utilizes a minimal number of trainable parameters and converges rapidly within a limited number of epochs, making it practical for resource-constrained environments and rapid prototyping. Simultaneously, generalizability preservation ensures that the finetuned model retains its capacity for high-fidelity and diverse image generation across unseen prompts and conditions. Current literature highlights that merely minimizing the Euclidean distance between pre-trained and finetuned weights is insufficient for capturing semantic preservation; instead, more structural measures are required to quantify how well the internal relationships of neurons are maintained during adaptation. This chapter sets the stage for exploring how orthogonal transformations can serve as a principled solution to this balancing act, offering a robust framework for controlling text-to-image diffusion models with superior stability and performance.

# Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations – Hyperspherical Energy and Semantic Structure

To effectively adapt large text-to-image diffusion models for specific downstream tasks, such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation, one must first understand the fundamental nature of how these models encode information. Traditional finetuning approaches often rely on minimizing the Euclidean distance between the weights of a pretrained model and a finetuned model, operating under the assumption that keeping the weights close in a linear sense preserves the model's capabilities. However, recent insights suggest that this assumption is flawed and fails to capture the true structural requirements for maintaining generative semantics. Instead, the geometry of neural networks reveals that the angular relationships between neurons—specifically their positions on a unit hypersphere—are far more critical than their magnitudes. This chapter establishes the theoretical groundwork for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) by exploring why preserving hyperspherical energy is essential for retaining the semantic knowledge embedded within a pretrained diffusion model.

## Neuron Representation on Unit Hyperspheres

In deep neural networks, particularly within fully connected and convolutional layers, neurons can be conceptualized as vectors in a high-dimensional space. Each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ possesses both a magnitude (length) and a direction (angle relative to other axes). While standard training procedures treat these components equally, emerging evidence indicates that normalizing these vectors onto a unit hypersphere provides a more faithful representation of the semantic content they carry. When we project all neurons in a specific layer onto a unit hypersphere, we normalize them such that $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm. This normalization process strips away the magnitude information, leaving only the directional orientation of each neuron.

The rationale behind focusing on this unit hypersphere representation stems from observations in deep learning theory regarding generalization and feature encoding. Research has demonstrated that training neural networks with neurons constrained to lie on a unit hypersphere can yield comparable or even superior generalization capabilities compared to unnormalized counterparts. This suggests that the "capacity" of a neural network to store and retrieve complex patterns is primarily encoded in the directions of its weight vectors rather than their lengths. In the context of text-to-image diffusion models, which rely on intricate hierarchical representations to synthesize photorealistic imagery, the arrangement of these directional vectors defines the semantic landscape of the model. If we view each neuron as pointing toward a specific concept or feature in this high-dimensional space, then preserving the relative angles between these pointers ensures that the conceptual relationships remain intact during adaptation. Consequently, any finetuning strategy that disrupts these angular configurations risks corrupting the underlying semantic structure required for coherent image generation.

## Hyperspherical Energy: Definition and Mathematical Formulation

To quantify the structural integrity of these neuronal arrangements on the unit hypersphere, we utilize a metric known as hyperspherical energy (HE). This concept serves as a rigorous mathematical tool to characterize the pairwise relational structure among all neurons within a single layer. Intuitively, hyperspherical energy measures how uniformly distributed or clustered the neurons are on the surface of the sphere; however, in our context, it specifically captures the sum of similarities between every pair of neurons. Mathematically, for a fully connected layer represented by a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $n$ is the number of neurons and $d$ is their dimensionality, we first define the normalized neuron vector as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

The hyperspherical energy of this layer is formally defined as:
$$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} $$
This formulation sums the inverse distances between all pairs of distinct normalized neurons $(\hat{\bm{w}}_i, \hat{\bm{w}}_j)$. Because these vectors lie on a unit sphere, their pairwise distance is directly related to their cosine similarity (angular separation). Specifically, if two neurons point in nearly identical directions (high cosine similarity), their Euclidean distance $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ will be small, causing their contribution to the sum to be large due to the inverse term. Conversely, if two neurons are orthogonal or widely separated in angle, their distance increases, reducing their contribution to the total energy. Therefore, minimizing or stabilizing this value reflects maintaining specific angular relationships among neurons. In our proposed framework for finetuning diffusion models, we hypothesize that an optimal finetuned model should exhibit minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to its pretrained counterpart. That is:
$$ \| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \| = 0 $$
where $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights and $\bm{W}$ represents the finetuned weights. Achieving this equality ensures that while individual weights may shift to accommodate new tasks (such as recognizing a specific subject), their collective geometric configuration—their "shape" on the hypersphere—remains invariant. This invariance is provably guaranteed if we apply an orthogonal transformation to all neurons simultaneously, forming the core theoretical justification for Orthogonal Finetuning.

## Angular Information vs. Magnitude Information in Deep Networks

A pivotal distinction in understanding why orthogonal transformations are effective lies in separating angular information from magnitude information within neural activations and weights. Empirical studies have long suggested that while magnitude scales features up or down based on confidence or intensity levels during training dynamics or inference scaling factors like LayerNorm often handle normalization dynamically), it is actually angular differences that best characterize semantic gaps between inputs and outputs. For instance, when comparing two images—one containing a cat and another containing a dog—the primary distinguishing factor captured by deep features tends to be found in how feature vectors align relative to one another (their angles) rather than how "strong" those features are (their magnitudes).

This dichotomy draws inspiration from signal processing principles akin to those found in Fourier analysis. Just as an image can be decomposed into magnitude spectra (representing texture strength) and phase spectra (representing spatial structure), neural representations appear to store semantic meaning predominantly in their "phase," or angular orientation relative to basis functions or other neurons. The phase spectrum contains enough information about object structure that combining it with random magnitude data can still reconstruct an image recognizable by humans without losing its core semantics. Translating this analogy back to neural networks implies that changing neuron directions allows us to semantically modify what an image looks like—for example, altering its style or subject identity—without destroying its coherence if done correctly. # Chapter 3: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Methodology

The fundamental challenge in adapting large-scale text-to-image diffusion models lies in balancing the need for specific downstream customization against the risk of catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses its broad, high-fidelity generative capabilities. Traditional finetuning approaches rely on updating neuron weights via gradient descent with a small learning rate or adding low-rank components, operating under the implicit assumption that minimizing the Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained weights ensures semantic preservation. However, recent insights suggest that Euclidean distance is an insufficient metric for capturing the structural integrity required for complex generative tasks. Instead, the semantic relationships within a neural network are better characterized by the angular configurations of its neurons on a unit hypersphere. This chapter introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled methodology that leverages orthogonal transformations to adapt diffusion models. By design, OFT provably preserves hyperspherical energy—a measure of pairwise neuron relationships—thereby ensuring that the intrinsic semantic structure learned during pretraining remains intact while allowing the model to acquire new control signals or subject-specific identities.

## The Principle of Orthogonal Transformation in Weight Updates

To understand why orthogonal transformation serves as an ideal mechanism for finetuning, one must first recognize what aspect of neural network weights actually encodes semantic information. Empirical observations in deep learning indicate that angular differences between features often characterize semantic gaps more effectively than magnitude differences. Consider a decomposition inspired by 2D Fourier analysis, where an image consists of a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The phase spectrum, which represents angular information relative to a basis, retains the majority of the image's semantic content; reconstructing an image with random magnitudes but correct phases yields a recognizable result, whereas retaining magnitudes with random phases destroys semantics entirely. Translating this intuition to neural networks, the directions (angles) of neurons play a dominant role in storing and processing semantic information, while their magnitudes are secondary. Consequently, modifying the direction of neurons is the most direct path to altering the semantic output of a model without disrupting its foundational knowledge base.

However, arbitrarily rotating individual neurons carries significant risks. If each neuron were rotated independently or with unstructured degrees of freedom, the delicate geometric relationships established during pretraining would be shattered, leading to model collapse or severe degradation in generation quality. The solution lies in applying a transformation that alters directions globally while strictly maintaining internal geometric consistency. An orthogonal transformation—which encompasses rotations and reflections—is uniquely suited for this purpose because it preserves angles between vectors. When an orthogonal matrix is applied to all neurons within a layer simultaneously, every pairwise angle between neurons remains invariant. This property creates a "sweet spot" between flexibility and regularity: it allows the model to shift its entire coordinate system to accommodate new tasks (such as recognizing a specific subject or adhering to edge maps) while mathematically guaranteeing that the relative structural relationships defining the pretrained knowledge remain untouched. This stands in stark contrast to standard finetuning, which lacks such structural guarantees and relies solely on heuristic constraints like small learning rates to prevent drift.

## Mathematical Proof: Preserving Hyperspherical Energy via Rotation/Reflection

The theoretical foundation of OFT rests on the concept of hyperspherical energy (HE), which quantifies the collective pairwise relational structure among neurons in a layer. For a fully connected layer represented by a weight matrix $\mathbf{W} = \{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each $\mathbf{w}_i$ is a neuron vector in $d$-dimensional space, we define normalized neurons as $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is formally defined as the sum of inverse distances between all pairs of these normalized vectors:
$$
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
$$
Since $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ is directly related to the cosine similarity (and thus the angle) between neurons $i$ and $j$, HE captures how uniformly or specifically distributed these neurons are on the unit hypersphere. The core hypothesis driving OFT is that preserving this energy value during finetuning is critical for maintaining generative performance. Mathematically, we seek to minimize the difference $\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\|$, where $\mathbf{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights.

We can prove that applying an orthogonal transformation achieves this minimization perfectly by reducing it to zero difference without requiring iterative regularization penalties that might fail to converge precisely. Let us consider transforming the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ such that $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, where $\mathbf{R}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I}$ (with determinant $1$ indicating rotation and $-1$ indicating reflection). Because orthogonal transformations preserve vector norms and dot products, they inherently preserve angles between vectors. Specifically, for any two neurons $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j$ in the pretrained layer, their transformed counterparts are $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i\|}$ (since norms are preserved). The distance between any pair remains invariant:
$$
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{j}}_j\| = \|(\frac{\hat{\omega}_k}{k}) - (\frac{l}{m})\| 
$$
More rigorously expressed: since $\|\cdot\|$ is rotation-invariant and linear operations commute with normalization when scaling factors are unity (which holds here), we have:
$$
\|\hat{\omega}_{new\_pair}\| = \| R (\frac{o}{p}) \| = \| o/p \| 
$$
Actually simpler: The term inside HE depends only on angles via cosine similarity $cos(\theta_{ij})$. Since $cos(Rv_i)^T(Rv_j) = v_i^T R^T R v_j = v_i^T v_j$, all pairwise similarities are identical before and after transformation. Therefore:
$$
\text{HE}(\bm W) = \text {HE } (\bm R W ^ 0 )= H E(W ^ 0 ) 
$$
This equality proves that if we restrict our search space for finetuning updates exclusively to orthogonal transformations applied layer-wise, we automatically satisfy the condition $\|\text {HE } (\bm W)-H E(W ^ 0 )|=0$. No matter how much we rotate or reflect the entire subspace spanned by these neurons through learning $\bm R$, their internal geometry—and consequently their encoded semantic relationships—remains perfectly conserved relative to one another. This provides a rigorous mathematical guarantee absent in standard gradient descent methods which merely hope that small steps equate to small structural changes.

## Formulating OFT: Optimizing Layer-Shared Orthogonal Matrices ($\bm R$)

Having established that orthogonal transformations preserve hyperspherical energy by construction, OFT reformulates the finetuning problem not as finding new absolute weight values close to old ones in Euclidean space, but as optimizing specific transformation matrices for each layer of the network. In traditional approaches like LoRA or full fine-tuning with small learning rates, one attempts to solve for additive updates or direct gradients on weights $\bm W$. In contrast OFT posits that every trainable parameter update should manifest as multiplication by an orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons within that specific layer formaly denoted as RMAT ∈ ℝ^(d×d). # Chapter 4: Efficient Implementation and Variants of OFT

While the theoretical foundation of Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) rests on the elegant premise of preserving hyperspherical energy to maintain semantic integrity, translating this concept into a practical tool for finetuning massive text-to-image diffusion models presents significant computational hurdles. The core mechanism of OFT involves learning an orthogonal transformation matrix $\bm{R}$ for each layer of the neural network to rotate the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$. In a standard scenario where the dimension of the neuron space is $d$, a full orthogonal matrix resides in the orthogonal group $\text{O}(d)$ and possesses $d(d-1)/2$ degrees of freedom. This results in a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ per layer. For modern diffusion models, where hidden dimensions can reach thousands, explicitly parameterizing and optimizing such large dense matrices would be prohibitively expensive in terms of memory usage and computational cost during backpropagation. Furthermore, maintaining strict orthogonality constraints during gradient descent updates is non-trivial; standard numerical methods like Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization, while theoretically sound, are often too computationally intensive to be viable for iterative deep learning training loops. To bridge the gap between theoretical elegance and practical utility, specific parameterization strategies must be employed to ensure that OFT remains efficient enough to compete with existing lightweight finetuning methods like LoRA.

To overcome the inefficiency of direct optimization and complex orthogonalization routines, we turn to Cayley parameterization as a differentiable and computationally efficient alternative. Instead of treating the elements of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as independent variables subject to hard constraints, Cayley parameterization constructs $\bm{R}$ from an unconstrained skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$. A matrix is skew-symmetric if it satisfies the condition $\bm{Q} = -\bm{Q}^\top$. Using this property, we define the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. This formulation guarantees that $\bm{R}$ is always orthogonal regardless of the values in $\bm{Q}$, thereby eliminating the need for explicit projection steps or costly normalization procedures during every training iteration. The primary advantage here is that we can optimize $\bm{Q}$ using standard gradient descent without worrying about violating orthogonality constraints. However, there is a subtle mathematical limitation inherent to this construction: Cayley transforms can only generate orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $+1$, meaning they belong specifically to the Special Orthogonal Group (representing pure rotations) rather than allowing reflections (determinant $-1$). Empirical evidence suggests that this restriction does not negatively impact performance in practice for image generation tasks, making Cayley parameterization a robust choice for implementing OFT efficiently.

Despite the elegance of Cayley parameterization, simply replacing a dense matrix with another dense representation derived from a skew-symmetric matrix does not fundamentally solve the quadratic growth in parameters relative to dimension $d$. To achieve true parameter efficiency comparable to low-rank adaptation methods, we introduce block-diagonal parameterization. Rather than learning a single monolithic $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, we decompose $\bm{R}$ into a block-diagonal structure consisting of $r$ smaller independent blocks along the diagonal. Mathematically, this takes the form $\bm{R} = \text{diag}(\bm{R}_1, \bm{R}_2, \dots, \bm{R}_r)$, where each sub-matrix $\bm{R}_i$ is itself an orthogonal matrix operating on a subspace of dimension $d/r$. This structural constraint drastically reduces the number of trainable parameters from roughly $d^2/2$ to approximately $(d/r)(d/r - 1)r/2$, which simplifies asymptotically to a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. By increasing the number of blocks $r$, we can linearly scale down the computational burden while retaining sufficient flexibility for finetuning. For instance, if we set $r=d$, each block becomes trivial (dimension 1), reducing parameters significantly but potentially limiting expressiveness; conversely, choosing an intermediate value allows us to strike an optimal balance between model capacity and efficiency. This approach ensures that OFT remains competitive with methods like LoRA in terms of memory footprint while offering superior stability properties derived from its geometric constraints.

Building upon these efficiency mechanisms, we can further refine OFT by introducing Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which addresses issues related to finetuning stability by explicitly limiting how far the transformed model can deviate from its pretrained state. While standard OFT preserves hyperspherical energy perfectly by definition through orthogonality, it technically allows for arbitrary rotations that might drift too far from the original pretrained manifold if left unchecked over many epochs. COFT adds an explicit radius constraint to ensure that the learned transformation stays within an epsilon-ball around the identity matrix. Formally, this requires satisfying both the orthogonality condition $\bm{R}^\top\bm{R} = \bm{I}$ and a deviation constraint $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| \leq \epsilon$. Implementing this dual constraint directly on Cayley-parameterized matrices requires careful handling; however, by utilizing a Neumann series approximation where $\bm{R} \approx \bm{I} + 2\BM Q$ for small perturbations, we can translate the deviation constraint on $\BM R$ into a simpler bound on the skew-symmetric matrix: $\|\BM Q\| \leq \epsilon'$. This allows us to enforce stability via projected gradient descent on $\BM Q$, ensuring that even as we adapt to new tasks like subject-driven generation or controllable editing, we never stray beyond a safe neighborhood that could degrade general performance or cause catastrophic forgetting.

Finally, recognizing that preserving angles is necessary but perhaps not sufficient for capturing all nuances required for high-quality generation, we explore Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning as an extension that reintroduces magnitude adjustments without compromising geometric principles. The core insight driving this variant is that hyperspherical energy depends solely on directions (angles) between neurons and is invariant under scaling operations; multiplying neurons by positive scalars normalizes away during energy calculation but alters their contribution magnitude in dot products used during forward passes. Consequently, Re-scaled OFT augments the standard forward pass equation by introducing a learnable diagonal scaling matrix $\BM D = \text{diag}(s_1, s_2, \dots, s_n)$ alongside the orthogonal rotation matrix $\BM R$. The updated computation becomes $\BM z = (\BM R \BM W^0 \BM D)^\top \BM x$, where both rotation and scaling are optimized jointly. This addition introduces only $n$ extra parameters per layer—a negligible increase compared to rotational components—yet grants significant flexibility in modulating feature importance post-rotation. Experimental observations indicate that while vanilla OFT already outperforms baseline methods due to its structural preservation properties incorporating re-scaling often yields marginal improvements in convergence speed and final generation quality by allowing finer control over feature magnitudes alongside directional shifts. Together with efficient implementations via Cayley transforms and block-diagonals these variants constitute a complete toolkit for deploying stable scalable adaptive solutions across diverse downstream applications involving large-scale diffusion models
