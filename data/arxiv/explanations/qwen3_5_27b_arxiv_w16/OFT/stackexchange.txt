\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does the Cayley parameterization restrict the search space to rotations only, excluding reflections?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors propose using Cayley parameterization to efficiently generate orthogonal matrices: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. They mention that this method 'can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1', meaning it belongs to the Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$.

My confusion stems from the theoretical implication of this restriction. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\det(\bm{R}) = \pm 1$. A value of $-1$ corresponds to a reflection (improper rotation), while $1$ corresponds to a pure rotation. Since the paper argues that preserving pairwise angles (hyperspherical energy) is sufficient for maintaining semantic structure, shouldn't allowing reflections also be valid?

Is there a specific reason why restricting the finetuning updates to only rotations (excluding reflections) is beneficial for text-to-image diffusion models, or is this purely a side-effect of choosing Cayley parameterization for computational efficiency?
Answer:
The restriction to rotations (matrices with determinant $+1$) rather than reflections (determinant $-1$) is primarily a consequence of the mathematical properties of the **Cayley parameterization** chosen for computational efficiency, rather than a fundamental requirement of the finetuning theory itself.

As explained in Section 3.3 ("Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization"), the authors utilize the formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. Mathematically, this specific construction guarantees that the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ always belongs to the **Special Orthogonal Group**, denoted as $SO(d)$, which consists exclusively of orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $+1$. This excludes the possibility of generating improper rotations (reflections), which would belong to the full Orthogonal Group $O(d)$ and have a determinant of $-1$.

However, this exclusion does not negatively impact the model's performance or its ability to preserve semantic structure. The paper explicitly states in Section 3.3 that "such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice." Theoretically, both rotations and reflections satisfy the core requirement of OFT: they are orthogonal transformations that preserve pairwise angles between neurons, thereby keeping the **hyperspherical energy** invariant (as noted in Section 3.2). Since the primary goal is to maintain the relative angular relationships among neurons to preserve pretrained generative capability, restricting the search space to $SO(d)$ is sufficient. The added flexibility of allowing reflections appears unnecessary for the downstream tasks tested (subject-driven and controllable generation), making the computationally cheaper Cayley parameterization—which avoids expensive operations like QR decomposition or Gram-Schmidt—a pragmatic choice that trades a negligible reduction in theoretical search space for significant gains in training efficiency and stability.

### Clarification on the definition of Hyperspherical Energy and its relation to Cosine Similarity
Question:
The paper defines Hyperspherical Energy (HE) in Equation 1 as:
$$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} $$
The authors claim that minimizing the difference in HE between the pretrained and finetuned models preserves semantic relationships. However, I am struggling to connect this specific formula to the concept of 'cosine similarity' mentioned earlier in the Introduction.

If we expand $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2 - 2(\hat{\bm{w}}_i \cdot \hat{\bm{w}}_j)$, then minimizing/maximizing HE seems related to maximizing/minimizing the sum of cosines. But why choose the inverse Euclidean distance formulation rather than just explicitly regularizing on cosine similarity? Furthermore, if $||...||^{-1}$ blows up when vectors are identical ($i=j$, though excluded here), how does this behave numerically when two neurons become nearly parallel during training? Is there a risk of numerical instability here?
Answer:
The confusion stems from conflating the *definition* of Hyperspherical Energy (HE) with the *mechanism* used to preserve it. While your algebraic expansion correctly links the Euclidean distance between normalized vectors to cosine similarity, the choice of the inverse distance formulation $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ is deliberate because it creates a strict "repulsive" force that prevents neuron collapse, whereas raw cosine similarity would allow neurons to overlap without penalty.

Mathematically, as you noted, for unit vectors $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^2 = 2(1 - \cos\theta_{ij})$. Consequently, minimizing the Euclidean distance corresponds to maximizing the cosine similarity (making vectors parallel). The HE formula sums the **inverse** of this distance. Therefore, as two neurons become nearly parallel ($\theta_{ij} \to 0$), the term $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ tends toward infinity. This singularity acts as a hard barrier: the energy explodes if any two neurons align perfectly. By minimizing or preserving this energy, the model is forced to maintain a specific distribution of angles where neurons are distinct and spread out on the hypersphere, rather than collapsing into redundant clusters. If one were to simply regularize on cosine similarity directly (e.g., trying to keep $\sum \cos\theta_{ij}$ constant), one could theoretically satisfy that constraint by having many pairs of neurons become identical while others diverge, which destroys the representational capacity of the layer. The inverse distance formulation ensures that *all* pairwise distances contribute meaningfully to preventing collapse.

Regarding your concern about numerical instability when vectors become nearly parallel: in practice, this scenario is precisely what Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) avoids entirely through its architectural design rather than relying solely on gradient penalties. The paper proves that applying a shared orthogonal transformation $\bm{R}$ to all neurons in a layer ($\bm{W} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$) **provably preserves** the exact pairwise angles between every neuron pair because orthogonal matrices are rigid rotations/reflections that do not alter dot products ($( \bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_i )^\top (\bm{R}\hat{\bm{w}}_j) = \hat{\bm{w}}_i^\top \hat{\bm{w}}_j$). Since OFT enforces this structural constraint by construction—learning only orthogonal matrices—the hyperspherical energy remains exactly constant throughout training regardless of how close any specific pair might get during intermediate steps of other methods. Thus, while the theoretical formula has a singularity at zero distance, OFT never encounters it because it operates in a subspace where relative angles are invariant by definition. This eliminates the need for numerical stabilization tricks usually required when optimizing repulsive potentials directly; instead, OFT sidesteps the optimization landscape issue entirely by restricting updates to isometries.

### Derivation of the equivalence between ||R - I|| <= epsilon and ||Q|| <= epsilon' in COFT
Question:
In Section 3.4 regarding Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), the authors wish to enforce an $l_2$ norm constraint on the deviation from identity: $||\bm{R}-\bm{I}|| \leq \epsilon$.

They utilize the Neumann series approximation for the Cayley transform: $\bm{R}\approx \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + \mathcal{o}(Q^2)$. Based on this, they state that the constraint is equivalent to enforcing $||2\bm{Q}|| \leq \epsilon$, or roughly $||\bm{Q}|| \leq \epsilon'$.

Could someone walk me through the rigorous justification for replacing the nonlinear constraint on $||(I+Q)(I-Q)^{-1} - I||$ with a linear constraint on $||Q||$? Specifically:
1. Under what conditions does ignoring higher-order terms ($O(Q^2)$) remain valid for typical finetuning steps?
2. Does projecting gradients onto a ball of radius $r$ in Q-space strictly guarantee that R stays within distance $e$ in R-space, or is this an approximation that might violate hard constraints near the boundary?
Answer:
The derivation relies on a first-order Taylor expansion of the Cayley transform around the zero matrix, which is valid specifically because COFT initializes the skew-symmetric matrix $\bm{Q}$ to $\bm{0}$ and operates under the assumption that finetuning updates are small. The Cayley transform defines the orthogonal matrix as $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$. Using the Neumann series expansion for $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1}$, which is valid when the spectral norm $\|\bm{Q}\| < 1$, we get $(\bm{I} - \bm{Q})^{-1} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bm{Q}^k = \bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \mathcal{O}(\|\bm{Q}\|^2)$. Multiplying this by $(\bm{I} + \bm{Q})$ yields $\bm{R} = (\bm{I} + \bm{Q})(\bm{I} + \bm{Q} + \dots) = \bm{I} + 2\bm{Q} + 2\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{Q}\|^2)$. Consequently, the deviation from identity simplifies to $\|\bm{R} - \bm{I}\| = \|2\bm{Q}\| + \|2\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{Q}\|^2)\|$.

Regarding your specific questions: first, ignoring higher-order terms is justified in typical finetuning scenarios because COFT explicitly enforces a constraint where $\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|$ (and thus $\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|^2$) remains small throughout training. Since the model starts at $\boldsymbol{\Omega}=0$ and gradients are projected to stay within a bounded region, the term $2\mathcal{o}(\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|^2)$ becomes negligible compared to $2\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|$, making the linear approximation highly accurate for practical step sizes. Second, projecting gradients onto a ball of radius $r$ in $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$-space does **not** strictly guarantee that $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ stays within distance $\epsilon$ in $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$-space; rather, it ensures that $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ stays within a *corresponding* bound derived from the linear approximation. As noted in Section 3.4, this substitution treats the constraint as an approximation ("roughly") where $\epsilon'$ is chosen such that $\|2\boldsymbol{\Omega}\| \approx \|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|$. Near the boundary of the feasible region, if $\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|$ grows large enough that higher-order terms become significant, strict adherence to $\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}-\mathbf{id}\| \leq \epsilon$ cannot be guaranteed solely by bounding $\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|$, though empirically this approximation holds well enough to provide the observed stability improvements over unconstrained OFT.

### Intuition behind Block-Diagonal Orthogonal Matrices vs. Low-Rank Updates in LoRA
Question:
Section 3 discusses parameter efficiency comparisons between OFT and LoRA. LoRA adds a low-rank update $(AB)$ where rank($r'$) << dimensions. OFT multiplies by an orthogonal matrix which is approximated as block-diagonal with block size determined by hyperparameter $r$.

The paper states: 'LoRA exploits low-rank structure... while OFT takes a different route by exploiting sparsity structure.'

I am confused about how these relate geometrically:
- LoRA effectively moves weights along a subspace defined by singular vectors.
- Block-diagonal OFT allows independent rotations within subspaces but forces zeros outside those blocks.

Does block-diagonal OFT actually impose *less* expressive power than full-rank LoRA for certain types of transformations? For instance, if a required update requires mixing features across different blocks (non-local interactions within a layer), wouldn't increasing $r$ (fewer blocks) be necessary until it becomes computationally prohibitive? How does one theoretically determine if block-diagonality is sufficient for preserving hyperspherical energy compared to low-rank perturbations?
Answer:
The confusion regarding whether block-diagonal Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) imposes less expressive power than full-rank updates stems from conflating the **structure of the update** with the **mechanism of transformation**. Geometrically, LoRA and OFT operate on fundamentally different manifolds: LoRA restricts the weight update to a low-dimensional linear subspace, whereas OFT restricts the transformation to a specific subset of rotations (block-diagonal orthogonal matrices).

While it is true that a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix cannot mix features across disjoint blocks (imposing a form of locality), this does not necessarily equate to lower expressiveness for the specific goal of preserving pretrained generative ability. The paper argues that the "sparsity" in OFT refers to the zero entries in the off-block regions of the rotation matrix $\bm{R}$, which enforces independent rotations within subspaces. Crucially, unlike LoRA which adds a low-rank perturbation $\Delta \bm{W} = \bm{A}\bm{B}$ to shift weights, OFT applies a multiplicative orthogonal transformation $\bm{W}_{new} = \bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Even if $\bm{R}$ is block-diagonal, if the base weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ is full-rank (which pretrained diffusion models typically are), the resulting updated weights $\bm{W}_{new}$ remain full-rank. Therefore, OFT does not suffer from the rank deficiency inherent in low-rank adaptations; it merely constrains *how* the neurons are rotated relative to each other.

Regarding your concern about non-local interactions requiring increased block size $r$: The paper acknowledges that reducing $r$ (increasing block count) acts as stronger regularization by limiting cross-feature mixing. However, theoretical sufficiency for preserving hyperspherical energy depends strictly on orthogonality, not on mixing across all dimensions simultaneously. The core insight of the paper is that preserving pairwise angles (hyperspherical energy) is more critical for semantic retention than arbitrary Euclidean proximity. Since any orthogonal matrix preserves these angles perfectly, even a highly restricted block-diagonal one maintains the intrinsic geometric relationships between neurons that define semantic content. Empirically, the authors find that moderate values of $r$ strike an optimal balance: they allow enough rotational freedom to adapt to downstream tasks while preventing the "model collapse" seen in unconstrained finetuning or standard LoRA when pushed too far. Thus, while increasing $r$ does increase expressivity toward a full rotation, the block-diagonal constraint is theoretically sufficient because it prioritizes angular preservation over unrestricted linear combinations, aligning with the hypothesis that neuron directions encode semantics while magnitudes are secondary.

### Interpretation of Re-scaled OFT and commutativity issues with Diagonal Scaling
Question:
In Section 3.5, Re-scaled OFT introduces a learnable diagonal matrix $D$: $z = (RW^0D)^Tx$. The footnote mentions an errata where they corrected the order from $(DRW^0)^Tx$. 

Since diagonal matrices commute with other diagonal matrices but not necessarily with general orthogonal matrices or weight matrices, does placing $D$ on the right versus left fundamentally change what is being scaled?
Specifically:
- If we scale columns of weights ($W^0 D$), we are scaling individual neurons' magnitudes before rotation.
- If we scale rows ($(RW^0) D$?), wait, $(RW^0 D)^T x = D W^{0,T} R^T x$. This scales the output activations per neuron index after rotation.

Given that Hyperspherical Energy depends only on directions (normalized vectors), scaling magnitudes shouldn't affect HE regardless of position. However, does applying scaling *before* vs *after* rotation impact how effectively we can decouple magnitude changes from directional changes during gradient descent? Why was $(RW^0D)^Tx$ chosen as the correct implementation over alternatives?
Answer:
The placement of the diagonal scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ relative to the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and the pretrained weights $\bm{W}^0$ is indeed critical because, unlike scalar multiplication, matrix multiplication is not commutative with respect to general matrices. The correction noted in the footnote—from $(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$—fundamentally shifts **what** is being scaled: moving from scaling the rotated output features to scaling the individual neuron vectors (columns of the weight matrix) before they undergo rotation.

Mathematically, let us analyze the two configurations. In the erroneous formulation $(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, expanding the transpose gives $\bm{x}^\top \bm{W}^{0,\top} \bm{R}^\top \bm{D}$. Here, $\bm{D}$ acts on the output of the rotation $\bm{R}^\top$. Since $\bm{D}$ is diagonal, this operation scales the components of the transformed feature vector independently. Effectively, this applies a non-uniform stretch to the coordinate axes *after* the entire subspace has been rotated. While this changes magnitudes, it distorts the geometry of the rotated space in a way that couples specific output dimensions with specific input rotations, potentially breaking the clean separation between directional updates (handled by $\bm{R}$) and magnitude adjustments.

In contrast, the corrected formulation $(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top \bm{x}$ expands to $\bm{x}^\top \bm{D} \bm{W}^{0,\top} \bm{R}^\top$. Here, $\BM D$ multiplies $\BM W^{0,\top}$ from the left (or equivalently, if we view weights as column vectors, it scales them). Specifically, if we denote $[\BM W^0]_{:,i}$ as the $i$-th neuron vector (column), then multiplying by $\BM D$ on the right in the untransposed form $(\BM R \BM W^0 \BM D)$ scales each neuron vector individually: $[\BM R (\BM W^0 \BM D)]_{:,i} = s_i (\BM R [\BM W^0]_{:,i})$. This means each neuron's magnitude is adjusted *before* or *during* its rotation by $\BM R$, but crucially, every component of that specific neuron vector is scaled by exactly one factor $s_i$. This aligns perfectly with the theoretical motivation of Re-scaled OFT: Hyperspherical Energy (HE) depends solely on the angles between normalized vectors. Scaling a vector by a positive scalar $s_i > 0$ leaves its direction (and thus its contribution to HE) invariant regardless of whether that scaling happens before or after rotation. However, placing $\BM D$ adjacent to $\BM W^0$ ensures that we are explicitly learning independent magnitude factors for each **neuron**, treating them as distinct entities whose norms can vary without affecting their angular relationships with other neurons.

From an optimization perspective regarding gradient descent and decoupling, placing $\DM D$ next to $\DM W^0$ creates a cleaner parameterization where $\DM R$ strictly governs rotational degrees of freedom (preserving angles) and $\DM D$ strictly governs radial degrees of freedom (scaling magnitudes). If placed elsewhere, such as post-rotation in a way that mixes coordinates, gradients flowing through $\DM D$ would interact complexly with off-diagonal elements of $\DM R$, making it harder for the optimizer to distinguish between "this needs a bigger magnitude" and "this needs a different orientation." The corrected order ensures that for any pair of neurons $i$ and $j$, their dot product scales cleanly by $s_i s_j$, allowing their normalized cosine similarity to remain purely a function of their directions defined by $\DM R$. This strict decoupling allows Re-scaled OFT to flexibly adapt feature magnitudes—which might be necessary for fitting downstream distributions—while rigorously preserving the hyperspherical energy structure that maintains semantic integrity from pretraining.
