Chapter 3: # Chapter 3: Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) Methodology

The fundamental challenge in adapting large-scale text-to-image diffusion models lies in balancing the need for specific downstream customization against the risk of catastrophic forgetting, where the model loses its broad, high-fidelity generative capabilities. Traditional finetuning approaches rely on updating neuron weights via gradient descent with a small learning rate or adding low-rank components, operating under the implicit assumption that minimizing the Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained weights ensures semantic preservation. However, recent insights suggest that Euclidean distance is an insufficient metric for capturing the structural integrity required for complex generative tasks. Instead, the semantic relationships within a neural network are better characterized by the angular configurations of its neurons on a unit hypersphere. This chapter introduces Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a principled methodology that leverages orthogonal transformations to adapt diffusion models. By design, OFT provably preserves hyperspherical energy—a measure of pairwise neuron relationships—thereby ensuring that the intrinsic semantic structure learned during pretraining remains intact while allowing the model to acquire new control signals or subject-specific identities.

## The Principle of Orthogonal Transformation in Weight Updates

To understand why orthogonal transformation serves as an ideal mechanism for finetuning, one must first recognize what aspect of neural network weights actually encodes semantic information. Empirical observations in deep learning indicate that angular differences between features often characterize semantic gaps more effectively than magnitude differences. Consider a decomposition inspired by 2D Fourier analysis, where an image consists of a magnitude spectrum and a phase spectrum. The phase spectrum, which represents angular information relative to a basis, retains the majority of the image's semantic content; reconstructing an image with random magnitudes but correct phases yields a recognizable result, whereas retaining magnitudes with random phases destroys semantics entirely. Translating this intuition to neural networks, the directions (angles) of neurons play a dominant role in storing and processing semantic information, while their magnitudes are secondary. Consequently, modifying the direction of neurons is the most direct path to altering the semantic output of a model without disrupting its foundational knowledge base.

However, arbitrarily rotating individual neurons carries significant risks. If each neuron were rotated independently or with unstructured degrees of freedom, the delicate geometric relationships established during pretraining would be shattered, leading to model collapse or severe degradation in generation quality. The solution lies in applying a transformation that alters directions globally while strictly maintaining internal geometric consistency. An orthogonal transformation—which encompasses rotations and reflections—is uniquely suited for this purpose because it preserves angles between vectors. When an orthogonal matrix is applied to all neurons within a layer simultaneously, every pairwise angle between neurons remains invariant. This property creates a "sweet spot" between flexibility and regularity: it allows the model to shift its entire coordinate system to accommodate new tasks (such as recognizing a specific subject or adhering to edge maps) while mathematically guaranteeing that the relative structural relationships defining the pretrained knowledge remain untouched. This stands in stark contrast to standard finetuning, which lacks such structural guarantees and relies solely on heuristic constraints like small learning rates to prevent drift.

## Mathematical Proof: Preserving Hyperspherical Energy via Rotation/Reflection

The theoretical foundation of OFT rests on the concept of hyperspherical energy (HE), which quantifies the collective pairwise relational structure among neurons in a layer. For a fully connected layer represented by a weight matrix $\mathbf{W} = \{\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where each $\mathbf{w}_i$ is a neuron vector in $d$-dimensional space, we define normalized neurons as $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i := \mathbf{w}_i / \|\mathbf{w}_i\|$. The hyperspherical energy is formally defined as the sum of inverse distances between all pairs of these normalized vectors:
$$
\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|^{-1}
$$
Since $\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{w}}_j\|$ is directly related to the cosine similarity (and thus the angle) between neurons $i$ and $j$, HE captures how uniformly or specifically distributed these neurons are on the unit hypersphere. The core hypothesis driving OFT is that preserving this energy value during finetuning is critical for maintaining generative performance. Mathematically, we seek to minimize the difference $\|\text{HE}(\mathbf{W}) - \text{HE}(\mathbf{W}^0)\|$, where $\mathbf{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights.

We can prove that applying an orthogonal transformation achieves this minimization perfectly by reducing it to zero difference without requiring iterative regularization penalties that might fail to converge precisely. Let us consider transforming the pretrained weight matrix $\mathbf{W}^0$ by an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{R}$ such that $\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}^0$, where $\mathbf{R}$ satisfies $\mathbf{R}^\top\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}^\top = \mathbf{I}$ (with determinant $1$ indicating rotation and $-1$ indicating reflection). Because orthogonal transformations preserve vector norms and dot products, they inherently preserve angles between vectors. Specifically, for any two neurons $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i$ and $\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_j$ in the pretrained layer, their transformed counterparts are $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i = \frac{\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i}{\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}^0_i\|}$ (since norms are preserved). The distance between any pair remains invariant:
$$
\|\hat{\mathbf{w}}_i - \hat{\mathbf{j}}_j\| = \|(\frac{\hat{\omega}_k}{k}) - (\frac{l}{m})\| 
$$
More rigorously expressed: since $\|\cdot\|$ is rotation-invariant and linear operations commute with normalization when scaling factors are unity (which holds here), we have:
$$
\|\hat{\omega}_{new\_pair}\| = \| R (\frac{o}{p}) \| = \| o/p \| 
$$
Actually simpler: The term inside HE depends only on angles via cosine similarity $cos(\theta_{ij})$. Since $cos(Rv_i)^T(Rv_j) = v_i^T R^T R v_j = v_i^T v_j$, all pairwise similarities are identical before and after transformation. Therefore:
$$
\text{HE}(\bm W) = \text {HE } (\bm R W ^ 0 )= H E(W ^ 0 ) 
$$
This equality proves that if we restrict our search space for finetuning updates exclusively to orthogonal transformations applied layer-wise, we automatically satisfy the condition $\|\text {HE } (\bm W)-H E(W ^ 0 )|=0$. No matter how much we rotate or reflect the entire subspace spanned by these neurons through learning $\bm R$, their internal geometry—and consequently their encoded semantic relationships—remains perfectly conserved relative to one another. This provides a rigorous mathematical guarantee absent in standard gradient descent methods which merely hope that small steps equate to small structural changes.

## Formulating OFT: Optimizing Layer-Shared Orthogonal Matrices ($\bm R$)

Having established that orthogonal transformations preserve hyperspherical energy by construction, OFT reformulates the finetuning problem not as finding new absolute weight values close to old ones in Euclidean space, but as optimizing specific transformation matrices for each layer of the network. In traditional approaches like LoRA or full fine-tuning with small learning rates, one attempts to solve for additive updates or direct gradients on weights $\bm W$. In contrast OFT posits that every trainable parameter update should manifest as multiplication by an orthogonal matrix shared across all neurons within that specific layer formaly denoted as RMAT ∈ ℝ^(d×d).
