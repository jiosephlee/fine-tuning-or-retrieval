Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The advent of large-scale text-to-image diffusion models has revolutionized the field of computer graphics and creative AI, enabling the synthesis of photorealistic images from natural language descriptions. Models such as Stable Diffusion, Imagen, and DALL-E 2 have demonstrated unprecedented capabilities in translating abstract textual concepts into coherent visual representations. These systems leverage vast amounts of paired text-image data to learn complex distributions of visual features, allowing users to generate high-fidelity imagery with remarkable ease. However, despite their impressive generative power, relying solely on text prompts presents significant limitations when precise control over specific attributes or identities is required. The inherent ambiguity of natural language often leads to outputs that fail to capture fine-grained details or adhere strictly to user intent, creating a pressing need for advanced mechanisms to guide these powerful models toward specialized downstream tasks.

To bridge the gap between broad generative capabilities and specific user requirements, researchers have identified two critical application domains: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Subject-driven generation focuses on the ability to insert a specific entity—such as a particular pet, person, or object—into various contexts while maintaining its unique identity. This task requires the model to learn from only a handful of reference images, a scenario known as few-shot learning, ensuring that the generated subject remains recognizable regardless of the surrounding environment described by the text prompt. Conversely, controllable generation addresses the need for structural precision by incorporating external conditioning signals beyond text. By integrating inputs such as Canny edge maps, segmentation masks, or facial landmarks, these methods allow users to dictate the exact layout, geometry, or spatial arrangement of the generated content. Both tasks fundamentally rely on the ability to adapt pre-trained diffusion models without degrading their original high-quality generative performance.

The pursuit of effective adaptation strategies reveals a profound tension between adaptability and stability in deep learning systems. When finetuning massive pre-trained models on narrow downstream tasks, there is a persistent risk of "catastrophic forgetting," where the model loses its general knowledge and ability to generate diverse, high-fidelity images in favor of overfitting to the specific training data. Traditional finetuning approaches often attempt to mitigate this by using extremely small learning rates or freezing most of the network parameters, hoping that minimal weight updates will preserve the pre-training distribution. However, these heuristic methods lack a principled theoretical foundation and offer no guarantee that the semantic structure encoded in the original model will remain intact. The challenge lies in developing a finetuning methodology that allows for sufficient flexibility to learn new tasks while rigorously preserving the intricate geometric relationships among neurons that define the model's pre-existing generative competence.

Addressing these challenges necessitates establishing clear criteria for what constitutes an effective finetuning method. Ideally, such a method must satisfy two primary desiderata: training efficiency and generalizability preservation. Training efficiency demands that the adaptation process utilizes a minimal number of trainable parameters and converges rapidly within a limited number of epochs, making it practical for resource-constrained environments and rapid prototyping. Simultaneously, generalizability preservation ensures that the finetuned model retains its capacity for high-fidelity and diverse image generation across unseen prompts and conditions. Current literature highlights that merely minimizing the Euclidean distance between pre-trained and finetuned weights is insufficient for capturing semantic preservation; instead, more structural measures are required to quantify how well the internal relationships of neurons are maintained during adaptation. This chapter sets the stage for exploring how orthogonal transformations can serve as a principled solution to this balancing act, offering a robust framework for controlling text-to-image diffusion models with superior stability and performance.
