Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Theoretical Foundations – Hyperspherical Energy and Semantic Structure

To effectively adapt large text-to-image diffusion models for specific downstream tasks, such as subject-driven generation or controllable generation, one must first understand the fundamental nature of how these models encode information. Traditional finetuning approaches often rely on minimizing the Euclidean distance between the weights of a pretrained model and a finetuned model, operating under the assumption that keeping the weights close in a linear sense preserves the model's capabilities. However, recent insights suggest that this assumption is flawed and fails to capture the true structural requirements for maintaining generative semantics. Instead, the geometry of neural networks reveals that the angular relationships between neurons—specifically their positions on a unit hypersphere—are far more critical than their magnitudes. This chapter establishes the theoretical groundwork for Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT) by exploring why preserving hyperspherical energy is essential for retaining the semantic knowledge embedded within a pretrained diffusion model.

## Neuron Representation on Unit Hyperspheres

In deep neural networks, particularly within fully connected and convolutional layers, neurons can be conceptualized as vectors in a high-dimensional space. Each neuron $\bm{w}_i$ possesses both a magnitude (length) and a direction (angle relative to other axes). While standard training procedures treat these components equally, emerging evidence indicates that normalizing these vectors onto a unit hypersphere provides a more faithful representation of the semantic content they carry. When we project all neurons in a specific layer onto a unit hypersphere, we normalize them such that $\hat{\bm{w}}_i = \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$, where $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm. This normalization process strips away the magnitude information, leaving only the directional orientation of each neuron.

The rationale behind focusing on this unit hypersphere representation stems from observations in deep learning theory regarding generalization and feature encoding. Research has demonstrated that training neural networks with neurons constrained to lie on a unit hypersphere can yield comparable or even superior generalization capabilities compared to unnormalized counterparts. This suggests that the "capacity" of a neural network to store and retrieve complex patterns is primarily encoded in the directions of its weight vectors rather than their lengths. In the context of text-to-image diffusion models, which rely on intricate hierarchical representations to synthesize photorealistic imagery, the arrangement of these directional vectors defines the semantic landscape of the model. If we view each neuron as pointing toward a specific concept or feature in this high-dimensional space, then preserving the relative angles between these pointers ensures that the conceptual relationships remain intact during adaptation. Consequently, any finetuning strategy that disrupts these angular configurations risks corrupting the underlying semantic structure required for coherent image generation.

## Hyperspherical Energy: Definition and Mathematical Formulation

To quantify the structural integrity of these neuronal arrangements on the unit hypersphere, we utilize a metric known as hyperspherical energy (HE). This concept serves as a rigorous mathematical tool to characterize the pairwise relational structure among all neurons within a single layer. Intuitively, hyperspherical energy measures how uniformly distributed or clustered the neurons are on the surface of the sphere; however, in our context, it specifically captures the sum of similarities between every pair of neurons. Mathematically, for a fully connected layer represented by a weight matrix $\bm{W} = \{\bm{w}_1, \dots, \bm{w}_n\} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n}$, where $n$ is the number of neurons and $d$ is their dimensionality, we first define the normalized neuron vector as $\hat{\bm{w}}_i := \bm{w}_i / \|\bm{w}_i\|$.

The hyperspherical energy of this layer is formally defined as:
$$ \text{HE}(\bm{W}) := \sum_{i \neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1} $$
This formulation sums the inverse distances between all pairs of distinct normalized neurons $(\hat{\bm{w}}_i, \hat{\bm{w}}_j)$. Because these vectors lie on a unit sphere, their pairwise distance is directly related to their cosine similarity (angular separation). Specifically, if two neurons point in nearly identical directions (high cosine similarity), their Euclidean distance $\|\hat{\bm{w}}_i - \hat{\bm{w}}_j\|$ will be small, causing their contribution to the sum to be large due to the inverse term. Conversely, if two neurons are orthogonal or widely separated in angle, their distance increases, reducing their contribution to the total energy. Therefore, minimizing or stabilizing this value reflects maintaining specific angular relationships among neurons. In our proposed framework for finetuning diffusion models, we hypothesize that an optimal finetuned model should exhibit minimal difference in hyperspherical energy compared to its pretrained counterpart. That is:
$$ \| \text{HE}(\bm{W}) - \text{HE}(\bm{W}^0) \| = 0 $$
where $\bm{W}^0$ represents the pretrained weights and $\bm{W}$ represents the finetuned weights. Achieving this equality ensures that while individual weights may shift to accommodate new tasks (such as recognizing a specific subject), their collective geometric configuration—their "shape" on the hypersphere—remains invariant. This invariance is provably guaranteed if we apply an orthogonal transformation to all neurons simultaneously, forming the core theoretical justification for Orthogonal Finetuning.

## Angular Information vs. Magnitude Information in Deep Networks

A pivotal distinction in understanding why orthogonal transformations are effective lies in separating angular information from magnitude information within neural activations and weights. Empirical studies have long suggested that while magnitude scales features up or down based on confidence or intensity levels during training dynamics or inference scaling factors like LayerNorm often handle normalization dynamically), it is actually angular differences that best characterize semantic gaps between inputs and outputs. For instance, when comparing two images—one containing a cat and another containing a dog—the primary distinguishing factor captured by deep features tends to be found in how feature vectors align relative to one another (their angles) rather than how "strong" those features are (their magnitudes).

This dichotomy draws inspiration from signal processing principles akin to those found in Fourier analysis. Just as an image can be decomposed into magnitude spectra (representing texture strength) and phase spectra (representing spatial structure), neural representations appear to store semantic meaning predominantly in their "phase," or angular orientation relative to basis functions or other neurons. The phase spectrum contains enough information about object structure that combining it with random magnitude data can still reconstruct an image recognizable by humans without losing its core semantics. Translating this analogy back to neural networks implies that changing neuron directions allows us to semantically modify what an image looks like—for example, altering its style or subject identity—without destroying its coherence if done correctly.
