\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning"}

### How does the Cayley parameterization restrict the search space to rotations only, excluding reflections?
Question:
In Section 3.3, the authors propose using Cayley parameterization to efficiently generate orthogonal matrices: $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is skew-symmetric. They mention that this method 'can only produce orthogonal matrices with determinant 1', meaning it belongs to the Special Orthogonal Group $SO(d)$.

My confusion stems from the theoretical implication of this restriction. An orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ satisfies $\det(\bm{R}) = \pm 1$. A value of $-1$ corresponds to a reflection (improper rotation), while $1$ corresponds to a pure rotation. Since the paper argues that preserving pairwise angles (hyperspherical energy) is sufficient for maintaining semantic structure, shouldn't allowing reflections also be valid?

Is there a specific reason why restricting the finetuning updates to only rotations (excluding reflections) is beneficial for text-to-image diffusion models, or is this purely a side-effect of choosing Cayley parameterization for computational efficiency?
Answer:
The restriction to rotations (matrices with determinant $+1$) rather than reflections (determinant $-1$) is primarily a consequence of the mathematical properties of the **Cayley parameterization** chosen for computational efficiency, rather than a fundamental requirement of the finetuning theory itself.

As explained in Section 3.3 ("Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization"), the authors utilize the formula $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. Mathematically, this specific construction guarantees that the resulting matrix $\bm{R}$ always belongs to the **Special Orthogonal Group**, denoted as $SO(d)$, which consists exclusively of orthogonal matrices with a determinant of $+1$. This excludes the possibility of generating improper rotations (reflections), which would belong to the full Orthogonal Group $O(d)$ and have a determinant of $-1$.

However, this exclusion does not negatively impact the model's performance or its ability to preserve semantic structure. The paper explicitly states in Section 3.3 that "such a limitation does not affect the performance in practice." Theoretically, both rotations and reflections satisfy the core requirement of OFT: they are orthogonal transformations that preserve pairwise angles between neurons, thereby keeping the **hyperspherical energy** invariant (as noted in Section 3.2). Since the primary goal is to maintain the relative angular relationships among neurons to preserve pretrained generative capability, restricting the search space to $SO(d)$ is sufficient. The added flexibility of allowing reflections appears unnecessary for the downstream tasks tested (subject-driven and controllable generation), making the computationally cheaper Cayley parameterization—which avoids expensive operations like QR decomposition or Gram-Schmidt—a pragmatic choice that trades a negligible reduction in theoretical search space for significant gains in training efficiency and stability.
