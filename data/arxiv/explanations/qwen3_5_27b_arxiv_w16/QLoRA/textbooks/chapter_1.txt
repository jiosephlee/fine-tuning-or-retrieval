Chapter 1: # Chapter 1: Introduction to Efficient LLM Finetuning

## The Scalability Crisis in LLM Finetuning: Memory Requirements of 16-Bit Training

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has ushered in an era where model performance is increasingly correlated with the sheer number of parameters within the architecture. While this scaling brings unprecedented capabilities in reasoning, generation, and instruction following, it has simultaneously created a severe bottleneck regarding the practical feasibility of adapting these models to specific tasks. Traditional finetuning, which involves updating all parameters of a pretrained model using full 16-bit floating-point precision (typically BF16 or FP16), demands prohibitive amounts of Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) memory. For instance, attempting to fully finetune a state-of-the-art 65-billion parameter model like LLaMA requires more than 780 gigabytes of GPU memory. Such a requirement necessitates clusters of high-end enterprise-grade GPUs connected via high-speed interconnects, placing the ability to customize cutting-edge models exclusively in the hands of large corporations and well-funded research institutions.

This memory burden arises not merely from storing the model weights themselves but from the complex ecosystem of data structures required during the training loop. During stochastic gradient descent, the system must maintain the original model weights, the gradients computed for every parameter, optimizer states (such as momentum and variance buffers), and activations required for backpropagation. Even when employing techniques like gradient checkpointing to reduce activation memory, the sheer volume of data associated with a 65B parameter model remains insurmountable for standard consumer hardware. Consequently, researchers and developers are forced to choose between using smaller, less capable models that fit within their memory constraints or accepting that they cannot adapt the most powerful available architectures to their specific domains. This disparity creates a "scalability crisis" where the most advanced tools are effectively locked away from independent innovators, educators, and smaller organizations who could benefit immensely from tailoring these technologies to niche applications.

## Limitations of Existing Quantization Methods: Inference-Only vs. Training

In response to the growing memory demands of deploying massive models, the field has developed various quantization techniques designed to compress model weights by representing them with fewer bits. Quantization involves mapping continuous high-precision values (like 32-bit or 16-bit floats) to a discrete set of lower-precision values (such as 8-bit integers or 4-bit floats). Several successful methods have emerged that allow for efficient inference—the process of running a trained model to generate predictions—with minimal loss in accuracy. Techniques such as SmoothQuant and LLM.int8() manage outlier features effectively, allowing models to run efficiently on consumer hardware during deployment. However, these methods share a critical limitation: they are generally designed solely for inference and break down completely when applied to training.

The fundamental reason existing quantization methods fail during training lies in the mechanics of backpropagation. Inference is a feed-forward process where quantized weights are dequantized temporarily for matrix multiplication; once the output is generated, no further modification is needed. Training, conversely, requires computing gradients with respect to every weight parameter and updating those weights iteratively. Standard quantization schemes often discard information necessary for calculating precise gradients or assume static weight distributions that do not hold when weights are being updated dynamically. Previous attempts at backpropagating through quantized weights have struggled with instability or significant performance degradation when scaled beyond roughly one billion parameters. Furthermore, naive application of low-bit arithmetic often leads to numerical instabilities where small errors compound rapidly during optimization steps. Thus, while we possess tools to *run* huge models cheaply on consumer devices, we lacked a mechanism to *teach* them new skills without reverting to massive 16-bit representations that exceed available memory capacities until recently.

## Overview of Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) and LoRA Basics

To circumvent the need for updating billions of parameters while retaining high performance, researchers turned toward Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT). Unlike full finetuning, PEFT freezes the vast majority of pretrained model weights and introduces a small set of additional trainable parameters that act as adapters or modifiers to the base model's behavior. Among these approaches, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has emerged as particularly effective and widely adopted due to its simplicity and efficacy. LoRA operates on the hypothesis that although language models contain billions of parameters during training updates, the actual change in weight space induced by downstream tasks is low-rank in nature; meaning that only a small fraction of dimensions actually shifts significantly when adapting to a new domain or task.

Technically, LoRA modifies linear projection layers within a Transformer architecture by decomposing weight updates into two smaller matrices rather than updating the full weight matrix directly. Given an original linear transformation $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}$ where $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times o}$ is frozen during training, LoRA injects an additional term consisting of two low-rank matrices $\mathbf{L}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times r}$ and $\mathbf{L}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times o}$ multiplied by a scalar $s$. The final computation becomes $\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2$. Here, $r$ represents the rank of the decomposition and is typically chosen to be very small relative to $h$ or $o$, often resulting in adapter weights comprising less than 0.2% of the original model size. Crucially, because only these tiny adapter matrices are updated via backpropagation while $\mathbf{W}$ remains frozen at full precision during inference but stored compactly during training checks if applicable contexts allow (though traditionally stored at full precision), memory usage drops dramatically compared to full finetuning despite maintaining comparable performance on many benchmarks.
