Chapter 2: # Chapter 2: Uncovering Non-Uniformities in Positional Information

The fundamental limitation preventing Large Language Models (LLMs) from processing extremely long sequences lies not merely in computational capacity, but in how the model encodes the sequential order of tokens. While Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) has become the standard mechanism for injecting positional information into modern transformers, extending the context window beyond the pre-trained range traditionally relies on positional interpolation. Conventional wisdom suggests that scaling the rotation angles uniformly or applying simple heuristic rules is sufficient to bridge the gap between short and long contexts. However, empirical investigation reveals a critical flaw in this assumption: positional information possesses a complex, non-uniform structure that varies significantly across both RoPE dimensions and specific token positions within a sequence. Ignoring these subtleties leads to severe information loss, causing performance collapse when models encounter out-of-distribution token positions. This chapter delves into the empirical evidence that dismantles the "one-size-fits-all" approach to interpolation, establishing the necessity for a multi-dimensional, adaptive strategy that respects the intrinsic heterogeneity of positional data.

## Empirical Analysis of RoPE Dimension Sensitivity

To understand why standard interpolation methods fail at extreme scales, one must first examine the mechanics of RoPE itself and how it interacts with sequence length expansion. In RoPE, a token at position $n$ is encoded by rotating its representation vector across pairs of dimensions. Mathematically, for an embedding dimension $d$, the encoding involves sine and cosine functions of rotary angles defined by $\theta_i = \theta^{-2i/d}$, where $i$ ranges from $0$ to $d/2 - 1$. The parameter $\theta$ serves as a base frequency controller, typically set to 10000. Crucially, this formulation creates a spectrum of frequencies: lower indices $i$ correspond to higher frequencies (rapid oscillation), while higher indices correspond to lower frequencies (slow oscillation).

When extending a context window from an original length $L$ to a target length $L'$ with an extension ratio $s = L'/L$, standard methods attempt to map new position indices back into the familiar range learned during pre-training. Linear Positional Interpolation (PI), for instance, simply divides all rotation angles by $s$. While conceptually straightforward, this approach treats every dimension identically regardless of its frequency characteristics. Similarly, NTK-based methods attempt to distribute this scaling unevenly but rely on rigid mathematical formulas derived from Neural Tangent Kernel theory rather than empirical observation of the specific model's behavior. YaRN attempts to improve upon this by manually grouping dimensions into high-, mid-, and low-frequency buckets and applying different strategies to each. However, these approaches assume that such coarse-grained groupings capture all relevant nuances of positional information.

Empirical analysis challenges these assumptions by revealing that the sensitivity of RoPE dimensions to interpolation is far more granular than previously thought. When researchers attempted to optimize interpolation factors for LLaMA2-7B using an evolutionary search algorithm guided by perplexity metrics on validation sets like PG19 and Proof-pile, they discovered that optimal rescaling factors varied distinctly for almost every single dimension. The search did not converge on a few distinct clusters as YaRN might suggest; instead, it identified a continuous landscape where each dimension required a unique adjustment factor $\lambda_i$. This finding implies that the "information entropy" associated with positional encoding is not uniform across the embedding space. Treating all high-frequency dimensions equally or assuming a smooth transition between groups ignores specific resonant properties inherent to certain dimensions that are critical for maintaining semantic coherence over long distances. Consequently, forcing a uniform or semi-uniform scaling distorts these delicate relationships, leading to what can be described as "catastrophic values" where the model struggles to distinguish between adjacent tokens or loses track of absolute positions entirely.

## Finding 1: Dimension-Specific Non-Uniformity

The first major insight derived from this deep dive into RoPE mechanics is that effective positional interpolation must account for substantial non-uniformities across RoPE dimensions themselves. Traditional methods operate under simplifications: PI assumes every dimension needs identical compression; NTK assumes a power-law distribution; YaRN assumes three discrete regimes. The experimental data demonstrates that none of these approximations are optimal for maximizing context retention without fine-tuning. When evaluating models extended via these standard heuristics against models using searched, dimension-specific rescale factors, the latter consistently achieved significantly lower perplexity on long-document tasks such as PG19 and Proof-pile without any parameter updates beyond the embedding modification.

Specifically, the analysis showed that lower RoPE dimensions (which correspond to higher frequencies) generally benefit from less aggressive interpolation compared to higher dimensions (lower frequencies). However, this relationship is not strictly monotonic nor perfectly predictable by existing theoretical bounds like those found in Dynamic NTK or YaRN's manual thresholds. For example, YaRN was observed to underperform relative even to basic PI on certain datasets because its fixed grouping failed to accommodate the specific target context window requirements; notably, YaRN's perplexity would spike dramatically once the context exceeded roughly 7k tokens in an 8k setting because its static grouping could not adapt to the changing density requirements of longer sequences. In contrast, allowing each dimension $i$ to possess its own independent rescale factor $\lambda_i$, determined through optimization rather than heuristic assignment, preserved key informational structures within the original RoPE embedding. By tailoring $\lambda_i$ individually, the method ensures that critical dimensions responsible for capturing local syntactic dependencies are compressed less aggressively than those handling global structural patterns. This precise calibration minimizes information loss during the mapping process, effectively allowing an 8-fold extension ($4\text{k} \to 32\text{k}$) purely through initialization changes before any fine-tuning occurs—a feat unattainable by previous linear or grouped approaches which typically plateau or degrade sharply beyond a 2-to-4-fold increase.

## Finding 2: Token-Position Non-Uniformity

Beyond dimensional variance exists another layer of complexity regarding *where* in the sequence tokens appear relative to one another: token-position non-uniformity. Intuitively one might assume that if position indices need rescaling due to length extension, they should be treated uniformly throughout the entire sequence regardless of whether they appear at index 5 or index 5000. However, empirical findings contradict this symmetry hypothesis by highlighting the disproportionate importance of initial tokens in attention mechanisms and downstream performance phenomena akin to streaming effects observed in other literature such as Streaming LLM and LM-Infinite.

The study posits that initial tokens in an input sequence receive disproportionately large attention scores and play a pivotal role in anchoring subsequent predictions within attention layers.
