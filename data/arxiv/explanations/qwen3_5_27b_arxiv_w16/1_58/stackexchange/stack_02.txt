\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does the 'absmean' quantization function behave during backpropagation if weights are hard-clipped?
Question:
In Section 2, the paper defines the quantization function for weights as:

$$ \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1) $$

where $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of $W$. The text mentions that this constrains weights to \{-1, 0, +1\}. 

My confusion lies in the training process. Since $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ involves rounding and clipping operations which are non-differentiable (the gradient is zero almost everywhere), how does the model learn? Is a Straight-Through Estimator (STE) being used here where gradients pass through unmodified? If so, how does the scaling factor $\gamma$, which depends on $W$, affect the gradient flow? Specifically, since $\gamma$ changes as $W$ updates, does it introduce a dynamic scaling issue during training that isn't explicitly discussed?
Answer:
The model learns during training by employing a **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**, a standard technique in quantization-aware training that allows gradients to bypass the non-differentiable `RoundClip` operation. Although the forward pass strictly constrains weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the backward pass treats the quantization function as an identity mapping (or a linear pass-through) for the gradient flow. This means that while the forward computation uses $\widetilde{W}$, the gradients $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}$ are calculated as if $W$ were continuous, allowing the underlying full-precision weights $W$ to be updated via standard gradient descent.

Regarding your concern about the dynamic scaling factor $\gamma$, which is defined as the average absolute value of $W$ ($\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$), this term is indeed differentiable and plays a crucial role in stabilizing training. Since $\gamma$ is computed directly from $W$, it acts as a learnable normalization factor that adapts to the magnitude of the weights at every step. During backpropagation, the chain rule applies to $\gamma$: as $W$ updates, $\gamma$ changes, and this change propagates back into the gradient calculation for $W$. This creates a feedback loop where the model learns not only the direction of each weight but also an appropriate global scale such that, when divided by $\gamma$, most weights fall within the range $[-1, 1]$ and round effectively to non-zero ternary values. This mechanism prevents "dynamic scaling issues" because $\gamma$ is not a fixed hyperparameter but an intrinsic part of the optimization landscape that co-evolves with $W$, ensuring that the quantization remains effective throughout training without requiring manual intervention or explicit discussion of scaling instability in the paper.
