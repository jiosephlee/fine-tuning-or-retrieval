\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Clarification on the activation scaling range change: [-Q_b, Q_b] vs [0, Q_b]
Question:
In Section 2 under 'Quantization Function', the paper describes a modification for activations:

> "Instead, the activations are all scaled to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token to get rid of the zero-point quantization."

In traditional symmetric quantization without a zero-point offset (like in many INT8 implementations), ranges are often centered around zero anyway. However, standard asymmetric quantization maps real-valued inputs $x_{min}$ and $x_{max}$ to integers $[0, Q_b]$, requiring an affine transformation involving a zero-point shift.

If we map directly to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, does this imply we are assuming activations are always symmetrically distributed around zero? If an activation distribution is heavily skewed positive (e.g., ReLU outputs), wouldn't mapping them symmetrically waste half of our representational range? Why did they choose this specific range over keeping a zero-point offset?
Answer:
The choice to scale activations to the symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ instead of the asymmetric $[0, Q_b]$ is driven primarily by **implementation simplicity and hardware efficiency**, rather than an assumption that activation distributions are perfectly symmetric around zero.

While your intuition regarding skewed distributions (such as those produced by ReLU) is correct—that forcing a symmetric range might appear to waste dynamic range—the paper explicitly states that this modification "introduces negligible effects to the performance." The key insight lies in what is being eliminated: the **zero-point**. In standard asymmetric quantization ($[0, Q_b]$), converting a real value $x$ to an integer requires an affine transformation involving both a scale factor and a zero-point offset ($z$): $\tilde{x} = \text{clip}(\lfloor x/s + z \rfloor, 0, Q_b)$. During de-quantization or subsequent operations, this offset must be carefully managed and subtracted or added at every layer boundary. By shifting the target range to $[-Q_b, Q_b]$, the authors effectively set the zero-point to zero for the quantized domain. This simplifies the operation to a pure linear scaling ($\tilde{x} \approx x/s$) followed by rounding and clipping, removing the need for extra add/subtract instructions associated with handling offsets.

This simplification is particularly valuable in the context of **1-bit LLMs** like BitNet b1.58, where the goal is to minimize computational overhead and maximize throughput. Eliminating the zero-point logic streamlines the data path for both software kernels (like those in `llama.cpp` or `FasterTransformer`) and potential custom hardware accelerators designed for ternary weights. Although a strictly positive distribution like ReLU's will indeed utilize mostly the upper half of the $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ interval (mapping small positive floats to small positive integers near 0), the remaining negative portion acts as a buffer against outliers or slight shifts in distribution caused by normalization layers (like RMSNorm) within the Transformer block. The authors' empirical results confirm that this "waste" of theoretical resolution does not degrade perplexity or downstream task performance significantly enough to justify retaining the complexity of zero-point management. Thus, the trade-off favors system-level optimization—reducing latency and memory bandwidth requirements for control logic—at a negligible cost to numerical precision.
