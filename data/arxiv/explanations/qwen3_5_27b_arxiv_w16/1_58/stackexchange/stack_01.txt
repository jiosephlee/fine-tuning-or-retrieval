\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Where does the '1.58' bit figure come from mathematically for ternary weights {-1, 0, 1}?
Question:
The paper title claims 'All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits' and refers to the model as \textbf{BitNet b1.58}. The abstract states that every parameter is ternary, taking values from \{-1, 0, 1\}. 

I understand that standard binary (2 states) corresponds to 1 bit ($\log_2(2)$). Since there are 3 possible states here, shouldn't the information theoretic representation be $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$ bits? Is the '1.58' strictly derived from $\log_2(3)$, or does it account for something else regarding the distribution of zeros vs non-zeros? Also, why add a decimal point instead of just calling it a 'ternary' or '3-state' model?
Answer:
The "1.58" figure in **BitNet b1.58** is indeed a direct derivation from information theory, specifically representing the theoretical storage requirement for a ternary variable. Since the model constrains every weight to exactly three discrete values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the minimum number of bits required to represent a single weight without redundancy is calculated as $\log_2(3)$. Mathematically, $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58496$, which rounds to $1.58$. This is distinct from standard binary quantization (which uses $\{-1, 1\}$ or $\{0, 1\}$ and requires exactly $1$ bit) and differs from fixed-width integer representations (like storing these values in a standard 2-bit integer format), which would waste space if not packed optimally.

The authors chose to name the model after this specific logarithmic value rather than simply calling it "ternary" or "3-state" to emphasize the precise efficiency gain relative to traditional bit-depth metrics used in deep learning hardware. In the context of LLMs, where model size is conventionally discussed in terms of total bits (e.g., a "7B parameter model in FP16"), expressing the precision as $1.58$ immediately communicates the compression ratio against standard floating-point formats (FP16/BF16) and highlights that this is a sub-2-bit architecture. Crucially, as noted in the paper's introduction, this value reflects the *information content* per parameter rather than an arbitrary distribution of zeros versus non-zeros; while the actual learned distribution might skew heavily toward zero (enabling sparse-like behavior and feature filtering), the encoding cost assumes worst-case entropy where all three states are equally probable possibilities within the ternary set. Thus, the name serves both as a rigorous mathematical descriptor of the parameter space cardinality and a marketing signal of its extreme compactness compared to existing low-bit methods like 4-bit or 2-bit quantization.
