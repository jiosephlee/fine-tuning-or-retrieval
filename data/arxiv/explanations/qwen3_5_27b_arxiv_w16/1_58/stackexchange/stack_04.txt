\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Clarification on 'feature filtering' and how zero-weights improve modeling capability
Question:
The paper claims: "its modeling capability is stronger due to its explicit support for feature filtering, made possible by the inclusion of 0 in the model weights".

Conceptually, I understand that setting a weight to zero effectively removes a connection (similar to dropout or pruning). However, in standard full-precision models (FP16), weights can naturally converge to very small values close to zero without being exactly zero.

Why is having *exactly* zero beneficial compared to having very small floating-point numbers? Does this force a form of hard sparsity that regularizes the model differently than L2 regularization or dropout? Furthermore, if we can prune connections post-training in FP models, why is it necessary to enforce this sparsity *during* training with a hard constraint?
Answer:
The benefit of having *exactly* zero weights in BitNet b1.58, as opposed to weights that merely converge to very small floating-point values, stems from the fundamental difference between **soft suppression** and **hard feature filtering**. In standard full-precision models (FP16), a weight like $10^{-7}$ is mathematically non-zero; it still participates in the matrix multiplication operation, contributing a tiny but non-vanishing signal to the output. While this value is negligible in magnitude, it still requires the hardware to perform a multiplication and an addition, consuming energy and bandwidth.

In contrast, the ternary constraint $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ in BitNet b1.58 enforces **hard sparsity** where a weight of exactly $0$ completely removes the connection between a specific input feature and an output neuron. This acts as an explicit "feature filter," allowing the model to deterministically ignore irrelevant inputs rather than just dampening them. The paper argues that this explicit support for feature filtering strengthens the modeling capability because it forces the optimization process to learn a more robust and discrete representation of which features are truly necessary for a task, rather than relying on the continuous spectrum of small values that can accumulate noise or require complex regularization (like L2) to manage.

Regarding your question on why this sparsity must be enforced *during* training rather than applied via post-training pruning: post-training pruning is an approximation that occurs after the model has already learned its parameters in full precision. It often requires retraining or fine-tuning to recover performance lost during the aggressive removal of connections, and it does not guarantee that the remaining network structure is optimal for the quantized domain. By enforcing the $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ constraint from scratch during training, BitNet b1.58 learns a representation that is natively adapted to this sparse topology. The gradients flow through a system where "zero" is a distinct state with no computational cost (in ideal hardware) and no numerical noise, leading to a model that achieves performance matching FP16 baselines while inheriting the massive efficiency gains of integer-only addition for inference. Essentially, hard sparsity during training allows the model to discover a more efficient "wiring diagram" that soft sparsity or post-hoc pruning cannot easily replicate without significant performance degradation or additional tuning overhead.
