\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Reconciling 'trained from scratch' with matching FP16 perplexity at small scales
Question:
The paper states: "It matches the full-precision ... Transformer LLM with the same model size and training tokens..." and later notes that this match occurs starting from a **3B** size.

Usually, quantizing weights severely limits the expressivity of linear layers because you lose fine-grained control over signal magnitude. Most post-training quantization methods suffer performance drops unless using sophisticated calibration.

Since \textbf{BitNet b1.58} is trained *from scratch* with these constraints baked into the gradients immediately (via Straight Through Estimator or similar implied by RoundClip), why doesn't it converge faster at smaller scales (e.g., <3B)? Is there a theoretical minimum number of parameters required for ternary weights to approximate complex distributions effectively enough that they don't collapse into trivial solutions?
Answer:
The observation that \text{BitNet b1.58} does not outperform or match full-precision baselines at very small scales ($<3$B) despite being trained from scratch stems from the interplay between model capacity, the representational limitations of ternary weights, and the specific training dynamics described in the paper. While training from scratch avoids the catastrophic collapse often seen in post-training quantization, it does not eliminate the fundamental constraint that ternary weights $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ possess significantly lower intrinsic expressivity per parameter compared to floating-point values. At small model sizes, the total number of parameters is insufficient to compensate for this reduction in granularity; essentially, a small ternary network lacks the sheer volume of degrees of freedom required to approximate complex probability distributions without collapsing into trivial solutions or suffering from high approximation error.

The paper explicitly identifies a threshold effect where this limitation is overcome: \text{BitNet b1.58} begins to match full-precision performance starting at the $3$B parameter scale. This suggests a form of scaling law unique to low-bit architectures where increasing model size provides enough redundancy and combinatorial complexity for the ternary weights to effectively simulate continuous functions. Below this threshold, the "feature filtering" advantage provided by the explicit zero weight—which is highlighted in the paper as a key benefit over standard $1$-bit models—is likely overwhelmed by the inability of the remaining non-zero connections ($+1$ and $-1$) to capture fine-grained signal magnitudes necessary for accurate next-token prediction in smaller contexts. Consequently, convergence at smaller scales may appear slower or result in lower final perplexity simply because the optimization landscape for tiny ternary networks is too constrained to find high-quality minima within standard training budgets, whereas larger networks can leverage their expanded search space to bypass these local optima and achieve parity with FP16 models.
