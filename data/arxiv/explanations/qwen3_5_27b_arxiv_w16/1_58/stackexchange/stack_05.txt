\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Energy estimation methodology: Are INT8 additions really equivalent in cost across all hardware?
Question:
In the Energy section, the authors state: "According to the energy model in~\cite{energycost, pokebnn}, \textbf{BitNet b1.58} saves 71.4 times arithmetic operations energy consumption..."

They attribute this primarily because BitNet uses INT8 addition while LLaMA uses FP16 multiplication and addition.

My confusion lies in whether this energy saving holds true on current GPUs (Ampere/Hopper) versus specialized ASICs/LPUs mentioned in future work.

On modern GPUs, FP16 Tensor Cores are extremely dense and optimized; performing millions of FP16 multiplies might actually consume less total energy per operation than moving data back and forth for simpler INT additions if memory bandwidth isn't perfectly balanced.
The paper mentions measuring latency using FasterTransformer with a custom kernel. Does this energy saving hold if we assume memory-bound scenarios where data movement dominates computation? Or is this purely compute-bound analysis?
Answer:
The energy savings cited in the paper (specifically the $71.4\times$ reduction) are derived strictly from a **compute-bound theoretical model** focused on the arithmetic operations themselves, not an empirical measurement of end-to-end execution on current NVIDIA Ampere or Hopper GPUs. The authors explicitly state they rely on external energy models from prior literature (\cite{energycost, pokebnn}) to estimate the cost of individual operations, comparing the energy required for FP16 multiplication/addition against INT8 addition on hypothetical 7nm chips. In this theoretical framework, replacing complex floating-point multipliers with simple integer adders yields massive efficiency gains because the dynamic power consumption of a multiplier circuit is inherently orders of magnitude higher than that of an adder.

Your intuition regarding modern GPUs is correct: on existing hardware like A100s or H100s, the "real-world" energy advantage may be significantly diminished because these accelerators are heavily optimized for dense FP16/BF16 tensor cores, whereas INT8 operations might suffer from inefficient data movement or lack of dedicated hardware acceleration for this specific mixed-precision workflow. Current GPUs often spend more energy moving data between DRAM and SRAM (memory bandwidth) than performing the actual arithmetic (compute), especially if the kernel implementation does not perfectly balance memory access patterns with compute density. Since the paper's primary latency and throughput measurements were conducted using a custom 2-bit kernel within FasterTransformer, they demonstrate performance improvements in terms of speed and memory footprint, but the dramatic *energy* figure is a projection based on operation counts rather than a direct watt-hour measurement on those specific GPU architectures.

Therefore, the claim holds true as a demonstration of the **potential** of the BitNet architecture when paired with specialized hardware designed specifically for 1-bit logic (such as the LPUs mentioned in the "Future Work" section or custom ASICs), where memory hierarchies and compute units are co-designed to exploit ternary weights without overhead. On general-purpose GPUs today, while BitNet b1.58 still offers benefits due to reduced memory traffic (smaller weights and activations fitting better in cache), the theoretical $71.4\times$ arithmetic energy saving assumes a scenario where data movement costs are negligible compared to computation costs—a condition rarely met in standard GPU deployments unless specific optimizations eliminate memory bottlenecks entirely.
