\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### Is the 'new scaling law' based on parameter count or effective compute efficiency?
Question:
In Section 3, under 'Throughput', the authors propose a new scaling law equivalence:
- "70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient... than 13B FP16 LLM"

This suggests that a much larger model in terms of parameter count (70B vs 13B) can be run more efficiently than a smaller full-precision model.

I am confused about what exactly constitutes this "scaling law." Is it implying that we should train larger models specifically because they become cheaper to run due to quantization? Or is it suggesting that for a fixed compute budget (energy/time), we can now afford significantly larger parameter counts?

Specifically, if I have a fixed GPU memory budget of X GB:
- Can I fit more parameters with BitNet?
- Does this mean I should train a larger BitNet model rather than optimizing hyperparameters for an FP model?
How does this change our understanding of Chinchua's optimal compute-to-data ratio?
Answer:
The "new scaling law" proposed in the paper is fundamentally based on **effective compute efficiency** (specifically inference throughput, latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption) rather than a change in the theoretical relationship between parameter count and model performance. The authors are not suggesting that we should train larger models *because* they become cheaper to run in isolation; rather, they are demonstrating that for a fixed hardware budget (e.g., a specific GPU memory limit or energy cap), you can now deploy a model with significantly more parameters using BitNet b1.58 than you could with a full-precision (FP16) model while achieving superior or equivalent performance.

The core mechanism driving this shift is the drastic reduction in the cost of the dominant operation in LLMs: matrix multiplication. In standard FP16 models, this requires expensive floating-point multiplications and additions, whereas BitNet b1.58 replaces these with integer additions because the weights are ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. As stated in the paper, this reduces arithmetic energy consumption by approximately $71.4$ times on $7\text{nm}$ chips and significantly lowers memory bandwidth requirements. Consequently, the "scaling law" equivalence you cited—where a $70\text{B}$ BitNet model is more efficient than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 model—means that the **inference cost** (time, memory, energy) of running the massive $70\text{B}$ quantized model is lower than running the smaller $13\text{B}$ full-precision model.

Regarding your specific scenario with a fixed GPU memory budget $X$ GB: **Yes, you can fit significantly more parameters with BitNet.** Since BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights (requiring roughly $2$ bits per weight effectively) compared to FP16 ($16$ bits), and considering the paper notes that activations are also scaled down to $8$ bits (with potential for further compression), the memory footprint for weights is drastically reduced. This allows you to load a much larger parameter count into the same $X$ GB of VRAM. For instance, the paper notes that BitNet b1.58 $70\text{B}$ can support up to $11$ times the batch size of LLaMA $70\text{B}$ on two A100 cards due to this memory efficiency.

However, this does not necessarily mean you should abandon hyperparameter optimization for FP models in favor of blindly training larger BitNets without consideration. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task performance starting from a **$3\text{B}$ parameter size**. Below this threshold, or if not trained from scratch with the specific quantization-aware training procedure described (using `absmean` quantization and ternary constraints), performance may degrade. The "recipe" implies that if your goal is maximum capability within a strict inference budget, you should aim for a larger BitNet architecture because its lower inference cost allows you to scale up parameters until you hit the same cost ceiling as a smaller FP model would have hit earlier.

Finally, regarding Chinchua's optimal compute-to-data ratio: The paper does not explicitly redefine Chinchua's training scaling laws regarding the ratio of training tokens to parameters ($C$ vs $N$). Instead, it shifts the paradigm for **deployment and inference scaling**. Chinchua's law suggests an optimal balance between data and compute during *training* to maximize performance per FLOP spent training. BitNet b1.58 changes the economics of *inference*, allowing models trained with optimal Chinchua ratios to be deployed at scales previously inaccessible due to hardware constraints. The "new scaling law" here is essentially an inference-time Pareto improvement: it decouples parameter count from inference cost so effectively that a larger model becomes cheaper to run than a smaller one was previously possible, enabling us to utilize larger models within fixed energy or latency budgets without violating training optimality principles.
