\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

### How does adding the value '0' to ternary weights result in exactly 1.58 bits of information?
Question:
The paper introduces \textbf{BitNet b1.58} and states that by adding an additional value of 0 to the original 1-bit BitNet (which used \{-1, 1\}), the model now has ternary weights \{-1, 0, 1\}, resulting in "1.58 bits". 

I understand that \log_2(2) = 1 bit for binary values, but I am confused about the calculation for ternary values. Is the "1.58" derived from \log_2(3)? If so, why is this metric referred to as "bits" in the title and throughout the paper when it represents a non-integer number of bits? Does this imply that the storage is actually fractional per parameter, or is this a theoretical information density metric?
Answer:
The "1.58" in **BitNet b1.58** is indeed derived directly from the logarithm base 2 of the number of possible weight values, specifically $\log_2(3) \approx 1.585$. In information theory, the amount of information required to represent one of $N$ equally likely states is defined as $\log_2(N)$ bits. The original BitNet architecture used binary weights $\{-1, 1\}$, which offers $N=2$ states, resulting in exactly $\log_2(2) = 1$ bit per parameter. By introducing the value $0$ to create a ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the number of distinct states increases to $N=3$, and the theoretical information density becomes $\log_2(3) \approx 1.58$ bits per parameter.

This metric is referred to as "bits" because it represents the **theoretical information capacity** or entropy of a single weight parameter, not necessarily the physical storage size in bytes or bits on a hard drive. In practice, you cannot store a single parameter using a fractional number of physical bits (e.g., you cannot allocate exactly $1.58$ bits of memory to one weight); instead, this value serves as a normalized measure to compare the efficiency and complexity of different quantization schemes against standard floating-point formats like FP16 (which uses $16$ bits). The paper uses this nomenclature to highlight that while the model moves beyond strict binary ($1$-bit) constraints by adding zero for feature filtering capabilities, it remains significantly more compact than standard low-precision models like $4$-bit or $8$-bit quantized networks. The "$1.58$-bit" label effectively communicates that the model's weight precision is roughly halfway between binary and ternary in terms of information content, yet it retains the computational advantages of integer-only arithmetic (specifically integer addition) described in the paper's discussion on energy and latency improvements.

### How does the 'absmean' quantization function behave during backpropagation if weights are hard-clipped?
Question:
In Section 2, the paper defines the quantization function for weights as:

$$ \widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1) $$

where $\gamma$ is the average absolute value of $W$. The text mentions that this constrains weights to \{-1, 0, +1\}. 

My confusion lies in the training process. Since $\mathrm{RoundClip}$ involves rounding and clipping operations which are non-differentiable (the gradient is zero almost everywhere), how does the model learn? Is a Straight-Through Estimator (STE) being used here where gradients pass through unmodified? If so, how does the scaling factor $\gamma$, which depends on $W$, affect the gradient flow? Specifically, since $\gamma$ changes as $W$ updates, does it introduce a dynamic scaling issue during training that isn't explicitly discussed?
Answer:
The model learns during training by employing a **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**, a standard technique in quantization-aware training that allows gradients to bypass the non-differentiable `RoundClip` operation. Although the forward pass strictly constrains weights to the ternary set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, the backward pass treats the quantization function as an identity mapping (or a linear pass-through) for the gradient flow. This means that while the forward computation uses $\widetilde{W}$, the gradients $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W}$ are calculated as if $W$ were continuous, allowing the underlying full-precision weights $W$ to be updated via standard gradient descent.

Regarding your concern about the dynamic scaling factor $\gamma$, which is defined as the average absolute value of $W$ ($\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$), this term is indeed differentiable and plays a crucial role in stabilizing training. Since $\gamma$ is computed directly from $W$, it acts as a learnable normalization factor that adapts to the magnitude of the weights at every step. During backpropagation, the chain rule applies to $\gamma$: as $W$ updates, $\gamma$ changes, and this change propagates back into the gradient calculation for $W$. This creates a feedback loop where the model learns not only the direction of each weight but also an appropriate global scale such that, when divided by $\gamma$, most weights fall within the range $[-1, 1]$ and round effectively to non-zero ternary values. This mechanism prevents "dynamic scaling issues" because $\gamma$ is not a fixed hyperparameter but an intrinsic part of the optimization landscape that co-evolves with $W$, ensuring that the quantization remains effective throughout training without requiring manual intervention or explicit discussion of scaling instability in the paper.

### Why do activations remain at 8-bit precision while weights are quantized to 1.58-bit?
Question:
The abstract and Section 2 state that \textbf{BitNet b1.58} has "every single parameter (or weight)... ternary" but also mentions it uses "8-bit activations".

If the goal is to create a "new era of 1-bit LLMs" and maximize energy savings by eliminating floating-point multiplication, why are activations not also quantized to a lower bit-width (e.g., INT4 or even ternary)? The paper mentions that matrix multiplication cost comes from floating-point operations. If activations are still INT8 (or FP8), doesn't this mean we are still performing mixed-precision arithmetic that might limit the theoretical speedup claimed for pure integer addition? Is there a specific reason why lowering activation precision below 8 bits causes performance collapse in this architecture?
Answer:
The decision to keep activations at 8-bit precision while quantizing weights to 1.58-bit (ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$) is a deliberate trade-off between computational efficiency and model representational capacity, driven by the specific way BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch. While the primary goal is to eliminate floating-point multiplications in the matrix multiplication layer ($Wx$), reducing activation precision below 8 bits would likely cause a catastrophic collapse in model performance because activations carry the high-dimensional, continuous feature representations necessary for the model to learn complex language patterns.

In the BitNet architecture, the weights are constrained to ternary values to transform the matrix multiplication operation into a series of integer additions and subtractions, effectively removing the energy-intensive floating-point multipliers from the critical path. However, the activations (the input vector $x$) must retain sufficient dynamic range and precision to capture the subtle variations in data as it propagates through the network layers. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58 is trained from scratch with this specific configuration (1.58-bit weights and 8-bit activations) and successfully matches full-precision baselines starting from a 3B parameter size. This suggests that during training, the model learns to distribute information such that 8-bit precision is sufficient for activations without degrading perplexity or end-task performance. If activations were quantized too aggressively (e.g., to ternary or INT4), the quantization noise would accumulate rapidly across layers, preventing the model from converging to a useful solution or matching full-precision baselines.

Furthermore, there is a practical hardware and implementation rationale for this choice. The paper notes that scaling activations to a symmetric range $[-Q_b, Q_b]$ per token simplifies system-level optimization by removing zero-point quantization overheads. While INT8 arithmetic still requires some hardware support, modern GPUs and CPUs have highly optimized native support for INT8 operations, making them significantly faster and more energy-efficient than FP16 or BF16 while avoiding the complexity of custom kernels required for lower-bit integer arithmetic on current general-purpose hardware. The paper acknowledges in its "Discussion" section that further lossless compression of activations to 4 bits or lower is possible but leaves it as future work, implying that while theoretically desirable for maximum efficiency, it currently presents an unsolved challenge for maintaining performance parity with full-precision models in this specific architecture. Thus, 8-bit activations serve as a "sweet spot" that preserves the massive energy savings of ternary weights (which dominate memory footprint and computation count) while ensuring the numerical stability required for high-quality language modeling.

### Clarification on 'feature filtering' and how zero-weights improve modeling capability
Question:
The paper claims: "its modeling capability is stronger due to its explicit support for feature filtering, made possible by the inclusion of 0 in the model weights".

Conceptually, I understand that setting a weight to zero effectively removes a connection (similar to dropout or pruning). However, in standard full-precision models (FP16), weights can naturally converge to very small values close to zero without being exactly zero.

Why is having *exactly* zero beneficial compared to having very small floating-point numbers? Does this force a form of hard sparsity that regularizes the model differently than L2 regularization or dropout? Furthermore, if we can prune connections post-training in FP models, why is it necessary to enforce this sparsity *during* training with a hard constraint?
Answer:
The benefit of having *exactly* zero weights in BitNet b1.58, as opposed to weights that merely converge to very small floating-point values, stems from the fundamental difference between **soft suppression** and **hard feature filtering**. In standard full-precision models (FP16), a weight like $10^{-7}$ is mathematically non-zero; it still participates in the matrix multiplication operation, contributing a tiny but non-vanishing signal to the output. While this value is negligible in magnitude, it still requires the hardware to perform a multiplication and an addition, consuming energy and bandwidth.

In contrast, the ternary constraint $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ in BitNet b1.58 enforces **hard sparsity** where a weight of exactly $0$ completely removes the connection between a specific input feature and an output neuron. This acts as an explicit "feature filter," allowing the model to deterministically ignore irrelevant inputs rather than just dampening them. The paper argues that this explicit support for feature filtering strengthens the modeling capability because it forces the optimization process to learn a more robust and discrete representation of which features are truly necessary for a task, rather than relying on the continuous spectrum of small values that can accumulate noise or require complex regularization (like L2) to manage.

Regarding your question on why this sparsity must be enforced *during* training rather than applied via post-training pruning: post-training pruning is an approximation that occurs after the model has already learned its parameters in full precision. It often requires retraining or fine-tuning to recover performance lost during the aggressive removal of connections, and it does not guarantee that the remaining network structure is optimal for the quantized domain. By enforcing the $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ constraint from scratch during training, BitNet b1.58 learns a representation that is natively adapted to this sparse topology. The gradients flow through a system where "zero" is a distinct state with no computational cost (in ideal hardware) and no numerical noise, leading to a model that achieves performance matching FP16 baselines while inheriting the massive efficiency gains of integer-only addition for inference. Essentially, hard sparsity during training allows the model to discover a more efficient "wiring diagram" that soft sparsity or post-hoc pruning cannot easily replicate without significant performance degradation or additional tuning overhead.

### How does BitNet b1.58 handle Rotary Embeddings (RoPE) with ternary weights?
Question:
Section 2 mentions that BitNet b1.58 adopts LLaMA-alike components, including rotary embedding (RoPE). RoPE typically involves multiplying token embeddings by rotation matrices containing sine and cosine values.

If all linear layers ($\text{nn.Linear}$) are replaced with $\text{BitLinear}$ using ternary weights \{-1, 0, 1\}, how are these rotation matrices implemented? Are they computed in full precision and then applied as an operation before/after the ternary matrix multiplication? Or are they also quantized? If they remain full precision, does this reintroduce floating-point multiplication costs that would negate some of the energy savings claimed for the matrix multiplication layers?
Answer:
In BitNet b1.58, Rotary Embeddings (RoPE) are implemented using full-precision floating-point arithmetic and are **not** quantized to ternary weights, nor are they replaced by a ternary matrix multiplication.

The paper explicitly states in Section 2 that BitNet b1.58 adopts "LLaMA-alike components," which includes RoPE, but clarifies that the quantization to ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ applies specifically to the parameters of the linear layers (replacing `nn.Linear` with `BitLinear`). RoPE is fundamentally a positional encoding mechanism that involves element-wise multiplication of token embeddings by rotation matrices containing sine and cosine values ($\sin(\theta)$ and $\cos(\theta)$). These rotation matrices are not learnable parameters; they are deterministic functions of the token position and dimension index. Consequently, they do not undergo the `absmean` quantization process described in Equation 1, which is reserved for the weight matrices $W$ of the linear projections.

Regarding your concern about energy savings, the full-precision computation required for RoPE does not negate the efficiency gains of BitNet b1.58 because RoPE operations constitute a negligible fraction of the total computational cost compared to the massive matrix multiplications in the feed-forward networks (FFN) and attention projections. As detailed in the "Energy" subsection of Section 3, the paper estimates that matrix multiplication contributes the vast majority of energy consumption in LLMs. While RoPE involves floating-point multiplications, these are applied element-wise to vectors (complexity $O(d)$ per token), whereas the quantized linear layers involve matrix-vector multiplications with dimensions scaling with hidden size and intermediate size (complexity $O(d^2)$ or higher). The paper notes that BitNet b1.58 achieves a 71.4x reduction in arithmetic operation energy for matrix multiplication specifically because it replaces FP16 multiplications with integer additions. Since RoPE remains a small overhead relative to these dominant matrix operations, retaining it in full precision allows the model to maintain compatibility with LLaMA's architecture and performance characteristics without significantly impacting the overall energy or latency benefits derived from quantizing the bulk of the model's parameters.

### Is the 'new scaling law' based on parameter count or effective compute efficiency?
Question:
In Section 3, under 'Throughput', the authors propose a new scaling law equivalence:
- "70B BitNet b1.58 is more efficient... than 13B FP16 LLM"

This suggests that a much larger model in terms of parameter count (70B vs 13B) can be run more efficiently than a smaller full-precision model.

I am confused about what exactly constitutes this "scaling law." Is it implying that we should train larger models specifically because they become cheaper to run due to quantization? Or is it suggesting that for a fixed compute budget (energy/time), we can now afford significantly larger parameter counts?

Specifically, if I have a fixed GPU memory budget of X GB:
- Can I fit more parameters with BitNet?
- Does this mean I should train a larger BitNet model rather than optimizing hyperparameters for an FP model?
How does this change our understanding of Chinchua's optimal compute-to-data ratio?
Answer:
The "new scaling law" proposed in the paper is fundamentally based on **effective compute efficiency** (specifically inference throughput, latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption) rather than a change in the theoretical relationship between parameter count and model performance. The authors are not suggesting that we should train larger models *because* they become cheaper to run in isolation; rather, they are demonstrating that for a fixed hardware budget (e.g., a specific GPU memory limit or energy cap), you can now deploy a model with significantly more parameters using BitNet b1.58 than you could with a full-precision (FP16) model while achieving superior or equivalent performance.

The core mechanism driving this shift is the drastic reduction in the cost of the dominant operation in LLMs: matrix multiplication. In standard FP16 models, this requires expensive floating-point multiplications and additions, whereas BitNet b1.58 replaces these with integer additions because the weights are ternary $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. As stated in the paper, this reduces arithmetic energy consumption by approximately $71.4$ times on $7\text{nm}$ chips and significantly lowers memory bandwidth requirements. Consequently, the "scaling law" equivalence you cited—where a $70\text{B}$ BitNet model is more efficient than a $13\text{B}$ FP16 model—means that the **inference cost** (time, memory, energy) of running the massive $70\text{B}$ quantized model is lower than running the smaller $13\text{B}$ full-precision model.

Regarding your specific scenario with a fixed GPU memory budget $X$ GB: **Yes, you can fit significantly more parameters with BitNet.** Since BitNet b1.58 uses ternary weights (requiring roughly $2$ bits per weight effectively) compared to FP16 ($16$ bits), and considering the paper notes that activations are also scaled down to $8$ bits (with potential for further compression), the memory footprint for weights is drastically reduced. This allows you to load a much larger parameter count into the same $X$ GB of VRAM. For instance, the paper notes that BitNet b1.58 $70\text{B}$ can support up to $11$ times the batch size of LLaMA $70\text{B}$ on two A100 cards due to this memory efficiency.

However, this does not necessarily mean you should abandon hyperparameter optimization for FP models in favor of blindly training larger BitNets without consideration. The paper explicitly states that BitNet b1.58 matches full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task performance starting from a **$3\text{B}$ parameter size**. Below this threshold, or if not trained from scratch with the specific quantization-aware training procedure described (using `absmean` quantization and ternary constraints), performance may degrade. The "recipe" implies that if your goal is maximum capability within a strict inference budget, you should aim for a larger BitNet architecture because its lower inference cost allows you to scale up parameters until you hit the same cost ceiling as a smaller FP model would have hit earlier.

Finally, regarding Chinchua's optimal compute-to-data ratio: The paper does not explicitly redefine Chinchua's training scaling laws regarding the ratio of training tokens to parameters ($C$ vs $N$). Instead, it shifts the paradigm for **deployment and inference scaling**. Chinchua's law suggests an optimal balance between data and compute during *training* to maximize performance per FLOP spent training. BitNet b1.58 changes the economics of *inference*, allowing models trained with optimal Chinchua ratios to be deployed at scales previously inaccessible due to hardware constraints. The "new scaling law" here is essentially an inference-time Pareto improvement: it decouples parameter count from inference cost so effectively that a larger model becomes cheaper to run than a smaller one was previously possible, enabling us to utilize larger models within fixed energy or latency budgets without violating training optimality principles.
