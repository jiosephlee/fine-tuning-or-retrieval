\title{A Textbook about the Paper: "The Era of 1-bit LLMs: All Large Language Models are in 1.58 Bits"}

Chapter 3: Chapter 3: The Computational Paradigm Shift

The fundamental architecture of modern Large Language Models (LLMs) has long been dominated by floating-point arithmetic, specifically 16-bit formats such as FP16 or BF16. In these standard configurations, the computational bottleneck is the matrix multiplication operation, which relies heavily on floating-point addition and multiplication. These operations are energy-intensive and impose significant constraints on latency and throughput. The introduction of BitNet b1.58 represents a radical departure from this norm by proposing a new computational paradigm where every single parameter in the model is ternary, taking values strictly from the set $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This shift is not merely a reduction in precision but a structural transformation of how computation is performed. By replacing complex floating-point multiplications with simple integer additions, the model fundamentally alters the cost structure of inference. The transition to this ternary system allows for a computation process that requires almost no multiplication operations for matrix multiplication, thereby unlocking orders of magnitude in energy savings and speed improvements. This chapter delves into the mathematical underpinnings of this shift, the specific role of the zero weight in enhancing model capability, and the profound implications for hardware design and scaling laws.

# From FP16 Multiplication to INT8 Addition: The math behind the speedup

To understand the magnitude of the speedup achieved by BitNet b1.58, one must first analyze the arithmetic complexity of standard LLM inference compared to the ternary approach. In a conventional full-precision Transformer, such as LLaMA, the core operation is matrix multiplication between weights $W$ and activations $x$. Mathematically, this involves computing $y = Wx$, where each element of $y$ is a sum of products: $y_i = \sum_j W_{ij} x_j$. In FP16 or BF16 formats, each term $W_{ij} x_j$ requires a floating-point multiplication followed by an addition. Floating-point units (FPUs) are complex circuits that consume significant power to handle exponent normalization and mantissa alignment for every single operation. As model sizes grow into billions or trillions of parameters, these billions of multiplications become the primary driver of latency and energy consumption.

In contrast, BitNet b1.58 replaces these floating-point weights with ternary values $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. When a weight $W_{ij}$ is multiplied by an activation $x_j$, the operation simplifies drastically based on the value of $W_{ij}$. If $W_{ij}$ is $0$, the product is zero; if it is $+1$, the product is simply $x_j$; and if it is $-1$, the product is $-x_j$. Consequently, the complex multiplication operation vanishes entirely from the critical path of matrix multiplication. The computation reduces to conditional integer additions or subtractions. Specifically, for a given output neuron, one simply sums all activations corresponding to positive weights and subtracts all activations corresponding to negative weights; activations corresponding to zero weights are ignored entirely. This transformation converts a sequence of expensive floating-point multiply-accumulate (FMA) operations into a sequence of integer additions (INT8). Since integer adders are significantly simpler and faster than floating-point multipliers in hardware implementation, this mathematical simplification translates directly into massive gains in computational efficiency. The paper notes that this change allows BitNet b1.58 to be significantly more cost-effective in terms of latency and throughput compared to its FP16 counterparts, effectively decoupling performance from the high energy cost traditionally associated with deep learning inference.

# The role of '0' in weights: Explicit feature filtering and sparsity benefits

While earlier 1-bit models utilized binary weights $\{-1, +1\}$, BitNet b1.58 introduces a critical innovation by including zero as a valid weight value, creating a ternary system $\{-1, 0, 1\}$. This inclusion is not merely an expansion of the value range but serves a distinct functional purpose: explicit feature filtering. In binary models where every weight must be either positive or negative, every input feature contributes to every output neuron with some magnitude; there is no mechanism within the weight layer itself to completely ignore specific input features for specific outputs without relying on post-hoc masking or complex activation functions. By allowing weights to be exactly zero, BitNet b1.58 gains the ability to explicitly prune connections during training and inference. If a weight $W_{ij}$ converges to zero during training, it signifies that input feature $j$ provides no useful signal for output neuron $i$, effectively filtering out that feature from contributing to that specific computation path.

This explicit sparsity mechanism enhances the modeling capability of 1-bit LLMs significantly. The ability to set weights to zero allows the model to learn more efficient representations by focusing only on relevant features for each task or context window. This "feature filtering" capability addresses a potential limitation of pure binary models where noise from irrelevant features might accumulate due to forced non-zero connections. Empirical results presented in the research indicate that this modification allows BitNet b1.58 to match full-precision baselines in perplexity and end-task performance starting from a 3B parameter size, whereas previous binary approaches struggled at similar scales without extensive architectural tweaks or larger model sizes. Furthermore, this sparsity interacts beneficially with memory bandwidth; since zero weights do not require data movement or processing during inference (as they contribute nothing to the sum), they effectively reduce both memory footprint and computational load simultaneously. The quantization function used to achieve this involves scaling weights by their average absolute value $\gamma = \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{ij} |W_{ij}|$ before rounding and clipping them to $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ via $\widetilde{W} = \mathrm{RoundClip}(\frac{W}{\gamma + \epsilon}, -1, 1)$, ensuring that the distribution of values naturally converges toward this sparse ternary set while preserving signal integrity through scaling factors like $\gamma + \epsilon$.

# Hardware implications: Reducing power consumption on 7nm chips

The shift from floating-point arithmetic to integer addition has profound implications for hardware design and power consumption, particularly at advanced manufacturing nodes like 7nm chips where power density is a limiting factor for compute performance. In standard LLM inference on GPUs or TPUs using FP16 precision, energy consumption is dominated by two factors: memory access (moving data from DRAM to SRAM) and arithmetic operations (specifically floating-point multiplications). While reducing memory footprint helps with data transfer costs, BitNet b1.58 offers an even more dramatic reduction in arithmetic energy costs because it eliminates multiplications entirely from matrix multiplication layers. On modern silicon architectures like those found in NVIDIA A100s or custom accelerators on 7nm processes, floating-point multipliers are among the most power-hungry components due to their circuit complexity involving carry-save adders and normalization logic required for handling exponents and mantissas.

By replacing these operations with simple integer additions (INT8), BitNet b1.58 leverages hardware paths that are orders of magnitude more energy-efficient according to established energy models such as those cited in *energycost* and *pokebnn*. The research estimates that for matrix multiplication on 7nm chips alone—excluding memory transfer overhead—BitNet b1.58 saves approximately 71.4 times more arithmetic operation energy compared to FP16 LLaMA baselines.
