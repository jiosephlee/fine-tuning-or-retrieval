# Chapter 1: Foundations of Tabular Learning and the LLM Paradigm Shift

## The Unique Characteristics and Challenges of Tabular Data vs. NLP/CV

Tabular data represents the backbone of countless real-world applications, ranging from financial risk assessment and medical diagnosis to customer churn prediction. Unlike images or natural language text, which possess inherent spatial or sequential structures that deep learning architectures like Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Transformers exploit naturally, tabular data is characterized by its heterogeneity. A single table may contain continuous numerical values, discrete integers, ordinal categories, and nominal strings, all co-existing within rows that share a uniform schema but lack the dense interdependencies found in pixels or word embeddings. This structural diversity creates a fundamental challenge: there is no universally applicable data augmentation strategy or architectural inductive bias that performs well across all tabular domains. While image data benefits from rotation or cropping, and text data leverages synonym replacement, finding meaningful transformations for tabular columns—where changing a "blood pressure" value slightly might be valid but changing a "gender" category is nonsensical—is notoriously difficult.

Furthermore, the scarcity of labeled data exacerbates these challenges in modern machine learning workflows. In many professional settings, acquiring labeled examples is prohibitively expensive due to the need for expert annotation or privacy regulations. This necessity gives rise to the few-shot learning regime, where models must generalize effectively from a handful of examples. In this low-data environment, traditional deep learning models often fail because they tend to overfit quickly to spurious correlations present in the tiny training set rather than learning the true underlying patterns. The rigid structure of tabular data means that without sufficient samples to establish statistical significance for every feature interaction, models struggle to distinguish signal from noise. Consequently, the field has sought ways to inject external knowledge into these systems to act as a regularizer or a source of prior belief, compensating for the paucity of observed training instances.

## Overview of Few-Shot Learning Regimes in Machine Learning

Few-shot learning refers to the paradigm where a model is tasked with solving a problem given only a very small number of labeled examples, often denoted as $k$-shot learning where $k$ is typically less than 10 or 20. The core difficulty lies in avoiding the trap of spurious correlations; with limited data, a model might accidentally latch onto an irrelevant feature that happens to align perfectly with the target label in the few available samples but fails catastrophically on new data. Historically, few-shot learning research began primarily in computer vision and natural language processing, leveraging massive pre-trained foundation models that had already learned rich semantic representations from vast amounts of unlabeled data. These models could be adapted to new tasks with minimal fine-tuning or even zero-shot prompting because their internal weights encoded general world knowledge applicable across domains.

In the context of tabular data, however, few-shot learning has historically lagged behind other modalities due to the lack of universal pre-training objectives that transfer well across disparate schemas. Early attempts focused on semi-supervised learning strategies that utilized large quantities of unlabeled tabular data alongside scarce labeled samples. Techniques involved masking parts of the table and reconstructing them (similar to BERT's masked language modeling) or using contrastive learning objectives where augmented views of the same row are pulled closer together in latent space. While these methods provide some regularization by forcing the model to learn robust representations consistent with the marginal distribution of features, they have shown limited efficacy in extreme few-shot settings where even unlabeled data might not be abundant enough to overcome the curse of dimensionality inherent in high-dimensional tables with hundreds of features. The reliance on purely statistical patterns derived from data alone leaves these models vulnerable when $k$ is extremely small.

## Traditional Baselines: Logistic Regression and Tree Ensembles

When evaluating performance on tabular tasks with limited data, several classical machine learning algorithms serve as robust baselines against which newer methods must compete. Among these, Logistic Regression remains a cornerstone due to its simplicity and interpretability. It operates by fitting a linear decision boundary (or boundaries) between classes using maximum likelihood estimation via gradient descent variants like Adam. Despite its linearity assumption regarding input features, logistic regression often performs surprisingly well in low-shot regimes because its low capacity acts as an implicit regularizer; it simply cannot overfit easily unless explicitly forced by high-degree polynomial feature expansions or insufficient regularization strength like L2 penalties. Its primary weakness lies in its inability to capture complex non-linear interactions between features without manual feature engineering by human experts who possess deep domain knowledge—a resource often unavailable in automated pipelines.

Complementing linear models are tree-based ensemble methods such as Random Forests and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (specifically XGBoost). These algorithms construct decision trees that recursively partition the feature space based on splitting criteria like Gini impurity or information gain. Random Forests build an ensemble of decorrelated trees by bootstrapping samples and selecting random subsets of features at each split, thereby reducing variance through averaging predictions across many trees. XGBoost takes a boosting approach sequentially adding trees that correct errors made by previous ones using gradient optimization techniques optimized for speed and performance metrics like Area Under the Curve (AUC). Both methods excel at handling heterogeneous feature types automatically and capturing non-linear relationships without extensive preprocessing. However, in strict few-shot scenarios with very small $k$, tree-based methods can suffer from instability; slight variations in which few samples are selected can lead to drastically different tree structures and poor generalization if the splits rely on coincidental patterns rather than robust signals inherent in the population distribution they are meant to represent.

## Pre-trained Tabular Models: TabPFN and Transfer Learning Approaches

Recognizing the limitations of training from scratch on small datasets, researchers developed pre-trained tabular models designed to transfer knowledge acquired from thousands of diverse tables down-stream tasks with minimal labeled examples. A prominent example is TabPFN (Tabular Probabilistic Framework Network), which employs a Bayesian neural network architecture pre-trained on millions synthetically generated datasets covering various distributions and complexities. # Chapter 2: The FeatLLM Framework Architecture

The landscape of applying Large Language Models (LLMs) to tabular data has traditionally focused on treating the model as a direct predictor. In these conventional end-to-end approaches, every single data sample must be serialized into text and fed into the LLM during inference to obtain a prediction. While effective in demonstrating the reasoning capabilities of these models, this paradigm introduces significant bottlenecks regarding computational cost, latency, and dependency on the model's context window limits. The \textsf{FeatLLM} framework represents a fundamental architectural departure from this status quo. Rather than asking the LLM to predict the outcome for each row of a table individually, \textsf{FeatLLM} reassigns the role of the LLM to that of an automated feature engineer. In this capacity, the model is tasked with analyzing the problem description, feature definitions, and a small set of few-shot examples to distill the underlying logic of the task into explicit decision rules. These rules are not merely suggestions but serve as the blueprint for constructing entirely new features that capture the essence of what distinguishes one class from another. By shifting the heavy lifting of reasoning to a one-time feature generation phase, \textsf{FeatLLM} decouples the complex cognitive process from the repetitive act of prediction, paving the way for a system that leverages the vast prior knowledge of LLMs while retaining the speed and simplicity of classical machine learning algorithms at deployment time.

This architectural shift is predicated on the insight that LLMs possess rich prior knowledge about domain relationships—such as medical risks associated with age or financial indicators linked to creditworthiness—which can be formalized into deterministic logic before any actual prediction occurs. Instead of relying on the LLM's internal weights to implicitly map input vectors to output classes during every query, \textsf{FeatLLM} explicitly extracts these mappings as "rules." Once these rules are identified, they are translated into executable code that transforms raw tabular data into binary features indicating whether a specific condition is met. The final prediction is then delegated to a simple downstream model, such as a linear regression or logistic regression variant, which learns to weigh these newly engineered binary features. This separation allows \textsf{FeatLLM} to bypass the need for sending thousands or millions of individual queries to an expensive API during inference. Instead, it requires only API-level access for the initial feature engineering phase, making it feasible even for proprietary models where training code or fine-tuning capabilities are unavailable. The result is a framework that maintains high performance in few-shot regimes while offering inference speeds comparable to traditional statistical models and eliminating the prohibitive costs associated with per-sample LLM invocation.

# Core Philosophy: LLMs as Feature Engineers rather than End-to-End Predictors

The foundational philosophy behind \textsf{FeatLLM} challenges the prevailing assumption that an LLM must serve as the final decision-making engine in tabular learning tasks. In standard end-to-end pipelines utilizing in-context learning or parameter-efficient fine-tuning, the LLM acts as a black box that consumes serialized data rows and outputs a class label directly. This approach inherently couples every inference step with an interaction with the massive language model, creating a scenario where computational expense scales linearly with the number of samples being predicted. Furthermore, this method struggles when faced with datasets containing hundreds of features, as serializing all columns into text often exceeds the token limits imposed by current API interfaces or degrades model performance due to context dilution. \textsf{FeatLLM} posits that these limitations stem not from a lack of intelligence in smaller models or insufficient data in larger ones, but from an inefficient allocation of resources: using a heavyweight reasoning engine for repetitive pattern matching rather than strategic rule discovery.

By reframing the LLM as a feature engineer, \textsf{FeatLLM} leverages what Large Language Models do best: synthesizing abstract concepts and logical relationships from natural language descriptions and sparse examples. The framework operates on the premise that if an expert human were asked to solve a tabular classification problem with limited data, they would first analyze feature definitions and known domain facts to hypothesize conditions under which certain outcomes occur (e.g., "If blood pressure is above 140 and cholesterol is high, then heart failure risk increases"). They would then codify these hypotheses into checkable criteria before applying them systematically to new patients. \textsf{FeatLLM} automates this exact cognitive workflow. It instructs the LLM to ignore raw numerical patterns initially and instead focus on inferring causal tendencies or strong correlations between features and targets based on general world knowledge encoded in its pre-training corpus. Only after establishing these theoretical relationships does it refine them against provided few-shot examples to generate concrete decision rules. This process filters out noise and spurious correlations early in the pipeline, ensuring that only semantically meaningful conditions proceed to become features for downstream modeling.

This philosophical pivot fundamentally alters how prior knowledge is integrated into tabular learning systems. Traditional methods attempt to inject knowledge by simply appending text descriptions alongside data points within a prompt hoping for implicit attention mechanisms to align them correctly; however, this often fails when data volume overwhelms context windows or when subtle distinctions require deeper logical deduction than attention heads alone can provide without fine-tuning. In contrast, \textsf{FeatLLM} forces an explicit articulation of knowledge through rule generation. The LLM is guided through a structured reasoning process where it first identifies potential causes without looking at specific examples (to rely purely on prior knowledge) and then validates or refines these causes using few-shot demonstrations (to incorporate empirical evidence). This two-stage inference mimics scientific hypothesis testing: forming theories based on background theory and then verifying them against experimental observations. Consequently, even if an individual rule generated by an LLM contains minor errors due to hallucination or misinterpretation—a known risk in generative AI—the subsequent stages of parsing and ensemble aggregation allow robustness mechanisms like bagging to mitigate these failures without compromising overall system integrity. Thus, treating LLMs as feature engineers maximizes their unique strengths in semantic understanding while offloading mechanical computation tasks faster specialized tools can handle efficiently at scale.

# Chapter 3: Prompt Engineering for Rule Extraction

## Introduction to Prompt Architecture in FeatLLM

The core innovation of the \textsf{FeatLLM} framework lies not merely in the application of a Large Language Model (LLM) to tabular data, but in the precise architectural design of the prompts that guide the model's behavior. Unlike traditional approaches that treat LLMs as black-box predictors requiring end-to-end inference for every single sample, \textsf{FeatLLM} repositions the LLM as an automated feature engineer. Its primary objective is to distill the "criteria" or underlying logic that determines class membership from a small set of labeled examples. To achieve this, the system must compel the LLM to move beyond pattern matching and engage in structured reasoning that mimics an expert human analyst. This chapter details the tripartite structure of the prompt design employed in \textsf{FeatLLM}, which is meticulously crafted to extract high-quality, interpretable rules. These rules serve as the foundation for generating new binary features, which are subsequently fed into a lightweight downstream model. The success of this pipeline depends entirely on how effectively the prompt orchestrates three distinct phases: providing basic contextual information, guiding a two-step reasoning process to isolate causal relationships, and enforcing strict formatting constraints to ensure deterministic parsing.

# Component 1: Basic Information Description (Task Framing and Feature Serialization)

The first critical component of the prompt architecture is the provision of "Basic Information," which serves as the foundational context for all subsequent reasoning. This section acts as the interface between raw tabular data and the linguistic capabilities of the LLM, ensuring that the model possesses all necessary metadata before attempting any analysis. In \textsf{FeatLLM}, this component is explicitly divided into three elements: a clear task description, detailed feature definitions, and serialized example demonstrations. The task description is framed as a direct question to anchor the model's attention on the specific prediction goal. For instance, rather than simply stating "predict heart failure," the prompt asks, "Does this patient's myocardial infarction complications data indicate chronic heart failure? Yes or no?" This interrogative format primes the LLM to seek a definitive binary or categorical answer, aligning its internal state with a classification objective rather than open-ended generation.

Following the task definition, the prompt includes rigorous descriptions of each feature present in the dataset. Since tabular data often contains heterogeneous variable types—ranging from continuous numerical values to discrete categorical labels—the LLM must be informed about these characteristics to avoid logical errors during rule construction. The feature description specifies the value type and may optionally include domain-specific definitions derived from external knowledge bases or dataset documentation. For categorical variables, it is particularly important to list possible categories within this section so that any generated rules reference valid states rather than hallucinated values. This explicit grounding prevents type mismatches later in the process and ensures that any condition derived by the model is semantically sound relative to the actual data schema available in real-world applications like healthcare or finance.

The third element of this component involves "serialization," which converts selected training samples into natural language text for inclusion in few-shot demonstrations. Because LLMs operate on token sequences rather than structured matrices, rows and columns must be flattened into readable sentences without losing informational integrity. \textsf{FeatLLM} adopts a standardized serialization format consistent with prior literature on language-interfaced tabular learning. Mathematically, given a sample $\mathbf{x}^i$ with $d$ dimensions and corresponding feature names $F = \{f_j\}_{j=1}^{d}$, along with its ground-truth label $\mathbf{y}^i$, the serialization function transforms this tuple into a textual narrative:
$$
\text{Serialize}(\mathbf{x}^i,\mathbf{y}^i, F) = "\ f_1\ \text{is}\ \mathbf{x}^i_1.\ ... \ f_d\  \text{is}\  \mathbf{x}^i_d.\ \ \text{Answer:}\  \mathbf{y}^i\ "
$$
This format creates a coherent story for each example demonstration (e.g., "Age is 65. Cholesterol is High... Answer: Yes"), allowing the LLM to observe concrete instances where specific feature combinations lead to specific outcomes. By embedding these serialized examples alongside task and feature descriptions, \textsf{FeatLLM} provides a complete microcosm of the problem domain within a single prompt window. This comprehensive setup ensures that when the model proceeds to generate rules, it does so with full awareness of what constitutes a valid input and what constitutes a correct output, laying a stable groundwork for complex reasoning tasks even in low-shot regimes where labeled data is scarce.

# Component 2: Reasoning Instructions (Chain-of-Thought Integration)

Once basic information is established, \textsf{FeatLLM} introduces "Reasoning Instructions" designed to enforce a logical progression similar to Chain-of-Thought prompting techniques used in advanced reasoning tasks. The central hypothesis driving this design is that immediate rule generation from raw data often leads models to latch onto spurious correlations—coincidental patterns present in small datasets but invalid generally—to satisfy their predictive drive instantly. To counteract this tendency toward superficial pattern recognition, \textsf{FeatLLM} decomposes the inference process into two distinct sequential steps within a single prompt interaction. This structural separation forces the LLM to pause its generative impulse and first construct an abstract understanding of causality before committing to concrete operational rules.

The first step of this guided reasoning process instructs the LLM to infer causal relationships or general tendencies between features and target classes without referencing any specific example demonstrations yet provided in few-shot mode. During this phase, relying solely on its vast pre-trained knowledge base and common sense reasoning capabilities stored within its parameters allows the model to activate relevant domain priors—for example, recognizing that older age generally correlates with higher cardiovascular risk regardless of specific patient records seen moments ago. By isolating this step from empirical evidence initially contained in few-shot examples, \textsf{FeatLLM} leverages external world knowledge encoded during pre-training while filtering out noise
