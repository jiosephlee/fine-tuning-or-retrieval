\title{Stack Exchange of the Paper: "Large Language Models Can Automatically Engineer Features for Few-Shot Tabular Learning"}

### Does FeatLLM violate data leakage constraints on datasets released after pre-training?
Question:
The paper evaluates FeatLLM on datasets like 'Cultivars' and 'NHANES', explicitly stating they were added to UCI after September 2023 and thus were not part of GPT-3.5's pre-training.

However, FeatLLM relies heavily on 'prior knowledge' extracted from the LLM (Section 1 and 3.1). If an LLM hasn't seen these specific datasets during training, how can it possibly infer valid causal relationships or rules for them based on 'prior knowledge' alone?

For example, if I ask an LLM about a specific soybean cultivar dataset it has never seen, how does it know which features are predictive without seeing examples? Is it relying purely on general agricultural knowledge (which might be generic), or is there an assumption that column names alone contain enough semantic information for zero-shot rule generation? This seems contradictory to their claim that they are extracting task-specific criteria.
Answer:
No, FeatLLM does not violate data leakage constraints on datasets released after pre-training, because the framework is explicitly designed to combine the LLM's general prior knowledge with specific information provided in the prompt, rather than relying solely on memorized dataset statistics.

The core of your concern lies in how an LLM can infer valid rules for a dataset it has never seen. The paper clarifies that FeatLLM operates through a two-step reasoning process within the prompt (Section 3.1). In the first step, the LLM is instructed to infer causal relationships or tendencies between features and the target based **only** on general world knowledge and common sense, without looking at any data examples. This leverages the semantic meaning embedded in column names and feature descriptions (e.g., knowing that "age" or "blood pressure" generally correlates with heart disease risk) which is part of the model's pre-training corpus. In the second step, the LLM uses this general reasoning alongside **few-shot examples** provided directly in the prompt to deduce specific rules for that particular dataset.

Crucially, for datasets like 'Cultivars' and 'NHANES' which were added to UCI after September 2023 (post-dating GPT-3.5's training), FeatLLM does not assume the LLM knows the specific data distribution or spurious correlations of those tables. Instead, it relies on the LLM's ability to understand domain concepts (e.g., agricultural factors affecting soybean yield or demographic variables affecting age groups) from its general training, and then refines these into actionable rules using the few labeled samples serialized in the prompt. The paper explicitly states that this approach allows the model to "utilize both prior knowledge and the knowledge from the data" (Introduction), meaning the specific task adaptation comes from the few-shot examples provided at inference time, not from memorization of the dataset itself. Furthermore, any rules generated that do not align with the actual data distribution are filtered out during the subsequent training phase of the simple linear model (Section 3.2), where weights are learned to maximize performance on the provided few-shot samples, effectively correcting any generic assumptions made by the LLM that do not hold for that specific unseen dataset.
