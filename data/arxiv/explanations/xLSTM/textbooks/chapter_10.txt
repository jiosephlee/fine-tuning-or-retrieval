\title{A Textbook about the Paper: xLSTM}

Chapter 10: Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocols

# Datasets: SlimPajama $15\mathrm{B}$ and $300\mathrm{B}$ tokens
All language-modeling experiments reported in the study use the SlimPajama corpus as the training corpus. Two different training-budget regimes are employed to probe behavior at medium and large data scales: a $15\mathrm{B}$-token regime used for broad method comparison and ablation studies, and a $300\mathrm{B}$-token regime used to evaluate xLSTM as a large language model (LLM). Models trained on the $15\mathrm{B}$ corpus are the primary basis for the controlled comparisons across many architectures (Transformers, State Space Models, RNNs and linear Transformers). The $300\mathrm{B}$ experiments are designed to show how xLSTM variants behave when trained with an LLM-sized data budget and to assess extrapolation, downstream task performance, and scaling laws at larger scale.

Evaluation of models trained on SlimPajama is reported primarily via next-token perplexity on a held-out validation set (referred to throughout as the validation perplexity), and via additional downstream and domain-specific evaluations described later. Where length extrapolation behavior is tested, models are trained on a fixed context length (typically $2048$ tokens) and evaluated at larger context lengths (up to $16384$ tokens) to measure generalization to longer contexts than seen during training.

# Model sizes (125M to $1.3\mathrm{B}$) and block configurations
The experiments examine multiple model sizes to study both absolute performance and scaling behavior. For the large-scale ($300\mathrm{B}$ training tokens) comparison the paper trains model sizes of $125\mathrm{M}$, $350\mathrm{M}$, $760\mathrm{M}$ and $1.3\mathrm{B}$ parameters. For the $15\mathrm{B}$-token method comparison, a family of models is constructed to match a GPT-3–style baseline at about $350\mathrm{M}$ parameters (embedding dimension $1024$ and $24$ residual blocks) so that methods are compared at a common scale.

xLSTM architectures are specified by the ratio of mLSTM-based to sLSTM-based blocks using the notation $xLSTM[a:b]$, where $a/b$ denotes the number of mLSTM blocks versus sLSTM blocks in the block mix. For example, $xLSTM[7:1]$ means seven mLSTM-based blocks for every one sLSTM-based block; when the total number of blocks is $48$, that corresponds to $42$ mLSTM-based blocks and $6$ sLSTM-based blocks. In experiments the authors report results for pure mLSTM ($xLSTM[1:0]$), pure sLSTM ($xLSTM[0:1]$), and mixed ratios such as $xLSTM[7:1]$ and $xLSTM[1:1]$ to probe the trade-offs between the matrix memory and scalar-memory-with-mixing designs.

# Training precision and implementation details
Training is performed with mixed-precision arithmetic in the majority of experiments. The paper notes that mixed-precision training was applied broadly, but for some alternative models (specifically RWKV-5, RWKV-6, GLA, and HGRN2) the mixed-precision training did not utilize PyTorch’s automated mixed precision (AMP) in the authors’ training setup; this detail is reported because it can affect numerical stability and wall-clock efficiency. For a subset of long runs (the $300\mathrm{B}$-token experiments), some recurrent-model variants (RWKV-5, RWKV-6, HGRN2) required adjustments to precision settings discovered only after training had already started, and consequently those particular runs are noted as having had training-precision issues.

Implementation-level distinctions between the two xLSTM memory types are important for runtime behavior and parallelism. The mLSTM variant uses a matrix memory $\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ with a covariance (outer-product) update and, crucially, contains no hidden--hidden recurrent mixing; this design allows a parallel formulation of the recurrence and makes mLSTM amenable to GPU parallelization. The sLSTM variant retains memory mixing via recurrent hidden-to-gate connections, and therefore is not intrinsically parallelizable across time; to mitigate this, the authors implemented a custom CUDA kernel for sLSTM with GPU memory optimizations down to register level, achieving a runtime that is typically less than two times slower than their parallel mLSTM implementation. Conversely, the current mLSTM CUDA implementation is not fully optimized and is reported to be about four times slower than highly optimized attention kernels such as FlashAttention; the authors indicate that further kernel-level optimization should reduce that gap.

Architecturally, xLSTM blocks are implemented in pre-LayerNorm residual backbones, following contemporary LLM design. For blocks that contain sLSTM cells the block uses a post up-projection residual formulation; for mLSTM-based blocks a pre up-projection residual formulation is used so that the larger matrix memory operates in the higher-dimensional projected space. The exact residual-block shapes and layer-normalization placements follow the descriptions given in the xLSTM block section.

# Synthetic tasks: formal languages and multi-query associative recall
The evaluation suite includes targeted synthetic tasks designed to probe specific capabilities of the memory and gating mechanisms.

Formal language tasks. To analyze state-tracking and the effect of exponential gating with memory mixing, the paper reimplements and extends formal-language tasks from prior work so they can be trained and evaluated under multi-length regimes (to permit length extrapolation analysis). Models are evaluated on the tokens that are relevant to the formal-language decision, and accuracy is scaled between $0$ (random) and $1$ (perfect). The comparisons use small architectures (two-block models) across many methods including pure sLSTM ($xLSTM[0:1]$), pure mLSTM ($xLSTM[1:0]$), mixed xLSTM ($xLSTM[1:1]$), Transformers (Llama), State Space Models (Mamba), and several RNN baselines (RWKV, LSTM). These tasks show that methods without memory mixing (standard Transformers and many SSMs) fail to solve certain state-tracking tasks, while sLSTM (with exponential gating and memory mixing) succeeds.

Multi-Query Associative Recall (MQAR). To probe raw memory capacity the MQAR task is employed and made substantially harder than in standard formulations: the number of stored key–value pairs is increased up to $256$, and the context length is expanded up to $2048$. Each sequence contains randomly chosen key–value pairs drawn from a large vocabulary; the model is required to store and later recall the correct value given a query. Retrieval accuracy on held-out queries is the evaluation metric. Transformers (with their implicit high-capacity associative encoding) are a strong baseline on MQAR; among non-Transformer models, $xLSTM[1:1]$ attains the best accuracy across a range of difficulty levels, and the inclusion of an sLSTM block does not reduce memory capacity — in fact, it can assist performance on the hardest settings. The paper also reports extrapolation analyses in the appendix showing that xLSTM’s enhanced memory structures help it generalize to longer contexts than seen during training.

# Long Range Arena for long-context evaluation
Long-context behavior is measured using the Long Range Arena (LRA) suite to assess models’ ability to handle long sequences across a variety of tasks (text, retrieval-like tasks, and structured inputs). xLSTM is evaluated alongside a variety of modern recurrent, SSM, and Transformer methods. The experiments show that xLSTM yields consistently strong performance on the LRA tasks considered, indicating that the combination of exponential gating and the two memory designs is effective for diverse long-context problems.

Where applicable (e.g., length extrapolation experiments described below), models are trained on a canonical context length ($2048$ tokens) and then evaluated at substantially longer context lengths (up to $16384$ tokens); this testing protocol directly assesses the capacity of the memory mechanism to avoid degradation as context length grows.

# Validation perplexity, downstream reasoning tasks (e.g., LMEval), PALOMA
Validation perplexity on the SlimPajama held-out set is the primary scalar metric used to compare autoregressive next-token modeling performance across families of models. In the $15\mathrm{B}$-token comparisons, models are configured at a common size (GPT-3–style $350\mathrm{M}$-parameter point) so validation perplexity differences reflect architectural differences rather than model scale. The paper reports that xLSTM variants achieve lower validation perplexity than the competing Transformers, SSMs, and RNN baselines in these controlled comparisons.

Beyond perplexity, the authors evaluate models on a suite of downstream reasoning tasks intended to measure common-sense and reasoning abilities. These downstream results are summarized in a table referenced as Table~\ref{tab:lmeval} and are reported alongside validation perplexity in the $300\mathrm{B}$-token experiments. The aggregate picture is that xLSTM models are top-performing on most downstream tasks across model sizes, with isolated tasks (e.g., ARC in some settings) where Mamba performs best.

PALOMA benchmark. To probe domain- and topic-level generalization for next-token prediction, the PALOMA suite (571 text domains) is used. The evaluation metric is token-prediction perplexity measured per domain. Results show that $xLSTM[1:0]$ (pure mLSTM) attains lower perplexity than competing models on the vast majority of domains: $568/571$ domains better than Mamba, $486/571$ better than Llama, and $570/571$ better than RWKV-4. This per-domain analysis highlights xLSTM’s robustness across varied text sources.

# Scaling law analysis: methodology and metrics
The study examines scaling behavior using a power-law methodology inspired by established scaling-law research. Models are trained at multiple parameter scales (e.g., $125\mathrm{M}$, $350\mathrm{M}$, $760\mathrm{M}$, $1.3\mathrm{B}$) and their validation perplexities are plotted versus model size; the fitted trend demonstrates an approximate power-law (straight line on log–log axes) with a slope that is broadly similar across method families but with different offsets. This allows comparisons of sample- and model-efficiency across architectures: a lower offset indicates systematically better performance at all scales for the same slope. The paper reports that xLSTM exhibits a favorable offset compared to Mamba, Llama, and RWKV-4, suggesting that at larger scales xLSTM remains competitive or superior.

In addition to parameter-scaling experiments, extrapolation of context length is treated as a separate scaling dimension. For these experiments, models trained on context length $2048$ are evaluated on lengths up to $16384$ to observe how perplexity changes with context length. xLSTM models maintain relatively low perplexities at extended lengths compared to the other evaluated methods, indicating better length-extrapolation behavior.

Finally, wall-clock inference characteristics (generation time and decoding throughput) are measured as practical scaling metrics. Generation-time experiments use generation with batch size $1$ and a pre-fill of $16$ tokens to favor cacheable Transformer workloads. Results exhibit linear generation-time scaling for recurrent models including xLSTM and quadratic scaling for the Transformer baseline (Llama). For decoding throughput, xLSTM benefits from constant-memory characteristics and can use larger batch sizes than the Transformer, producing higher maximal throughput in the reported experiments. These runtime measurements are presented alongside the algorithmic scaling laws to give a fuller picture of how architecture choices trade off statistical efficiency and computational practicalities.

# Summary of evaluation protocol and reproducibility notes
The experimental protocol combines synthetic diagnostic tasks (formal languages, MQAR, LRA) with large-scale language-model training on SlimPajama at two data budgets ($15\mathrm{B}$ and $300\mathrm{B}$ tokens). Models are compared at matched parameter points where possible, and a clear notation ($xLSTM[a:b]$) describes mixed-block architectures. The principal metrics are next-token perplexity, task-specific accuracy (scaled $0$ to $1$) or recall in synthetic tasks, downstream-task performance summarized in tabular form, per-domain perplexities on PALOMA, context-length extrapolation behavior, and computational/runtime metrics including generation latency and throughput.

Important implementation caveats that affect experimental reproducibility are explicitly reported: mixed-precision training was used widely but some baselines did not employ PyTorch AMP in the authors’ runs; custom CUDA kernels were implemented for sLSTM to reduce the parallelism disadvantage; the mLSTM CUDA kernels are not fully optimized and are slower than highly tuned attention kernels; and certain long runs required post-hoc precision fixes for some baseline models. These details should be considered when reproducing exact wall-clock numbers or when attempting to compare runtimes across architectures.
