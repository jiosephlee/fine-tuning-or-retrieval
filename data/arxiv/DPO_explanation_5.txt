CHAPTER 4 — DIRECT PREFERENCE OPTIMISATION: METHODOLOGY AND DERIVATION (ALL MATH IN LaTeX)

This chapter derives the DPO objective and presents the practical steps needed to train a policy with it. The derivation starts from the KL-regularised objective used in reinforcement-learning-based approaches and ends with a single supervised loss that can be optimised with the same tools used for supervised fine-tuning.

We start from the KL-regularised reward maximisation problem. For a policy \(\pi\), a reward function \(r\), a reference policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\), and a regularisation coefficient \(\beta>0\), we wish to maximise
\[
\max_{\pi} \;\; \mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \pi}\!\big[r(x,y)\big] \;-\; \beta \, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big).
\]
Under mild assumptions, the corresponding optimal policy for a fixed reward has the closed-form expression
\[
\pi_r(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)}{Z(x)},
\quad
Z(x) \;=\; \sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).
\]
If we take logarithms of both sides and rearrange, we can express the reward as
\[
r(x,y) \;=\; \beta \Big( \log \pi_r(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) \Big) + \beta \log Z(x).
\]

We now invoke a pairwise preference model to remove the partition function. In the Bradley–Terry model, the probability that the response \(y_w\) is preferred to the response \(y_l\) for the same prompt \(x\) is equal to
\[
\Pr(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\big( r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l) \big).
\]
Substituting the log expression for the reward shows that the additive term \(\beta \log Z(x)\) cancels. If we write
\[
\Delta(y) \;=\; \log \pi(y\mid x) \;-\; \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x),
\]
then the resulting preference probability becomes
\[
\Pr(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) \;=\; \sigma\!\Big( \beta \big( \Delta(y_w) - \Delta(y_l) \big) \Big).
\]
This equation depends only on log-probability ratios with respect to the reference policy and the scalar \(\beta\). It therefore invites a maximum-likelihood fit of \(\pi\) directly, without an explicit reward model and without any reinforcement-learning loop.

The DPO loss follows immediately. If \(\mathcal{D}\) is a dataset of triples \((x,y_w,y_l)\), then with a parameterised policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) we define
\[
\Delta_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \;-\; \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x),
\]
and we minimise the expected negative log-likelihood
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[ -\log \sigma\!\Big( \beta\big( \Delta_{\theta}(x,y_w) - \Delta_{\theta}(x,y_l) \big) \Big) \bigg].
\]
Intuitively, this objective increases the relative log-probability of the preferred completion and decreases that of the dispreferred completion, both measured against the reference policy. The parameter \(\beta\) acts as a global lever that controls how far the model may move from the reference.

It is instructive to examine the gradient. If we define an implicit reward by
\[
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \,\Delta_{\theta}(x,y) \;=\; \beta \big( \log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x) \big),
\]
then a convenient form of the gradient is
\[
\nabla_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} \;=\; -\beta \,\mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sigma\!\big( \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w) \big) \,\Big( \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x) \;-\; \nabla_{\theta} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x) \Big) \right].
\]
The factor \(\sigma\!\big(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_l) - \hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y_w)\big)\) is largest when the model currently prefers the losing response, which focuses updates precisely where the model is wrong.

The remaining ingredients are practical. When scoring sequences, one must decide whether to sum per-token log-probabilities or to average them, and one must apply the same convention to both the trainable policy and the reference policy. In most implementations, the log-probabilities are computed over the completion portion of the sequence rather than over the prompt. For a completion \(y_{1:T}\), the log-probability is
\[
\log \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \pi_{\theta}(y_t \mid x, y_{<t}).
\]
A sequence-level KL can be estimated by summing token-level divergences,
\[
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\Vert \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}; x,y) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{T} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\Big( \pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x, y_{<t}) \,\Vert\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x, y_{<t}) \Big).
\]
Monitoring the sequence-level KL alongside validation win rates, histograms of margins, and the fraction of misordered pairs provides a detailed picture of training progress. If a proxy reward exists, a reward–KL frontier summarises the trade-off between improvement and deviation from the reference.

In summary, the DPO method replaces the reinforcement-learning loop with a single supervised loss derived from pairwise preferences and a soft-optimal policy parameterisation. By grounding every update in the log-probability ratio with respect to the reference and by using a single scalar \(\beta\) to calibrate the strength of the shift, DPO offers a stable, efficient route to preference alignment that fits naturally into existing supervised training pipelines.
