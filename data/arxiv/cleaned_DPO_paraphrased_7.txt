\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop some reasoning capabilities, precisely directing their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to achieve such control rely on gathering human judgments about the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the LM to conform to these preferences, commonly using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process that involves first training a reward model to represent human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the large unsupervised LM to maximize this learned reward while preventing excessive deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that permits direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling us to address the conventional RLHF objective through a straightforward classification loss.}  
The method we introduce, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} sampling from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences as well as or better than existing techniques. In particular, fine-tuning with DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and achieves comparable or improved quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being considerably simpler to implement and train.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Massive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop unexpectedly powerful skills~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models learn from data produced by humans who have diverse aims, priorities, and expertise levels. Some of these aims and skills may not be appropriate to replicate; for instance, although it is useful for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors in order to fix them, when generating code, we prefer to steer the model toward the (possibly uncommon) high-quality coding abilities embedded in its training examples. Likewise, we might want the language model to \textit{know about} a widespread misconception held by about half of the population, but we definitely do not want it to assert that this misconception is true in half of all related queries! Put differently, choosing the model’s \emph{preferred responses and behaviors} from its vast range of \textit{knowledge and skills} is essential for creating AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While current techniques usually guide LMs to align with human preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we will demonstrate that the RL objective commonly employed can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly simplifying the process of learning from preferences.

At a broad level, current approaches incorporate desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully curated collections of human preferences that reflect behaviors considered safe and useful by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial stage involving large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest technique for preference learning is supervised fine-tuning based on human demonstrations of high-quality outputs, the most effective methods utilize reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches involve training a reward model on datasets of human preferences and then applying reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy to generate responses with high reward while avoiding significant divergence from the base model. Despite yielding models with strong conversational and coding performance, the RLHF workflow is substantially more complex than supervised learning, requiring multiple language models to be trained and continuous sampling from the policy during training, which results in considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to directly fine-tune a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{\methodfull} (\methodac)}, an approach that implicitly optimizes the same goal as current RLHF techniques (maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint) while being easier to implement and train. Conceptually, the {\methodac} update boosts the relative log-likelihood of preferred responses over less favored ones, incorporating a dynamic, per-sample importance weighting that mitigates the model degradation observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to prior methods, {\methodac} depends on a theoretical preference model (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how well a candidate reward function fits observed preference data. Nevertheless, unlike existing strategies that use this model to create a preference loss for training a separate reward model followed by policy optimization, {\methodac} reformulates the preference loss directly in terms of the policy through a change of variables. Consequently, given a dataset of human preferences on model outputs, {\methodac} optimizes the policy using a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring the training of an explicit, separate reward model or the need for sampling from the policy during training}{yielding the optimal policy aligned with an implicit reward function inferred from preference data}.

The primary contribution of this work is {\methodfull} (\methodac), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Our experimental results demonstrate that {\methodac} performs comparably to current approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, when learning from preferences on tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, as their size grows, demonstrate the ability to perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with only a few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced through fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, often called `instruction-tuning,’ allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize to instructions beyond those seen during tuning and typically improves their overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven successful, it is frequently easier to gather \textit{relative} human assessments of output quality than expert demonstrations. Consequently, recent research has refined LLMs using datasets of human preferences, which has led to advances in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to align with the preference data under preference frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and subsequently fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related avenue involves leveraging LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback on instructions to create synthetic preference datasets targeting specific traits like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak supervision provided by textual rubrics for the model’s annotations. This line of work synthesizes two research domains: one focused on training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another on broad methodologies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of using relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning for fine-tuning large language models remains a substantial practical hurdle; the present study offers a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without resorting to RL.

Beyond language-related tasks, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods developed. When preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this scenario is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute reward signals, theoretical treatments of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} competing policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in CDB frameworks, preference feedback is typically obtained in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences often involves a fixed dataset containing offline preference-annotated pairs of actions \citep{yan2022human}. Similarly, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) relies on binary preference signals derived from an \textit{unknown} latent `scoring' function instead of direct reward values \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are numerous PbRL algorithms, including approaches capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, which commonly involve first estimating this hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model) explicitly, followed by policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified, single-stage policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to align with the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF workflow as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently expanded upon in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process typically consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model using supervised learning on carefully curated data relevant to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate a preference for one of the answers, expressed as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the favored and less favored outputs within the pair $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. It is assumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is inaccessible to us. Several methods exist for modeling these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a widely adopted option (although more general ranking models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be integrated into this framework when multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model expresses the human preference probability $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of preference comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, we introduce a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. When framed as a binary classification problem, the objective corresponds to minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model applications, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is frequently initialized based on the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented by a linear layer added on top of the final transformer output that yields a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variability in the reward signal, previous studies normalize the rewards so that the expectation satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}[r_{\phi}(x, y)] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward function is utilized to guide the language model through feedback. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the coefficient $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the baseline reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ for the language model is initialized as $\pisft$. Incorporating this regularization term is crucial since it restricts the policy from straying too far from the data distribution where the reward model performs reliably, helps preserve diversity in generation, and avoids collapsing to a limited set of high-reward outputs. Because language generation is inherently discrete, this objective is non-differentiable and is generally optimized using reinforcement learning methods. The common strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and then maximizing it via PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of deploying reinforcement learning techniques on large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference data. Contrary to previous RLHF approaches, which first learn a reward function and subsequently optimize it through reinforcement learning, our method \rev{circumvents the reward modeling phase and directly tunes a language model based on preferences}{employs a specific reward model parameterization allowing direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for an RL training loop}.  
As we will elaborate shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical relationship between reward functions and their optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined on reward functions into one defined on policies.  
This change-of-variables technique \rev{enables us to omit the separate reward modeling step}{prevents the requirement to fit a distinct, explicit reward model}, while still optimizing according to established frameworks of human preference, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously encodes both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective used in previous studies, as given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, involving a general reward function $r$. Following the approaches in earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy maximizing the KL-constrained reward objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ acts as the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when substituting the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, complicating the practical use of this form. Nevertheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation, we arrive at:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends solely on the difference in rewards between two outputs, specifically ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} causes the partition function terms to cancel out, allowing the human preference probabilities to be represented entirely through the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
For the detailed proof, see Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations can be obtained for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, which are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data with respect to the optimal policy instead of the reward model, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{In doing so, we eliminate the need for explicit reward modeling and avoid reinforcement learning optimization altogether.}{This approach effectively fits an implicit reward through a different parameterization where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from established theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate further on these theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related work in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient relative to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward estimate is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where the implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ is defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ in relation to the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the likelihood of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing the likelihood of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the contribution of each training instance is modulated by how much the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly favors the less preferred output, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting the strength of the KL divergence constraint. Our empirical findings highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as omitting it leads to deterioration in language model performance (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO framework overview.}  
The typical workflow for DPO involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then label them according to human preferences to create an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using the given reference policy $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a chosen parameter $\beta$.  
In practical settings, it is often preferable to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting human labels. Since such datasets are usually generated by sampling from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, formally defined as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the mismatch between the true, unknown reference distribution and the proxy $\piref$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
This section offers a deeper explanation of the DPO approach, presents supporting theoretical foundations, and connects the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered by actor-critic algorithms applied in RLHF, including methods like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as an Implicit Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need for \rev{explicit reward estimation}{directly estimating a reward function} as well as avoiding reinforcement learning by employing a single maximum likelihood objective to learn the policy. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Consequently, optimizing our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ is effectively the same as optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after an appropriate variable transformation. In this part, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the set of attainable reward models, and prove it enables precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this relation is an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be formulated as follows:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically under the Bradley-Terry framework, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the identical equivalence class yield the same optimal policy within the constrained reinforcement learning framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are quite direct and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This initial lemma highlights a well-recognized under-specification problem inherent to the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to enforce additional identifiability conditions to secure guarantees for the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma confirms that all reward functions within a given equivalence class produce an identical optimal policy, implying that our ultimate goal is to recover any representative reward function from that optimal set. The following theorem is demonstrated in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including the Bradley-Terry variant) can be expressed through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a given model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a specified reference model.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ which defines a corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as described in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that an equivalent reward function within $r$’s equivalence class can be written using the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ essentially normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on the context $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the expression on the right side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward), we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. In other words, the projection $f$ yields a reward function of the specified form within $r$’s equivalence class, so adopting this reparameterization does not restrict the generality of our reward model.
\end{sproof}
Another perspective on Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class is chosen by the DPO reparameterization, specifically the reward satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (all probabilities are positive and sum to one).
Referring back to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we recognize Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} as the partition function for the optimal policy associated with the reward $r(x, y)$.
The central insight behind the DPO method is that by enforcing particular constraints on the otherwise under-specified Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) family of preference models, we retain the set of representable reward functions while simultaneously making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly computable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our proposed framework can also help identify instability problems in common actor-critic approaches employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}, following the typical RLHF workflow. This setup aligns with the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL problem given in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid\mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy derived in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} based on the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following objective function:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \bigg[ \underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective corresponds to the one optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that utilize the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this context, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function for the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it may result in policy gradients with high variance, potentially causing instability during training. One can address this normalization via a learned value function; however, this approach can be challenging to optimize. Alternatively, prior methods have normalized rewards by subtracting a baseline derived from human completions, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward formulation that eliminates the need for any baseline correction.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO’s capability to directly train policies using preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances the maximization of reward against the minimization of KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, in comparison to popular preference learning methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as summarization and dialogue generation. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks, including RLHF with PPO and selecting the top trajectory from $N$ samples according to a learned reward model. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental setup; further information is available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In every experiment, the algorithms develop a policy based on a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ corresponds to the beginning segment of a movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is tasked with producing an output $y$ that exhibits positive sentiment. To enable systematic evaluation, we \textit{construct} preference pairs by comparing generated outputs using a pretrained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. Regarding supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence on the training portion of the IMDb reviews (additional details can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the case of \textbf{summarization}, the input $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy aims to generate a concise summary $y$ capturing the key points of the post. Consistent with earlier research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} accompanied by human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The human preference data was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from samples produced by a different yet similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, the input $x$ represents a user query that may range from astrophysics questions to requests for relationship advice. The policy’s role is to generate a helpful and engaging response $y$ to the query. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170,000 exchanges between humans and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two responses generated by a large (though unspecified) language model, paired with a label indicating the human-preferred reply. Since no pre-existing pretrained SFT model is available in this context, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation methods. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective in the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm’s performance based on the trade-off curve between the achieved reward and the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this curve can be determined since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical applications where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of the reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response recorded in the test data. Although prior work indicates that language models can serve as more reliable automated evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human evaluation study (see Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}) to validate our use of GPT-4 for assessment. Our results demonstrate a strong correlation between GPT-4’s evaluations and human judgments, with agreement levels between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding those among human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. Additionally, we test the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned through supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ drawn either from the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised technique is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method optionally incorporates a coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ to weight the ‘unlikelihood’ loss. We also evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, using a reward model derived from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant trained with access to the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment experiments. For sentiment tasks, we implement two PPO-GT versions: a standard off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified one that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters for enhanced performance (these adjustments are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, which involves generating $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selecting the top-scoring one according to a reward function trained on the preference dataset. Although this method achieves strong results by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive for even moderate values of $N$, as it requires generating $N$ completions per query during inference.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among all methods, only DPO shows improvement compared to the selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout the training process. The enhancement provided by DPO relative to the dataset labels remains consistently stable during training, regardless of sampling temperature.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework commonly employed in RLHF methods strikes a balance between maximizing reward and limiting the policy’s divergence from a reference policy. Thus, when evaluating different algorithms, it is essential to consider both the reward obtained and the KL divergence; a marginal increase in reward accompanied by a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the trade-off curve between reward and KL divergence across various algorithms within the sentiment task. For each algorithm, we perform multiple training experiments, varying the hyperparameters controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values of $\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta$ values of $\{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha$ values of $\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, as well as different random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. At every 100 training steps up to convergence, we assess each policy on a test prompt set by calculating the average reward according to the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the sum of KL divergences at each timestep.} relative to the reference policy, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO consistently forms the most effective frontier, delivering the highest rewards while maintaining low KL values. This outcome is significant for several reasons: firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the identical objective, DPO is markedly more efficient, with its reward-KL balance clearly outperforming PPO. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO’s frontier even when PPO is provided access to the true reward signals (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed by assessing the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models via PPO using human preference data yields better summaries. Our evaluation compares several approaches by generating completions on the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test set and calculating the average win rate relative to the reference completions. For all methods, completions were sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} was fine-tuned using DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT. Results show that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO’s best win rate of about 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO surpasses the peak win rate achieved by the best baseline method among $N$. It is important to highlight that we performed minimal tuning of DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting that these findings might underrepresent DPO’s full potential. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates significantly greater stability across sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose effectiveness diminishes at higher temperatures, approaching that of the underlying GPT-J model. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the base SFT model. A direct comparison of DPO and PPO using human evaluations is presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test portion from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, specifically focusing on interactions involving one step between a human and an assistant. GPT-4 evaluations leverage the preferred completions from the test set as a benchmark to calculate the win rate across different methods. Since no standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model exists for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions to ensure the outputs remain within the model’s distribution, and subsequently proceed with training via DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top-performing completion out of 128 Preferred-FT samples (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of these methods at their optimal temperature settings. We also test an RLHF model trained using PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing on our TL;DR outcomes and the fact that both methods optimize the identical reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering comparable or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 approach. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\section{Discussion}  
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach to developing capable and well-aligned language models. We have presented DPO, an uncomplicated training method that enables language models to be trained using preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the language model to be optimized for human preferences \textit{directly} through a straightforward cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. With minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches; as a result, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models guided by human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Directions for Future Research.} Our findings prompt several key questions to be addressed moving forward. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize on par with PPO-based methods, but a more thorough investigation is warranted. For instance, can leveraging self-labeling from the DPO policy enable effective utilization of unlabeled prompts in a similar manner? Additionally, what form does reward over-optimization take within the direct preference optimization framework, and could the slight performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this phenomenon? Furthermore, although our experiments cover models up to 6 billion parameters, extending DPO to considerably larger, state-of-the-art models represents a promising avenue for future exploration. On the evaluation side, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate assessments are influenced by the prompt design; future work might focus on optimizing prompt strategies to extract reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, beyond the scope of training language models from human preferences, DPO holds potential for a variety of other applications, including training generative models in different modalities.
