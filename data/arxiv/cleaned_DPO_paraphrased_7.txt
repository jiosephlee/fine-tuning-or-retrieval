\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely directing their outputs remains challenging due to their fully unsupervised training regime. Current approaches to achieve such controllability rely on collecting human annotations that rank the quality of model outputs, subsequently fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to conform to these preferences, frequently employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF involves a complicated and often unstable two-step process: first training a reward model to capture human preferences, then applying reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM to maximize this reward while preventing excessive deviation from the original model.  
\rev{In this paper, we leverage a mapping between reward functions and optimal policies to show that this constrained reward maximization problem can be \emph{optimized exactly} with a single stage of policy training, essentially solving a classification problem on the human preference data.}{Here, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that allows direct recovery of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, thereby converting the typical RLHF task into a straightforward classification objective.}  
The method we introduce, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for \rev{fitting a reward model,}{} generating samples from the LM during fine-tuning, or extensive hyperparameter tuning.  
Empirical results demonstrate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences at least as well as existing techniques. Importantly, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs and equals or improves upon response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being considerably easier to implement and train.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets develop remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from data produced by humans who have diverse intentions, priorities, and expertise. Some of these intentions or skills may not be ideal to replicate; for instance, although it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to fix them, when it comes to generating code, we want to steer the model towards the (possibly infrequent) instances of high-quality coding found in its training corpus. Likewise, a language model might need to be \textit{cognizant} of a widespread misconception held by half of the population, but it should certainly not assert this misconception as fact in half of the cases when queried. Put differently, it is essential to carefully select the model’s \emph{preferred responses and conduct} from its broad \textit{knowledge and skills} to develop AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While current approaches often guide LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed by these methods can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby greatly simplifying the process of learning preferences.

At a broad level, current techniques embed the preferred behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully collected sets of human preferences that reflect the kinds of responses humans consider safe and useful. This preference learning phase follows an initial step of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on human-provided examples of high-quality outputs, the most effective approaches are based on reinforcement learning guided by human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches train a reward model on a dataset of human preferences and subsequently apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy to generate responses that receive high reward scores, while avoiding significant divergence from the original model. Despite yielding models with strong conversational and coding performance, the RLHF framework is substantially more intricate than supervised learning, as it requires training multiple language models and continuously sampling from the policy during training, leading to considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to optimize a language model so that it aligns with human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same goal as current RLHF approaches—maximizing rewards while enforcing a KL-divergence constraint—but is easier to implement and train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to dispreferred ones, incorporating a dynamic, example-specific importance weight that helps avoid model degradation observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function matches observed preference data. However, unlike traditional methods that employ this preference model to define a loss for training a separate reward model before optimizing a policy based on it, DPO performs a variable transformation that allows defining the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Consequently, given a dataset of human preference judgments over model outputs, DPO can optimize the policy through a simple binary cross-entropy loss, \rev{without requiring the training of an explicit standalone reward model or policy sampling during training}{yielding the optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function derived from the preference data}.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that does not rely on reinforcement learning for training language models based on preference data. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs comparably to current approaches, including PPO-driven RLHF, when learning from preferences across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models with up to 6 billion parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, as their size grows, can perform certain tasks either zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or by leveraging few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning them on datasets composed of instructions paired with human-crafted completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models (LLMs) to generalize beyond the original instruction dataset and generally boosts their practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has shown strong results, gathering \textit{relative} human assessments of output quality is often simpler than acquiring expert demonstrations. Consequently, later studies have fine-tuned LLMs using datasets reflecting human preferences, leading to improved capabilities in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction compliance \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by training a neural reward model to align with the preference data based on models like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related direction involves utilizing LLMs that have been fine-tuned with human feedback for instruction following to generate synthetic preference data focusing on specific traits like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision through textual rubrics for annotation. These strategies reflect a fusion of two research streams: one centered on reinforcement learning for training language models toward diverse objectives \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and another on general techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning for fine-tuning large language models remains challenging in practice; this paper introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the realm of language, the problem of deriving policies from preference information has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to a variety of proposed techniques. When preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandit problems, this setting is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute reward values, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} alternative policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, in CDB scenarios, preference information is obtained in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences generally involves training on a fixed, offline dataset containing preference-labeled pairs of actions \citep{yan2022human}. Similarly, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) relies on binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} underlying ‘scoring’ function instead of explicit reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A range of PbRL algorithms have been developed, many of which can incorporate off-policy preference data; however, these methods typically require an initial step of explicitly inferring the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model), followed by policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified, single-stage approach for policy learning that directly optimizes a policy to align with the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We examine the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further expanded upon in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This approach generally consists of three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts with fine-tuning a pre-trained language model using supervised learning on carefully curated data relevant to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who indicate a preference for one response, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ correspond to the favored and less favored answers within the pair $(y_1, y_2)$ respectively. It is presumed that these preferences arise from an underlying, unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Various methods exist to capture these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being widely adopted (although more comprehensive Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be applied when multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model defines the probability of human preference $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{\exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_1)\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_1)\bigr) + \exp\bigl(r^*(x, y_2)\bigr)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison tuples $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can parameterize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters by maximizing likelihood. Framing this as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\log \sigma\bigl(r_{\phi}(x, y_w) - r_{\phi}(x, y_l)\bigr)\right]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language modeling scenarios, the model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is frequently initialized from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented with an additional linear layer atop the last transformer block to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variability in the reward predictions, prior studies normalize the reward values so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward function provides guidance to the language model. Consistent with previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization task is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ can diverge from the base reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the initial supervised fine-tuned model $\pisft$.  
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ starts from $\pisft$. This KL divergence term plays a critical role by restricting the policy to remain close to the distribution where the reward model is reliable, ensuring diversity in generated outputs and avoiding collapse to a few dominant high-reward responses. Because language generation is discrete, the objective is not differentiable and thus is commonly optimized via reinforcement learning methods. The conventional technique \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties encountered when implementing reinforcement learning algorithms on extensive tasks like fine-tuning language models, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference data. In contrast to earlier RLHF techniques that first estimate a reward function and subsequently optimize it using reinforcement learning, our method \rev{eliminates the need for a separate reward modeling phase and directly trains the language model based on preference information}{employs a specific parameterization of the reward model which permits deriving the optimal policy analytically, removing the requirement for an RL training loop}. 

As we will elaborate shortly, the central idea is to exploit a closed-form relationship mapping reward functions to their optimal policies, allowing us to convert a loss defined on reward functions into one defined on policies. This change-of-variables strategy \rev{enables us to omit the explicit reward modeling step}{bypasses the necessity to explicitly fit a separate reward model}, while still operating within established frameworks of human preference modeling, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network simultaneously encapsulates both the language model and the \rev{}{(implicit)} reward.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective as previous studies, given by Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, involving a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y} \piref(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x,y)\right)$ serves as the partition function. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when utilizing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, estimating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which complicates the practical application of this formulation. Nonetheless, by manipulating Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of the associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reparameterization applies to the true reward function $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the difference in rewards between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the reparameterized reward from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} causes the partition function terms to cancel out, enabling the human preference probabilities to be represented solely by the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2 \mid x)}{\piref(y_2 \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1 \mid x)}{\piref(y_1 \mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}
The detailed derivation is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While this result relies on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations for the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also presented in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Given that we now express the probability of human preference data based on the optimal policy rather than relying on the reward model, we are able to establish a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (refer to Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy objective is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
\rev{This approach allows us to circumvent explicitly modeling the reward function and also eliminates the necessity for reinforcement learning optimization.}{In this manner, we implicitly fit a reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$.} Additionally, because this method aligns with fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits certain theoretical guarantees such as consistency given reasonable assumptions about the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides a more detailed discussion of the theoretical aspects of DPO in comparison to related work.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward estimate errs}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the implicit reward assigned by the language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\piref$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while decreasing the probability of the less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the update weights each example according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the value of the disfavored completion, scaled by $\beta$, reflecting how inaccurately the implicit reward ranks the outputs and incorporating the influence of the KL regularization. Our empirical findings highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as a simplified variant that omits it can lead to degeneration of the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The standard DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completion pairs $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ and collect human preference labels to form the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the target parameter $\beta$.  
In practical scenarios, it is often preferable to utilize existing public preference datasets instead of generating new samples and obtaining fresh human labels. Because these preference datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when such a model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, formally expressed as ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ used in DPO. Additional details on implementation and tuning parameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}  
Here, we delve deeper into the DPO approach by offering additional insights, presenting a theoretical foundation, and connecting the benefits of DPO to the challenges encountered with actor-critic methods commonly employed in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as an Implicit Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents both \rev{explicit reward estimation}{the need to fit an explicit reward} and reinforcement learning by directly optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model with a reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our training of the parametric policy model $\pi_{\theta}$ is thus mathematically equivalent to optimizing a reward model as in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} when applying an appropriate change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the expressiveness of the learned reward functions, and prove that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if their difference can be expressed as $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$ for a certain function $f$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically the Bradley-Terry setting, any two reward functions belonging to the identical class generate an equivalent preference distribution.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate an identical optimal policy when solving the constrained reinforcement learning problem.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are simple and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. This first lemma highlights a well-known under-specification challenge inherent to the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is often necessary to introduce extra identifiability conditions in order to obtain guarantees on the MLE solutions derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions within the same equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy. Therefore, for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any single reward function from this optimal class. The following theorem, whose proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this idea:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, every reward class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model family (and specifically the Bradley-Terry model) admits a representation through the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is some model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a fixed reference model.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ according to Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed using the reparameterization stated above. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ effectively normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends only on the prefix $x$, the resulting function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the same equivalence class as the original reward $r(x, y)$. Lastly, substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward function) yields 
\[
f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}.
\]
Thus, the projection $f$ produces a representative from $r$’s equivalence class that conforms to the desired reparameterization, ensuring no loss of generality in modeling rewards this way.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as identifying the particular reward function within each equivalence class that the DPO reparameterization chooses, namely the one satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which means that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution (i.e., probabilities are nonnegative and sum to unity).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, this condition corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy determined by the reward function $r(x, y)$.
The crucial insight behind the DPO method is that by imposing suitable constraints on the inherently underdetermined Plackett-Luce (and in particular the Bradley-Terry) preference models, one can maintain the full class of representable reward functions while making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically manageable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Unstable Behavior in Actor-Critic Methods}  
Our framework can also be applied to identify sources of instability in common actor-critic algorithms utilized in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure, concentrating on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating this to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation presented in \ref{eq:RL}, we consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)$ and aim to minimize the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y \mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} that emerges from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the optimization objective:  
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}  
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)} \bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta \log \sum_{y} \piref(y \mid x) \exp \left(\frac{1}{\beta} r_{\phi}(x, y) \right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}}_{\text{KL}} \bigg]  
\end{equation}  
This objective matches those optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which use the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this context, the normalization component inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the optimal policy itself, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to exhibit high variance, thereby destabilizing the learning process. One approach to handle this normalization term is by employing a learned value function, though this can present its own optimization challenges. Alternatively, prior methods have normalized rewards through a baseline based on human completions, effectively approximating the normalization term with a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate. In contrast, the DPO reformulation provides a reward function that inherently eliminates the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}  
This section presents an empirical assessment of DPO's capacity to directly train policies based on preferences. Initially, within a carefully controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward against minimizing the KL-divergence relative to a reference policy, in comparison to standard preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we assess DPO's effectiveness on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue generation. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong benchmarks like RLHF using PPO, as well as outperforming the top trajectory among $N$ samples evaluated under a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these outcomes, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all tasks, the algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, the input $x$ corresponds to the beginning of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the model is tasked with producing an output $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To facilitate controlled assessment, for this task we \textit{create} preference pairs by leveraging a pre-trained sentiment classifier such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we fine-tune GPT-2-large on the training portion of the IMDb reviews until convergence (additional details in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the objective is for the policy to generate a summary $y$ that captures the main ideas of the post. Following earlier research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We utilize an SFT model fine-tuned on human-crafted forum post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} within the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for RLHF. The human preference dataset was obtained from samples generated by a distinct, yet similarly trained, SFT model as described by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} scenario, $x$ is a user query that could range from questions about astrophysics to requests for relationship guidance. The policy must generate an engaging and informative reply $y$ to the user’s input. For this, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k conversations between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two candidate responses produced by a large (but unspecified) language model, accompanied by a label indicating the human-preferred answer. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists in this setting, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model solely on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We adopt two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximizaton objective, within the controlled sentiment generation context, we measure performance by the frontier defined by achieved reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier can be computed since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in real-world scenarios where the true reward function is unknown, we evaluate algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, employing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For the summarization task, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response found in the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models may serve as superior automated evaluators compared to conventional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we validate our use of GPT-4 through a human evaluation study presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show a strong correlation between GPT-4 assessments and human judgments, with agreement levels between humans and GPT-4 generally matching or exceeding those among human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we investigate a variety of established techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we examine zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in the dialogue setting. We also assess the performance of the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected completion $y_w$ derived either from the SFT model (applied in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (used in single-turn dialogue). Another approach based on pseudo-supervision is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while \textit{reducing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this includes an optional parameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$ that weights the ‘unlikelihood’ component. Additionally, we incorporate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} optimized with a reward function learned from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment experiments. For the sentiment tasks, we implement two versions of PPO-GT: a standard off-the-shelf variant \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance results (these adjustments are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ samples from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest-scoring output based on a reward function trained from the preference data. Although this approach often yields strong performance by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive at test time, as it requires generating $N$ completions for each input, even at moderate values of $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for one-step dialogue using the Anthropic-HH dataset; among all methods, only DPO shows improvement compared to the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates tracked across various sampling temperatures during training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels throughout training regardless of the sampling temperature used.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework commonly employed in RLHF algorithms aims to balance the exploitation of rewards with limiting the policy’s deviation from the reference policy. Thus, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the corresponding KL divergence; a marginally increased reward accompanied by a substantially larger KL divergence is not always preferable. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, the reward-KL tradeoff frontier is illustrated for various algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. Multiple training runs are conducted for each algorithm, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in each run (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha \in \{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT). This comprehensive sweep comprises a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, each policy is evaluated on a set of test prompts by calculating the average reward based on the true reward function along with the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} relative to the reference policy, denoted as $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our observations indicate that DPO achieves the most effective frontier, delivering the highest rewards while maintaining low KL values. This finding is significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize an identical objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency; its reward/KL balance clearly surpasses that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even when PPO is provided access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to authentic preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
In this section, we assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, standard automatic metrics like ROUGE often exhibit weak correlation with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning language models via PPO using human preferences leads to more effective summaries. We compare several approaches by generating completions on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate against the reference completions provided in the test set. Completions across all methods are sampled with temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, and the resulting win rates are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The models fine-tuned by DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our findings show that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, surpassing PPO’s best performance of roughly 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO outperforms the best $N$ baseline in terms of maximum win rate. It is important to highlight that we did not extensively tune the $\beta$ hyperparameter for DPO, so these results might undervalue its full potential. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates considerably greater robustness to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance can deteriorate to the level of the original GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT shows minimal improvement over the initial SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also provide direct human evaluations comparing DPO and PPO, where samples generated by DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

For the single-turn dialogue task, we assess various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH test split \citep{bai2022training} that includes a single human-assistant interaction step. GPT-4 evaluations rely on preferred completions from the test set as references to calculate the win rates across different methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on selected completions to ensure they align with the model's distribution, and subsequently perform training with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top completion from 128 Preferred-FT outputs (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline levels off at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each method. We further test an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Considering the outcomes from TL;DR and the shared reward optimization between these techniques, we treat Best of 128 as a rough estimate for PPO-level effectiveness. In summary, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient approach that enhances preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset while delivering comparable or superior results relative to the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its peak performance fairly rapidly.

\section{Discussion}  
Preference-based learning offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning challenge into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL techniques, DPO uncovers a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the language model to be trained to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss—eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and preserving full generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves comparable or superior performance to current RLHF approaches, including those employing PPO, thereby significantly lowering the complexity involved in training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several key questions to explore moving forward. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward function? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize similarly to models trained with PPO, but more thorough investigation is required. For instance, is it possible that training with self-labeling based on the DPO policy can effectively leverage unlabeled prompts as well? Additionally, how does reward over-optimization appear within the direct preference optimization framework, and could the modest performance drop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be a manifestation of this phenomenon? Moreover, although our evaluations cover models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models represents a promising avenue for future research. In terms of assessment, we observe that GPT-4’s win rate metrics are influenced by the prompt wording; subsequent studies might investigate optimal strategies for eliciting reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO has potential applications beyond fine-tuning language models on human preferences, such as training generative models across different data modalities.
