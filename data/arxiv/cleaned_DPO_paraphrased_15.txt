\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and demonstrate certain reasoning capabilities, exerting precise control over their outputs remains challenging due to the lack of supervision during their training process. Current approaches for improving such controllability often rely on collecting human preference data to compare model outputs and then fine-tuning the base LM to match these preferences, most commonly through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). RLHF, however, is a complicated and frequently unstable process: it requires first fitting a reward model based on human judgments, followed by tuning the large LM with reinforcement learning to maximize this learned reward signal while preventing excessive deviation from the original model. In this work, we present a novel formulation of the reward model in RLHF that enables direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, making it possible to address the conventional RLHF task using a straightforward classification objective. This approach, which we term \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is efficient, robust, and easy to implement, removing the necessity for model sampling during fine-tuning and eliminating extensive hyperparameter optimization. Experimental results indicate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to align with human preferences as well as, or better than, established techniques. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and equals or outperforms existing methods in summarization and single-turn dialogue response quality, while being much simpler to train and deploy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Massive unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on enormous corpora, develop a range of impressive abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these systems are trained on human-produced data that reflects a broad spectrum of intentions, preferences, and expertise. Some of these characteristics are not always suitable to emulate; for instance, while it is useful for an AI programming assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent coding errors in order to address them, we still want the model to \textit{favor} the (often infrequent) high-caliber examples present in its training material when producing code. Similarly, though it can be beneficial for a language model to \textit{identify} a widespread misconception held by 50\% of people, we would certainly not want the model to assert this false belief in half of the cases where the topic arises! Put differently, effectively \emph{choosing the model's outputs and actions} from its vast pool of \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is essential for creating AI tools that are reliable, safe, and steerable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques generally employ reinforcement learning (RL) to guide LMs towards reflecting human preferences, we will demonstrate that the RL-based objective commonly used in such approaches can be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches teach language models to exhibit desired behaviors by utilizing carefully constructed collections of human preferences, which reflect what users perceive as safe and useful conduct. This preference learning typically follows an initial phase in which the model is pre-trained in an unsupervised manner on extensive text corpora. Although the most direct form of preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on examples of high-quality human-generated responses, the most effective methods employ reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques involve training a reward model on a dataset containing human preference information, and then applying reinforcement learning to optimize the language model’s policy to generate outputs rated highly by the reward model, while ensuring that the model remains reasonably close to its original parameters. Despite the fact that RLHF leads to language models with remarkable abilities in dialogue and code generation, the RLHF process is substantially more complicated than supervised learning, requiring the training of several LMs and iterative policy sampling, which results in substantial computational overhead.

In this work, we demonstrate a method to fine-tune a language model according to human preferences without resorting to explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same objective as standard RLHF methods (i.e., maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence regularization), yet remains both easy to implement and efficient to train. Conceptually, DPO modifies the relative log-likelihood of preferred versus non-preferred outputs, introducing a dynamic, per-instance weighting factor that avoids the model degradation observed when using a simple probability ratio loss. Similar to prior approaches, DPO is grounded in a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}), which quantifies the agreement between a reward function and observed preference labels. However, whereas traditional methods employ the preference model to create a loss for training a reward model—subsequently using the reward model to train a policy—DPO leverages a variable transformation to directly express the preference loss in terms of the policy. With a dataset capturing human preferences over generated responses, DPO is thus able to train a policy through a basic binary cross-entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy under an implicit reward function that matches the preference annotations.

The core contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models from preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs on par with or better than current approaches, including PPO-based RLHF, for preference-based tasks like sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, even when scaling to language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models, as they grow larger, are capable of completing certain tasks without any training examples \citep{radford2019language}, or with just a handful of examples provided in prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their ability to align with user instructions can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning them on collections of instructions paired with human-generated outputs \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning', enables large language models to adapt to new instructions not present in the training set, thereby improving their general utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has achieved considerable progress, it is often simpler to gather \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality than to obtain high-quality expert-written examples. As a result, later research has focused on fine-tuning language models using datasets based on human preferences, leading to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically involve first training a neural reward model to align with the preference data—often relying on a framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—then optimizing the language model with reinforcement learning methods to maximize the learned reward, using algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related strategies \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Closely related research uses language models already instruction-tuned with human feedback to generate synthetic preference datasets focusing on aspects like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, requiring only minimal human supervision via a text-based rubric for the model's annotations. These techniques blend two main threads of prior work: reinforcement learning-based training of language models for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and general strategies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
Despite the advantages of utilizing relative human preferences, it remains a substantial practical obstacle to fine-tune large language models with reinforcement learning; the present work introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize for relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond language tasks, research on learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with a variety of methodologies introduced. In contextual bandit problems, where preferences or action rankings are used in place of explicit reward signals, this is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}) scenario. Without access to absolute rewards, theoretical analysis of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with the \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy that achieves at least a 50\% expected win rate when compared to any other policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. A key distinction, however, is that in the CDB formulation, preference feedback is received interactively, whereas in human preference learning, models are typically trained on a static collection of pre-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns from binary preference signals derived from an \textit{unknown} 'scoring' function, rather than from explicit rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Multiple strategies have been proposed for PbRL—including those capable of leveraging previously collected preference data—but these methods usually begin with the explicit estimation of the underlying scoring function (i.e., the reward model) and then optimize policies accordingly \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified approach that directly trains a policy to align with given preferences, bypassing explicit reward model estimation.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (see also \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This pipeline generally consists of three stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) gathering preferences and training a reward model; and (3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by applying supervised fine-tuning to a pre-trained LM using carefully curated datasets relevant to the target application (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: In this subsequent stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. Human annotators are then shown these pairs and asked to indicate their preference, represented as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ correspond to the favored and unfavored responses among $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. These preferences are modeled as being determined by an underlying, unobservable reward function $r^*(y, x)$. A number of statistical models are available for modeling such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a widely adopted choice (though more complex models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can be used when rankings over more than two responses are available). The BT model defines the probability of human preference $p^*$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed set of comparison data $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we introduce a parameterized reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters via maximum likelihood estimation. By casting this as a binary classification task, the associated negative log-likelihood objective is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In practice, for language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, supplemented with a linear projection on top of the last transformer block to output a single scalar representing the reward \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To stabilize the reward estimation and reduce variance, it is common to normalize rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In this phase, the reward model provides evaluative signals to the language model. Consistent with previous work~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\ \|\ \piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ controls how much the policy may diverge from the base reference policy $\piref$, typically set as the initial SFT model $\pisft$.
Usually, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$ as well. The inclusion of this KL regularization term is essential to keep the model’s behavior close to the data distribution where the reward model is reliable, in addition to preserving diversity in generations and mitigating mode collapse. Because language outputs are discrete, this objective is not directly differentiable and is thus commonly handled through reinforcement learning techniques. The prevailing method~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} is to define the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and to maximize this using PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of implementing reinforcement learning algorithms in large-scale settings like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that utilizes preference data directly. Unlike conventional RLHF techniques, which first train a reward model and then use reinforcement learning to maximize it, our method exploits a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to analytically obtain the optimal policy without requiring an RL training loop.

As we will elaborate on shortly, the central idea is to use a closed-form relationship between reward functions and optimal policies, allowing us to reparameterize a loss function defined over reward models as a loss over policies. This change-of-variables strategy eliminates the need for training a separate, explicit reward model, while still optimizing according to familiar preference models like the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network serves as both the language model and the (implicit) reward function.

\textbf{Formulating the DPO Objective.} We begin from the standard RL objective used in previous work, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, considering a generic reward function $r$. As outlined in prior studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the solution optimizing the KL-regularized reward maximization in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} adopts the following structure:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where the normalization constant $Z(x)$ is given by $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. For a detailed derivation, refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. In practice, even when we substitute the true reward $r^*$ with its MLE estimate $r_{\phi}$, evaluating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits the usability of this form. Nonetheless, it is possible to re-express Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to represent the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. This is accomplished by taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing simple manipulations, yielding:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
Applying this transformation to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$, we note that the Bradley-Terry model only depends on reward differences between candidate outputs, namely, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Inserting the form from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference probability in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels, allowing us to write the human preference probability using only the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Therefore, the ideal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry framework satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The complete derivation is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} employs the Bradley-Terry formulation, analogous results for the broader Plackett-Luce family~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the human preference probabilities now expressed in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can establish a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Mirroring the methodology used in reward modeling (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our objective for training the policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this manner, we are effectively learning an implicit reward via a different parameterization, such that the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since this approach is mathematically equivalent to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical characteristics, including consistency given appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We will further explore and analyze DPO’s theoretical attributes compared to related frameworks in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update function?} To gain a deeper, mechanistic intuition for DPO, examining the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ proves useful. The gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$ takes the following form:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))}_\text{greater weight for misclassified completions}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{lower probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implied by the language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for further discussion). In essence, the loss gradient pushes the model to make preferred responses $y_w$ more probable, while reducing the likelihood of less desirable outputs $y_l$. Notably, the degree to which each example impacts the update depends on how much the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly favors the less preferred completion, scaled by $\beta$—effectively measuring the KL constraint’s influence and the reward model’s error. Our results highlight that this weighting mechanism is vital: removing it (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}) can lead to language model collapse.

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The typical DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate two completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, annotate them with human preference labels, and assemble the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, using the selected $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target $\beta$.  
In real-world applications, it is common to make use of existing public preference datasets instead of producing new samples and collecting human labels. Because these datasets are typically built from samples drawn using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ if it is available. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we initialize $\piref$ by fitting to the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, by setting ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach aims to reduce the gap between the ideal (but inaccessible) reference distribution and the $\piref$ actually used in DPO. Additional implementation and hyperparameter information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, supply theoretical justification, and connect the strengths of DPO to the limitations found in actor-critic methods commonly used for RLHF (for instance, PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO manages to avoid both direct reward fitting and reinforcement learning, instead using a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model where rewards are expressed as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We then optimize our parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$, which is mathematically analogous to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after a change of variables. In this section, we will lay out the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the set of possible reward models learned, and prove that the optimal policy can still be exactly recovered. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are called equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, including the Bradley-Terry model, any two reward functions belonging to the same class result in identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class yield identical optimal policies under the constraints of the RL formulation.
\end{lemma}
The arguments supporting these claims are elementary and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a classical under-specification challenge associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is common to apply further identifiability constraints in order to establish theoretical guarantees for the MLE solutions derived from Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions within a single equivalence class give rise to the same optimal policy; therefore, when optimizing our objective, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from this optimal set. We demonstrate the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class that aligns with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically, Bradley-Terry) models can be parameterized as ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be an arbitrary reward function, which induces the optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ specified in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to show that any reward function in the equivalence class of $r$ admits the reparameterization above. Define the transformation $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ rescales the reward by subtracting the log-partition function associated with $\pi_r$. As this normalization depends only on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ still belongs to the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ by the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds generally for any reward function), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, $f$ yields a reward function of the prescribed form within the equivalence class of $r$, ensuring our reward model loses no expressivity with the proposed reparameterization.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} precisely identifies which representative from each equivalence class is chosen by the DPO parameterization: the reward function for which
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (i.e., non-negative values summing to $1$).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} specifies the partition function for the optimal policy induced by the reward $r(x, y)$. 
The central idea behind the DPO method is to introduce specific constraints into the otherwise ambiguous Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference models, so that we retain the set of feasible reward functions, yet ensure that the optimal policy expression in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} becomes analytically manageable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms}
Our framework can also be applied to analyze instabilities in standard actor-critic approaches used for RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF pipeline and specifically examine the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. There are direct links to the control as inference framework \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL problem specified in \ref{eq:RL}. Suppose we use a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seek to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy derived from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} corresponding to the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After some manipulations, this leads to the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This is the same learning objective as in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, employing the DPO-equivalent reward for the class of rewards $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to have large variance, leading to instability in training. One way to handle this normalization is to introduce a learned value function, but this presents its own optimization challenges. Another method, used in previous works, is to normalize rewards with a human completion baseline, which essentially provides a Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization using a single sample. In contrast, the DPO reformulation produces a reward function that naturally avoids the need for any baselines.

\section{Experiments}
Here, we empirically investigate how well DPO can train policies based solely on preferences. To start, in a controlled text-generation environment, we examine how effectively DPO balances reward maximization with KL-divergence minimization relative to standard preference optimization approaches like PPO. We then assess DPO's capabilities on larger model scales and more challenging RLHF problems, such as summarization and conversation tasks. Our findings show that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO typically matches or surpasses robust baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as outperforming the strategy of selecting the top trajectory from $N$ samples based on a learned reward. Prior to discussing these outcomes, we outline our experimental procedures; supplementary information is included in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our experimental setup covers three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all experiments, the algorithms are trained to optimize a policy using a dataset of preference samples, $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents the beginning of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective is for the policy to produce a continuation $y$ expressing positive sentiment. For this task, to enable controlled evaluation, we \textit{construct} preference pairs by generating outputs and ranking them with a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we adapt GPT-2-large to the IMDB training split by supervised fine-tuning, training until convergence (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for additional information). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum thread, and the policy must output a summary $y$ capturing the core ideas of the post. We follow previous approaches using the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, along with human preference judgments collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT baseline is fine-tuned on human-crafted Reddit summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and utilizes the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} implementation for RLHF. Notably, the human preference data comes from samples generated by a related but not identical SFT model, as described by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Lastly, the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task presents $x$ as a human message, ranging from scientific inquiries to personal advice requests. The policy must generate a relevant and useful response $y$. Here, we leverage the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170,000 conversations between people and an AI assistant, with each record ending in two model-generated responses and a preference label that identifies which response the human favored. In this scenario, since there is no available pre-trained SFT model, we create one by supervised fine-tuning a generic language model solely on preferred completions.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments utilize two distinct methods for evaluation. To investigate how effectively each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we assess each algorithm in the controlled sentiment generation scenario by plotting its trade-off frontier between achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy. This is feasible since we have access to the ground-truth reward function, provided by a sentiment classifier. Conversely, in practical scenarios where the true reward function is unavailable, we evaluate algorithms by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 serves as an automated judge, standing in for human evaluation of summary quality in summarization and of helpfulness in single-turn dialogue. In the summarization task, we take the test set's reference summaries as the baseline; in dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response from the test data. Although prior work indicates that LMs can act as more reliable automated evaluators than conventional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we also run a human study (see Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}) to validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation. Our results reveal that GPT-4 assessments show a strong correlation with human judgments, with human-GPT-4 agreement generally matching or surpassing the agreement between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a range of established strategies for training language models to align with human preferences. For the summarization task, we try zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, and for the dialogue task, we apply 2-shot prompting using \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. Additionally, we consider both the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, the latter being a model trained via supervised learning using the selected response $y_w$—sourced from either the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another semi-supervised approach we evaluate is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which modifies the objective to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while explicitly decreasing the likelihood of $y_l$, with an optional scaling factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood component. We further include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, where the reward signal is derived from preference-based data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle model that learns from the true reward function available in the controlled sentiment scenario. For sentiment tasks, we compare two PPO-GT implementations: one off-the-shelf \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and one customized version with normalized rewards and tuned hyperparameters for enhanced performance (these enhancements are also applied when using PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we examine the \textbf{Best of $N$} strategy, where $N$ completions are generated from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue), and the top-scoring completion—according to a reward model trained on preference data—is selected. While this approach is effective, it is computationally expensive for even moderate $N$, since it necessitates generating $N$ completions per input at inference time.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates estimated by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue; among the evaluated methods, only DPO surpasses the performance of the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. The gains achieved by DPO over the dataset labels remain relatively consistent at different sampling temperatures during training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-penalized reward maximization objective commonly utilized in RLHF methods ensures a trade-off between leveraging reward signals and constraining policy divergence from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating and comparing different methods, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the magnitude of the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the expense of a much greater KL is often not preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL tradeoff curves for multiple approaches in the sentiment analysis scenario. We conduct several training runs for every algorithm, varying the policy conservativeness parameter in each trial (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 experiments. Every 100 training iterations, up to convergence, we assess each model on a batch of test prompts by calculating both the mean reward (using the actual reward function) and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{This means the total sum of the KL-divergence computed at each timestep.} compared to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO establishes a markedly better frontier, securing the highest rewards while maintaining low KL values. This outcome is significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO target the same underlying objective, DPO demonstrates greater efficiency; DPO’s reward-to-KL ratio consistently outperforms that of PPO. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO’s frontier even in cases where PPO has access to the true reward signals (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We then assess how well DPO performs when fine-tuned on summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks. For summarization, traditional metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies indicate that tuning language models with PPO guided by human preferences leads to better summaries. Our evaluation compares various approaches by generating outputs on the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test split and calculating the mean win rate against reference outputs. For each method, outputs are sampled at temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with results displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all use the same GPT-J SFT model as their starting point\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. At a temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, surpassing PPO’s best win rate of approximately 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO achieves a greater peak win rate than the $N$-best baseline. It is worth mentioning that we did not specifically optimize the $\beta$ hyperparameter for DPO, so its results may be a lower bound on its possible effectiveness. DPO also demonstrates greater robustness to changes in temperature compared to PPO, which can perform as poorly as the original GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT, on the other hand, yields little improvement over the SFT baseline. Furthermore, in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we directly compare DPO and PPO using human raters, finding that outputs from DPO at temperature 0.25 were selected over PPO outputs at temperature 0 approximately 58\% of the time.

For single-turn dialogues, we assess the various approaches using a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split, specifically focusing on interactions consisting of a single exchange between human and assistant. For GPT-4 assessments, we use the preferred test completions as references to calculate the win rates across methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this benchmark, we initialize with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on selected completions to ensure outputs match the model's distribution, and subsequently proceed with DPO training. We also compare against the strongest of 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at $N=128$ for this scenario; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model, observing that DPO achieves equal or superior results at optimal sampling temperatures for each method. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained using PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but are unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that surpasses the base Pythia-2.8B model’s performance. Based on our TL;DR findings and the shared reward function optimization, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method that consistently surpasses the preferred completions within the Anthropic HH dataset, while delivering results on par with or better than the much more resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Furthermore, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO rapidly reaches peak performance.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing competent, aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward method for training language models using preferences, all without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit conventional RL frameworks for the sake of using standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows models to be optimized to reflect human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy objective, removing the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. DPO achieves comparable or superior performance to current RLHF techniques, including those utilizing PPO, and requires minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO substantially lowers the obstacles to training language models aligned with human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings highlight a number of significant directions for future investigation. For instance, how does the generalization ability of the DPO policy outside the training distribution compare to that of policies trained via explicit reward signals? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize on par with those trained using PPO, but further systematic analysis is warranted. Another open question is whether utilizing self-labeling from the DPO policy can be as effective for leveraging unlabeled prompts. Additionally, it remains unclear how reward over-optimization appears under the direct preference optimization paradigm, and whether the modest performance drop seen in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is an example of this phenomenon. While our assessments have been limited to models with up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO approaches to much larger, state-of-the-art models presents an intriguing avenue for upcoming research. On the topic of evaluation, we observe that win rates derived from GPT-4 are sensitive to prompt choice; subsequent work could explore optimal strategies for eliciting reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, the applications of DPO extend well beyond training language models from human feedback, with potential for use in training generative models across various domains.
