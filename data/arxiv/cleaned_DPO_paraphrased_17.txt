\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, exerting precise control over their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised.
Current approaches to improving steerability involve gathering human judgments comparing model outputs, then fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to reflect these preferences, commonly using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
Nonetheless, RLHF is a complicated and sometimes unstable process, involving first training a reward model to capture human preferences, and subsequently optimizing the unsupervised LM through reinforcement learning to maximize the predicted reward while keeping the LM close to its original state.
In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model in RLHF that allows for analytic extraction of the optimal policy, reducing the RLHF procedure to a straightforward classification loss.
We refer to this approach as \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO). DPO is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the need for LM sampling during fine-tuning and significantly reducing hyperparameter tuning requirements.
Our experiments demonstrate that DPO is capable of fine-tuning LMs to follow human preferences at least as well as, and sometimes better than, existing techniques. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling output sentiment, and either matches or outperforms these baselines in response quality for summarization and single-turn dialogue, while also being notably easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora demonstrate remarkable abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these systems learn from human-generated data encompassing a vast array of intentions, values, and proficiencies. Certain behaviors or skills found in this data might not be desirable for imitation; for instance, although it is important for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent coding errors so it can correct them, we would prefer the model to favor the (possibly uncommon) examples of strong coding expertise within the training set when actually producing code. In a similar vein, we may want the model to be \textit{informed} about a widespread misconception held by half the population, yet it would be undesirable for the LM to reflect that mistaken belief in half of its answers to relevant questions. Put simply, it is essential to distinguish and select the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{repertoire of information and skills} to construct AI tools that are safe, reliable, and easy to direct \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although most current techniques align LMs with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the objective underlying RL-based alignment approaches can be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, streamlining the process of preference modeling.

Broadly speaking, current approaches teach language models the intended behaviors by utilizing carefully selected collections of human preferences, which reflect what people deem to be safe and beneficial. This process of preference learning takes place following an initial phase of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human-provided demonstrations of high-quality answers, the most effective techniques to date rely on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on annotated human preferences, and reinforcement learning is then applied to adjust the language model policy so that it generates responses with high assigned rewards, while remaining close to the pre-trained model’s behavior. Despite the fact that RLHF yields models with remarkable capabilities in dialogue and code generation, the RLHF process is substantially more intricate than standard supervised learning, requiring the training of several language models and policy sampling during optimization, which leads to much higher computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for directly tuning a language model to match human preferences, without the need for explicit reward models or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that inherently targets the same objective as standard RLHF algorithms (maximizing reward while regularizing with a KL-divergence constraint), yet offers a straightforward and easy-to-train framework. Conceptually, DPO updates the model by boosting the relative log-likelihood of preferred responses over non-preferred ones, but it adds a per-sample, dynamically adjusted importance weight. This mechanism prevents the model collapse that arises from using a simple probability ratio objective. DPO, like previous approaches, utilizes a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how accurately a reward function reflects real-world preference data. However, instead of employing the preference model to create a loss for training a reward model and then separately training a policy to maximize this learned reward, DPO performs a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. With a dataset containing human preference comparisons of model outputs, DPO can thus train a policy using a binary cross entropy loss, resulting in an optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function that best fits the observed preferences.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward approach that eliminates the need for reinforcement learning when training language models based on preference data. Experimental results indicate that DPO performs on par with, or better than, established techniques—such as PPO-based RLHF—for preference-driven learning across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and conversational modeling, even when applied to language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of increasing size have demonstrated the ability to solve some problems in a zero-shot fashion \citep{radford2019language}, or when given just a few examples as prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user instructions can be greatly enhanced by additional fine-tuning on corpora consisting of instructions and human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' helps LLMs generalize to tasks beyond those explicitly covered in the tuning data and overall makes them more practical for users \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction-tuning has yielded notable results, collecting \textit{relative} assessments of response quality from people is often more feasible than gathering expert-curated demonstrations. For this reason, later research has fine-tuned LLMs on human preference data, which has led to improvements in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These techniques first train a reward model—often based on preference models like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—to reflect human preferences, and then further tune the language model to maximize this reward signal through reinforcement learning strategies, such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another closely related approach leverages LLMs already refined for instruction adherence with human feedback to produce new, synthetic preference data focused on qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}. In these cases, supervision consists only of textual guidelines for the LLM's labeling. This intersection unifies advances in both reinforcement learning-based training of language models for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and general-purpose frameworks for learning from human feedback \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of relying on relative human preferences, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models poses substantial practical barriers. This study introduces a theoretically sound method for optimizing based on relative preferences, eliminating the need for RL.

Beyond the realm of language, the task of learning policies from preferences has been examined in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of various methodologies. When contextual bandit algorithms rely on preferences or rankings over actions instead of traditional reward signals, this paradigm is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In settings where absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical studies on CDBs replace the standard concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, which is a policy that achieves an expected win probability of at least 50\% against \textit{every} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, CDB frameworks typically assume preference labels are provided in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences often uses a fixed, offline dataset consisting of preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) focuses on learning from binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} scoring function instead of explicit rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Several PbRL strategies have been proposed, including algorithms capable of utilizing off-policy preference data, but these approaches generally require first learning the underlying scoring (reward) function before subsequently optimizing it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a single-stage policy learning method that seeks to directly optimize the policy according to observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This pipeline typically involves three main steps: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and (3) optimization via reinforcement learning.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with supervised fine-tuning of a pre-trained LM on high-quality datasets relevant to the target application (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During the second stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are evaluated by human annotators, who indicate their preference for one answer, written as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the chosen and non-chosen completions from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. The preference data are presumed to stem from an unknown reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains inaccessible. Various preference modeling strategies exist, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a standard approach (though more general ranking systems like the Plackett-Luce models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be applied when multiple ranked responses are available). In the BT model, the probability distribution over human preferences $p^*$ is described as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a static collection of comparison data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can define a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Treating this as a binary classification task, the corresponding negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ stands for the sigmoid activation. When dealing with LMs, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ typically initializes from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, with a linear head added atop the final transformer block to output a single scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variance, previous studies often normalize the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the language model receives feedback from the reward function it has learned. As in previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ serves as a regularization coefficient that determines how much the current policy $\pi_\theta$ is allowed to diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which is the pretrained SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$. Including this KL constraint is essential, as it restricts the model from straying excessively from the data distribution on which the reward model is valid, while also helping to preserve diversity in the generated outputs and avoiding mode collapse to only a few high-reward responses. Because language generation involves discrete tokens, the objective cannot be optimized through standard gradient-based methods and is usually approached using reinforcement learning algorithms. The typical method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} constructs the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and maximizes it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of implementing reinforcement learning methods on large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward policy optimization method that works directly with preferences. In contrast to traditional RLHF approaches—which first fit a reward model and then optimize it using reinforcement learning—our method uses a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows us to determine the optimal policy analytically, removing the need for an RL training process.

As we detail below, our central idea is to use an analytic relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies. This enables us to reformulate a loss over reward functions as a loss over policies. By employing this change-of-variables method, we sidestep the need to learn an explicit, separate reward model, yet we continue to optimize according to established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Effectively, the policy network simultaneously embodies the language model and an implicit reward model.

\textbf{Formulating the DPO objective.} We begin with the standard RL objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, considering a general reward function $r$. As established by previous research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the optimal policy for the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ denotes the normalization factor. Refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1} for the full derivation. Even when employing the MLE estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally challenging~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which limits the direct practical use of this parameterization. Nonetheless, we can manipulate Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to reformulate the reward in terms of its optimal policy $\pi_r$, the base policy $\piref$, and the unknown $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
We can substitute this formulation for the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Conveniently, the Bradley-Terry preference model only involves the reward gap between two candidates, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging in the reparameterized form of $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the normalization terms cancel out, allowing the human preference probability to be expressed strictly in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, under the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies the following preference probability:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
A full derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is built on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations can be carried out for the more general Plackett-Luce family~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having now expressed the probability of human preferences in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our objective for the policy is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Through this formulation, we are effectively capturing an implicit reward via a different parameterization, for which the optimal policy is simply $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because this method is mathematically equivalent to optimizing a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it possesses some desirable theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate further on these theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related approaches in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update function?} To gain a mechanistic intuition of DPO, it's instructive to inspect the gradient of the DPO loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when the reward prediction is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{encourage $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{discourage $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward that is implicitly determined by both the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (explored further in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ serves to boost the probability of preferred responses $y_w$ and lower the probability of less preferred responses $y_l$. Crucially, the influence of each example is modulated by how much more highly the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ scores the less preferred response, scaled by $\beta$—essentially, this weighting reflects the degree to which the implicit reward model misorders the completions, while factoring in the strength of the KL penalty. Our empirical findings highlight the necessity of this weighting mechanism: a simple version omitting the weighting factor can lead the language model to fail (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The typical DPO workflow includes: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$ and annotating them with human preference data, resulting in an offline set of preference pairs $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the DPO loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using the selected $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a chosen $\beta$ value.  
In real-world settings, it is desirable to leverage publicly available preference datasets rather than collect new samples and human annotations. Because these preference collections are typically drawn from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever that model exists. If $\pisft$ is missing, we define $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$: specifically, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the mismatch between the inaccessible true reference distribution and the reference $\piref$ employed by DPO. Additional implementation details and hyperparameter settings are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, present supporting theoretical arguments, and connect the strengths of DPO to the limitations present in actor-critic methods applied in RLHF scenarios (like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}
DPO eliminates the need to explicitly fit a reward or to use RL for policy training by employing a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} aligns with a Bradley-Terry model whose rewards are expressed as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we train our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, mirroring the reward model optimization in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} via a change of variables. This section develops the underlying theory for this reparameterization, demonstrates that it imposes no additional limitations on the reward model class, and ensures precise retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)$.    
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. This leads us to the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the context of the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry variant, any two reward functions belonging to the same class yield identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class will produce the identical optimal policy when solving the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and are presented in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma illustrates a common under-specification issue inherent in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Owing to this ambiguity, additional identifiability restrictions are typically necessary to ensure well-defined MLE solutions in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function in a given equivalence class determines the same optimal policy, so for our main objective, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from the optimal equivalence class. The following Theorem is established in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild regularity conditions, all reward classes compatible with the Plackett-Luce model (including Bradley-Terry as a special case) can be captured via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, for some policy $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be an arbitrary reward function, which determines the optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed using the stated reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the log-partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since this normalization depends only on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Now, substituting $r$ from the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds for any $r$), yields $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, $f$ constructs a canonical representative from the equivalence class of $r$ in the required form, confirming that the proposed reparameterization does not reduce the expressive power of our reward model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as precisely identifying which reward within each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization, namely, the reward satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Theorem~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a valid probability distribution (i.e., non-negative and sums to 1).
Using Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we observe that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} is equivalent to the partition function for the optimal policy corresponding to $r(x, y)$.
The central insight of the DPO algorithm is that we can enforce specific constraints within the under-specified Plackett-Luce (and especially the Bradley-Terry) preference models in a way that preserves the full class of reward models, but ensures that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} is always analytically computable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms}
Our framework can also help identify sources of instability in widely used actor-critic methods for RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF process, we focus on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating the problem to the control as inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation given in \ref{eq:RL}, we proceed. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, derived from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Some algebra leads us to the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective is identical to those used in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} which apply the DPO-equivalent reward function within the $r_{\phi}$ reward family. Here, the normalization inside $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function for the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not alter the optimal policy, excluding it can increase the variance of the policy gradient, thus making training unstable. To address this, one can learn a value function to estimate the normalization, but this approach can be challenging in practice. Another strategy found in earlier works is to normalize rewards by subtracting a baseline reward computed from a human completion, effectively serving as a single-sample Monte-Carlo estimate of the normalization term. Conversely, the DPO formulation naturally results in a reward function that eliminates the need for any baseline.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we provide an empirical assessment of DPO’s effectiveness at learning policies directly from preference data. We begin by examining a controlled text-generation scenario, investigating how well DPO balances reward maximization against KL-divergence to the reference policy, especially in comparison with widely used preference optimization approaches like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF applications, such as summarization and dialogue. Our experiments show that, even with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or outperforms strong baselines like PPO-based RLHF, as well as the strategy of choosing the best out of $N$ sampled trajectories using a learned reward model. Prior to reporting these findings, we outline our experimental methodology; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We evaluate our methods across three distinct open-ended text generation scenarios. In all cases, the algorithms learn a policy based on a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ refers to a partial movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is tasked with generating a continuation $y$ that conveys a positive sentiment. To objectively assess performance, we synthesize preference pairs over generated continuations using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_l)$. For the SFT baseline, we adapt GPT-2-large through fine-tuning on training reviews from IMDb until performance stabilizes (additional specifics can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). For the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ consists of a Reddit forum post, and the policy must summarize the core ideas in $y$. Following established protocols, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization set \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference data compiled by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We train an SFT model on reference forum summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF framework. The human preferences were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on outputs from a separate, but comparably-trained, SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ is a user prompt, which may range from scientific inquiries to personal advice. Here, the policy is required to provide an informative and engaging answer $y$; for this we leverage the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170,000 conversations between users and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two model-generated responses and a human preference indicating which one is favored. Because there is no existing SFT model for this dataset, we create one by fine-tuning a pre-trained language model only on the preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We utilize two distinct evaluation methods in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we first analyze performance in a controlled sentiment generation scenario. Here, we plot each algorithm’s Pareto frontier of attained reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is possible because we have direct access to the actual reward function (a sentiment classifier). In contrast, in realistic scenarios where the true reward function is unavailable, we measure algorithm performance using their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy. For this, we rely on GPT-4 as an automated surrogate for human evaluation—assessing summary quality and helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. In summarization, the test set’s reference summaries serve as the baseline; for dialogue, we use the preferred responses in the dataset as our baseline. Although prior work indicates that LMs may outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we supplement our analysis with a human study, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, to validate our choice of GPT-4 for evaluation. Our results show that GPT-4’s assessments have a high correlation with those of humans, with human-GPT-4 agreement often matching or exceeding agreement between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a range of existing methods for training language models to align with human preferences. As baseline approaches, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on the summarization task and employ 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue generation. We further include the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, the latter being a model trained with supervised fine-tuning on the preferred completion $y_w$—originating either from the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization tasks) or from a standard LM (in the single-turn dialogue setting). Another method, \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, optimizes the policy by encouraging higher likelihood for $y_w$ and lower likelihood for $y_l$; an adjustable coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ controls the strength of the ‘unlikelihood’ component. We also experiment with \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, where a reward model trained on preference data is used, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, which acts as an oracle model trained with access to the true reward function in the controlled sentiment setup. For sentiment tasks, we run both a standard PPO-GT implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a version with reward normalization and additional hyperparameter tuning for enhanced results (these enhancements are also adopted for regular PPO runs with learned rewards). As an additional strong baseline, we consider the \textbf{Best of $N$} method: this involves sampling $N$ candidate completions from either the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue tasks), and selecting the one with the highest score according to a reward model learned from preference data. Although this approach effectively separates reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive, since each test query requires $N$ samples.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 assessed win rates for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogues; among the methods, only DPO surpasses the performance of selected summaries within the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across varying sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms dataset labels across training for different temperature settings.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-penalized reward maximization objective commonly used in RLHF approaches aims to optimize reward while preventing the policy from straying too far from a reference policy. As a result, evaluating algorithms requires considering both the attained reward and the KL divergence; increasing reward at the cost of significantly higher KL is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward-versus-KL frontier for different algorithms within the sentiment context. For each algorithm, we conduct several training runs, each with a distinct policy conservativeness parameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. Every 100 training steps, up to convergence, we assess each policy on a collection of test prompts, recording both the mean reward under the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the total KL divergence across all timesteps.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO yields the most effective frontier, obtaining the greatest reward for a given KL, while also maintaining low KL values. This is particularly remarkable for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO are trained on the same objective, DPO is substantially more sample-efficient; DPO's reward-KL curve consistently outperforms that of PPO. Second, DPO surpasses the best frontier achieved by PPO, \emph{even when PPO is granted access to ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of handling large-scale real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess DPO's fine-tuning capabilities on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, standard automated metrics like ROUGE are known to have weak correlations with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous research has demonstrated that leveraging PPO to fine-tune language models based on human preferences can result in better-quality summaries. Our evaluation involves generating outputs on the TL;DR summarization dataset's test set and calculating the mean win rate relative to reference completions. Outputs for each method are sampled across temperature values from 0.0 to 1.0, with the corresponding win rates depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all use the same GPT-J SFT model as their starting point\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results indicate that DPO achieves a win rate near 61\% at a sampling temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO, which attains roughly 57\% at its best temperature setting of 0.0. In addition, DPO attains a higher peak win rate than the best of $N$ baseline. It's worth mentioning that the $\beta$ hyperparameter for DPO was not extensively optimized, implying that there may be further gains possible. Furthermore, DPO displays considerably greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance may drop to the level of the original GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT yields only marginal gains over the SFT baseline. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO in human studies is presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, showing that DPO completions sampled at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO completions sampled at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various methods using the subset of the Anthropic HH test split \citep{bai2022training} that contains just one human-assistant exchange. In GPT-4 evaluations, we use the test set's preferred completions as a reference to calculate the win rates across methods. Since there is no established SFT model for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions to ensure the outputs are in-distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. We also benchmark against the strongest of 128 Preferred-FT completions (our experiments show the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at 128 completions for this scenario; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or outperforms these approaches at their optimal temperature settings. Additionally, we test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we do not identify a prompt or temperature configuration that allows it to surpass the base Pythia-2.8B performance. Given our TL;DR results and the fact that both strategies optimize the same reward, we use Best of 128 as an approximate stand-in for PPO-level effectiveness. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient approach that consistently exceeds the performance of the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and rivals or surpasses the resource-intensive Best of 128 method. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO quickly reaches its optimal performance.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers an effective and scalable approach for developing competent, aligned language models. In this work, we have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that allows language models to learn from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning challenge to fit a conventional RL framework for the sake of leveraging standard RL tools, DPO establishes a correspondence between reward functions and language model policies. This makes it possible to train models to adhere to human preferences \textit{directly} using a basic cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. DPO achieves comparable or superior results to current RLHF approaches, such as those utilizing PPO, and requires almost no hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO significantly lowers the difficulty of training language models with human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings open up a number of important avenues for subsequent research. One outstanding question is how well the DPO policy can generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios in comparison to approaches that learn from an explicit reward function. Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies might generalize on par with models trained using PPO, but a deeper investigation is warranted. Another interesting possibility is whether self-labeling during DPO training can leverage unlabeled prompts as effectively. Furthermore, questions remain regarding how reward over-optimization appears in the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the modest drop in performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is a reflection of this phenomenon. Although our evaluation encompasses models with up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to significantly larger, cutting-edge models remains a promising area for further exploration. In terms of evaluation, we observe that win rates assessed via GPT-4 are sensitive to the phrasing of the prompt, suggesting that future work should consider strategies for obtaining more reliable automated judgments. Lastly, DPO has a wide range of potential applications beyond preference-based language model training, such as optimizing generative models in alternative modalities.
