\title{Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale text-to-image diffusion models demonstrate remarkable ability to create photorealistic images from textual descriptions. However, effectively steering or controlling these powerful models to accomplish various downstream tasks remains a significant open challenge. To address this, we propose a principled finetuning approach—Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT)—for adapting text-to-image diffusion models to specific downstream tasks. Unlike prior methods, OFT is theoretically guaranteed to preserve hyperspherical energy, which quantifies the pairwise relationships among neurons on the unit hypersphere. We discover that maintaining this property is vital for retaining the semantic generative capacity of text-to-image diffusion models. To further enhance finetuning stability, we introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which incorporates an additional radius constraint on the hypersphere. Our focus is on two key finetuning tasks: subject-driven generation, where the objective is to create subject-specific images conditioned on a few images and a text prompt, and controllable generation, where the model integrates extra control signals. Empirically, we demonstrate that our OFT approach surpasses existing methods in terms of generation quality and convergence speed.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent advancements in text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical,rombach2022high} have resulted in impressive capabilities for text-guided high-fidelity image synthesis. Despite these advances, relying solely on text prompts can be ambiguous and insufficient for delivering detailed and precise control over generated images. To overcome these limitations, this paper addresses two categories of text-to-image generation tasks:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

    \item \textbf{Subject-driven generation}~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}: The task is to produce images of a particular subject in novel contexts based on only a few reference images and a text prompt.
    \item \textbf{Controllable generation}~\cite{zhang2023adding,mou2023t2i}: The objective is to generate images conditioned on an additional control signal (e.g., canny edges, segmentation maps) alongside a text prompt.
\end{itemize}

Both tasks fundamentally involve determining how to finetune text-to-image diffusion models effectively without compromising the generative performance acquired during pretraining. We outline the key requirements for an effective finetuning approach as: (1) \emph{training efficiency}: minimizing the number of trainable parameters and training epochs, and (2) \emph{generalizability preservation}: maintaining high-quality and diverse generative capabilities. To achieve this, finetuning is generally performed either by adjusting neuron weights with a small learning rate (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}) or by incorporating a small additional component with re-parameterized neuron weights (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{hulora2022,zhang2023adding}). Although straightforward, neither finetuning method reliably ensures the retention of pretrained generative performance. Moreover, there is a lack of foundational insights for designing an effective finetuning strategy and selecting appropriate hyperparameters, such as the number of training epochs. A central challenge is the absence of a metric to quantify how well the pretrained generative ability is preserved. Current finetuning techniques implicitly presume that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained models corresponds to better retention of pretrained capabilities. Consequently, finetuning is often conducted with very small learning rates. While this assumption may sometimes hold true, we contend that Euclidean distance alone does not fully reflect the extent of semantic preservation. Therefore, adopting a more structural measure to characterize the differences between the finetuned and pretrained models could significantly enhance both the preservation of pretrained performance and the stability of finetuning.

Motivated by empirical findings that hyperspherical similarity effectively captures semantic information~\cite{liu2017deep,liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular}, we employ hyperspherical energy~\cite{liu2018learning} to describe the pairwise relational structure among neurons. Hyperspherical energy is defined as the aggregate of hyperspherical similarities (such as cosine similarity) between every pair of neurons within the same layer, reflecting the degree of neuron uniformity on the unit hypersphere~\cite{liu2021learning}. We posit that an optimal finetuned model should exhibit minimal changes in hyperspherical energy relative to the pretrained model. A straightforward approach might involve adding a regularizer to maintain constant hyperspherical energy throughout finetuning; however, this does not guarantee effective minimization of the energy difference. To address this, we leverage a key invariance property of hyperspherical energy—pairwise hyperspherical similarities remain unchanged under a shared orthogonal transformation applied to all neurons. Inspired by this invariance, we introduce Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), which adapts large text-to-image diffusion models for downstream tasks while preserving their hyperspherical energy. The core concept is to learn a layer-wise shared orthogonal transformation for neurons that maintains their pairwise angles. OFT can also be interpreted as modifying the canonical coordinate system of neurons within the same layer. Additionally, considering that a smaller Euclidean distance between the finetuned and pretrained models correlates with better retention of pretraining performance, we propose a variant called Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT). COFT restricts the finetuned model to lie within a hypersphere of fixed radius centered on the pretrained neurons.

The rationale behind why orthogonal transformation is effective for finetuning neurons partly derives from the 2D Fourier transform, which decomposes an image into magnitude and phase spectra. The phase spectrum, representing the angular relationship between the input and basis, retains most of the semantic information. For instance, an image’s phase spectrum combined with a random magnitude spectrum can still reconstruct the original image without losing semantic content. This observation implies that modifying the directions of neurons is crucial for semantically altering the generated image, which is the objective of both subject-driven and controllable generation. Nevertheless, allowing neurons to change direction with high freedom tends to disrupt the pretrained generative capabilities. To limit this freedom, we propose preserving the angle between every pair of neurons, grounded on the hypothesis that inter-neuron angles are essential for encoding neural network knowledge. With this insight, it naturally follows to learn layer-wise orthogonal transformations for neurons such that the hyperspherical energy remains invariant.

Additionally, we draw motivation from orthogonal over-parameterized training~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}, which trains classification neural networks from scratch by applying orthogonal transformations to a randomly initialized network. This approach relies on the fact that a randomly initialized network has an expectedly low hyperspherical energy, and the goal of \cite{liu2021orthogonal} is to maintain this low hyperspherical energy during training (since lower energy correlates with better classification generalization~\cite{liu2018learning,lin2020regularizing}). \cite{liu2021orthogonal} demonstrates that orthogonal transformation provides enough flexibility to train neural networks that generalize well for classification tasks. In contrast, our focus is on finetuning text-to-image diffusion models to improve controllability and enhance downstream generative performance. We highlight two main differences between OFT and \cite{liu2021orthogonal}. First, while \cite{liu2021orthogonal} aims to minimize hyperspherical energy, OFT seeks to maintain the same hyperspherical energy as the pretrained model, thereby preserving the intrinsic pretrained structure during finetuning. Minimizing hyperspherical energy when finetuning diffusion models might disrupt the original semantic structures. Second, OFT strives to minimize deviation from the pretrained model, resulting in a constrained formulation, whereas \cite{liu2021orthogonal} does not enforce such constraints. Effective finetuning involves balancing flexibility and stability, and we contend that our OFT framework successfully accomplishes this balance. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

    \item We introduce a new finetuning technique—Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)—designed to enhance the controllability of text-to-image diffusion models. To boost stability further, we present a constrained version that restricts the angular deviation from the original pretrained model.
    \item Unlike existing finetuning approaches, OFT carries out model finetuning while provably maintaining the hyperspherical energy, which our experiments indicate is a crucial metric for preserving the pretrained model’s generative semantic integrity.
    \item We demonstrate OFT on two applications: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Through extensive empirical evaluation, we show marked improvements over previous methods in generation quality, convergence speed, and finetuning stability. Additionally, OFT exhibits superior sample efficiency, achieving strong convergence with substantially fewer training images and epochs.
    \item For controllable generation, we propose a novel control consistency metric to quantitatively assess controllability. The key concept involves estimating the control signal from the generated image and comparing it to the original control input. This metric further supports the high controllability achieved by OFT.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Text-to-image diffusion models}. Significant advancements~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical,rombach2022high,gu2022vector,nichol2022glide} have been achieved in text-to-image synthesis, largely driven by rapid progress in diffusion-based generative modeling~\cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score,song2021denoising,dhariwal2021diffusion} and vision-language representation learning~\cite{su2020vlbert,lu2019vilbert,radford2021learning,wang2021simvlm,li2022blip,li2023blip,alayrac2022flamingo,schuhmann2022laion}. GLIDE~\cite{nichol2022glide} and Imagen~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic} operate by training diffusion models directly in pixel space. GLIDE jointly trains the text encoder with a diffusion prior using paired text-image datasets, whereas Imagen utilizes a frozen pretrained text encoder. Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} and DALL-E2~\cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} train diffusion models in latent spaces. Stable Diffusion employs VQ-GAN~\cite{esser2021taming} to learn a visual codebook for its latent representation, while DALL-E2 leverages CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} to create a shared latent embedding space for images and text. Beyond diffusion models, text-to-image generation has also been explored with generative adversarial networks~\cite{reed2016generative,zhang2017stackgan,xu2018attngan,li2019controllable} and autoregressive models~\cite{ramesh2021zero,ding2021cogview,wu2022nuwa,yuscaling}. OFT is a fundamentally model-agnostic finetuning strategy and can be applied to any text-to-image diffusion architecture.

\textbf{Subject-driven generation}. To avoid altering the subject, approaches such as \cite{avrahami2022blended,nichol2022glide} treat a user-provided mask as an additional conditioning input. Inversion techniques~\cite{choi2021ilvr,dhariwal2021diffusion,ramesh2022hierarchical,gal2022image} enable modifications to the context without changing the subject itself. The method in \cite{hertz2022prompt} supports both local and global edits without requiring input masks. However, these methods generally struggle to maintain identity-specific details of the subject. Pivotal Tuning~\cite{roich2022pivotal} finetunes the generator near an initially inverted latent code with added regularization to preserve identity. Similarly, \cite{nitzan2022mystyle} learns a personalized generative prior for faces from a collection of that individual’s images. The approach in \cite{casanova2021instance} produces diverse variations of an instance but may fail to retain unique instance characteristics. DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} finetunes a text-to-image diffusion model using a few subject images with a customized token, combining reconstruction loss and class-specific prior preservation loss. OFT builds on the DreamBooth framework but, instead of straightforward finetuning with a low learning rate, applies orthogonal transformations during finetuning.

\textbf{Controllable generation}. Image-to-image translation can be interpreted as a type of controllable generation, with earlier approaches predominantly utilizing conditional generative adversarial networks~\cite{isola2017image,zhu2017toward,wang2018high,park2019semantic,choi2018stargan}. Diffusion models have also been applied to image-to-image translation tasks~\cite{saharia2022palette,voynov2022sketch,wang2022pretraining}. More recently, ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} introduces a method to control a pretrained diffusion model by fine-tuning and adapting it with supplementary control signals, achieving remarkable controllable generation results. Another parallel and analogous work, T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}, similarly fine-tunes a pretrained diffusion model to enhance controllability over the generated outputs. In line with the task framework established in \cite{zhang2023adding,mou2023t2i}, we apply OFT to fine-tune pretrained diffusion models, consistently achieving improved controllability with fewer training samples and reduced fine-tuning parameters. Notably, OFT does not add any extra computational cost during inference.

\textbf{Model finetuning}. Fine-tuning large pretrained models for downstream applications has become increasingly prevalent~\cite{devlin2018bert,brown2020language,he2022masked}. Within this context, adaptation techniques (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{hulora2022,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion}) have been extensively explored in natural language processing. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} represents the work most closely related to OFT, positing a low-rank structure for the additive weight modifications during fine-tuning. By contrast, OFT employs a layer-shared orthogonal transformation to adjust neuron weights multiplicatively, which provably preserves the pairwise angles between neurons within the same layer, resulting in enhanced stability.

\section{Orthogonal Finetuning}

\subsection{Why Does Orthogonal Transformation Make Sense?}

We begin by examining why orthogonal transformation is beneficial when fine-tuning text-to-image diffusion models. This question can be divided into two sub-questions: (1) why it is important to fine-tune the angles of neurons (i.e., their directions), and (2) why orthogonal transformation is the chosen approach to adjust these angles.

Regarding the first question, we take inspiration from the empirical findings in \cite{liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular} that angular differences in features effectively capture the semantic gap. SphereNet~\cite{liu2017deep} demonstrates that training a neural network with all neurons normalized onto a unit hypersphere maintains comparable capacity and even enhances generalizability, suggesting that neuron directions alone can encapsulate the most critical information from data. To further illustrate the significance of neuron angles, we carry out a simple experiment depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}, where a conventional convolutional autoencoder is trained on several flower images. During training, the feature map is generated using the standard inner product ($z$ represents the element output of the convolution kernel $\bm{w}$ and $\bm{x}$ is the input within the sliding window). At test time, we compare three methods of generating the feature map: (a) the inner product as used in training, (b) the magnitude component, and (c) the angular component. The outcomes in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae} reveal that the angular information of neurons nearly perfectly reconstructs the input images, whereas neuron magnitude carries no meaningful information. It is important to note that the cosine activation (c) was not employed during training, which was solely based on the inner product. These findings suggest that neuron angles (directions) play a predominant role in encoding the semantic content of input images. Consequently, finetuning neuron directions is likely more effective for altering image semantics.

For the second question, the most straightforward method to finetune neuron directions involves simultaneously rotating or reflecting all neurons within the same layer, which naturally corresponds to an orthogonal transformation. Although more flexible angular transformations that rotate neurons by different angles could be considered, orthogonal transformations strike a balance between flexibility and structure. Additionally, \cite{liu2021orthogonal} establishes that orthogonal transformations possess sufficient capacity for learning neural networks. To reinforce our claim, we conduct an experiment highlighting the effective regularization imposed by the orthogonality constraint. Using the ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} configuration, we perform controllable generation experiments, with results shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ortho}. We observe that our standard OFT maintains stability and achieves precise control after training completes (epoch 20). In contrast, OFT without the orthogonality constraint fails to produce realistic images and shows no control effect. This experiment substantiates the critical role of the orthogonality constraint in OFT.

\subsection{General Framework}

The traditional finetuning approach generally employs gradient descent with a low learning rate to adjust a model (or specific layers within a model). This low learning rate implicitly promotes minimal deviation from the pretrained model, meaning that conventional finetuning essentially aims to optimize the model while implicitly minimizing $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ denotes the finetuned model parameters and $\bm{M}^0$ represents the pretrained model parameters. While this implicit constraint is intuitive, it can still allow too much flexibility when finetuning large models. To tackle this, LoRA imposes an extra low-rank restriction on the weight update, that is, $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0)=r'$, where $r'$ is chosen to be a small value. Unlike LoRA, OFT introduces a constraint based on pairwise neuron similarity: $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \| \text{HE}(\bm{M})-\text{HE}(\bm{M}^0) \|=0$, where $\text{HE}(\cdot)$ denotes the hyperspherical energy of a weight matrix. To illustrate, consider a fully connected layer $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ corresponds to the $i$-th neuron (with $\bm{W}^0$ as the pretrained weights). The output vector $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ of this layer is calculated as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$, where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is the input vector. OFT can be viewed as minimizing the difference in hyperspherical energy between the finetuned and pretrained models:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:oft_principle}
\footnotesize
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|~~\Leftrightarrow~~\min_{\bm{W}} \bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}
\end{equation}

Here, $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ represents the normalized $i$-th neuron, and the hyperspherical energy of the fully connected layer $\bm{W}$ is defined as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. It is straightforward to observe that the minimal value attainable for Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_principle} is zero. This minimum is achieved if and only if $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ differ solely by a rotation or reflection, \emph{i.e.}, $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is an orthogonal matrix (with determinant $1$ indicating rotation and $-1$ indicating reflection). This embodies the central concept behind OFT: finetuning a neural network by learning orthogonal matrices shared across layers to transform

\begin{equation}\label{eq:oft_general}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}
\end{equation}

where $\bm{W}$ represents the OFT-finetuned weight matrix and $\bm{I}$ is the identity matrix. OFT is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:block}. In analogy to the zero initialization used in LoRA, it is necessary to ensure that OFT fine-tunes the pretrained model starting precisely from $\bm{W}^0$. To accomplish this, we initialize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as the identity matrix so that the fine-tuned model begins with the pretrained weights. To maintain the orthogonality of $\bm{R}$, we can apply differentiable orthogonalization techniques discussed in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,lezcano2019cheap}. We will elaborate on methods to enforce orthogonality efficiently in terms of computation.

\subsection{Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization}\label{sect:eff_ortho}

Conventional orthogonalization approaches like the Gram-Schmidt process, although differentiable, tend to be computationally expensive in practice~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}. To improve efficiency, we utilize the Cayley parameterization to construct the orthogonal matrix. In particular, we define the orthogonal matrix as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. This approach yields efficiency at a minor cost—the Cayley parameterization produces only orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$, i.e., those belonging to the special orthogonal group. Fortunately, we observe that this restriction does not impair performance in practical scenarios. Even with the Cayley transform for parameterizing the orthogonal matrix, $\bm{R}$ can remain highly parameter-intensive when the dimension $d$ is large. To mitigate this, we propose representing $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix composed of $r$ blocks, resulting in the form:

\begin{equation}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{R}_1\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) &  &\\
    &\ddots & \\
    & & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r})
    \end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d)
\end{equation}

where $\text{O}(d)$ represents the orthogonal group in dimension $d$, and $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as well as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r},\forall i$ are orthogonal matrices. In the special case where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=1$, the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix simplifies to a conventional unconstrained orthogonal matrix. For an orthogonal matrix sized $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d\times d$, the parameter count is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d(d-1)/2$, yielding a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. When dealing with an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix, the number of parameters is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d(d/r-1)/2$, leading to a complexity of $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Additionally, one can optionally share the block matrices to decrease parameter count further, that is, $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i \neq j$. This sharing reduces the parameter complexity to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Despite these approaches to enhance parameter efficiency, it is important to highlight that the matrix $\bm{R}$ remains orthogonal, ensuring no compromise in maintaining hyperspherical energy.

We now compare OFT and LoRA in terms of parameter efficiency. For LoRA with a low-rank parameter $r'$, the trainable parameter count is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r'(d+n)$. Assuming both $r$ and $r'$ scale with the neuron dimension $d$ (for instance, $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r = r' = \alpha d$, where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu 0 < \alpha \leq 1$ is a constant), the parameter complexity for LoRA becomes $\mathcal{O}(d^2 + dn)$, whereas that for OFT is $\mathcal{O}(d)$. To illustrate the difference concretely, consider a weight matrix of size $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu 128 \times 128$: LoRA with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r' = 8$ involves $2,048$ trainable parameters, while OFT with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r = 8$ (without block sharing) uses $960$ trainable parameters.

\subsection{Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning}

The flexibility of the original OFT can be further restricted by confining the finetuned model to remain close to the pretrained model. Specifically, COFT employs the following forward pass:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:coft_general}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I},~\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}\leq \epsilon
\end{equation}

where there is both an orthogonality constraint and a constraint limiting the deviation from the identity matrix to within $\epsilon$. The orthogonality constraint is enforced via the Cayley parameterization discussed in Section~\ref{sect:eff_ortho}. However, integrating the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint into the Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrix is nontrivial. To better understand the Cayley transform, we use the Neumann series to approximate $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ as $\thick

The series converges in the operator norm. Consequently, we can incorporate the constraint $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ directly within the Cayley transform. This is equivalent to the constraint $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq\epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ represents the all-zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a distinct error hyperparameter from $\epsilon$. Enforcing this new constraint on the matrix $\bm{Q}$ is straightforward using projected gradient descent. To initialize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as the identity, we start with $\bm{Q}$ set to the all-zero matrix. COFT can be understood as a combination of two explicit constraints: minimizing hyperspherical energy difference and restricting deviation from the pretrained model. While the latter is typically implicit in existing finetuning approaches, COFT explicitly enforces it. Although OFT performs excellently, we find that COFT provides even greater finetuning stability owing to this explicit deviation constraint. Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft} illustrates how $\epsilon$ influences COFT's performance. It is evident that $\epsilon$ governs the flexibility of finetuning; a larger $\epsilon$ results in a COFT-finetuned model closer to the OFT-finetuned one, whereas a smaller $\epsilon$ causes the COFT-finetuned model to behave increasingly like the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model.

\subsection{Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning}

We introduce a straightforward extension to the original OFT by learning a scaling factor for each neuron’s magnitude. This is inspired by the observation that rescaling neurons does not affect the hyperspherical energy (since magnitude is normalized out). Specifically, the forward pass is given by: $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$\footnote{\textbf{Errata}: In the NeurIPS camera ready version, the forward pass of re-scaled OFT was incorrectly reported as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{z}=(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. The original implementation is correct, so the results in Appendix~\ref{app:rescaled} remain valid.} where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\cdots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is a learnable diagonal matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries $s_1,\cdots,s_n$. Unlike the original OFT forward pass in Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_general}, where only $\bm{R}$ is learnable, here both the diagonal matrix $\bm{D}$ and the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ are trainable. This re-scaled OFT enhances the flexibility of OFT while introducing a negligible number of extra parameters. Although we use the original OFT in experiments to isolate the effectiveness of orthogonal transformation alone, we observe that the re-scaled OFT generally performs better (see Appendix~\ref{app:rescaled}).

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{OFT is architecture-agnostic}. In principle, OFT can be applied to any neural network architecture. For Transformers, LoRA is commonly used on attention weights~\cite{hulora2022}. To ensure a fair comparison with LoRA, we restrict OFT finetuning to attention weights in our experiments. Besides fully connected layers, OFT is also well-suited for finetuning convolutional layers, since the block-diagonal structure of $\bm{R}$ has meaningful interpretations in convolution layers (unlike LoRA). When the number of blocks matches the number of input channels, each block operates on a unique neuron channel, analogous to learning depth-wise convolution kernels~\cite{chollet2017xception}. Conversely, if all blocks in $\bm{R}$ are shared, OFT applies the same orthogonal matrix transformation across channels. We present a preliminary study on finetuning convolution layers using OFT in Appendix~\ref{app:convolution}.

\textbf{Connection to LoRA}. LoRA introduces a low-rank matrix addition to prevent significant changes in the pretrained weight matrix's information. In contrast, OFT applies an orthogonal (full-rank) transformation to the pretrained weight matrix, ensuring the pretraining information is preserved without being disrupted. The forward pass of OFT can be expressed as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ functions similarly to LoRA's low-rank weight update. Given that $\bm{W}^0$ is generally full-rank, OFT effectively performs a low-rank weight update when $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is of low rank. Like LoRA's rank parameter $r'$, OFT employs a diagonal block parameter $r$ to reduce the number of parameters to be trained. Notably, LoRA and OFT embody two distinct strategies for parameter efficiency: LoRA leverages low-rank structures to minimize trainable parameters, whereas OFT utilizes sparsity structures, specifically block-diagonal orthogonality.

\textbf{Why OFT converges faster}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}, the most impactful way to alter semantic representations is by changing neuron directions, which aligns precisely with OFT's design. Additionally, OFT can be interpreted as fine-tuning neurons on a smooth hyperspherical manifold, leading to an improved optimization landscape. This observation is further supported by empirical evidence in \cite{liu2021orthogonal}.

\textbf{Why not minimize hyperspherical energy}. A major distinction from \cite{liu2021orthogonal} is that we do not seek to minimize hyperspherical energy. In classification tasks, it is desirable for neurons to be non-redundant. Minimizing hyperspherical energy results in neurons being evenly distributed on the hypersphere, which is not a suitable goal for fine-tuning, as it might disrupt the information learned during pretraining.

\textbf{Balance between flexibility and regularization in finetuning}. We identify an intrinsic trade-off between flexibility and regularity. Conventional finetuning offers maximum flexibility but tends to suffer from low stability and is prone to model collapse. Despite its simplicity, OFT effectively balances flexibility and regularity by maintaining pairwise neuron angles. Additionally, the block-diagonal parameterization can be interpreted as a stronger form of regularization applied to the orthogonal matrix.

\textbf{No extra inference cost}. In contrast to ControlNet, the OFT framework does not impose any additional inference overhead on the finetuned model. During inference, we simply multiply the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ with the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ to produce an equivalent weight matrix $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Therefore, the inference speed remains identical to that of the pretrained model.

\section{Experiments and Results}

\textbf{Overall settings}. In our experiments, we utilize Stable Diffusion v1.5~\cite{rombach2022high} as the pretrained text-to-image model. To ensure fairness, images generated by each method are randomly selected. For subject-driven generation, we generally follow the protocol established by DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}. For controllable generation, we mostly adhere to the approaches used in ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}. To maintain a fair comparison with LoRA, we apply OFT or COFT only to the same layers where LoRA is applied. Additional results and implementation details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}.

\subsection{Subject-driven Generation}

\textbf{Experimental setup}. We use DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} and LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} as baseline methods. All methods employ the same loss function as specified in DreamBooth. For both DreamBooth and LoRA, we generally follow the original papers and adopt their best hyperparameter configurations. Further results are available in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}, \ref{app:coft_oft}, \ref{app:more_qualitative}, and \ref{app:failure}.

\textbf{Finetuning stability and convergence}. We begin by assessing the finetuning stability and the speed of convergence for DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT. The results are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} and Figure~\ref{fig:db_convergence}. It is evident that both COFT and OFT can finetune the diffusion model with considerable stability. After 400 iterations, DreamBooth and OFT variants both demonstrate effective control, whereas LoRA fails to maintain the subject identity. Upon reaching 2000 iterations, DreamBooth starts producing degraded images, and LoRA cannot generate a yellow shirt, instead producing yellow fur. In contrast, OFT and COFT continue to provide stable and consistent control over the generated images. These findings confirm the rapid yet stable convergence of our OFT framework in subject-driven generation tasks. We emphasize that the robustness to the number of finetuning iterations is highly beneficial, as it reduces the difficulty of tuning iteration counts for different subjects. For both OFT and COFT, it is feasible to set a relatively large number of iterations without precise tuning. Furthermore, with an appropriate $\epsilon$, COFT makes setting both the learning rate and the number of iterations straightforward.

\textbf{Quantitative comparison}. Following the methodology of \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, we perform a quantitative evaluation measuring subject fidelity (using DINO~\cite{caron2021emerging} and CLIP-I~\cite{radford2021learning}), text prompt fidelity (CLIP-T~\cite{radford2021learning}), and sample diversity (LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}). CLIP-I calculates the average pairwise cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings between generated and real images. DINO is similar to CLIP-I but uses ViT S/16 DINO embeddings instead. CLIP-T measures the average cosine similarity between the CLIP embeddings of the text prompt and the generated images. We also compute the average LPIPS cosine similarity across generated images of the same subject conditioned on the same text prompt. As shown in Table~\ref{table:dreambooth_final}, both COFT and OFT outperform DreamBooth and LoRA by a significant margin in the DINO and CLIP-I metrics, while maintaining comparable or slightly better results in prompt fidelity and diversity metrics. For each method, we finetune the identical pretrained model 30 times with different random seeds to reduce randomness. The findings demonstrate that our OFT framework not only achieves superior convergence and stability but also consistently delivers improved final results.

\textbf{Qualitative comparison}. To gain a clearer and more intuitive insight into the advantages of OFT, we present several randomly selected examples of subject-driven generation in Figure~\ref{fig:dreambooth_final}. For an equitable comparison, all samples are produced using the same finetuned model for each respective method, ensuring that no individual text prompt is separately optimized for the final output. For each approach, we choose the model that attains the highest validation CLIP metrics. Observing the results in Figure~\ref{fig:dreambooth_final}, it is evident that both OFT and COFT excel in preserving the semantic identity of the subject, whereas LoRA frequently fails to maintain subject identity (e.g., in the bowl example, LoRA completely loses the subject’s identity). Additionally, both OFT and COFT demonstrate significantly better adherence to the text prompts, whereas DreamBooth, although effective at preserving subject identity, often does not accurately follow the textual guidance (e.g., the first row in the bowl example). This qualitative assessment highlights that our OFT framework simultaneously achieves superior controllability and subject preservation. Furthermore, OFT’s performance is robust across a range of iteration counts, maintaining quality even with a high number of iterations, unlike DreamBooth or LoRA. Additional qualitative examples can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:more_qualitative}. We also perform a human evaluation detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:human}, which further confirms the advantages of OFT.

\subsection{Controllable Generation}

\textbf{Settings}. We employ ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}, T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}, and LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} as baseline methods. The main paper focuses on three demanding controllable generation tasks: Canny edge to image (C2I) on the COCO dataset~\cite{lin2014microsoft}, segmentation map to image (S2I) on the ADE20K dataset~\cite{zhou2017scene}, and landmark to face (L2F) on the CelebA-HQ dataset~\cite{karras2018progressive,xia2021tedigan}. All approaches finetune Stable Diffusion (SD) v1.5 on these datasets for 20 epochs. More results are provided in Appendices~\ref{app:more_qualitative}, \ref{app:more_control_task}, and \ref{app:failure}.

\textbf{Convergence}. We assess the convergence rates of ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA, and COFT on the L2F task, providing both quantitative and qualitative analyses. For the evaluation metric, we calculate the average $\ell_2$ distance between the control face landmarks and the predicted face landmarks. In Figure~\ref{fig:face_convergence}, we display the face landmark error for models finetuned over varying numbers of epochs. It is evident that both COFT and OFT exhibit notably faster convergence. LoRA requires 20 epochs to reach the performance level that our OFT framework achieves by the 8th epoch. We observe that OFT and COFT have a comparable count of trainable parameters to LoRA (significantly fewer than ControlNet), yet they converge much more efficiently than existing approaches. Moreover, the rapid convergence of OFT is further supported by the results shown in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}. The right example in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} demonstrates that OFT is considerably more data-efficient compared to ControlNet and LoRA, as it can converge effectively using only 5\% of the ADE20K dataset. For qualitative comparison, we concentrate on OFT, COFT, and ControlNet, since ControlNet attains landmark errors closest to ours. The outcomes in Figure~\ref{fig:face_convergence_qual} reveal that both OFT and COFT converge steadily, with the generated face pose progressively aligning with the control landmarks. Unlike our smooth and stable convergence, ControlNet's controllability abruptly appears after the 8th epoch, mirroring the sudden convergence behavior reported in \cite{zhang2023adding}. This stability in convergence makes our OFT framework far more user-friendly in practical applications, as its training progression is significantly smoother and more predictable. Consequently, determining suitable hyperparameters for OFT becomes easier.

\textbf{Quantitative comparison}. We propose a control consistency metric to assess the effectiveness of controllable generation. The core idea is to extract the control signal from the generated image and then compare it with the original input control signal. For the C2I task, we calculate IoU and F1 score. For the S2I task, we measure mean IoU, mean accuracy, and overall accuracy. For the L2F task, we evaluate the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the control landmarks and the predicted landmarks. Additional details about the consistency metrics can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}. For all the methods compared, we use their optimal hyperparameter configurations. The results shown in Table~\ref{table:control_gen} indicate that both OFT and COFT achieve significantly stronger and more precise control than the other approaches. We note that adapter-based methods (e.g., T2I-Adapter and ControlNet) demonstrate slower convergence and produce inferior final outcomes. Compared with ControlNet, LoRA performs better on the S2I task but worse on the C2I and L2F tasks. Overall, we observe that LoRA’s performance ceiling remains relatively low, even with careful hyperparameter tuning. In contrast, the performance of our OFT framework has not yet plateaued, as we empirically observe further improvement with an increased number of trainable parameters. We emphasize that our quantitative evaluation method for controllable generation represents one of our novel contributions, as it can precisely assess the control capabilities of finetuned models (subject to the accuracy of the utilized off-the-shelf segmentation/detection model).

\textbf{Qualitative comparison}. We also perform a qualitative comparison between OFT and COFT and existing state-of-the-art techniques, such as ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. The randomly generated images in Figure~\ref{fig:control_final} illustrate that OFT and COFT not only produce high-fidelity and realistic images but also achieve precise control. For the S2I task, it is evident that LoRA fails entirely to generate images aligned with the input segmentation map, whereas ControlNet, OFT, and COFT effectively control the generated images. Compared to ControlNet, both OFT and COFT generate high-fidelity images featuring more vivid details and more plausible geometric structures while using significantly fewer model parameters. In the C2I task, OFT and COFT successfully generate semantically consistent images based on rough Canny edges, outperforming T2I-Adapter and LoRA, which perform considerably worse. In the L2F task, our approach achieves the most precise pose control for the generated faces, even in challenging face orientations. Across all three control tasks, we demonstrate that OFT and COFT produce qualitatively superior images compared to current state-of-the-art baselines, highlighting the effectiveness of our OFT framework for controllable generation. To provide a more thorough qualitative comparison, additional examples for all three control tasks are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:control_exp}. Furthermore, we show that OFT performs well on additional control tasks, including dense pose to human body, sketch to image, and depth to image, detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:more_control_task}.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Open Problems}

Inspired by the insight that angular relationships among neurons play a vital role in defining visual semantics, we introduce a straightforward yet powerful finetuning technique—orthogonal finetuning (OFT)—for controlling text-to-image diffusion models. Our approach focuses on two specific text-to-image tasks: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. In comparison with current methods, OFT offers enhanced controllability and greater stability during finetuning while requiring fewer parameters to be adjusted. Crucially, OFT does not add any extra computational cost during inference, resulting in a model that is efficient and easy to deploy.

OFT also raises several intriguing open questions. First, the orthogonality constraint is maintained through Cayley parametrization, which necessitates computing a matrix inverse. This somewhat restricts the scalability of OFT. Although we mitigate this issue by employing a block diagonal parametrization, finding a faster differentiable approach for matrix inversion remains a challenge. Second, OFT holds distinctive promise for compositionality because the orthogonal matrices obtained from different finetuning tasks can be combined via multiplication, yielding another orthogonal matrix. Investigating whether this set of orthogonal matrices can retain the knowledge from all downstream tasks is an interesting avenue for future research. Lastly, the parameter efficiency of OFT largely depends on the block diagonal structure, which inevitably introduces some bias and constrains flexibility. Developing methods to enhance parameter efficiency in a more effective and less biased manner constitutes a significant open problem.
