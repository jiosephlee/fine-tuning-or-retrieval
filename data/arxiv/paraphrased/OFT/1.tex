\title{Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning}

\begin{abstract}
Text-to-image diffusion models of substantial scale have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in synthesizing photorealistic visuals from natural language prompts. Nonetheless, a central challenge that remains is how to effectively steer these robust models for a variety of downstream applications. In response, we present Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a systematic approach for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models to meet specific task requirements. Distinct from conventional finetuning techniques, OFT is theoretically shown to conserve hyperspherical energy—a metric reflecting the inter-neuronal relationships on a unit hypersphere. Our investigations indicate that maintaining this characteristic is critical to upholding the semantic image synthesis capabilities of diffusion models. To bolster the stability of finetuning, we also introduce Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which enforces an auxiliary constraint on the radius of the hypersphere. We focus our study on two notable finetuning applications for text-to-image models: (1) subject-driven generation, which involves synthesizing personalized subject images in new contexts based on limited subject data and text prompts, and (2) controllable generation, where the model is directed by supplementary control signals in addition to text prompts. Experimental results demonstrate that our OFT methodology surpasses prior approaches in both sample quality and speed of convergence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

State-of-the-art text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical,rombach2022high} have achieved notable advances in generating high-resolution images directed by text input. However, despite these strong capabilities, textual prompts alone may still fall short in delivering precise or granular control over the attributes of the generated images. To remedy these limitations, this work concentrates on two principal categories of text-to-image synthesis tasks:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

    \item \textbf{Subject-driven generation}~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}: Provided with a handful of sample images of a specific subject, the aim is to create new images depicting this subject in varied settings using textual instructions.
    \item \textbf{Controllable generation}~\cite{zhang2023adding,mou2023t2i}: When furnished with an auxiliary control input (\emph{e.g.}, canny edge maps, segmentation masks), the objective is to generate images that conform to both the control guidance and textual descriptions.
\end{itemize}

The core challenge of both tasks lies in developing strategies for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models so that the strengths of pretraining, particularly in generative capability, are not compromised. The key requirements for a robust finetuning approach are: (1) \emph{training efficiency}, which refers to minimizing the number of parameters needing updates as well as reducing the total training epochs, and (2) \emph{generalizability preservation}, meaning that the model continues to produce high-quality, diverse outputs. Current approaches generally update the model by adjusting its neuron weights incrementally with a low learning rate (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}), or by attaching lightweight modules that introduce new, re-parameterized weights (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{hulora2022,zhang2023adding}). However, despite being straightforward, these methods do not provide any theoretical guarantees regarding the retention of generative performance achieved during pretraining. Moreover, the field lacks a systematic framework for crafting effective finetuning schemes and tuning hyperparameters, such as selecting an optimal number of epochs. The main challenge stems from the absence of a robust metric to evaluate how much of the pretrained generative capacity is preserved post-finetuning. Existing methods often operate under the assumption that maintaining a small Euclidean distance between the original and finetuned model weights leads to better retention of prior abilities. Consequently, practitioners generally employ very small learning rates. Although this assumption may sometimes be valid, we contend that relying solely on Euclidean proximity fails to comprehensively reflect the model’s preservation of semantics. Thus, adopting a more structurally informed measure of model differences could substantially enhance both the maintenance of pretraining performance and the overall stability during finetuning.

Building upon empirical findings that hyperspherical similarity effectively encodes semantic relations~\cite{liu2017deep,liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular}, our approach utilizes hyperspherical energy~\cite{liu2018learning} to analyze the pairwise interactions among neurons. Hyperspherical energy, defined as the aggregate of hyperspherical similarities (\emph{e.g.}, cosine similarity) across all neuron pairs within a layer, reflects the degree of uniform distribution of neurons on the unit hypersphere~\cite{liu2021learning}. We posit that optimal finetuning should minimally alter the hyperspherical energy relative to the pretrained state. While one might straightforwardly impose a regularization term to maintain hyperspherical energy during finetuning, this method does not inherently minimize differences in hyperspherical energy. Instead, we leverage an intrinsic invariance: if the same orthogonal transformation is applied to every neuron, the pairwise hyperspherical similarities remain unchanged. This insight leads to our method, Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT), which adapts large text-to-image diffusion models for new tasks while conserving the original hyperspherical energy. The main strategy involves training a shared orthogonal transformation for neurons in each layer so that their pairwise angular relationships are retained. OFT can also be interpreted as recalibrating the canonical basis for neurons within the same layer. Additionally, recognizing that maintaining a small Euclidean distance between the pretrained and finetuned models aids in preserving pretraining effectiveness, we introduce a variant named Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning~(COFT), which enforces the finetuned model to remain within a hyperspherical region of fixed radius centered at the pretrained neuron configuration.

The rationale underlying the effectiveness of orthogonal transformation for neuron finetuning is partially inspired by the principles of the 2D Fourier transform, which allows images to be represented through their magnitude and phase spectra. The phase spectrum encapsulates the angular relationship between the input and basis elements, and it is responsible for preserving most of the semantic content of an image. For instance, reconstructing an image using its original phase spectrum combined with a randomly chosen magnitude spectrum will often yield a result that retains the original semantics. This observation underscores the idea that adjusting neuron orientations is central to meaningful semantic modification of generated images—a crucial objective in both subject-driven and controllable image generation. Nevertheless, if neuron directions are altered too freely, the pretrained model’s generative quality is likely to suffer. To control this, we propose preserving the angles between all pairs of neurons, motivated by the hypothesis that such angular relationships are vital for encoding the network’s learned knowledge. Accordingly, we adopt a strategy of applying a layer-wise, shared orthogonal transformation to the neurons in each layer, thereby maintaining constant hyperspherical energy.

Further conceptual support comes from orthogonal over-parameterized training~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}, which approaches the training of classification neural networks by starting with a randomly initialized model and applying orthogonal transformations throughout training. The motivation here is that random initialization leads to a theoretically low expected hyperspherical energy, and the method in \cite{liu2021orthogonal} strives to retain low hyperspherical energy throughout learning—a condition that has been linked to better generalization performance in classification~\cite{liu2018learning,lin2020regularizing}. Empirical findings in \cite{liu2021orthogonal} establish that orthogonal transformations provide enough flexibility to train robust and generalizable networks for classification tasks. However, our work departs from this by addressing the finetuning of text-to-image diffusion models to enhance both control and generative capability. The distinction between OFT and \cite{liu2021orthogonal} is twofold. First, \cite{liu2021orthogonal} is intended to further decrease hyperspherical energy during training, while OFT is specifically designed to maintain the hyperspherical energy at the level of the pretrained model, thereby preserving its original internal structure during finetuning. For diffusion model finetuning, reducing hyperspherical energy further might disrupt the underlying semantic integrity. Second, OFT minimizes deviation from the pretrained weights, resulting in a more constrained adaptation, whereas \cite{liu2021orthogonal} does not employ such constraints. Achieving an optimal compromise between adaptability and preservation of pretrained knowledge is central to effective finetuning, and we posit that the OFT method accomplishes this balance. Our principal contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

    \item We introduce Orthogonal Finetuning, a new finetuning strategy aimed at enhancing the controllability of text-to-image diffusion models. To bolster stability, we also propose a constrained version that restricts angular deviation from the parameters of the pretrained model.
    \item Unlike previous finetuning techniques, OFT maintains the hyperspherical energy of the model throughout finetuning, which we establish empirically as a key indicator of semantic retention from the original pretrained generative model.
    \item We evaluate OFT across two applications: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Through extensive experiments, we observe considerable advancements relative to earlier approaches, notably in generative quality, training convergence rate, and finetuning robustness. Additionally, OFT demonstrates superior sample efficiency, achieving effective convergence with notably fewer training samples and epochs.
    \item For controllable generation, we present a novel control consistency metric to assess model controllability. This metric involves inferring the control signal from the produced image and comparing it to the initial control signal, providing further quantitative support for the high degree of control afforded by OFT.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Text-to-image diffusion models}. Significant advancements~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical,rombach2022high,gu2022vector,nichol2022glide} in generating images from text have been achieved, propelled by innovations in diffusion-based generative modeling~\cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score,song2021denoising,dhariwal2021diffusion} and vision-language learning frameworks~\cite{su2020vlbert,lu2019vilbert,radford2021learning,wang2021simvlm,li2022blip,li2023blip,alayrac2022flamingo,schuhmann2022laion}. In particular, GLIDE~\cite{nichol2022glide} and Imagen~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic} operate in the pixel space; GLIDE jointly optimizes a text encoder alongside the diffusion model leveraging paired data, while Imagen utilizes a fixed pretrained text encoder throughout training. Conversely, Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} and DALL-E2~\cite{ramesh2022hierarchical} focus on modeling within a latent space: Stable Diffusion employs VQ-GAN~\cite{esser2021taming} for visual tokenization, and DALL-E2 leverages CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} for integrated image-text representations. Beyond diffusion models, both generative adversarial networks~\cite{reed2016generative,zhang2017stackgan,xu2018attngan,li2019controllable} and autoregressive strategies~\cite{ramesh2021zero,ding2021cogview,wu2022nuwa,yuscaling} are explored for this generative task. The OFT approach is model-agnostic by design, making it broadly compatible with any text-to-image diffusion architecture.

\textbf{Subject-driven generation}. Approaches such as \cite{avrahami2022blended,nichol2022glide} mitigate undesired alterations of the subject by incorporating user-supplied masks as auxiliary conditions. Inversion techniques~\cite{choi2021ilvr,dhariwal2021diffusion,ramesh2022hierarchical,gal2022image} allow editing of the contextual elements without affecting the subject itself, while \cite{hertz2022prompt} offers the ability to execute both local and global modifications absent any input mask. However, these strategies struggle to consistently preserve details essential to subject identity. Pivotal Tuning~\cite{roich2022pivotal} tackles this by finetuning the generator proximate to an inverted latent representation, combined with regularization to retain identity features. Likewise, \cite{nitzan2022mystyle} establishes a personal generative facial prior via a set of images from an individual, and \cite{casanova2021instance} enables generating multiple variants of an instance, sometimes at the expense of fidelity to instance-specific details. DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} enhances subject retention by adapting the diffusion model through finetuning on a handful of subject-specific images using both reconstruction and class-prior preservation losses, facilitated by custom tokens. Our OFT method follows the DreamBooth structure but substitutes direct finetuning for orthogonal transformations, offering advantages over standard low-rate updates.

\textbf{Controllable generation}. Image-to-image translation can be regarded as a subset of controllable generation, with earlier approaches largely relying on conditional generative adversarial networks~\cite{isola2017image,zhu2017toward,wang2018high,park2019semantic,choi2018stargan}. More recently, diffusion models have also been explored for this application~\cite{saharia2022palette,voynov2022sketch,wang2022pretraining}. ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}, for example, enhances the controllability of pretrained diffusion models by introducing finetuning mechanisms tailored to new control inputs, thereby attaining notable improvements in controllable image synthesis. Similarly, T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i} fine-tunes existing diffusion models to improve the alignment between generated images and desired control signals. Building on the settings outlined in \cite{zhang2023adding,mou2023t2i}, we utilize OFT for finetuning diffusion models, which consistently results in improved controllability while requiring less training data and a reduced number of finetuned parameters. Importantly, OFT maintains inference speed, adding no computational overhead at test time.

\textbf{Model finetuning}. The paradigm of refining large pretrained models for specific downstream tasks has become increasingly widespread~\cite{devlin2018bert,brown2020language,he2022masked}. Among various finetuning strategies, adaptation techniques (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{hulora2022,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion}) have received extensive attention, especially in the context of natural language processing. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}, the method most related to OFT, leverages a low-rank structure to modify weight matrices through additive updates during finetuning. By contrast, OFT adopts layer-shared orthogonal transformations, applying multiplicative updates to the neuron weights. This mechanism guarantees that pairwise angles among neurons within a layer are mathematically preserved, contributing to enhanced training stability.

\section{Orthogonal Finetuning}

\subsection{Why Does Orthogonal Transformation Make Sense?}

To motivate the use of orthogonal transformation for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models, we break the problem into two key considerations: (1) the rationale for adjusting neuron angles (i.e., directions) during finetuning, and (2) the justification for selecting orthogonal transformations as the method to perform such angle adjustments.

To address the first question, we take motivation from the empirical findings in \cite{liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular}, which indicate that the angular difference between features effectively encapsulates the semantic disparity. SphereNet~\cite{liu2017deep} demonstrates that enforcing normalization of neural network activations to lie on a unit hypersphere not only maintains representational capacity but can also enhance generalization, suggesting that neuron orientation alone suffices to represent key information in data. To illustrate the pivotal role of neuron angles, we design a simple experiment shown in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}. Here, we train a basic convolutional autoencoder on a set of flower images, employing the standard inner product for feature extraction during training (where $z$ is the output of convolving kernel $\bm{w}$ with the sliding-window input $\bm{x}$). During evaluation, we consider three alternative methods for computing feature maps: (a) the standard inner product as used in training, (b) using only the magnitude, and (c) retaining just the angular (directional) component. As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}, retaining solely the angular information enables near-perfect reconstruction of input images, whereas magnitude alone provides negligible informative content. It is important to note that the cosine activation in (c) is not used during training, which is conducted exclusively with the inner product. These outcomes highlight that the angles or directions of neurons encode most of the semantic content in input images, implying that adjusting neuron directions is likely the most impactful way to manipulate semantic representations.

For the second question, a straightforward approach to adjust the orientation of neurons involves rotating or reflecting all neurons within the same layer in unison, corresponding to the application of an orthogonal transformation. Although other angular manipulations could allow for neuron-specific rotations, our investigations reveal that orthogonal transformations strike an effective balance between expressiveness and structural regularity. Additionally, prior work~\cite{liu2021orthogonal} confirms the adequacy of orthogonal transformations for neural network learning. To substantiate this perspective, we conduct an experiment that examines the regularizing effect of the orthogonality constraint, using the controllable image synthesis framework from ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}. Results in Figure~\ref{fig:ortho} demonstrate that our default implementation of OFT, which enforces orthogonality, delivers stable performance and accurate control by the end of training (epoch 20). Conversely, removing the orthogonality constraint from OFT results in an inability to produce realistic outputs or exhibit any controllability. These findings establish the critical role of the orthogonality constraint within OFT.

\subsection{General Framework}

The standard approach to finetuning involves employing gradient descent with a low learning rate, updating either all or a subset of a model's parameters. Using a small learning rate acts as a regularizer, ensuring that updates remain close to the original pretrained weights, thereby implicitly minimizing the term $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ represents the parameters after finetuning, and $\bm{M}^0$ refers to those from the pretrained model. While this restriction aligns well with intuition, it may not be stringent enough when adapting large-scale models. To mitigate this, LoRA proposes an explicit low-rank constraint on the modifications to the weights, formulated as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0)=r'$ for a small $r'$. In contrast, OFT enforces a constraint on the similarity between neurons in terms of their pairwise structure: $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \| \text{HE}(\bm{M})-\text{HE}(\bm{M}^0) \|=0$, where $\text{HE}(\cdot)$ indicates the hyperspherical energy of the weight matrix. 

Consider, for instance, a fully connected layer parameterized as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each column vector $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ corresponds to the $i$-th neuron (and $\bm{W}^0$ stands for the pretrained layer weights). For a given input $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the layer output $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is computed via $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$. Within this context, OFT aims to minimize the discrepancy in hyperspherical energy between the tuned and original model:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:oft_principle}
\footnotesize
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|~~\Leftrightarrow~~\min_{\bm{W}} \bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}
\end{equation}

Here, the normalized neuron $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ is used, and hyperspherical energy is defined as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$. Notably, it is straightforward to verify that the objective in Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_principle} achieves its minimum value of zero when $\bm{W}$ differs from $\bm{W}^0$ only through an orthogonal transformation, specifically $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ an orthogonal matrix (a determinant of $1$ or $-1$ denotes rotation or reflection, respectively). The fundamental principle of OFT is thus to adapt the pretrained network by applying a learnable, layer-wise orthogonal transformation. Concretely, OFT focuses on finding an optimal orthogonal matrix $\

\begin{equation}\label{eq:oft_general}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}
\end{equation}

Here, $\bm{W}$ signifies the weight matrix updated via OFT, while $\bm{I}$ stands for the identity matrix. Figure~\ref{fig:block} provides an overview of OFT. As with LoRA’s zero initialization scheme, it is crucial for OFT to ensure fine-tuning is carried out strictly from $\bm{W}^0$. To this end, we set the initial value of the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix, thereby ensuring that the fine-tuned parameters begin as the pretrained weights. To maintain the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$, we can utilize differentiable orthogonalization approaches as described in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,lezcano2019cheap}. In the following, we elaborate on computationally efficient methods to enforce orthogonality.

\subsection{Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization}\label{sect:eff_ortho}

Typical orthogonalization procedures, such as the Gram-Schmidt process, although differentiable, tend to incur considerable computational overhead in real-world scenarios~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}. To achieve higher efficiency, we employ the Cayley parameterization for orthogonal matrix construction. Explicitly, we express the orthogonal matrix as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, with $\bm{Q}$ denoting a skew-symmetric matrix such that $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The gain in computational tractability is accompanied by a slight restriction: matrices produced via Cayley parameterization have a determinant equal to $1$, thus forming part of the special orthogonal group. Empirically, we observe that this constraint has negligible influence on actual model performance. However, parameterizing $\bm{R}$ via the Cayley transform can result in substantial parameter count, especially when $d$ is large. To mitigate this inefficiency, we recommend decomposing $\bm{R}$ as a block-diagonal matrix with $r$ constituent blocks, yielding:

\begin{equation}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{R}_1\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) &  &\\
    &\ddots & \\
    & & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r})
    \end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d)
\end{equation}

Here, $\text{O}(d)$ represents the group of all orthogonal matrices in $d$-dimensional space, with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as well as block matrices $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r}$ for each $i$, all of which are orthogonal. When the block size parameter is set to $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=1$, the structure simplifies to a conventional orthogonal matrix without block constraints. For a general orthogonal matrix of size $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d\times d$, the count of free parameters is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d(d-1)/2$, which leads to a computational complexity of order $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. When utilizing an $r$-block diagonal orthogonal matrix, the total number of parameters becomes $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d(d/r-1)/2$, so the resulting complexity is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. Moreover, further reduction in parameter count is possible by enforcing block sharing, that is, setting $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j$ for all $i\neq j$, which brings the parameter complexity down to $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Despite these parameter reduction techniques, the overall matrix $\bm{R}$ still retains its orthogonality, ensuring that the hyperspherical energy is preserved.

Let us compare the parameter efficiency of OFT with LoRA. Considering LoRA with a low-rank configuration parameterized by $r'$, its number of learnable parameters is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r'(d+n)$. If both $r$ and $r'$ scale with $d$ (for example, taking $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=r'=\alpha d$ with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu 0<\alpha\leq 1$ being constant), then LoRA has complexity $\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$ while OFT attains $\mathcal{O}(d)$. For an illustrative case, consider a weight matrix sized $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu 128\times 128$; LoRA has $2,048$ trainable parameters with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r'=8$, whereas OFT contains $960$ trainable parameters for $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=8$ when block sharing is not in effect.

\subsection{Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning}

One can restrict the expressive power of the original OFT method by requiring the finetuned model to stay within a prescribed neighborhood around the pretrained weights. Concretely, COFT implements the following computation:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:coft_general}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I},~\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}\leq \epsilon
\end{equation}

This formulation imposes both an orthogonality requirement and a proximity constraint such that $\bm{R}$ must not deviate from the identity by more than $\epsilon$. The orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ can be realized using the Cayley transform as discussed in Section~\ref{sect:eff_ortho}. However, it remains a challenge to embed the $\epsilon$-proximity restriction within the Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrices. To better understand the properties of the Cayley transform, we can utilize the Neumann series to approximate $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm

Given the convergence of the series in the operator norm, it is permissible to incorporate the constraint $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ within the Cayley transform. This leads to an equivalent restriction of $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq\epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ stands for the zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ is a different tolerance parameter (distinct from $\epsilon$). This alternative constraint placed upon the matrix $\bm{Q}$ lends itself naturally to enforcement via projected gradient descent. To initialize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as the identity, one simply sets $\bm{Q}$ to be the zero matrix. COFT can thus be understood as the superposition of two explicit regularizations: ensuring a minimal difference in hyperspherical energy, and limiting the degree of departure from the pretrained network. The latter constraint, while often present as an implicit bias in contemporary finetuning approaches, is rendered explicit by COFT. While OFT demonstrates robust performance, our observations suggest that COFT enhances the stability of the finetuning process even further, owing to this explicit constraint on deviation. Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft} illustrates the impact of varying $\epsilon$ on COFT performance. It is evident that $\epsilon$ governs how freely the model adapts during finetuning. At high values of $\epsilon$, the results of COFT approximate those of OFT; conversely, at low values, the COFT solution aligns more closely with the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model.

\subsection{Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning}

We introduce a straightforward extension to the basic OFT framework by enabling each neuron to additionally learn a scaling factor. The rationale stems from the observation that rescaling neurons does not alter hyperspherical energy, since normalization will absorb any magnitude changes. Concretely, the forward computation follows: $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$\footnote{\textbf{Errata}: The NeurIPS camera ready manuscript incorrectly specifies the forward computation for re-scaled OFT as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{z}=(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$; the original code implementation, and consequently the results in Appendix~\ref{app:rescaled}, are correct.}, with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{D} = \text{diag}(s_1,\ldots,s_n) \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ a diagonal matrix whose entries $s_1,\ldots,s_n$ are trainable and strictly positive. Unlike the standard OFT approach in Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_general}, where learning is restricted to $\bm{R}$, both the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ and the scaling matrix $\bm{D}$ are jointly optimized here. This re-scaled OFT grants greater adaptability to OFT, with only a negligible increase in parameter count. Nevertheless, to isolate the effects of the orthogonal transformation, our experiments are based on the standard OFT, but we note that the re-scaled variant tends to yield superior performance in general (refer to Appendix~\ref{app:rescaled}).

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{OFT is agnostic to architectural choices}. The OFT method is, in principle, applicable to a broad array of neural network architectures. For instance, while LoRA is predominantly utilized on attention weights in Transformers~\cite{hulora2022}, for the sake of an equitable comparison in our experiments, we similarly constrain OFT’s application to the finetuning of attention parameters. Beyond standard fully connected layers, OFT is also naturally suited for adapting convolutional layers; this is facilitated by the block-diagonal configuration of $\bm{R}$, which carries unique significance for convolutions—a characteristic not mirrored in LoRA. For example, by matching the number of blocks in $\bm{R}$ with the number of input channels, each block manipulates only its corresponding channel, analogous to training depth-wise convolutional kernels~\cite{chollet2017xception}. Should all blocks in $\bm{R}$ be identical, the same orthogonal matrix transformation is imposed across all channels. A preliminary investigation into applying OFT to convolutional layers is presented in Appendix~

\textbf{Connection to LoRA}. LoRA introduces a low-rank matrix, ensuring the pretrained weight matrix largely retains its original information and is not drastically altered. OFT, on the other hand, constrains the transformation applied to the pretrained weight matrix to be orthogonal and full-rank, thus safeguarding against loss of information encoded during pretraining. The forward computation for OFT can be expressed as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ parallels the low-rank update in LoRA. Given that $\bm{W}^0$ generally possesses full rank, OFT reduces to a low-rank update in scenarios where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}-\bm{I}$ has low rank. Analogous to LoRA's rank hyperparameter $r'$, OFT incorporates a block-diagonal parameter $r$ to manage the total trainable parameter count. More broadly, LoRA and OFT typify two separate strategies for achieving parameter efficiency: LoRA leverages the low-rank structure for compactness, whereas OFT capitalizes on a sparse configuration via block-diagonal orthogonality.

\textbf{Why OFT converges faster}. There is evidence, such as in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}, that the most impactful method for altering semantic representations is by adjusting neuron directions—a capability for which OFT is specifically tailored. From another perspective, OFT effectively performs finetuning on neurons constrained to a smooth hyperspherical manifold, which enhances the optimization trajectory. Empirical results supporting this perspective are reported in \cite{liu2021orthogonal}.

\textbf{Why not minimize hyperspherical energy}. Contrary to \cite{liu2021orthogonal}, our approach does not seek to minimize hyperspherical energy. For classification tasks, having non-redundant neurons is preferable. Achieving minimal hyperspherical energy would space neurons evenly around the hypersphere, but this does not serve the purpose of finetuning, as it risks compromising the beneficial information inherited from pretraining.

\textbf{Balancing flexibility and regularity in finetuning}. Our analysis reveals a fundamental trade-off between flexibility and regularity during model finetuning. While conventional finetuning grants the highest degree of flexibility, it suffers from instability issues and is prone to model collapse. Remarkably, OFT, through its straightforward approach, achieves an effective compromise between maintaining flexibility and enforcing regularity by conserving the pairwise angular relationships among neurons. In addition, the block-diagonal parameterization provides an even more stringent regularization on the orthogonal matrix.

\textbf{Inference efficiency remains unchanged}. In contrast to ControlNet, the OFT framework incurs no extra computational burden during inference for the adapted model. During the inference phase, it suffices to left-multiply the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ with the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$, resulting in an updated weight matrix $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W} = \bm{R} \bm{W}^0$. Consequently, the inference runtime matches that of the original pretrained model.

\section{Experiments and Results}

\textbf{Experimental setup}. Our experiments are conducted using Stable Diffusion v1.5~\cite{rombach2022high} as the base pretrained text-to-image architecture. To maintain fairness, image samples are randomly drawn from outputs of each method under evaluation. For subject-driven tasks, we largely adopt the protocols in DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}. For experiments in controllable image generation, procedures are aligned with ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}. To directly compare with LoRA, we restrict the application of OFT or COFT to the same layers utilized by LoRA. Further implementation specifics and comprehensive results are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}.

\subsection{Subject-driven Generation}

\textbf{Configuration}. We consider DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} and LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} as baseline approaches. The loss function employed across all compared methods is identical to that used in DreamBooth. When deploying DreamBooth and LoRA, we generally follow the configurations and optimal hyperparameter choices outlined in the respective original publications. Additional results and further experimental details are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:settings},\ref{app:coft_oft},\ref{app:more_qualitative},\ref{app:failure}.

\textbf{Finetuning stability and convergence}. We begin by examining the robustness and convergence rate of DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT during finetuning. The outcomes, depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} and Figure~\ref{fig:db_convergence}, indicate that both COFT and OFT provide stable adaptation of the diffusion model. At the 400-iteration mark, both DreamBooth and the OFT-based approaches offer strong control, whereas LoRA exhibits a failure in maintaining subject identity. As training extends to 2000 iterations, DreamBooth exhibits mode collapse, generating suboptimal images, while LoRA produces yellow fur instead of the expected yellow shirt. In contrast, OFT and COFT consistently maintain stable and effective control throughout image generation. These findings confirm the rapid yet robust convergence characteristics of the OFT framework when dealing with subject-driven synthesis tasks. Notably, the robustness to the number of finetuning iterations is crucial, as it mitigates the need for subject-specific tuning of iteration counts. For both OFT and COFT, it is practical to use a generous upper bound for the number of iterations without precise adjustment. Specifically, when employing COFT with a suitable $\epsilon$, both the learning rate and iteration number are simplified, making them easy to set.

\textbf{Quantitative comparison}. Adopting the evaluation strategy from \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, we quantitatively assess subject fidelity (using DINO~\cite{caron2021emerging}, CLIP-I~\cite{radford2021learning}), text prompt fidelity (CLIP-T~\cite{radford2021learning}), and sample diversity (LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}). The CLIP-I metric is calculated by averaging the pairwise cosine similarities of CLIP embeddings between generated and reference images. For DINO, a comparable process is used, but with ViT S/16 DINO embeddings. CLIP-T quantifies the mean cosine similarity between CLIP embeddings of text prompts and generated images. To assess diversity, we calculate the average LPIPS cosine similarity among images generated for the same subject and prompt. As reported in Table~\ref{table:dreambooth_final}, both COFT and OFT surpass DreamBooth and LoRA on DINO and CLIP-I metrics by a significant margin, while also delivering marginally superior or comparable results in text fidelity and diversity. Each method is evaluated by repeatedly finetuning an identical pretrained model across 30 different random seeds to reduce stochastic variability. Altogether, these results demonstrate that our OFT approach not only excels in convergence speed and stability but also reliably delivers enhanced final performance.

\textbf{Qualitative comparison}. To provide a clearer illustration of the advantages offered by OFT, Figure~\ref{fig:dreambooth_final} presents a selection of randomly chosen examples from subject-driven generation experiments. For unbiased evaluation, all samples are created using an identically finetuned model across different methods; no prompts are further tailored to each resulting image. Within each approach, the version attaining the highest validation CLIP scores is utilized. Examining Figure~\ref{fig:dreambooth_final}, it is evident that both OFT and COFT excel at preserving the subject's semantic content, whereas LoRA frequently struggles to retain subject identity (\emph{e.g.}, the bowl subject is completely lost in the LoRA example). Additionally, OFT and COFT enable notably stronger prompt-based control compared to DreamBooth, which, although effective at maintaining subject identity, frequently fails to produce images that match the textual instructions (\emph{e.g.}, see the first row in the bowl example). These observations confirm that our OFT framework effectively balances prompt controllability with faithful subject preservation. Furthermore, OFT’s performance remains stable regardless of iteration count, exhibiting robustness even with more iterations, in contrast to DreamBooth and LoRA, which do not share this property. Supplementary examples can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:more_qualitative}. In addition, Appendix~\ref{app:human} details a human study substantiating OFT’s superior results.

\subsection{Controllable Generation}

\textbf{Settings}. As baselines for comparison, we utilize ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}, T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}, and LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}. Our main paper explores three complex tasks for controllable image generation: canny edge-to-image (C2I) on the COCO dataset~\cite{lin2014microsoft}, segmentation map-to-image (S2I) on ADE20K~\cite{zhou2017scene}, and landmark-to-face (L2F) on CelebA-HQ~\cite{karras2018progressive,xia2021tedigan}. Each method is used to finetune Stable Diffusion~(SD) v1.5 on the respective dataset for a total of 20 epochs. Additional findings, as well as further control tasks and failure analyses, are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:more_qualitative},~\ref{app:more_control_task},~\ref{app:failure}.

\textbf{Convergence}. To assess convergence properties, we examine ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA, and COFT on the L2F benchmark, conducting both quantitative and qualitative analyses. Quantitatively, we calculate the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the landmarks derived from the control face and those generated by the model. Figure~\ref{fig:face_convergence} presents the evolution of face landmark error as a function of training epochs for each fine-tuned model. From this figure, it is evident that COFT and OFT converge notably faster compared to other approaches. Notably, LoRA requires 20 epochs to match the performance level that OFT achieves after only 8 epochs of training. Importantly, OFT and COFT maintain a trainable parameter count similar to that of LoRA (which is substantially less than ControlNet), yet both demonstrate much swifter convergence relative to established baselines. This rapid learning dynamic for OFT is further corroborated in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, where the rightmost panel illustrates that OFT achieves successful convergence using merely 5\% of the ADE20K dataset, outperforming both ControlNet and LoRA in terms of data efficiency. For our qualitative analysis, we primarily compare OFT, COFT, and ControlNet, as ControlNet yields landmark errors most comparable to our own methods. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:face_convergence_qual}, both OFT and COFT display stable convergence behavior, with generated face poses progressively matching the target control landmarks over time. By contrast, ControlNet exhibits a delayed, abrupt improvement in controllability after the 8th epoch—a phenomenon in line with the sudden convergence effect reported by \cite{zhang2023adding}. This enhanced stability in convergence not only renders OFT more straightforward to deploy, but also makes the training trajectory smoother and more predictable, simplifying the selection of hyperparameters for OFT.

\textbf{Quantitative comparison}. To assess controllable generation, we propose a metric for control consistency. This metric involves extracting the control signal from the generated output and comparing it directly to the initial input control signal. For the C2I task, Intersection over Union (IoU) and F1 scores are measured; for the S2I task, we report mean IoU along with both mean and overall accuracy; for the L2F task, evaluation is based on the mean $\ell_2$ distance calculated between the actual control landmarks and those predicted. Additional information about these consistency measures is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}. All methods are evaluated under optimal hyperparameter configurations. As detailed in Table~\ref{table:control_gen}, both OFT and COFT consistently provide more precise and reliable control than competing approaches. Notably, adapter-based models (\emph{e.g.}, T2I-Adapter and ControlNet) demonstrate slower convergence rates and deliver inferior final performance. While LoRA surpasses ControlNet on the S2I task, its results fall short in C2I and L2F. Overall, LoRA’s upper-bound performance remains modest, even with thorough hyperparameter optimization. In contrast, OFT exhibits continued performance gains as the model capacity increases, suggesting its ceiling has not yet been reached. Our quantitative evaluation framework for controllable generation represents a significant contribution, as it enables rigorous assessment of control capabilities in fine-tuned models, constrained only by the underlying accuracy of the off-the-shelf segmentation or detection model.

\textbf{Qualitative comparison}. We further conduct a qualitative evaluation of OFT and COFT, benchmarking them against established state-of-the-art techniques such as ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:control_final}, randomly sampled outputs indicate that both OFT and COFT produce images of high realism and fidelity, while simultaneously maintaining precise control over generation. For the S2I task, LoRA fails to align the generated images with the provided segmentation maps, whereas ControlNet, OFT, and COFT successfully guide image synthesis according to the input. Notably, compared to ControlNet, OFT and COFT generate images with richer details and more plausible geometric structures, all while using substantially fewer model parameters. In the context of the C2I task, OFT and COFT are able to generate semantically coherent images from sparse Canny edges, a scenario in which T2I-Adapter and LoRA struggle considerably. For the L2F task, our proposed method attains the most precise pose alignment for synthesized facial images, even in the presence of complex pose variations. Across all three evaluated control tasks, the results demonstrate that OFT and COFT consistently produce images of superior quality compared to leading baselines, underscoring the efficacy of the OFT framework for controllable image generation. To extend our qualitative assessment, we include further examples spanning all three control tasks in Appendix~\ref{app:control_exp}, and also showcase OFT's robustness on additional tasks—such as dense pose to human body, sketch to image, and depth to image—in Appendix~\ref{app:more_control_task}.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Open Problems}

Drawing inspiration from the observation that angular relationships among neurons play a fundamental role in defining visual semantics, we introduce a straightforward yet robust finetuning approach—orthogonal finetuning—for exerting control over text-to-image diffusion models. Our method is applied to two primary scenarios: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Relative to prevailing techniques, OFT achieves greater control and stability during finetuning while utilizing a smaller set of tunable parameters. Notably, OFT incurs no additional cost during inference, making it suitable for practical deployment.

OFT brings forth several compelling questions for further research. To enforce orthogonality, OFT utilizes Cayley parametrization, which requires computing a matrix inverse. This component imposes certain restrictions on the scalability of OFT. While adopting a block diagonal parametrization partially mitigates this constraint, efficiently performing differentiable matrix inversion remains an unresolved issue. Additionally, OFT exhibits promising properties for compositionality: orthogonal matrices generated by separate OFT finetuning tasks can be multiplied, yielding another orthogonal matrix. It remains to be seen whether these orthogonal matrices maintain the knowledge acquired across multiple downstream tasks—a topic ripe for future investigation. Lastly, the parameter efficiency achieved by OFT largely relies on the block diagonal structure, which can introduce bias and restrict adaptability. Discovering approaches that further enhance parameter efficiency while minimizing bias is an important avenue for continued exploration.
