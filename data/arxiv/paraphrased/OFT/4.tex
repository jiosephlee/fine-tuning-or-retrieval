\title{Controlling Text-to-Image Diffusion by Orthogonal Finetuning}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale text-to-image diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable proficiency in synthesizing photorealistic images from textual descriptions. An outstanding open question remains: how can these potent models be systematically guided or controlled to fulfill diverse downstream objectives? To this end, we present Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT), a principled approach for finetuning text-to-image diffusion models toward various downstream tasks. In contrast to prior techniques, OFT rigorously maintains the hyperspherical energy, which encodes the pairwise interactions between neurons mapped onto the unit hypersphere. Our investigations suggest that this preservation is vital to maintaining the semantic generative capabilities inherent in text-to-image diffusion models. To bolster the stability of the finetuning procedure, we introduce an enhanced variant, Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning (COFT), which applies an additional constraint on the hypersphere’s radius. Our focus centers on two significant finetuning scenarios: subject-driven generation, aiming to create subject-specific images from a limited set of example images and a corresponding text prompt; and controllable generation, where the model incorporates supplementary control inputs. Experimental evidence shows that the OFT methodology achieves superior generation fidelity and accelerates convergence relative to existing approaches.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent advances in text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical,rombach2022high} have significantly improved the fidelity and controllability of image generation driven by text. Nonetheless, despite such progress, reliance solely on text prompts often leads to inherent ambiguities, falling short of providing the precise and detailed control sometimes required for the output images. This work concentrates on addressing two particular types of text-to-image tasks:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

    \item \textbf{Subject-driven generation}~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}: This task involves synthesizing images of a specified subject in novel contexts by utilizing only a handful of subject images along with a text prompt for guidance.
    \item \textbf{Controllable generation}~\cite{zhang2023adding,mou2023t2i}: Here, the goal is to produce images that adhere to both an external control signal (\emph{e.g.}, canny edges, segmentation masks) and a textual prompt.
\end{itemize}

Both of these tasks fundamentally involve developing approaches to finetune text-to-image diffusion models in a way that maintains their pretrained generative capabilities. The key objectives for an effective finetuning procedure can be summarized as follows: (1) \emph{training efficiency}, which includes minimizing both the number of parameters to be trained and the total training time (in terms of epochs); and (2) \emph{generalizability preservation}, ensuring the retention of the pretrained model's ability to produce high-quality and diverse outputs. Commonly, finetuning is performed either by making incremental updates to the existing neuron weights with a small learning rate (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}), or by introducing a lightweight module featuring re-parameterized neuron weights (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{hulora2022,zhang2023adding}). Despite their straightforwardness, these approaches do not reliably ensure that the model's original generative prowess is preserved. Moreover, there remains an insufficiently developed theoretical framework for designing effective finetuning strategies or selecting appropriate hyperparameters, such as the optimal number of training epochs. A primary challenge stems from the absence of a quantitative metric to evaluate how well the finetuned model retains its pretrained generative qualities. Existing approaches to finetuning often operate under the assumption that reducing the Euclidean distance between the weights of the finetuned and original models correlates with greater preservation of pretrained performance. Consequently, small learning rates are commonly adopted. However, while this principle might occasionally prove effective, we contend that measuring Euclidean distance alone falls short of adequately assessing semantic retention. Therefore, employing a more structural metric to evaluate the distinctions between the finetuned and pretrained models could significantly improve both the maintenance of the pretrained model's performance and the overall robustness of the finetuning process.

Building on empirical findings that hyperspherical similarity effectively captures semantic relationships~\cite{liu2017deep,liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular}, we employ hyperspherical energy~\cite{liu2018learning} to analyze pairwise connections among neurons. Specifically, hyperspherical energy is computed as the aggregate of hyperspherical similarities (such as cosine similarity) across all neuron pairs within a layer, providing a measure of neuron dispersion on the unit hypersphere~\cite{liu2021learning}. Our underlying assumption is that optimal finetuned models should exhibit minimal shifts in hyperspherical energy relative to their pretrained counterparts. Although one straightforward approach would be to regularize the training objective to keep hyperspherical energy constant throughout finetuning, this does not ensure that deviations in hyperspherical energy are actually minimized. To address this, we leverage a fundamental invariance: pairwise hyperspherical similarity remains unchanged when all neurons undergo the same orthogonal transformation. Drawing on this property, we introduce Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT), a technique that adapts large text-to-image diffusion models to new tasks while maintaining their original hyperspherical energy. The key mechanism involves learning a shared orthogonal transformation for each layer, thereby preserving the pairwise angular relationships between neurons. OFT can also be interpreted as redefining the canonical coordinate basis for neurons within a layer. Furthermore, acknowledging that smaller Euclidean distances between finetuned and pretrained models enhance retention of pretraining performance, we put forward a variant called Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning~(COFT). This method further limits the finetuned model to remain within a hypersphere of fixed radius centered at the pretrained neuron positions.

The rationale behind using orthogonal transformations for finetuning neurons is partly inspired by the properties of the 2D Fourier transform, which allows an image to be represented through its magnitude and phase spectra. Importantly, the phase spectrum—which encodes the angular relationship between the input and the basis vectors—retains most of the semantic content of the image. Notably, reconstructing an image by combining its original phase spectrum with an arbitrary magnitude spectrum generally yields an image that maintains its semantic essence. This observation indicates that altering the directions of neurons is central to effecting semantic changes in generated images, aligning with the objectives of both subject-driven and controllable image generation. Nonetheless, if neuron directions are modified with excessive flexibility, this can undermine the generative performance established during pretraining. To restrict this flexibility, we advocate maintaining the pairwise angles between neurons, premised on the assumption that such angular relationships encapsulate critical knowledge within neural networks. Accordingly, we introduce a layer-wise orthogonal transformation shared among neurons, which guarantees that the hyperspherical energy of the network is conserved.

Our approach is further motivated by orthogonal over-parameterized training~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}, a method for training classification neural networks from the ground up via orthogonal transformations applied to random initializations. Randomly initialized neural networks are known to possess, on average, low hyperspherical energy, and the strategy in~\cite{liu2021orthogonal} is to retain this low energy state throughout training—a factor correlated with improved classification generalization~\cite{liu2018learning,lin2020regularizing}. The findings in~\cite{liu2021orthogonal} demonstrate that orthogonal transformations provide sufficient capacity for building well-generalizing networks for classification tasks. Our work, however, centers on finetuning text-to-image diffusion models, targeting enhanced controllability and superior generative results in downstream applications. The distinction between OFT and the method described in~\cite{liu2021orthogonal} is notable in two respects. Firstly, whereas~\cite{liu2021orthogonal} endeavors to minimize hyperspherical energy, our OFT approach strives to maintain the hyperspherical energy at the same level as that of the pretrained model, thereby safeguarding its foundational structure during finetuning. This preservation is crucial, as reducing hyperspherical energy during diffusion model finetuning may compromise the original semantic composition. Secondly, OFT incorporates an explicit mechanism to minimize deviation from the pretrained configuration, resulting in a more constrained approach compared to~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}, which imposes no such restriction. Striking an optimal balance between adaptability and preservation of pretrained information is at the heart of effective finetuning; we contend that our OFT method accomplishes this balance robustly. Our principal contributions are enumerated below:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]

    \item We introduce Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a new approach to finetuning designed to enhance the controllability of text-to-image diffusion models. Additionally, to address stability concerns, we propose a constrained form of OFT that restricts angular divergence from the original pretrained parameters.
    \item In contrast with prevailing finetuning techniques, OFT conducts adaptation of the model while mathematically ensuring conservation of hyperspherical energy—a property that our empirical analysis identifies as crucial for retaining the generative semantics embedded in the pretrained model.
    \item Our evaluation spans two application domains: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. Through extensive experiments, we demonstrate that OFT delivers substantial gains over existing methods with respect to sample fidelity, training speed, and stability during finetuning. OFT also demonstrates higher data efficiency, exhibiting robust convergence with far fewer training images and epochs than prior baselines.
    \item To quantitatively assess controllable generation, we propose a novel control consistency metric. This measure quantifies the alignment between the control signal inferred from the output image and the original control directive, providing further empirical support for the superior controllability achieved by OFT.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Text-to-image diffusion models}. Recent years have seen notable advancements in text-to-image generation~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic,ramesh2022hierarchical,rombach2022high,gu2022vector,nichol2022glide}, propelled primarily by progress in diffusion models~\cite{ho2020denoising,song2021score,song2021denoising,dhariwal2021diffusion} as well as vision-language pretraining~\cite{su2020vlbert,lu2019vilbert,radford2021learning,wang2021simvlm,li2022blip,li2023blip,alayrac2022flamingo,schuhmann2022laion}. In the pixel domain, GLIDE~\cite{nichol2022glide} jointly learns a text encoder and diffusion process using paired datasets, while Imagen~\cite{saharia2022photorealistic} retains a frozen language encoder throughout training. Latent-space diffusion models, such as Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} and DALL-E2~\cite{ramesh2022hierarchical}, leverage learned codebooks or joint latent spaces—Stable Diffusion employs VQ-GAN~\cite{esser2021taming} for its visual codebook, and DALL-E2 utilizes CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} to connect image and text semantics. Beyond diffusion, text-to-image synthesis has also been explored with generative adversarial networks~\cite{reed2016generative,zhang2017stackgan,xu2018attngan,li2019controllable} and autoregressive generators~\cite{ramesh2021zero,ding2021cogview,wu2022nuwa,yuscaling}. OFT functions as a generic finetuning strategy, applicable across the spectrum of text-to-image diffusion architectures.

\textbf{Subject-driven generation}. Approaches to preserve subject integrity include user-supplied masks as auxiliary input~\cite{avrahami2022blended,nichol2022glide}. Inversion-based techniques~\cite{choi2021ilvr,dhariwal2021diffusion,ramesh2022hierarchical,gal2022image} adjust context while safeguarding the primary subject, whereas~\cite{hertz2022prompt} enables edits at both local and global levels without explicit masks. However, these solutions often fail to maintain fine-grained identity features. Pivotal Tuning~\cite{roich2022pivotal} modifies generators around an inverted latent code while enforcing identity preservation through regularization, and similar ideas are found in~\cite{nitzan2022mystyle}, which tailors a generative facial prior from individual photo sets. Although~\cite{casanova2021instance} can render various forms of a given instance, instance-specific details may be compromised. DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} customizes diffusion models using few subject images and a personalized token, leveraging reconstruction and class-preserving losses. Building upon DreamBooth’s setup, OFT replaces conventional finetuning (which typically uses reduced learning rates) with finetuning realized via orthogonal transformations, thus offering an alternative update mechanism.

\textbf{Controllable generation}. Image-to-image translation is essentially a case of controllable generation, and earlier approaches predominantly utilize conditional generative adversarial networks~\cite{isola2017image,zhu2017toward,wang2018high,park2019semantic,choi2018stargan} for this purpose. Diffusion models have more recently been leveraged for the same task~\cite{saharia2022palette,voynov2022sketch,wang2022pretraining}. Among notable advances, ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} introduces a strategy to modulate pretrained diffusion models by finetuning them in accordance with extra control signals, thus achieving remarkable performance in guided image generation. Similarly, the contemporaneous T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i} further enhances control over generated images through finetuning of diffusion models. Adhering to the evaluation protocols set out by \cite{zhang2023adding,mou2023t2i}, we implement OFT for finetuning pretrained diffusion models and observe consistently superior controllability, even when using reduced training data and fewer parameters during finetuning. Importantly, OFT incurs no additional computational burden during inference.

\textbf{Model finetuning}. There has been a surge of interest in the adaptation of large pretrained models for specific downstream applications~\cite{devlin2018bert,brown2020language,he2022masked}. Various adaptation strategies, such as those in~\cite{hulora2022,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion}, have been especially explored in natural language processing. Among them, LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} is closely related to OFT and is predicated on modeling the weight updates as low-rank additions during finetuning. By contrast, OFT utilizes a multiplicative, layer-wise orthogonal transformation of neuron weights, preserving pairwise angles among neurons in the same layer and thereby promoting greater training stability.

\section{Orthogonal Finetuning}

\subsection{Why Does Orthogonal Transformation Make Sense?}

We begin by examining the rationale for employing orthogonal transformations when finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. We address this topic by dividing it into two fundamental aspects: (1) the motivation for adjusting neuron angles (\emph{i.e.}, directions) during finetuning, and (2) the reasoning behind adopting orthogonal transformations for this adjustment.

To address the first question, we are motivated by empirical findings from \cite{liu2018decoupled,chen2020angular}, which indicate that differences in the angular features effectively describe the semantic gap. SphereNet~\cite{liu2017deep} demonstrates that constraining all neurons to lie on a unit hypersphere during network training provides similar representational power and, in some cases, improved generalization, suggesting that neuron direction alone encapsulates the essential information present in the data. To further highlight the significance of neuron angles, we present a simple experiment as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}: we train a basic convolutional autoencoder on flower images, using the standard inner product to calculate feature maps in the training phase (here, $z$ is the output from applying the convolution kernel $\bm{w}$ to the input $\bm{x}$ in a moving window). In the testing phase, we generate feature maps in three distinct ways: (a) using the original inner product, (b) isolating the magnitude component, and (c) extracting the angular component. The visualization in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae} reveals that angular features of neurons are almost solely responsible for reconstructing the original images, whereas the magnitude provides negligible useful information. Notably, cosine activation (option (c)) is not used during training, which solely relies on inner product computations. These outcomes point to the pivotal role of neuron angles (directions) in encoding semantic content of images. Therefore, altering the semantic attributes of images can be more effectively achieved by adjusting neuron directions.

Regarding the second question, one straightforward strategy to refine neuron directions involves applying a global rotation or reflection to all neurons within a layer, which inherently involves an orthogonal transformation. While one could consider more adaptable angular adjustments that independently rotate each neuron by different angles, our investigations reveal that orthogonal transformation provides an optimal compromise between adaptability and structured regularity. Furthermore, findings in \cite{liu2021orthogonal} establish the efficacy of orthogonal transformation for learning in neural networks. To substantiate our perspective, we perform an experiment assessing the regularizing effect of enforcing orthogonality. Adopting the controllable generation setup from ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}, we report results in Figure~\ref{fig:ortho}. The data show that standard OFT maintains consistent performance and precise control at the conclusion of training (epoch 20), whereas omitting the orthogonality constraint in OFT prevents the network from producing realistic images or achieving any meaningful control. These experimental results underscore the necessity of the orthogonality constraint in OFT.

\subsection{General Framework}

In the standard finetuning approach, gradient descent is applied using a low learning rate to modify a model's parameters (or selected layers), which serves to keep updates minimal and thereby limits divergence from the initial pretrained model. Effectively, this process seeks to optimize the model while implicitly constraining $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \| \bm{M} - \bm{M}^0\|$, where $\bm{M}$ and $\bm{M}^0$ correspond to the finetuned and pretrained weights, respectively. While this implicit regularization aligns with intuition, it may still grant excessive flexibility, especially when adapting large models. To counteract this, LoRA supplements finetuning by imposing an explicit low-rank condition on the update, specifically, $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \text{rank}(\bm{M} - \bm{M}^0)=r'$, where $r'$ is a chosen small integer. Contrasting with LoRA, OFT enforces a different form of regularization, targeting neuron pairwise similarity via the constraint $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \| \text{HE}(\bm{M})-\text{HE}(\bm{M}^0) \|=0$, where $\text{HE}(\cdot)$ represents the hyperspherical energy for a given weight matrix. 

To illustrate this concept, consider a fully connected layer denoted by $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\{\bm{w}_1,\cdots,\bm{w}_n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, where each column $\bm{w}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ signifies the $i$-th neuron's weights and $\bm{W}^0$ is the corresponding pretrained weight matrix. Given an input $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$, the output is calculated as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}$, with $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ as the layer output. Within this framework, OFT can be interpreted as minimizing the discrepancy in hyperspherical energy between the fine-tuned weights and their pretrained counterparts:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:oft_principle}
\footnotesize
\min_{\bm{W}} \left\| \text{HE}(\bm{W})-\text{HE}(\bm{W}^0)\right\|~~\Leftrightarrow~~\min_{\bm{W}} \bigg{\|} \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}-\sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}^0_i-\hat{\bm{w}}^0_j\|^{-1}\bigg{\|}
\end{equation}

where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \hat{\bm{w}}_i:=\bm{w}_i/\|\bm{w}_i\|$ is the normalized vector for the $i$-th neuron, and $\text{HE}(\bm{W}):= \sum_{i\neq j} \|\hat{\bm{w}}_i-\hat{\bm{w}}_j\|^{-1}$ defines the hyperspherical energy of $\bm{W}$. Notably, the minimum value of zero in Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_principle} can always be attained, specifically when $\bm{W}$ and $\bm{W}^0$ are related by a rotation or reflection, i.e., $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$, for an orthogonal matrix $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ (with $\det(\bm{R}) = 1$ for rotation and $\det(\bm{R}) = -1$ for reflection). This forms the core of the OFT approach: rather than directly updating all weights, the finetuning process is limited to learning an orthogonal transformation, applied

\begin{equation}\label{eq:oft_general}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I}
\end{equation}

In this context, $\bm{W}$ signifies the weight matrix that has been finetuned using OFT, while $\bm{I}$ represents the identity matrix. An overview of OFT can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:block}. Drawing a parallel to LoRA’s use of zero initialization, it is crucial to configure OFT so that finetuning starts from the pretrained model’s parameterization $\bm{W}^0$. This is achieved by initializing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as an identity matrix, ensuring that the starting point coincides with the pretrained weights. Maintaining the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$ can be efficiently addressed through differentiable orthogonalization approaches, as presented in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,lezcano2019cheap}. In subsequent discussion, we elaborate on approaches to enforce orthogonality without incurring significant computational overhead.

\subsection{Efficient Orthogonal Parameterization}\label{sect:eff_ortho}

While the Gram-Schmidt process provides a differentiable means for orthogonalization, it is typically computationally prohibitive for practical use~\cite{liu2021orthogonal}. For greater efficiency, we utilize the Cayley parameterization to form the orthogonal matrix. This technique constructs the orthogonal matrix as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(I-\bm{Q})^{-1}$, where $\bm{Q}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. The primary limitation of the Cayley parameterization is that it generates only orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$—i.e., matrices from the special orthogonal group—but we empirically observe this does not harm model performance. Nevertheless, even under Cayley parameterization, when $d$ is large, using a full $\bm{R}$ remains parameter-intensive. Therefore, we introduce a block-diagonal structure for $\bm{R}$, consisting of $r$ individual blocks, resulting in the formulation:

\begin{equation}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{R}=\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{R}_1\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r}) &  &\\
    &\ddots & \\
    & & \bm{R}_r\in \text{O}(\frac{d}{r})
    \end{bmatrix}\in \text{O}(d)
\end{equation}

Here, $\text{O}(d)$ represents the orthogonal group in $d$ dimensions. The matrices $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ and $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d/r\times d/r},\forall i$ are both orthogonal. If $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=1$, then the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix reduces to a regular, fully unconstrained orthogonal matrix. For a standard orthogonal matrix of shape $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d\times d$, there are $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d(d-1)/2$ free parameters, giving it a computational complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$. In contrast, for a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix partitioned into $r$ blocks, the parameter count becomes $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu d(d/r-1)/2$ and the overall complexity decreases to $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \mathcal{O}(d^2/r)$. By choosing to tie the parameters across different blocks, i.e., enforcing $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{R}_i=\bm{R}_j,\forall i\neq j$, one can further lower the parameter count, reaching a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d^2/r^2)$. Importantly, in all these variants, the matrix $\bm{R}$ is always orthogonal, so the hyperspherical structure, and consequently the energy, is strictly preserved.

To compare OFT and LoRA with respect to parameter efficiency, consider that LoRA's parameterization with rank $r'$ introduces $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r'(d+n)$ trainable parameters. When both $r$ for OFT and $r'$ for LoRA are linked to the dimension $d$—for example, by setting $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=r'=\alpha d$ for some constant $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu 0<\alpha\leq 1$—LoRA's parameter count scales as $\mathcal{O}(d^2+dn)$, whereas OFT achieves a parameter complexity of $\mathcal{O}(d)$. As an illustrative case, suppose the underlying weight matrix is $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu 128\times 128$. With $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r'=8$, LoRA uses $2,048$ parameters, while OFT, without block sharing and with $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu r=8$, employs only $960$ trainable parameters.

\subsection{Constrained Orthogonal Finetuning}

The flexibility of OFT can be reduced further by restricting the adapted model to reside within a defined vicinity of the original, pretrained solution. In particular, COFT proposes the following computation for each forward pass:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:coft_general}
    \footnotesize
    \bm{z}=\bm{W}^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{R}\cdot\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x},~~~\text{s.t.}~\bm{R}^\top\bm{R}=\bm{R}\bm{R}^\top=\bm{I},~\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}\leq \epsilon
\end{equation}

Here, the optimization is subject to two constraints: $\bm{R}$ must remain orthogonal, and its deviation from the identity, measured by the norm $\norm{\bm{R}-\bm{I}}$, must not exceed a threshold $\epsilon$. Orthogonality is enforced using the Cayley parameterization (see Section~\ref{sect:eff_ortho}). However, ensuring that the $\epsilon$-deviation constraint is satisfied in the space of Cayley-parameterized orthogonal matrices presents additional challenges. Given that the series converges in the operator norm, the constraint $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\|\bm{R}-\bm{I}\|\leq\epsilon$ may be equivalently expressed within the domain of the Cayley transform as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \|\bm{Q}-\bm{0}\|\leq\epsilon'$, where $\bm{0}$ represents a zero matrix and $\epsilon'$ denotes a distinct error tolerance parameter from $\epsilon$. Enforcing the constraint on $\bm{Q}$ becomes straightforward through the use of projected gradient descent. To initialize the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ at the identity, $\bm{Q}$ is set initially to the zero matrix. The COFT methodology effectively imposes two explicit forms of regularization: one bounding the hyperspherical energy variation, and another restricting divergence from the original pretrained parameters. While many prior finetuning strategies implement this latter restriction in an implicit fashion, COFT makes it an overt constraint. Notably, even though OFT already demonstrates strong performance, our experiments indicate that the explicit deviation constraint in COFT further enhances finetuning robustness. Figure~\ref{fig:eps_coft} illustrates the relationship between $\epsilon$ and COFT performance. Increasing $\epsilon$ allows the COFT model to approach the behavior of OFT, whereas reducing $\epsilon$ results in finetuning outcomes that more closely align with the original, pretrained text-to-image diffusion model.

\subsection{Re-scaled Orthogonal Finetuning}

We introduce a straightforward augmentation to OFT by permitting the training of an additional scaling factor per neuron. This extension is justified by the property that scaling neuron outputs does not alter the hyperspherical energy, as normalization subsequently removes the effect of magnitude. Concretely, the forward computation is given by $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0\bm{D})^\top\bm{x}$\footnote{\textbf{Errata}: In the NeurIPS camera-ready submission, the formula for re-scaled OFT's forward pass was incorrectly reported as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu\bm{z}=(\bm{D}\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$. The implemented code and results in Appendix~\ref{app:rescaled} remain accurate and unaffected.}, where $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{D}=\text{diag}(s_1,\cdots,s_n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is a trainable diagonal matrix, with each entry $s_1,\cdots,s_n$ constrained to be positive. While OFT originally only learns the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ (see Eq.~\eqref{eq:oft_general}), in this variant both $\bm{D}$ and $\bm{R}$ are jointly optimized. This re-scaled OFT model achieves greater expressive capacity than vanilla OFT, incurring only a minor increase in parameter count. For our experiments, we retain standard OFT to isolate the impact of orthogonal transformation, yet we observe that the addition of re-scaling generally brings further improvement (as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:rescaled}).

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{OFT is compatible with diverse neural network structures.} In theory, OFT may be integrated with arbitrary neural architectures. For example, LoRA is generally tailored to attention matrices within Transformer models~\cite{hulora2022}. To facilitate fair comparison with LoRA, our evaluations limit the application of OFT to attention weight finetuning. In addition to fully connected modules, OFT is readily applicable to convolutional layers, benefiting from the interpretability of its block-diagonal $\bm{R}$ formulation. This feature offers distinct advantages not found in LoRA. When $\bm{R}$ is partitioned into as many blocks as there are input channels, each individual block modifies only its respective channel—effectively emulating depth-wise convolution kernels~\cite{chollet2017xception}. Alternatively, sharing all blocks in $\bm{R}$ enables a single orthogonal transformation applied universally across channels. Preliminary experiments investigating convolutional layer finetuning with OFT are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:convolution}.

\textbf{Connection to LoRA}. LoRA incorporates a low-rank component, effectively safeguarding the integrity of the original pretrained weights by minimizing drastic changes. In contrast, OFT maintains an orthogonal (full-rank) transformation applied to the pretrained weight matrix, ensuring that pre-existing information from pretraining is not compromised by the update. The OFT forward pass can be re-expressed as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}=(\bm{W}^0+(\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where the term $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu (\bm{R}-\bm{I})\bm{W}^0$ resembles the low-rank modification introduced by LoRA. Given that $\bm{W}^0$ generally possesses full rank, OFT is capable of producing low-rank adjustments if $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}-\bm{I}$ is itself low-rank. Analogous to LoRA’s use of a rank hyperparameter $r'$, OFT introduces a diagonal block parameter $r$ as a means to limit the number of parameters subject to learning. Notably, the approaches differ in their strategies for parameter efficiency: LoRA takes advantage of a low-rank construction, while OFT leverages sparsity—specifically, block-diagonal orthogonal matrices.

\textbf{Why OFT converges faster}. As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:toy_ae}, the most impactful approach for altering model semantics involves rotating neuron directions, which aligns directly with OFT’s intended mechanism. From another perspective, OFT can be interpreted as optimizing over neuron directions constrained on a smooth hyperspherical manifold, a property that favors more desirable optimization characteristics. Empirical results reported in \cite{liu2021orthogonal} corroborate these optimization advantages.

\textbf{Why not minimize hyperspherical energy}. Diverging from the objectives in \cite{liu2021orthogonal}, the aim here is not to directly minimize hyperspherical energy. For classification tasks, it is preferable that neurons are non-redundant, yet forcing neurons to achieve minimal hyperspherical energy—that is, distributing them evenly on the hypersphere—may in fact disrupt useful pretraining knowledge. Therefore, such an objective does not serve finetuning purposes.

\textbf{Balancing flexibility and regularity in finetuning}. Our analysis reveals a fundamental tension between flexibility and regularization during finetuning. Although conventional finetuning offers the greatest adaptability, it frequently suffers from instability and is prone to model collapse. In contrast, the OFT approach achieves a notable compromise between adaptability and regularization by constraining the pairwise angles between neurons. Meanwhile, the block-diagonal formulation can be interpreted as enforcing a stricter regularization on the orthogonal transformation matrix.

\textbf{Zero inference overhead}. Distinct from frameworks like ControlNet, the OFT framework does not incur any extra computational costs during inference. At inference time, the learned orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ can be merged with the pretrained parameter matrix $\bm{W}^0$, resulting in an effective weight matrix $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{W}=\bm{R}\bm{W}^0$. Consequently, the inference process runs at the same speed as the original pretrained model.

\section{Experiments and Results}

\textbf{Experimental protocol}. For empirical evaluation, Stable Diffusion v1.5~\cite{rombach2022high} serves as the foundational text-to-image architecture. To avoid selection bias, image samples are chosen randomly for each approach. The protocol for subject-driven image synthesis largely follows DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, whereas controllable generation experiments adhere to the methodologies established in ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding} and T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}. For a fair head-to-head with LoRA, OFT or COFT are exclusively deployed on layers that are also targeted by LoRA. The Appendix~\ref{app:settings} provides further methodological details and additional results.

\subsection{Subject-driven Generation}

\textbf{Protocol}. Baselines include DreamBooth~\cite{ruiz2023dreambooth} and LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}. All methods utilize the identical loss formulation described in DreamBooth. For both DreamBooth and LoRA, experimental configurations and hyperparameters are selected according to the guidelines and best practices presented in their respective original works. Extended experimental outcomes can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:settings},~\ref{app:coft_oft},~\ref{app:more_qualitative},~\ref{app:failure}.

\textbf{Finetuning stability and convergence}. We begin by examining the robustness and convergence rate during finetuning for DreamBooth, LoRA, OFT, and COFT. The outcomes, presented in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} and Figure~\ref{fig:db_convergence}, indicate that both COFT and OFT enable stable adaptation of the diffusion model. Within 400 training steps, DreamBooth and both OFT variants demonstrate effective subject control, whereas LoRA struggles to maintain the subject's identity. After extending to 2000 iterations, DreamBooth exhibits mode collapse, producing implausible outputs, and LoRA incorrectly synthesizes a yellow shirt as yellow fur. In sharp contrast, OFT and COFT maintain steady and coherent manipulation of the outputs throughout. These observations confirm that OFT enables rapid yet reliable convergence for subject-driven image generation tasks. Furthermore, the robustness with respect to the number of finetuning steps is a notable advantage, as it reduces the need for careful adjustment of this hyperparameter across varying subjects. For both OFT and COFT, a generously high iteration count may be set a priori, without extensive hyperparameter search. Specifically for COFT, choosing an appropriate $\epsilon$ renders both learning rate and iteration count selection almost trivial.

\textbf{Quantitative comparison}. In accordance with the protocol in \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, we quantitatively assess subject faithfulness (using DINO~\cite{caron2021emerging} and CLIP-I~\cite{radford2021learning}), prompt adherence (via CLIP-T~\cite{radford2021learning}), and diversity (using LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018unreasonable}). CLIP-I is measured by averaging pairwise cosine similarities between CLIP embeddings of synthesized and ground-truth images. DINO mirrors this process with ViT S/16 DINO features. CLIP-T calculates the mean cosine similarity between the CLIP embedding of the guiding text and that of generated outputs. Additionally, LPIPS is computed as the average cosine distance among generated samples corresponding to the same subject and prompt. According to the results summarized in Table~\ref{table:dreambooth_final}, both COFT and OFT surpass DreamBooth and LoRA by a notable margin in the DINO and CLIP-I scores, and demonstrate marginally better or equivalent results in both prompt fidelity and output diversity. For fairness, each approach is evaluated by retraining from the same initial model 30 times, each with a distinct random seed, to control for stochasticity. Collectively, the findings illustrate that our OFT framework not only improves convergence behavior and finetuning stability, but also delivers superior, reproducible outcomes.

\textbf{Qualitative comparison}. To offer a clearer and more direct illustration of the advantages provided by OFT, we present a selection of randomly sampled outputs from subject-driven generation in Figure~\ref{fig:dreambooth_final}. In order to maintain fairness, each example is generated from the same finetuned model with only the method of interest being changed, and no individual text prompt optimization is performed for any final results. For each approach, we use the variant that attains the highest validation CLIP score. Upon examining the outcomes in Figure~\ref{fig:dreambooth_final}, it is evident that both OFT and COFT are highly effective at preserving the semantic identity of the subject, whereas LoRA frequently fails to retain subject identity (\emph{e.g.}, in the bowl example, LoRA does not preserve the subject at all). Additionally, OFT and COFT exhibit notably improved prompt-driven control compared to DreamBooth, which, despite keeping the subject identity, regularly produces images inconsistent with the text prompt (\emph{e.g.}, as seen in the top row of the bowl example). These qualitative results highlight that the OFT framework offers a stronger balance of control and subject identity preservation. Furthermore, OFT’s performance is robust to the choice of iteration number, delivering stable results even with a high number of iterations, unlike DreamBooth or LoRA. Further qualitative results are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:more_qualitative}. Additionally, Appendix~\ref{app:human} describes a human evaluation that further substantiates the superiority of OFT.

\subsection{Controllable Generation}

\textbf{Settings}. ControlNet~\cite{zhang2023adding}, T2I-Adapter~\cite{mou2023t2i}, and LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} are included as baseline methods. The study focuses on three challenging controllable generation scenarios in the main text: converting Canny edges to images (C2I) on the COCO dataset~\cite{lin2014microsoft}, translating segmentation maps to images (S2I) on ADE20K~\cite{zhou2017scene}, and generating faces from landmarks (L2F) using CelebA-HQ~\cite{karras2018progressive,xia2021tedigan}. Every approach finetunes Stable Diffusion (SD) v1.5 across all three datasets for 20 epochs. Additional experimental results can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:more_qualitative}, \ref{app:more_control_task}, and \ref{app:failure}.

\textbf{Convergence}. We analyze the rate of convergence for ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, LoRA, and COFT on the L2F task through both quantitative and qualitative assessments. For our quantitative metric, we utilize the mean $\ell_2$ distance between predicted and control face landmarks. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:face_convergence}, we display the facial landmark errors corresponding to various numbers of finetuning epochs across models. The data reveals that COFT and OFT exhibit substantially quicker convergence relative to alternatives. For instance, while OFT reaches a given performance by the 8th epoch, LoRA requires 20 epochs to attain a comparable level. It should be emphasized that OFT and COFT possess a trainable parameter count similar to LoRA—significantly less than that of ControlNet—while converging much more swiftly than established baselines. Supporting this, Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} further attests to OFT’s rapid convergence, demonstrating that OFT can achieve strong results utilizing only 5\% of the ADE20K dataset, showcasing its superior data efficiency over both ControlNet and LoRA. In terms of qualitative analysis, we concentrate on OFT, COFT, and ControlNet, as ControlNet’s landmark error is closest to the performance achieved by our methods. Visualizations in Figure~\ref{fig:face_convergence_qual} show that both OFT and COFT progress steadily, resulting in synthesized face poses that increasingly match the control landmarks throughout training. By contrast, ControlNet’s controllability effect manifests suddenly after the 8th epoch, reflecting the abrupt convergence described in \cite{zhang2023adding}. This inherent stability in OFT’s convergence translates to more consistent and foreseeable training dynamics, simplifying hyperparameter selection and practical implementation of our framework.

\textbf{Quantitative comparison}. We propose a control consistency metric designed to assess the effectiveness of controllable generation. This metric operates by extracting the control signal from the synthesized image and measuring its alignment with the initial input control signal. For the C2I task, we utilize both IoU and F1 score as evaluation metrics. In the case of the S2I task, we report mean IoU along with mean and overall accuracy. For the L2F task, we assess performance by calculating the mean $\ell_2$ distance between the ground-truth control landmarks and the landmarks predicted in the generated outputs. Further technical specifications of these consistency metrics are elaborated in Appendix~\ref{app:settings}. For every method under comparison, the most favorable hyperparameter configuration is employed. According to the results presented in Table~\ref{table:control_gen}, both OFT and COFT exhibit substantially greater control accuracy and effectiveness relative to other baseline methods. Additionally, we notice that methods reliant on adapters (\emph{e.g.}, T2I-Adapter and ControlNet) show slower convergence rates and ultimately attain suboptimal results. While LoRA surpasses ControlNet in the S2I setting, its performance trails behind in both the C2I and L2F scenarios. More broadly, our experiments reveal that LoRA’s achievable performance remains limited, even with comprehensive hyperparameter optimization. Conversely, the OFT framework does not show signs of reaching its maximum capability; empirical observations indicate its effectiveness continues to increase as the number of tunable parameters grows. It is worth highlighting that our approach to quantitatively assessing controllable generation stands as a novel aspect of our contribution, since it enables precise measurement of control fidelity in finetuned models, bounded only by the precision of the segmentation or detection models used.

\textbf{Qualitative comparison}. We further present a qualitative assessment of OFT and COFT relative to leading methods such as ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, and LoRA. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:control_final}, randomly sampled outputs demonstrate that both OFT and COFT consistently deliver images with high realism and fidelity, alongside precise control capabilities. For the S2I task, LoRA is unable to generate images adhering to the provided segmentation map, whereas ControlNet, OFT, and COFT successfully guide image synthesis based on the control input. Notably, compared with ControlNet, OFT and COFT not only maintain or surpass image fidelity and detailed realism but also achieve more coherent geometric arrangements, all with a substantially smaller model size. When evaluating the C2I task, OFT and COFT are effective in producing semantically consistent results from coarse Canny edge inputs, while T2I-Adapter and LoRA display significantly poorer performance. In the context of the L2F task, our approach exhibits the most precise control over facial pose generation, even in scenarios involving complex facial orientations. Across these three control settings, our experimental results indicate that OFT and COFT yield qualitatively superior outputs compared to existing advanced baselines, substantiating the value of the OFT approach for controllable image generation. To ensure a thorough comparison, we present additional qualitative results covering all three control scenarios in Appendix~\ref{app:control_exp}, and further demonstrate OFT’s adaptability to a wider array of control challenges—such as dense pose-to-human body, sketch-to-image, and depth-to-image tasks—in Appendix~\ref{app:more_control_task}.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Open Problems}

This work stems from the insight that the angular relationships among neurons play a pivotal role in shaping visual semantics. Building upon this observation, we present a straightforward yet robust finetuning approach—orthogonal finetuning—designed for controlling text-to-image diffusion models. Our method is applied to two principal scenarios: subject-driven generation and controllable generation. OFT exhibits enhanced control capabilities and more stable finetuning outcomes, while requiring fewer parameters than competing techniques. Crucially, OFT introduces no additional computational overhead during inference, making it an attractive option for deployment.

Several noteworthy open questions are raised by OFT. First, ensuring orthogonality relies on the Cayley parametrization, which necessitates matrix inversion and can hinder OFT’s scalability. While our adoption of block diagonal parametrization helps mitigate this constraint, identifying differentiable and efficient methods to accelerate matrix inversion is still an open challenge. Second, OFT shows intriguing promise in compositionality; specifically, orthogonal matrices obtained through various OFT finetuning tasks can be sequentially multiplied, with the product still remaining orthogonal. Investigating whether this set of matrices effectively encapsulates the knowledge of all combined downstream tasks is a compelling avenue for future exploration. Lastly, the method’s parameter efficiency is closely tied to its block diagonal design, which may introduce unintended biases and restrict flexibility. Identifying strategies to enhance parameter efficiency with reduced bias and greater generality remains a significant unresolved issue.
