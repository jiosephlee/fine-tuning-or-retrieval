\title{Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization}

\begin{abstract}
Large foundation models are increasingly prevalent, but training them from the ground up is prohibitively costly. Consequently, efficiently adapting these powerful models to downstream tasks is of growing importance. In this work, we explore a well-founded finetuning strategy—Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT)—for downstream task adaptation. Although OFT shows strong generalization, it still requires a substantial number of trainable parameters because of the high dimensionality of orthogonal matrices. To tackle this, we begin by analyzing OFT through the lens of information transmission, then pinpoint several essential criteria that improve parameter efficiency. Drawing inspiration from how the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm facilitates efficient information transmission, we introduce a parameter-efficient orthogonal representation based on butterfly structures. We integrate this parameterization into OFT, resulting in a novel, parameter-efficient finetuning approach called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). By encompassing OFT as a special case, BOFT offers a more general orthogonal finetuning framework. Finally, we present a comprehensive empirical evaluation of adapting large vision transformers, large language models, and text-to-image diffusion models to diverse downstream tasks in both vision and language.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent models like ChatGPT~\cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} and Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high} showcase the impressive generalization capabilities of large foundation models. The rapid advancements in model performance have been accompanied by a substantial growth in parameter counts (e.g., GPT-3 contains approximately 175 billion parameters). As a consequence, training foundation models from scratch has become increasingly difficult for researchers. Achieving broad progress in the field thus hinges on the ability to adapt these models efficiently without retraining them entirely. In other words, we need to effectively tailor existing foundation models for downstream tasks. There are mainly three approaches for efficient task adaptation: \emph{model finetuning}~\cite{hulora2022,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,raffel2020exploring,qiu2023controlling,zhang2022adaptive,chavan2023one}, where the model architecture is kept fixed and a subset of parameters is fine-tuned; \emph{adapter tuning}~\cite{rebuffi2017learning,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion,lin2020exploring,he2021towards}, which adds extra trainable parameters to the base model; and \emph{prompt tuning}~\cite{li2021prefix,lester2021power}, where trainable prefix tokens are appended to the input. Among these methods, model finetuning stands out as a straightforward yet effective technique that importantly introduces no additional inference latency.

The core idea underlying model finetuning is to maintain similarity between the pretrained model and the finetuned model according to specific criteria, thereby preserving the knowledge acquired during pretraining. For example, many existing finetuning methods use a small learning rate as a heuristic to minimize the difference between the pretrained and finetuned models. Considering the structured characteristics of weight matrices, a more rigorous strategy seeks to retain the relational properties of these matrices, specifically the pairwise angles between neurons. This concept has given rise to a new finetuning framework called Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT)~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, where neurons within the same layer undergo transformation by a shared orthogonal matrix, ensuring that the pairwise angles between neurons are mathematically guaranteed to remain unchanged during finetuning. While OFT has shown encouraging results in terms of generalization and convergence when finetuning text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, the dimensionality of orthogonal matrices leads to a large number of trainable parameters. To mitigate this, OFT employs a block-diagonal structure to reduce parameter count. Nevertheless, this gain in parameter efficiency comes with drawbacks: the orthogonal matrix exhibits a fixed sparsity pattern, and the orthogonal transformation operates independently across different blocks. Although this block-diagonal sparsity reduces the number of parameters, it may introduce unwanted inductive biases (for instance, the block-diagonal orthogonal matrix limits expressiveness and cannot emulate classical linear transformations), and crucially, the method for determining an optimal sparsity pattern remains unclear.

The crucial aspect of tackling this challenge lies in creating a dense orthogonal matrix while maintaining parameter efficiency. Although this may initially appear impractical since a $d$-dimensional dense orthogonal matrix typically demands $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, we introduce a novel strategy that constructs a dense orthogonal matrix by combining multiple factorized sparse matrices. This method is motivated by the insight that the number of trainable parameters can be decreased by exchanging computation time for memory space. Since the orthogonal matrix is represented as a product of sparse matrices, this multiplication must be executed repeatedly during each training iteration. To contextualize this matrix factorization, we frame the generation of a dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem. Within this framework, producing a dense orthogonal matrix through the multiplication of sparse matrices can be interpreted as transmitting information across a grid-structured graph. This information transmission perspective allows us to develop various strategies for sparse matrix factorization that restrict the number of trainable parameters while retaining sufficient expressiveness to yield dense matrices.

To attain parameter efficiency within our information transmission framework, we draw inspiration from the butterfly structures employed in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, where information can be exchanged efficiently between different nodes~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting}. In particular, the butterfly graph intrinsic to the Cooley-Tukey algorithm naturally suggests a method for sparse matrix factorization, known as butterfly factorization. Utilizing butterfly factorization enables us to generate a $d$-dimensional dense matrix as a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing $\mathcal{O}(d)$ non-zero elements. By guaranteeing that every sparse matrix is orthogonal, we obtain a highly efficient orthogonal parameterization with only $\mathcal{O}(d \log d)$ parameters, marking a substantial reduction from the original parameter count. Exploiting such an efficient orthogonal parameterization, we introduce a novel parameter-efficient finetuning technique called Orthogonal Butterfly (BOFT). BOFT generalizes OFT as a special case and offers a comprehensive orthogonal finetuning framework. A common feature shared by the block-diagonal and butterfly structures is sparsity; both incorporate data sparsity into orthogonal matrices to decrease the number of effective trainable parameters. It is instructive to compare our approach with the low-rank structure utilized in LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}; both low-rank and sparse matrices constitute principal categories of structured matrices~\cite{candes2011robust} that promote parameter efficiency.

In contrast to the block-diagonal structure employed by OFT to balance expressiveness and regularity, BOFT utilizes the butterfly structure to create a more continuous interpolation between matrices from the full orthogonal group (expressivity) and identity matrices (regularity). This approach allows us to identify a more compact hypothesis class within the orthogonal group suitable for downstream tasks. Given the extensive application of the butterfly structure in many fast linear transforms, such as the discrete Fourier and discrete cosine transforms, we contend that our structured method for parameter efficiency imparts an implicit inductive bias that enhances generalization and reduces overfitting. Our reasoning is based on the fact that the butterfly structure can readily reproduce numerous classical linear transforms, a feat unattainable by the block-diagonal structure used in OFT. Our main contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item We investigate the challenge of parameter efficiency in orthogonal finetuning through a novel information transmission framework. This framework recasts the task of designing a parameter-efficient dense orthogonal matrix as an information transmission problem within a grid-structured graph.
    \item Drawing inspiration from the butterfly structures in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, we introduce Orthogonal Butterfly, a parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning approach framed within the information transmission framework.
    \item We offer several theoretical explanations for why BOFT can substantially reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining strong expressivity and training stability. Owing to matrix factorization, BOFT also features an interesting weight interpolation mechanism (refer to Figure~\ref{fig:boft_interpolation}).
    \item For the first time, we apply orthogonal finetuning to diverse tasks beyond controllable text-to-image generation~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, demonstrating its significant potential as a universal model finetuning method. In this context, we deploy BOFT across various adaptation tasks spanning computer vision and natural language processing. BOFT surpasses existing state-of-the-art methods by a substantial margin, confirming its superior parameter efficiency and generalization capacity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT)}. As foundation models (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{brown2020language,radford2021learning,rombach2022high,kirillov2023segment}) continue to grow in size and capability, there has been significant interest in finetuning these models for downstream applications in a parameter-efficient manner~\cite{treviso2023efficient,ding2023parameter,lialin2023scaling}. Among the various PEFT techniques~\cite{houlsby2019parameter,aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona,wang2022adamix,gheini2021cross,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,sung2021training,ansell2022composable,lester2021power,li2021prefix,vu2022spot,he2021towards,mao2021unipelt,karimi2021compacter,liu2022few,sung2022lst,chen2022parameter,jia2022visual,chen2022adaptformer,zhang2022neural,jie2023fact,lian2022scaling,luo2023towards}, reparameterization-based approaches~\cite{aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona} are the most pertinent to our study. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} modifies the pretrained weight matrix by incorporating the product of two low-rank matrices, yielding strong results in natural language processing tasks. Given that LoRA fixes the rank of the added matrices, several approaches \cite{zhang2022adaptive,valipour2022dylora,zhang2023increlora} propose dynamically adjusting the rank across layers to allocate parameters more effectively. Due to its straightforwardness, this low-rank weight reparameterization technique has become widely adopted \cite{dettmers2023qlora,zi2023delta,chavan2023one}. Drawing inspiration from how hyperspherical energy relates to generalization~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal}, \cite{qiu2023controlling} introduces orthogonal finetuning as an alternative and effective means of finetuning text-to-image diffusion models. Specifically, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix that transforms neurons within the same layer, achieving superior generalization and more stable training compared to LoRA. Although OFT delivers strong results, it generally involves more trainable parameters than LoRA. Consequently, improving the parameter efficiency of OFT is a valuable objective. Additionally, it remains unclear whether OFT can be applied to a broader array of adaptation tasks beyond text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling}. BOFT enhances OFT’s parameter efficiency by employing butterfly factorization. This advancement enables us to showcase the effectiveness of orthogonal finetuning across general adaptation tasks.

\textbf{Butterfly structures}. The radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm recursively decomposes the $N$-point discrete Fourier transform into two discrete Fourier transforms of size $\frac{N}{2}$, leading to a butterfly structure representable as a product of multiple sparse matrices (the product is known as a butterfly matrix). Butterfly matrices have been previously employed to parameterize orthogonal matrices for avoiding pivoting during Gaussian elimination and boosting efficiency~\cite{parker1995random}, to stabilize training in recurrent neural networks~\cite{jing2017tunable}, and in kernel approximation techniques~\cite{munkhoeva2018quadrature}. Works such as \cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} learn fast linear transforms utilizing butterfly parameterizations. Additionally, \cite{chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} leverage butterfly matrices to facilitate efficient neural network training. Butterfly structures have also demonstrated usefulness in fast matrix-vector multiplications~\cite{michielssen1996multilevel,o2010algorithm}, approximating data-sparse matrices~\cite{li2015butterfly}, and network transmission~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting,li2003linear,rajasingh2016transmission}. Unlike prior research, our focus is on exploiting butterfly structures to improve the parameter efficiency of OFT.

\section{An Information Transmission View on Orthogonal Finetuning}

We begin by reviewing some basic concepts of OFT. To fine-tune the pretrained weight matrix, OFT re-expresses the new weight matrix as the product of a learnable orthogonal matrix and the fixed pretrained weight matrix. Unlike LoRA, which updates weights by adding a low-rank matrix, OFT updates weights through multiplication by an orthogonal matrix. For parameter efficiency, LoRA employs a low-rank structure, whereas the original OFT uses a block-diagonal orthogonal structure~\cite{qiu2023controlling} (with smaller diagonal blocks yielding greater parameter efficiency). An intuitive comparison is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:comp_lora}. The rationale for applying an orthogonal transformation in fine-tuning the weight matrix is to maintain the pairwise angles between neurons~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}, thereby largely preserving the semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining. Specifically, OFT optimizes an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear layer $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, changing the forward computation from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ denote the input and output vectors, respectively. To enable zero initialization, $\bm{R}$ is initialized as the identity matrix. To maintain orthogonality of $\bm{R}$ during fine-tuning, we follow \cite{qiu2023controlling,liu2021orthogonal} and utilize Cayley parameterization, i.e., $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}$ being a skew-symmetric matrix satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. To improve parameter efficiency, the block-diagonal structure is introduced by representing the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ as $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$, where each $\bm{R}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times b}$ is a small orthogonal matrix and $br=d$. The parameter efficiency obtained from this block-diagonal structure comes with a trade-off: it imposes the assumption that the dimensions of a neuron (i.e., a column vector of $\bm{W}^0$) are partitioned into $r$ groups, with each group transformed independently by a different orthogonal matrix. Although the block-diagonal structure has demonstrated empirical success, segmenting neuron dimensions into $r$ groups based solely on their indices lacks meaningful justification, which motivates the appeal of dense orthogonal matrices. This leads to a natural question: \emph{Is it possible to design a dense orthogonal matrix that maintains parameter efficiency?}

To tackle this issue, we suggest constructing a dense orthogonal matrix by multiplying several sparse orthogonal matrices. To offer a cohesive and intuitive framework for examining the sparsity structure of orthogonal matrix factorization, we interpret the task of producing a dense orthogonal matrix in OFT as an information transmission problem. More precisely, generating a dense matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ as a product of $m$ square matrices $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can be seen as conveying information across a grid with $d\times (m+1)$ nodes, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}. This information transmission perspective stems from the observation that a dense $d$-dimensional square matrix can be regarded as a dense connectivity between $d$ nodes and another $d$ nodes. For matrix $\bm{R}$, if the element $R_{ij}$ equals zero, it means that information from the $j$-th node cannot reach the $i$-th node. Conversely, a non-zero $R_{ij}$ indicates successful information transfer. Thus, expressing the dense matrix $\bm{R}$ as a product of multiple matrices $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can equivalently be interpreted as a sequence of information exchanges governed by the graphs defined by each $\bm{B}_i, \forall i$. The information flows begin according to $\bm{B}_1$ and conclude with $\bm{B}_m$. As a concrete illustration in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}, we examine the factorization $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$, with its sparsity patterns and corresponding graph visualized. The graph shown is the result of expanding the matrix multiplication. Within this induced graph, matrix $\bm{B}_i$ represents the connectivity between nodes at level $i$ and level $i+1$. Specifically, the $(j_1,j_2)$ entry of $\bm{B}_i$ indicates the presence of a directed edge from the $j_2$-th node at level $i$ to the $j_1$-th node at level $i+1$ (zero signifies no edge). For $\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ to yield a dense matrix, every node at the first level must be capable of transmitting information to all nodes at the sixth level. Considering only $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$, which relates the source nodes in the first level to receiver nodes in the fifth level, it becomes apparent that information from node 1 cannot be passed to node 3. Hence, the sparsity pattern of $\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ contains a zero at position $(3,1)$. Similar relationships hold for $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ and $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ between their induced graphs and sparsity patterns. In general, for a matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ to be dense, the $m$ factor matrices $\bm{B}_m,\ldots,\bm{B}_1$ must define a set of directed edges on a $d\times (m+1)$ grid where each directed edge connects nodes only between adjacent levels (i.e., columns), ensuring information from every node at the first level can reach every node at the $(m+1)$-th level.

The perspective of information transmission aids in better comprehending the sparsity pattern found in factorization matrices within OFT. Figure~\ref{fig:blk_diag} illustrates the block-diagonal configuration of $\bm{R}$ in the original OFT. Although this block-diagonal structure decreases the number of trainable parameters, it does not enable the formation of a dense matrix $\bm{R}$. Our objective is to assemble a dense orthogonal matrix by combining $m$ sparse orthogonal matrices while utilizing as few effective trainable parameters as possible. From the standpoint of information transmission, the key desired properties for achieving this are \emph{(i)} \textbf{dense connectivity}: ensuring every node in the initial layer connects via at least one path to every node in the final layer, and \emph{(ii)} \textbf{minimum free edges}: keeping the total number of edges as low as possible while satisfying the orthogonality constraint. It is important to highlight that orthogonality imposes a subtle constraint on the edges linking adjacent layers. For instance, for each matrix $\bm{B}_i$ to be full-rank (a necessary condition for orthogonality), there must be $d$ edges establishing a bijection between all nodes in the $i$-th layer and all nodes in the $(i+1)$-th layer, which sets the minimum number of edges between adjacent layers to at least $d$ (e.g., 4 in the example from Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}). These $d$ edges are essential for maintaining orthogonality and should not be included in the count of trainable edges because these elements are fixed (e.g., for a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix with $d$ non-zero elements, these elements can only be $\pm 1$). Since orthogonal matrices require fewer parameters than general full matrices, the orthogonality condition introduces additional dependencies among edges. For example, in a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix, having a zero at position $(1,1)$ implies there must be a zero at position $(2,2)$ as well (i.e., one missing edge may necessitate another missing edge). Thus, for any valid configuration of edges, orthogonality may enforce the addition or removal of certain edges. Visualizing the non-zero pattern of the resulting composed orthogonal matrix, the information transmission framework proves particularly advantageous for OFT, as we are mainly concerned with the trainable non-zero elements of $\bm{R}$, while the exact values of these elements are not critical.

A straightforward dense connection between two levels requires $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ edges (i.e., a single dense orthogonal matrix), resulting in $d^2-d$ edges (for instance, when $d=4$, this equals 12 edges). Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} illustrates an example of a valid matrix factorization that uses a total of $10$ edges, which is actually fewer than those required by a single dense orthogonal matrix. This framework allows us to analyze the parameter efficiency of OFT from a graphical viewpoint, and it facilitates the identification of valid factorizations. We draw motivation from a notable topology used in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, known as butterfly graphs~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, which can connect $d$ source nodes to $d$ receiver nodes densely and efficiently with only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges. For example, the structure in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} requires $10$ edges for dense connectivity, whereas the butterfly network needs only $8$ edges. In the following, we describe how the butterfly structure enhances parameter efficiency.

\section{Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization}

Originally employed in the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for fast Fourier transform, the butterfly structure is designed to efficiently capture the relationship where a local modification in the frequency domain causes a global effect in the spatial domain. This concept closely parallels our information transmission challenge—where each node at the initial level can convey information to every node at the final level. The butterfly structure has also gained popularity as a computer network topology~\cite{solihin2015fundamentals,li2011linear} for effective information exchange. Assuming $k \geq 2$ is a power of $2$, we define the \emph{butterfly factor} $\bm{B}^F(k)$ as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:but_factor}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
    \end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where each $\bm{d}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}, \forall i$ is a vector. For $d=2^N$, the $d$-dimensional \emph{butterfly matrix} $\bm{B}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is then defined recursively as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}(d) = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \cdot \begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2}) & \bm{0} \\
    \bm{0} & \bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
    \end{bmatrix} = \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d) \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2}) \cdots \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2})$ and $\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})$ represent two butterfly matrices each of dimension $\frac{d}{2}$. We then introduce the \emph{butterfly component} as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k))$, which forms a block-diagonal matrix of size $d \times d$ with blocks of size $k$. Here, each $\bm{B}^F_i(k), \forall i$ denotes butterfly factors as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:but_factor}. We are now prepared to utilize the butterfly matrix for parameterizing an orthogonal matrix. To do this, it suffices to ensure that all multiplicative factors $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k), \forall k$ composing the butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ are orthogonal. We begin by examining the block-diagonal matrix $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ with block size 2; it is straightforward to guarantee the orthogonality of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ by employing the Cayley transform (or a 2-dimensional rotation) to parameterize each block, as done in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}. The sparsity pattern of each butterfly component can be interpreted as a permutation of the sparsity pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, enabling all butterfly components to be naturally parameterized as orthogonal matrices. This approach yields an efficient orthogonal matrix parameterization constructed from numerous $2 \times 2$ orthogonal blocks. Following \cite{chen2022pixelated}, we extend the butterfly matrices by defining a block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ where each element in $\bm{d}_i, \forall i$ becomes a $b \times b$ matrix. To ensure the orthogonality of the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, we parameterize each $2b \times 2b$ block matrix to be orthogonal. The sparsity patterns of other butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k), k > 2$ are block-wise permutations of the sparsity pattern of $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$ and can thus be analogously parameterized as orthogonal matrices. Putting these components together, the forward pass in BOFT is given by

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}\nonumber
    \bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},~~~~\text{s.t.}~\bigg{\{}\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}~~\&~~\underbrace{\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\!=\bm{I}_d}_{\forall j\in [1, m]}\bigg{\}},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where for simplicity, we write $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m - i+1})$ as $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, and $\bm{I}_d$ stands for the $d$-dimensional identity matrix. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is constructed by multiplying several orthogonal butterfly matrices. For ease of notation, we define BOFT with $\bm{R}(m,\frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT($m$,$b$), where $b\geq 2$. When $m=1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) simplifies to the block-diagonal OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling} having block size $b$. BOFT($1$,$d$) reduces to the original OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling} with an unconstrained full orthogonal matrix. BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$,$b$) employs the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ as $\bm{R}$, producing a dense orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$. Generally, BOFT($m$,$b$) involves $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective trainable parameters when fine-tuning a linear layer sized $d\times n$. If the butterfly matrix is used, i.e., $m=\log d$ and $b=2$, BOFT requires $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. In contrast, the original OFT with a fully dense orthogonal matrix demands $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, while block-diagonal OFT with $r$ blocks uses $\mathcal{O}(bd)$. Consequently, the original OFT must choose block size $b=d$ to create a dense orthogonal matrix, whereas BOFT can select any $b$ to achieve the same.

\textbf{Identity initialization for BOFT}. Fine-tuning approaches typically begin with the exact pretrained model to ensure the fine-tuned model remains close to the pretrained one. For instance, LoRA initializes low-rank weights to zero. In BOFT, we initialize all butterfly components as identity matrices (i.e., the skew-symmetric matrix in the Cayley transform is initialized to zero).

\textbf{Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT}. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} additionally introduces a Dropout layer for the low-rank weight updates to mitigate overfitting. While standard Dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} applies naturally to LoRA, it is incompatible with BOFT due to the multiplicative nature of the weight update. To overcome this, we propose a multiplicative Dropout tailored for BOFT. Since the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ consists of $m$ orthogonal butterfly components, which can be rearranged into $2b\times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal matrices, the multiplicative Dropout randomly selects $p_1$ percent of butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of diagonal blocks within each butterfly component, then replaces these blocks with identity matrices.

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{Expressivity of BOFT}. The combination of the butterfly structure and permutations can exactly replicate many traditional fast linear transforms~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} (such as the fast Fourier transform and Hadamard transform). However, the capability of our orthogonal butterfly matrix to approximate a general orthogonal matrix remains unclear. To investigate this, we perform a simulation to approximate a random dense orthogonal matrix~\cite{anderson1987generation} of size $512 \times 512$. The outcomes shown in Figure~\ref{fig:para_eff} are averaged over 10 random seeds. The y-axis represents the approximation error, while the x-axis indicates the number of effective trainable parameters. Curves sharing the same color correspond to BOFT with the same block size, with the leftmost point representing the error of BOFT($1$,$b$) (i.e., the original block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$). In general, BOFT demonstrates greater parameter efficiency than OFT. For instance, BOFT($9$,$2$) exhibits superior expressiveness compared to BOFT($1$,$16$) while using significantly fewer parameters. BOFT with smaller $b$ and larger $m$ tends to be more parameter-efficient; for example, BOFT($6$,$4$) uses considerably fewer parameters but achieves a comparable approximation error to BOFT($2$,$16$). Overall, the butterfly matrix forms a more structured subset within the orthogonal group (relative to the block-diagonal structure), rendering BOFT provably more expressive than OFT with the same block size.

\begin{theorem}[Expressivity of BOFT]\label{thm:exp_boft}
    BOFT exhibits greater expressiveness than OFT for an identical block size. To approximate any orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$ using butterfly matrices, one can multiply butterfly matrices as $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$, where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d)$ are butterfly matrices for all $i,j$.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:exp_boft} points to a straightforward extension of BOFT, where the final orthogonal matrix generalizes to $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2,b_2)$, with $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m,b)$ denoting the orthogonal matrix employed in BOFT. When $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, the matrix $\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ can represent the entire orthogonal group. This form of matrix composition is also known as the kaleidoscope hierarchy~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}. Nevertheless, it is important to note that increased expressiveness does not necessarily translate to improved performance during finetuning; full finetuning, although universally expressive, often results in unsatisfactory outcomes. The balance between expressivity and regularity is crucial for the generalizability of model finetuning. BOFT expands the finetuning parameter space while incorporating structural priors, enabling a more favorable balance between expressivity and regularity.

\textbf{Spectral properties}. Orthogonal finetuning typically achieves superior spectral characteristics compared to LoRA because it exactly maintains the spectral norm of the pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. This can be understood through the singular value decomposition: $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix of singular values. Both OFT and BOFT apply an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ on the left, resulting in the finetuned weights $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, which does not alter the largest singular value (i.e., the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$). This preservation has been demonstrated to significantly improve training stability and generalization~\cite{miyato2018spectral,yoshida2017spectral}. Additional intriguing mathematical properties are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:sn_property}.

\textbf{Orthogonal finetuning as learning bilinear similarity}. BOFT can be interpreted as learning the bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i\bm{R}\bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ denotes the $i$-th neuron (i.e., column vector) of the weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$. Essentially, BOFT involves learning a bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ under the strong constraint that $\bm{R}$ must be orthogonal, establishing an intrinsic link to distance metric learning~\cite{xing2002distance} and bilinear forms~\cite{roman2005advanced}.

\textbf{Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT}. Since the matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in BOFT typically represents a structured subset of the orthogonal group, it restricts the hypothesis space and thereby naturally induces an inductive bias. We contend that the structured inductive bias introduced by butterfly factorization enhances generalization, given its shared structural patterns with many classical linear transforms~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}, including the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transform, and Hadamard transform. Furthermore, the sparse matrix factorization employed in BOFT might also contribute implicit inductive biases~\cite{gunasekar2017implicit,li2018algorithmic,liu2019neural}.

\textbf{Comparison to butterfly-based sparse training}. Several studies~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope,chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} have explored sparse training using butterfly parameterizations. These works generally emphasize reparameterizing weight matrices directly via the butterfly parameterization and training neural networks from the ground up. In \cite{dao2022monarch}, the focus is on finetuning pretrained weights by initially projecting them onto a variant of butterfly matrices, followed by optimizing the projected components for specific downstream tasks. BOFT introduces a distinctly different finetuning approach by transforming the weights using layer-shared weight matrices.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Limitations}

This paper introduces Orthogonal Butterfly, a broadly applicable and parameter-efficient finetuning method for foundation models based on the butterfly architecture. The central idea behind achieving enhanced parameter efficiency is to represent a dense orthogonal matrix as a product of multiple sparse orthogonal matrices. To facilitate the discovery of viable matrix factorizations, we propose a graphical information transmission framework. Within this framework, we observe that the butterfly structure effectively meets our criteria for sparse orthogonal matrix factorization. We verify the practical benefits of BOFT by finetuning large language models, large vision models, and text-to-image generative models. Our experimental results further demonstrate the advantage of BOFT as a versatile finetuning technique.

Despite its empirical success, BOFT is not without limitations. Because the final orthogonal matrix in BOFT results from the multiplication of multiple orthogonal matrices, its training runtime incurs a slight overhead compared to OFT. Finding ways to enhance BOFT’s training efficiency remains an open challenge. Fortunately, once finetuning is completed, the orthogonal matrices learned by BOFT can be directly integrated into the pretrained model, incurring no extra inference latency. Additionally, it remains unclear whether the butterfly network represents the most efficient topology for information transmission. Our information transmission framework also opens avenues for inspiration from a different field—computer networking—where the efficiency of network topologies in transmitting information is extensively investigated. We anticipate that alternative network structures may offer even more efficient means for composing dense orthogonal matrices.
