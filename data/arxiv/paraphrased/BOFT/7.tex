\title{Parameter-Efficient Orthogonal Finetuning via Factorization}

\begin{abstract}
The widespread adoption of large foundation models is being hindered by the exorbitant costs associated with training them from scratch. Consequently, finding efficient methods for adapting these powerful pretrained models to specific downstream applications is increasingly critical. In this work, we focus on Orthogonal Finetuning (OFT), a theoretically-grounded approach for model adaptation. While OFT achieves notable generalization, it still involves updating a substantial set of parameters, primarily owing to the high dimensionality characteristic of orthogonal matrices. To overcome this inefficiency, we analyze OFT through the lens of information transmission, isolating a set of criteria crucial for improving parameter-efficiency. Drawing inspiration from the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm’s efficacy in transmitting information, we introduce an efficient orthogonal parameterization constructed via butterfly structures. Integrating this parameterization into OFT yields a new finetuning method, which we term Orthogonal Butterfly~(BOFT). BOFT extends the capabilities of OFT, offering a generalized framework for orthogonal finetuning while encompassing OFT as a special scenario. Comprehensive experiments demonstrate the adaptability of BOFT for large vision transformers, language models, and text-to-image diffusion models across a diverse set of tasks in both vision and language domains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The extraordinary generalization abilities of large foundation models—exemplified by recent advancements such as ChatGPT~\cite{brown2020language,chen2021evaluating} and Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach2022high}—have attracted considerable attention. These advances have coincided with a dramatic escalation in model scale

(\emph{e.g.}, GPT-3 contains approximately 175B parameters), making the process of training such models from scratch increasingly infeasible for most researchers. To foster progress across the community, it is imperative to devise methods for adapting these pre-existing foundation models without full retraining. In other words, efficiently leveraging pretrained models for downstream tasks has become essential. Approaches for efficient adaptation mainly fall into three categories: \emph{model finetuning}~\cite{hulora2022,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,raffel2020exploring,qiu2023controlling,zhang2022adaptive,chavan2023one}, which retains the original architecture but only adjusts part of its parameters; \emph{adapter tuning}~\cite{rebuffi2017learning,houlsby2019parameter,pfeiffer2020adapterfusion,lin2020exploring,he2021towards}, where new trainable modules are appended to the model; and \emph{prompt tuning}~\cite{li2021prefix,lester2021power}, where trainable prefixes are prepended to the inputs. Among these strategies, model finetuning stands out due to its simplicity, robust performance, and—crucially—because it avoids adding any additional latency during inference.

The core idea underlying model finetuning is to maintain similarity between the pretrained and finetuned models according to specific metrics, thereby retaining the information acquired during pretraining. A common strategy in current finetuning practices involves employing a small learning rate, which effectively limits the deviation between the pretrained model and its finetuned counterpart. Recognizing the structured composition of weight matrices, a more systematic perspective is to focus on conserving the relational structure within these matrices—specifically, the angles between neuron vectors. This principle gives rise to the Orthogonal Finetuning~(OFT)~\cite{qiu2023controlling} framework, where, within each layer, all neurons are jointly transformed via a single orthogonal matrix, ensuring that inter-neuronal angles remain unchanged during finetuning. OFT has shown encouraging results in both the generalizability and convergence of finetuned text-to-image diffusion models~\cite{qiu2023controlling}; however, implementing full orthogonal matrices introduces substantial parameter overhead, especially given their high dimensionality. To mitigate this, OFT employs a block-diagonal scheme, effectively decreasing the total parameter count. Nevertheless, this parameter reduction is not without tradeoffs—the imposed block-diagonal sparsity constrains the orthogonal matrices to a specific pattern and applies the transformation separately within each block. While this design economizes on parameters, it may also lead to suboptimal inductive biases—such as decreased representational power and the inability to approximate general linear mappings—while leaving the challenge of determining an optimal sparsity structure unresolved.

The central challenge lies in constructing a dense orthogonal matrix while maintaining efficiency in the number of parameters. Traditionally, creating a dense $d$-dimensional orthogonal matrix necessitates $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, which is often prohibitive. Our methodology introduces an alternative: synthesizing a dense orthogonal matrix through the sequential multiplication of several sparse matrices, each factorized appropriately. This strategy is based on the observation that we can reduce the parameter count by accepting additional computation per iteration. Since our representation expresses the orthogonal matrix as a product of sparse matrices, repeated multiplication is needed throughout training. To clarify this matrix factorization process, we recast the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix as a problem of information propagation. In this setting, forming such a matrix through sparse matrix multiplication parallels information transmission over a grid-structured graph. This perspective grants us the flexibility to explore a variety of sparse matrix factorizations, each designed to constrain the number of learnable parameters without sacrificing the expressiveness necessary for generating dense orthogonal matrices.

For parameter-efficient designs in this information transmission paradigm, we take cues from the butterfly architecture found in the Cooley-Tukey fast Fourier transform algorithm~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, where inter-node information flow is highly optimized~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting}. The butterfly graph used in the Cooley-Tukey method naturally facilitates a sparse factorization approach, known as butterfly factorization. This allows us to construct a $d$-dimensional dense matrix using a product of $\mathcal{O}(\log d)$ sparse matrices, each containing only $\mathcal{O}(d)$ nonzero elements. By ensuring orthogonality for every constituent sparse matrix, this leads to an orthogonal parameterization that uses merely $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters—a substantial reduction relative to conventional parameterizations. Harnessing this efficient orthogonal representation, we introduce Orthogonal Butterfly~(BOFT), a new finetuning approach with a strong parameter efficiency advantage. BOFT generalizes and includes OFT as a particular instance, presenting a comprehensive orthogonal finetuning scheme. Both the block-diagonal and butterfly matrix constructions share a reliance on sparsity to achieve parameter reduction in orthogonal matrices. Drawing a comparison with LoRA~\cite{hulora2022}, our method and LoRA respectively exploit sparsity and low-rank structures—two prominent forms of structured matrices~\cite{candes2011robust} employed for efficient parameterization.

In contrast to the block-diagonal configuration leveraged by OFT, which negotiates a balance between expressive power and structural regularity, BOFT employs the butterfly architecture to yield a more gradual transition between matrices drawn from the complete orthogonal group (capturing expressivity) and the identity matrices (imposing regularity). This approach facilitates the identification of a more compact and task-relevant subset of the orthogonal group for subsequent learning problems. Notably, because butterfly patterns are foundational in the construction of numerous efficient linear operations—such as those underlying the discrete Fourier transform and discrete cosine transform—we posit that our method, by leveraging such structured parameterizations, implicitly embeds an inductive bias conducive to enhanced generalization and resistance to overfitting. Our reasoning rests on the observation that the butterfly configuration can readily instantiate several well-known linear transforms, a capability notably absent in the block-diagonal approach adopted by OFT. Our principal contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item We investigate the parameter efficiency challenge in orthogonal finetuning by introducing an innovative information transmission paradigm. This perspective reformulates the search for compact, trainable dense orthogonal matrices as an information flow problem on a grid-based graph.
    \item Drawing inspiration from the butterfly networks central to the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, we introduce Orthogonal Butterfly, a new parameter-efficient orthogonal finetuning method framed within the aforementioned information transmission scheme.
    \item We supply several theoretical motivations explaining BOFT's pronounced reduction in trainable parameters while maintaining robust expressive capabilities and stable optimization dynamics. The underlying matrix decomposition inherent to BOFT also naturally affords a flexible weight interpolation mechanism (refer to Figure~\ref{fig:boft_interpolation}).
    \item To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to demonstrate the utility of orthogonal finetuning across a spectrum of tasks beyond its original scope in controllable text-to-image generation~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, underscoring its promise as a broadly applicable finetuning technique. Our experimental validation includes adaptation tasks in computer vision and natural language processing, where BOFT consistently achieves superior results relative to leading alternatives, confirming its exceptional efficiency and generalization strengths.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT)}. With the continual scaling and increasing capability of foundation models (\emph{e.g.}, \cite{brown2020language,radford2021learning,rombach2022high,kirillov2023segment}), efficiently finetuning these large models for diverse downstream applications using minimal additional parameters has drawn notable attention~\cite{treviso2023efficient,ding2023parameter,lialin2023scaling}. Among the plethora of PEFT strategies~\cite{houlsby2019parameter,aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona,wang2022adamix,gheini2021cross,zaken2022bitfit,guo2020parameter,sung2021training,ansell2022composable,lester2021power,li2021prefix,vu2022spot,he2021towards,mao2021unipelt,karimi2021compacter,liu2022few,sung2022lst,chen2022parameter,jia2022visual,chen2022adaptformer,zhang2022neural,jie2023fact,lian2022scaling,luo2023towards}, approaches based on reparameterization~\cite{aghajanyan2020intrinsic,hulora2022,edalati2022krona} are most directly related to this study. In particular, LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} modifies pretrained weights by appending a product of two low-rank matrices, achieving strong results for many language modeling tasks. Whereas LoRA applies a fixed rank across all layers for its added matrices, other works \cite{zhang2022adaptive,valipour2022dylora,zhang2023increlora} dynamically select ranks per layer to better fit the total parameter constraints. This straightforward low-rank parameterization has found broad adoption due to its effectiveness and simplicity~\cite{dettmers2023qlora,zi2023delta,chavan2023one}. Drawing motivation from research on hyperspherical energy and its role in generalization~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal}, \cite{qiu2023controlling} introduced orthogonal finetuning, which provides a different but effective paradigm for adapting text-to-image diffusion models. Here, OFT learns an orthogonal transformation within each layer to modify neuron activations, delivering improved generalization and reliably stable training compared to LoRA. Nevertheless, OFT tends to involve more trainable parameters relative to LoRA. Hence, enhancing the parameter efficiency of OFT remains an important objective. Additionally, it has not been established whether OFT can extend beyond text-to-image diffusion model adaptation~\cite{qiu2023controlling} to other transfer learning scenarios. BOFT advances the efficiency of OFT through butterfly factorization, allowing us to showcase the advantages of orthogonal finetuning across general adaptation problems.

\textbf{Butterfly structures}. The radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm simplifies the computation of the $N$-point discrete Fourier transform by recursively dividing it into two $\frac{N}{2}$-point transforms, creating a computation pattern termed the butterfly structure. This structure is formalized as a product of several sparse matrices—collectively referred to as a butterfly matrix. Prior work has exploited butterfly matrices for various goals, including the parameterization of orthogonal matrices to bypass pivoting in Gaussian elimination and to boost computational efficiency~\cite{parker1995random}, for more stable training in recurrent neural networks~\cite{jing2017tunable}, and in kernel approximation tasks~\cite{munkhoeva2018quadrature}. Other studies~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} have explored learning rapid linear mappings with butterfly-based designs, and applications to efficient neural network training have appeared in~\cite{chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch}. Moreover, butterfly structures have demonstrated utility in fast matrix-vector computations~\cite{michielssen1996multilevel,o2010algorithm}, sparse matrix approximations~\cite{li2015butterfly}, and network transmission protocols~\cite{klasing1994broadcasting,li2003linear,rajasingh2016transmission}. Distinct from these previous applications, our contribution is to apply butterfly structures as a means of making OFT more parameter-efficient.

\section{An Information Transmission View on Orthogonal Finetuning}

We begin by outlining the foundational concepts of OFT. In order to adapt the pretrained weight matrix, OFT reformulates the new weights as the product of a trainable orthogonal matrix and the fixed pretrained matrix. Whereas LoRA utilizes the sum of a low-rank matrix for weight updates, OFT instead applies a multiplicative update using an orthogonal matrix. To enhance parameter efficiency, LoRA relies on the low-rank property, but the standard version of OFT adopts a block-diagonal orthogonal matrix~\cite{qiu2023controlling}; reducing the size of each diagonal block increases OFT’s parameter efficiency. Figure~\ref{fig:comp_lora} provides an intuitive illustration of this comparison. The underlying rationale for using an orthogonal transformation during weight adaptation is the preservation of inter-neuronal pairwise angles~\cite{liu2018learning,liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}, which serves to maintain much of the semantic information encoded during pretraining. In practical terms, OFT learns an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ for a pretrained linear transformation $\bm{W}^0\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$, altering the forward computation from $\bm{z}=(\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$ to $\bm{z}=(\bm{R}\bm{W}^0)^\top\bm{x}$, where $\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denotes the input, and $\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ the output. To initialize with zero update, OFT sets $\bm{R}$ to the identity matrix initially. To maintain the orthogonality constraint on $\bm{R}$ throughout training, we utilize the Cayley parameterization as described in \cite{qiu2023controlling,liu2021orthogonal}, which formulates $\bm{R}=(\bm{I}+\bm{Q})(\bm{I}-\bm{Q})^{-1}$ with $\bm{Q}$ being a skew-symmetric matrix, satisfying $\bm{Q}=-\bm{Q}^\top$. For the sake of parameter efficiency, the orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ adopts a block-diagonal form given by $\text{diag}(\bm{R}_1,\bm{R}_2,\cdots,\bm{R}_r)$, where each block $\bm{R}_i\in\mathcal{R}^{b\times b},\forall i$ is an orthogonal submatrix, and the overall dimension is determined by $br=d$. The advantage of this block-diagonal organization is offset by its assumption: the features of each neuron (that is, columns of $\bm{W}^0$) are partitioned into $r$ groups, and the features in different groups undergo independent orthogonal transformations via separate blocks. While this approach demonstrates empirical success, segmenting a neuron's features strictly by index lacks a principled justification, underscoring the appeal of fully dense orthogonal matrices. This leads to a central question: \emph{Is it feasible to design a dense orthogonal matrix that retains parameter efficiency?}

In response to this problem, we suggest expressing a dense orthogonal matrix as a composition of several sparse orthogonal matrices. To develop a coherent and intuitive understanding of the sparsity structure inherent in orthogonal matrix factorizations, we conceptualize the construction of a dense orthogonal matrix within the OFT framework as an analogy to an information transmission scenario. Concretely, producing a dense matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ via the product $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}=\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ is analogous to the transfer of information across a $d\times(m+1)$ node grid, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}. This information transmission framework is inspired by the insight that a dense $d$-dimensional square matrix captures complete connectivity between two sets of $d$ nodes. For the matrix $\bm{R}$, a zero in position $R_{ij}$ signifies the absence of a pathway for information to move from node $j$ to node $i$, while a non-zero entry permits the transfer. Consequently, decomposing the dense matrix $\bm{R}$ into the sequence $\bm{B}_m\bm{B}_{m-1}\cdots\bm{B}_1$ can be interpreted as staged information exchange processes governed by the connectivity structures determined by each $\bm{B}_i$. The flow of information proceeds sequentially, beginning with $\bm{B}_1$ and concluding with $\bm{B}_n$. As an illustrative case in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}, consider the decomposition $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$; here, both the corresponding sparsity patterns and the resulting graphical structure are presented. The associated graph in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} emerges from a stepwise expansion of the matrix product. In this induced structure, $\bm{B}_i$ functions as a connectivity matrix bridging nodes at the $i$-th level with those at level $(i+1)$. Explicitly, entry $(j_1, j_2)$ in $\bm{B}_i$ designates the existence (or absence) of a directed link from node $j_2$ at level $i$ to node $j_1$ at level $(i+1)$ (with a zero signifying no link). For the product $\bm{B}_5\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ to yield a dense result, every node at the initial level must have a pathway to each node in the sixth level. Examining $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$, corresponding to sources at level one and destinations at level five, reveals, for instance, that information cannot traverse from node $1$ to node $3$. Thus, the sparsity configuration of $\bm{B}_4\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ necessarily includes a zero in position $(3,1)$. This relationship between the connectivity of the unrolled computation graph and the resulting sparsity pattern holds similarly for shorter products like $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_3\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$ and $\bm{R}=\bm{B}_2\bm{B}_1$. More generally, achieving density in a matrix $\bm{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ using $m$ such factors $\bm{B}_m,\cdots,\bm{B}_1$ requires that these matrices collectively define a set of directed edges over the $d\times (m+1)$ grid, where each edge links nodes between adjacent levels (i.e., successive columns), ensuring information from each starting node at level one can reach every node at the $(m+1)$-th level.

The information transmission perspective offers valuable insights into the sparsity configuration of factorization matrices within OFT. As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:blk_diag}, the matrix $\bm{R}$ in standard OFT exhibits a block-diagonal layout. While this structure effectively cuts down the count of trainable parameters, it inherently limits $\bm{R}$ to being non-dense. The overarching aim is to assemble a dense orthogonal matrix using $m$ constituent sparse orthogonal matrices, while minimizing the number of trainable parameters required for this composition. Within the context of the information transmission framework, our central objectives can be summarized as: \emph{(i)} \textbf{dense connectivity}, meaning each node at the initial level maintains at least one communication path to every node at the terminal level, and \emph{(ii)} \textbf{minimum free edges}, indicating that the total edge count must be as low as possible, yet conform to the orthogonality requirement. 

Orthogonality introduces specific constraints on the permissible connections (edges) between consecutive levels. In particular, each $\bm{B}_i$ must possess full rank—an essential requirement for orthogonality—which entails constructing a bijective correspondence between all nodes at the $i$-th and $(i+1)$-th layers using $d$ edges (as illustrated by the four-edge scenario in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}). These $d$ connections are mandatory for preserving orthogonality, are non-trainable, and thus should be excluded from the edge count since their values are fixed (\emph{e.g.}, a $d\times d$ orthogonal matrix with $d$ non-zeros where each non-zero is strictly $\pm 1$). The orthogonality restriction decreases the number of free parameters compared to fully dense matrices, but also introduces dependencies among edges: for example, in a $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrix, a zero at position $(1,1)$ automatically necessitates a zero at $(2,2)$ (\emph{i.e.}, removing one edge can entail the removal of another). Accordingly, orthogonality may require the addition or elimination of specific edge connections for certain sparse patterns. The utility of the information transmission viewpoint in OFT is underscored by its focus on the trainable, non-zero elements of $\bm{R}$, rendering the exact values irrelevant while emphasizing their connectivity structure.

A straightforward fully-connected scheme between two hierarchical layers necessitates $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ connections (specifically, a full-rank dense orthogonal matrix), leading to $d^2-d$ individual edges—for instance, when $d=4$, this amounts to 12 edges. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans}, a particular example of matrix factorization achieves dense connectivity using only $10$ edges, which is fewer than required by a full dense orthogonal connection. This representation allows for analyzing the parameter-efficiency of OFT from a graph-theoretical angle and facilitates the discovery of valid matrix factorizations using this approach. Inspired by a notable structure from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, known as butterfly graphs~\cite{cooley1965algorithm}, which permit all-to-all connectivity between $d$ sources and $d$ targets using just $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ edges, we note that while the configuration in Figure~\ref{fig:info_trans} uses $10$ edges, the butterfly topology reduces this to $8$. In the following, we elaborate on how employing the butterfly configuration enhances parameter efficiency.

\section{Orthogonal Parameterization by Butterfly Factorization}

Originating from the Cooley-Tukey algorithm for the fast Fourier transform, the butterfly structure provides an efficient mechanism for information propagation. Within the context of the Fourier transform, a change localized in the frequency domain can result in broad modifications in the spatial domain, mirroring the challenge in our information transfer context—namely, achieving connectivity such that each node at the initial stage can communicate with every node at the final stage. The butterfly graph has also found widespread use as a network topology~\cite{solihin2015fundamentals,li2011linear} in computer networks designed for optimal data exchange. Considering $k\geq 2$ where $k$ is a power of $2$, we introduce the \emph{butterfly factor} $\bm{B}^F(k)$ as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:but_factor}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}^F(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_1) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_2) \\
    \text{diag}(\bm{d}_3) & \text{diag}(\bm{d}_4)
    \end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $\bm{d}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{\frac{k}{2}}$ for $i=1,2,3,4$ are certain vector parameters. When $d=2^N$, the $d$-dimensional \emph{butterfly matrix} $\bm{B}(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ is defined recursively as

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \bm{B}(d)=\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\!\cdot\!\begin{bmatrix}
    \bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2})\!\!\!\!\! & \bm{0} \\
    \bm{0}\!\!\!\!\! &\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})
    \end{bmatrix}= \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,d)\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,\frac{d}{2})\cdots\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $\bm{B}_1(\frac{d}{2})$ and $\bm{B}_2(\frac{d}{2})$ denote two butterfly matrices of dimension $\frac{d}{2}$. We introduce the \emph{butterfly component} as $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)=\text{diag}(\bm{B}^F_1(k),\cdots,\bm{B}^F_{d/k}(k))$, representing a block-diagonal matrix of dimension $d\times d$ with individual blocks of size $k$. Here, each $\bm{B}^F_i(k)$, for all $i$, corresponds to a butterfly factor as defined in Equation~\ref{eq:but_factor}. Utilizing the butterfly matrix for orthogonal matrix parameterization now becomes feasible. The key requirement is to ensure that every factor $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,k)$ within the butterfly matrix $\bm{B}(d)$ remains orthogonal for all $k$. We first examine the block-diagonal structure $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$ with block size $2$, for which orthogonality can be readily maintained by applying the Cayley transform (i.e., a $2$-dimensional rotation) to each block, consistent with the methods described in \cite{liu2021orthogonal,qiu2023controlling}. The arrangement of non-zero elements in each butterfly component is simply a permutation of the pattern found in $\tilde{\bm{B}}(d,2)$, which makes it straightforward to ensure orthogonality for all butterfly components through suitable parameterizations. This method allows us to efficiently parameterize orthogonal matrices using numerous $2\times 2$ orthogonal matrices. Extending this construction following \cite{chen2022pixelated}, we define the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$, in which every entry $\bm{d}_i$ becomes a $b\times b$ matrix. To maintain orthogonality for the block butterfly component $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, each $2b\times 2b$ block must be parameterized as an orthogonal matrix. For the remaining butterfly components $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,k)$ with $k>2$, the pattern of non-zero blocks results from a block-wise permutation of the pattern in $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2)$, thus allowing for a similar orthogonal parameterization. By assembling these elements, the forward computation in BOFT is expressed as

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}\nonumber
    \bm{z}=\big(\bm{R}(m,b)\cdot\bm{W}^0\big)^\top\bm{x},~~~~\text{s.t.}~\left\{\bm{R}(m,b)=\prod_{i=1}^m \tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}~~\&~~\underbrace{\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}=\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big(\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,j)}\big)^\top\!=\bm{I}_d}_{\forall j\in [1, m]}\right\},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b(d,2^{m - i+1})$ is notationally shortened to $\tilde{\bm{B}}^b_{(d,i)}$, and $\bm{I}_d$ denotes the $d$-dimensional identity matrix. The orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ arises from multiplying several orthogonal butterfly blocks. For notational clarity, we write BOFT with $\bm{R}(m,\frac{b}{2})$ as BOFT($m$,$b$), with the constraint $b\geq 2$. When $m=1$, BOFT($1$,$b$) coincides with the block-diagonal OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling}, where each block has dimension $b$. Choosing $b=d$, BOFT($1$,$d$) simplifies to the unconstrained OFT~\cite{qiu2023controlling} with a full orthogonal matrix. Meanwhile, BOFT($\log \frac{2d}{b}$,$b$) applies the block butterfly matrix $\bm{B}^{\frac{b}{2}}(d)$ for $\bm{R}$, resulting in a fully dense orthogonal transformation. More generally, BOFT($m$,$b$) leverages $\frac{1}{2}(b-1)dm$ effective learnable parameters for updating a linear layer of shape $d\times n$. When utilizing the standard butterfly structure with $m=\log d, b=2$, BOFT requires only $\mathcal{O}(d\log d)$ parameters. By contrast, the classic OFT (fully dense orthogonal case) needs $\mathcal{O}(d^2)$ parameters, and the block-diagonal version with $r$ blocks utilizes $\mathcal{O}(bd)$. As a result, the classical OFT must set $b=d$ to construct a dense orthogonal matrix, whereas BOFT can achieve this density for any chosen $b$.

\textbf{Identity initialization for BOFT}. To maintain the initial output distribution close to that of the pretrained network, most finetuning paradigms retain the original parameters in initialization. LoRA, for example, initializes its low-rank adaptations at zero. Analogously, for BOFT, each butterfly block is initialized as the identity, meaning all skew-symmetric matrices (which determine the butterfly parameters via the Cayley transform) start from zeros.

\textbf{Multiplicative Dropout for BOFT}. LoRA~\cite{hulora2022} adds Dropout to the low-rank update to encourage generalization. While standard Dropout~\cite{srivastava2014dropout} is directly compatible with LoRA's additive structure, it is not appropriate for BOFT due to its multiplicative formulation. Therefore, we introduce a novel multiplicative Dropout approach tailored to BOFT. Given that $\bm{R}(m,b)$ is composed of $m$ orthogonal butterfly blocks, each block can be arranged into $2b\times 2b$ block-diagonal orthogonal form. Our method randomly selects $p_1$ percent of the butterfly components and $p_2$ percent of diagonal blocks within these components, subsequently replacing the selected blocks by identity matrices during training.

\section{Intriguing Insights and Discussions}

\textbf{Expressivity of BOFT}. The butterfly architecture, when combined with permutations, has the capacity to exactly model a range of well-known fast linear transforms~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope} (\emph{e.g.}, fast Fourier transform, Hadamard transform). However, the extent to which our orthogonal butterfly matrix can approximate an arbitrary orthogonal matrix has not been fully characterized. To explore this, we carry out a simulation in which a random dense orthogonal matrix~\cite{anderson1987generation} of size $512\times 512$ is approximated. Figure~\ref{fig:para_eff} presents results averaged over 10 independent random initializations. Here, the vertical axis reflects the error in the matrix approximation, while the horizontal axis displays the count of effective learnable parameters. Each colored curve represents BOFT configurations with an identical block size; the leftmost marker on each corresponds to BOFT($1$,$b$) (\emph{i.e.}, standard block-diagonal OFT with block size $b$). Overall, BOFT achieves greater parameter efficiency in comparison to OFT. For instance, BOFT($9$,$2$) exhibits higher expressiveness than BOFT($1$,$16$), while utilizing substantially fewer parameters. BOFT configurations with a smaller $b$ and higher $m$ generally deliver better parameter efficiency. As another example, BOFT($6$,$4$) requires significantly fewer parameters but achieves nearly the same approximation error as BOFT($2$,$16$). On the whole, the butterfly structure captures a richer and more organized subset of the orthogonal group relative to the block-diagonal form, rendering BOFT provably more expressive than OFT given equal block sizes.

\begin{theorem}[Expressivity of BOFT]\label{thm:exp_boft}
    BOFT possesses strictly greater expressivity than OFT when both share the same block size. To approximate any orthogonal matrix of dimension $d$, a product of butterfly matrices of the form $\bm{B}_{d-1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{d-1,2}(d)\cdots\bm{B}_{1,1}(d)\bm{B}^\top_{1,2}(d)$ is sufficient, where $\bm{B}_{i,j}(d), \forall i, \forall j$ are all butterfly matrices.
\end{theorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:exp_boft} points towards a natural extension of BOFT: the resulting orthogonal matrix takes the form $\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=2mu \bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)=\bm{R}_{l,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{l,2}^T(m_2,b_2)\cdots\bm{R}_{1,1}(m_1,b_1)\bm{R}_{1,2}^T(m_2,b_2)$, with $\bm{R}_{i,j}^T(m,b)$ denoting an orthogonal matrix in the BOFT framework. If one sets $m_1=m_2=\log d$, $b_1=b_2=2$, and $l=d-1$, then the generalized construction $\bm{R}^G(m_1,b_1,m_2,b_2,l)$ becomes sufficient to span the entire orthogonal group—this arrangement is sometimes referred to as the kaleidoscope hierarchy~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}. It is important to acknowledge, however, that increased expressivity does not necessarily equate to better fine-tuning outcomes; for example, complete fine-tuning, while theoretically maximally expressive, may result in subpar empirical results. The critical factor in model adaptation is striking a balance between expressivity and regularization. BOFT expands the space of finetuning parameters by embedding structural priors, allowing for a more optimal equilibrium between expressivity and regularity.

\textbf{Spectral properties}. Orthogonal finetuning methods tend to exhibit superior spectral characteristics compared to LoRA, primarily because they maintain the spectral norm of the original pretrained weight matrix $\bm{W}^0$ without alteration. To clarify, consider the singular value decomposition of $\bm{W}^0$: $\bm{W}^0=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$, where $\bm{U}$ and $\bm{V}$ are orthogonal matrices, and $\bm{\Sigma}$ is a diagonal matrix containing singular values. Both OFT and BOFT update the weights by applying an orthogonal matrix $\bm{R}$ from the left, resulting in weights of the form $\bm{R}\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^\top$. This procedure leaves the largest singular value, i.e., the spectral norm of $\bm{W}^0$, unchanged. Such invariance has been recognized as contributing positively to both training stability and the ability to generalize~\cite{miyato2018spectral,yoshida2017spectral}. Further mathematical insights into these properties are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:sn_property}.

\textbf{Orthogonal finetuning as learning bilinear similarity}. The BOFT framework can be interpreted as optimizing for the bilinear similarity $\bm{w}^0_i\bm{R}\bm{x}$, where $\bm{w}^0_i$ represents the $i$-th neuron (i.e., column vector) of $\bm{W}^0$. Within this perspective, BOFT is equivalent to learning the bilinear similarity matrix $\bm{R}$ under the strict requirement that $\bm{R}$ remain orthogonal. This links the method directly to established concepts in distance metric learning~\cite{xing2002distance} and the theory of bilinear forms~\cite{roman2005advanced}.

\textbf{Inductive bias and generalization in BOFT}. The matrix $\bm{R}(m,b)$ in BOFT is generally chosen from a structured subset within the broader orthogonal group, effectively narrowing the hypothesis space and imposing a form of inductive bias. We propose that the inductive bias arising from butterfly factorization is advantageous for generalization, as it introduces structural regularity similar to that found in a variety of classical linear transformations~\cite{dao2019kaleidoscope}, including the discrete Fourier transform, discrete sine/cosine transforms, and Hadamard transforms. In addition, the sparsity inherent in BOFT’s matrix factorization can also confer implicit inductive biases~\cite{gunasekar2017implicit,li2018algorithmic,liu2019neural}.

\textbf{Relation to butterfly-based sparse training}. Numerous studies~\cite{dao2019learning,dao2019kaleidoscope,chen2022pixelated,dao2022monarch} have investigated the use of the butterfly parameterization for sparse neural network training. Typically, these methods involve directly parameterizing weight matrices using the butterfly structure and subsequently training models from the ground up. \cite{dao2022monarch} explores the finetuning of pretrained models by initially projecting the weights onto a specialized butterfly matrix form and then refining the projected components for various downstream applications. In contrast, BOFT introduces a novel finetuning technique that modifies weights by applying weight matrices shared across layers.

\section{Concluding Remarks and Limitations}

In this work, we present Orthogonal Butterfly, a versatile and parameter-efficient finetuning approach for foundation models, grounded in the butterfly structure. Our main contribution is showing that improved parameter efficiency can be attained by expressing a dense orthogonal matrix as the product of several sparse orthogonal matrices. To facilitate practical matrix decompositions, we introduce a graphical information transmission paradigm. Within this framework, we find that the butterfly structure naturally meets the requirements for sparse orthogonal matrix factorization. Through extensive experiments, we verify the effectiveness of BOFT for finetuning across diverse large-scale models, including large language models, vision models, and text-to-image generators. Our results further confirm BOFT's capabilities as a robust, broadly applicable finetuning method.

While BOFT demonstrates strong empirical performance, certain limitations remain. Specifically, constructing the final orthogonal matrix via the product of multiple orthogonal matrices introduces slightly higher training overhead compared to OFT. Addressing BOFT's training efficiency thus remains an open challenge. Notably, after finetuning, the orthogonal matrices obtained via BOFT can be integrated directly into the pretrained models, incurring no additional computational cost at inference. Furthermore, it is still an open question whether the butterfly structure is optimal for information transmission. Our information transmission viewpoint encourages leveraging insights from computer networking, a field that has rigorously investigated optimal network topologies for efficient communication. We anticipate that novel, more efficient network architectures could facilitate even better compositions for dense orthogonal matrices.
