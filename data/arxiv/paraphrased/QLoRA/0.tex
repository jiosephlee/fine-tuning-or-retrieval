\title{QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs}

\begin{abstract}
We introduce \textsc{QLoRA}, an effective finetuning method that significantly reduces memory consumption, enabling the finetuning of a 65 billion parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while maintaining full 16-bit finetuning task accuracy. \textsc{QLoRA} performs backpropagation through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model into Low Rank Adapters~(LoRA). Our top-performing model family, named \textbf{Guanaco}, surpasses all previously publicly available models on the Vicuna benchmark, achieving 99.3\% of ChatGPT's performance, with only 24 hours of finetuning on one GPU. \textsc{QLoRA} incorporates several innovations to conserve memory without compromising performance: (a) 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), a novel data format that is information-theoretically optimal for weights with normal distributions, (b) Double Quantization, which lowers the average memory usage by further quantizing the quantization parameters, and (c) Paged Optimizers to handle memory spikes efficiently. We apply \textsc{QLoRA} to finetune over 1,000 models, offering an in-depth evaluation of instruction-following and chatbot capabilities across eight instruction datasets, various architectures (LLaMA, T5), and model sizes that are impractical with conventional finetuning methods (e.g., 33B and 65B parameter models). Our findings demonstrate that QLoRA finetuning on a small, high-quality dataset achieves state-of-the-art outcomes, even with smaller models than prior leading approaches. We provide comprehensive chatbot performance analyses based on both human and GPT-4 assessments, showing that GPT-4 evaluations present an affordable and reliable alternative to human evaluation. Additionally, we observe that current chatbot benchmarks fail to reliably measure chatbot performance levels. A targeted error analysis illustrates the specific scenarios where \textbf{Guanaco} underperforms relative to ChatGPT. We release all models and code, including CUDA kernels optimized for 4-bit training.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/artidoro/qlora} and \url{https://github.com/TimDettmers/bitsandbytes}}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) is an extremely effective approach for enhancing their capabilities \citep{min2021metaicl, wei2021finetuned, ouyang2022training, wang2022super, wang2022self, liu2022few} and for introducing desired behaviors or eliminating unwanted ones \citep{ouyang2022training,askell2021general,bai2022training}. Nonetheless, fine-tuning very large models remains prohibitively costly; for example, standard 16-bit fine-tuning of a LLaMA 65B parameter model~\citep{touvron2023llama} demands over 780 GB of GPU memory. Although recent quantization methods have been developed to lower the memory usage of LLMs \citep{dettmers2022llm, dettmers2022case, frantar2022gptq, xiao2022smoothquant}, these approaches are limited to inference and fail when applied during training \citep{wortsman2023stable}.

In this work, we present the first demonstration that fine-tuning a quantized 4-bit model is achievable without any loss in performance. Our technique, \textsc{QLoRA}, introduces a novel high-precision quantization method to compress a pretrained model to 4-bit precision, and then incorporates a small set of learnable Low-rank Adapter (LoRA) weights \citep{hu2021lora} which are optimized by backpropagating gradients through the quantized parameters.

\textsc{QLoRA} dramatically reduces the typical memory requirements for fine-tuning a 65B parameter model from more than 780 GB of GPU memory down to under 48 GB, while maintaining runtime efficiency and predictive accuracy on par with a fully fine-tuned 16-bit baseline. This represents a major advancement in making LLM fine-tuning more accessible: it enables fine-tuning the largest publicly available models to date on a single GPU. Leveraging \textsc{QLoRA}, we develop the \textbf{Guanaco} series of models, with the second-best model achieving 97.8\% of ChatGPT’s performance on the Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023} benchmark, all trainable within 12 hours on a single consumer GPU. Using a single professional GPU for 24 hours, our largest model reaches 99.3\%, nearly closing the gap with ChatGPT on Vicuna. At deployment, our smallest \textbf{Guanaco} model (7B parameters) requires only 5 GB of memory and surpasses a 26 GB Alpaca model by over 20 percentage points on the Vicuna benchmark (Table~\ref{tbl:vicuna_eval}).

\textsc{QLoRA} presents several novel techniques aimed at minimizing memory consumption while maintaining high performance: (1) \textbf{4-bit NormalFloat}, a quantization format that is information-theoretically optimal for normally distributed data, producing superior empirical outcomes compared to 4-bit Integers and 4-bit Floats. (2) \textbf{Double Quantization}, a technique that further compresses the quantization constants, achieving an average saving of about 0.37 bits per parameter (roughly 3 GB for a 65B parameter model). (3) \textbf{Paged Optimizers}, which leverage NVIDIA unified memory to prevent memory spikes caused by gradient checkpointing during the processing of mini-batches with long sequence lengths. These elements are integrated into an improved LoRA approach featuring adapters at every layer of the network, effectively eliminating nearly all accuracy compromises observed in earlier methods.

The memory efficiency of \textsc{QLoRA} allows for a comprehensive investigation of instruction finetuning and chatbot capabilities at model sizes that would be infeasible for conventional finetuning due to excessive memory demands. Consequently, we train over 1,000 models spanning various instruction tuning datasets, architectures, and sizes ranging from 80M to 65B parameters. Beyond demonstrating that \textsc{QLoRA} matches 16-bit precision performance (\S\ref{sec:comp_to_std}) and producing a cutting-edge chatbot, \textbf{Guanaco}, (\S\ref{sec:pushing}), we explore trends observed across the trained models. Our findings indicate that data quality significantly outweighs dataset size; for instance, a 9k sample dataset (OASST1) surpassed a 450k sample dataset (a subsampled FLAN v2) in chatbot performance, despite both targeting instruction following generalization. Additionally, we reveal that excelling on the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark does not necessarily correspond to superior results on the Vicuna chatbot benchmark, and vice versa—highlighting that the relevance of the dataset to the specific task is more critical than sheer size.

Additionally, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation of chatbot performance utilizing assessments from both human judges and GPT-4. Our approach employs tournament-style benchmarking, where models face off in matches to generate the superior response to a given prompt. The victor of each match is determined by either GPT-4 or human annotators. The outcomes of these tournaments are compiled into Elo scores~\citep{elo1967proposed, elo1978rating} which establish the ranking hierarchy of chatbot effectiveness. Our findings reveal a general agreement between GPT-4 and human evaluations regarding model performance rankings in the tournaments; however, we also observe cases of notable disagreement. Therefore, we emphasize that while model-based evaluation offers a cost-effective alternative to human annotation, it also entails certain uncertainties.

We complement our chatbot benchmark results with a qualitative examination of the \textbf{Guanaco} models. This analysis sheds light on both successes and failures that quantitative benchmarks failed to capture.

We make available all model outputs alongside human and GPT-4 annotations to support further research. Our codebase and CUDA kernels are open-sourced, and our techniques are integrated into the Hugging Face transformers framework \citep{wolf2019huggingface}, ensuring broad accessibility. We provide a suite of adapters for models of sizes 7B, 13B, 33B, and 65B, each trained on eight distinct instruction-following datasets, resulting in a total of 32 different open-source fine-tuned models.

\section{Background}
\paragraph{Block-wise k-bit Quantization} Quantization refers to the process of mapping data from a representation with higher precision to one with lower precision. Typically, this involves converting data types with greater bit-widths to those with fewer bits, such as converting 32-bit floating point numbers to 8-bit integers. To make full use of the lower-bit range, the input data is usually normalized by the absolute maximum value of its elements, often arranged in a tensor, so that the values fit within the target datatype's range. For instance, when quantizing a 32-bit floating point (FP32) tensor into an Int8 tensor with values in the range $[-127, 127]$, the operation is:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X}^{ \text{Int8}} = \text{round}\left( \frac{127}{{\text{absmax}}(\mathbf{X}^{{\text{FP32}}})}\mathbf{X}^{{\text{FP32}}} \right) = \text{round}(c^{\text{FP32}}\cdot \mathbf{X}^{{\text{FP32}}}),
\end{equation}

where $c$ represents the {\em quantization constant} or {\em quantization scale}. The inverse operation, dequantization, is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{dequant}(c^{\text{FP32}}, \mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}) = \frac{\mathbf{X}^{\text{Int8}}}{c^{\text{FP32}}} = \mathbf{X}^{\text{FP32}}.
\end{equation}

The issue with this method is that when a value with a large magnitude (i.e., an outlier) appears in the input tensor, the quantization bins—specific bit patterns—are inefficiently utilized, with some bins containing few or no quantized values. To address the outlier problem, a common strategy is to divide the input tensor into blocks that are quantized independently, each having its own quantization constant $c$. Formally, we split the input tensor $\mathbf{X}\in \mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ into $n$ consecutive blocks of size $B$ by flattening the tensor and segmenting the linear array into ${n = ({b\times h})/{B}}$ blocks. These blocks are quantized separately using Equation~1, resulting in a quantized tensor and $n$ individual quantization constants $c_i$.

\paragraph{Low-rank Adapters} Low-rank Adapter (LoRA) finetuning \citep{hu2021lora} is a technique designed to decrease memory usage by employing a small number of trainable parameters, commonly called adapters, while keeping the main model parameters fixed. During stochastic gradient descent, gradients propagate through the unchanged pretrained model weights to the adapter, which is updated to minimize the loss function. LoRA enhances a linear projection by adding an extra factorized projection. For a projection ${\mathbf{X} \mathbf{W} = \mathbf{Y}}$ with $\mathbf{X}\in  \mathbb{R}^{b\times h}$ and $\mathbf{W}\in  \mathbb{R}^{h\times o}$, LoRA calculates:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W} + s\mathbf{X}\mathbf{L}_1\mathbf{L}_2,
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{L}_1\in  \mathbb{R}^{h\times r}$, $\mathbf{L}_2\in  \mathbb{R}^{r\times o}$, and $s$ is a scalar factor.

\paragraph{Memory Requirement of Parameter-Efficient Finetuning} A key aspect to consider is the memory demand of LoRA during training, specifically regarding the quantity and size of adapters employed. Due to LoRA's minimal memory footprint, it is feasible to utilize a greater number of adapters to enhance performance without substantially increasing total memory consumption. Although LoRA is designed as a Parameter Efficient Finetuning (PEFT) approach, the majority of memory usage in LLM finetuning stems from activation gradients rather than the learned LoRA parameters. For instance, when training a 7B LLaMA model on FLAN v2 with a batch size of 1, and using LoRA weights equivalent to the commonly used 0.2\% of the original model weights \citep{hu2021lora,liu2022few}, the memory occupied by LoRA input gradients is 567 MB, whereas the LoRA parameters only consume 26 MB. With gradient checkpointing~\citep{chen2016training}, the input gradients are reduced to an average of 18 MB per sequence, making them more memory-intensive than all the LoRA weights combined. By contrast, the 4-bit base model requires 5,048 MB of memory. This underscores the significance of gradient checkpointing and also indicates that substantially decreasing the amount of LoRA parameters offers only modest memory savings. Consequently, more adapters can be employed without a notable increase in overall training memory usage (refer to Appendix~\ref{app:memory} for a comprehensive breakdown). As elaborated later, this is vital for achieving full 16-bit precision performance.

\section{\textsc{QLoRA} Finetuning}

\textsc{QLoRA} attains high-accuracy 4-bit finetuning by employing two methods we propose—4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) quantization and Double Quantization. Furthermore, we present Paged Optimizers to avoid memory surges during gradient checkpointing, which have traditionally led to out-of-memory issues and complicated finetuning of large models on a single machine.

\textsc{QLoRA} utilizes one low-precision storage format, typically 4-bit in our experiments, alongside a computational data type, usually BFloat16. Practically, this means that whenever a \textsc{QLoRA} weight tensor is accessed, it is first dequantized to BFloat16, and then the matrix multiplication is carried out in 16-bit precision.

Next, we describe the components of \textsc{QLoRA} and provide a formal definition of the method.

\paragraph{4-bit NormalFloat Quantization} The NormalFloat (NF) data type extends Quantile Quantization \citep{dettmers2022optimizers}, which is an information-theoretically optimal approach ensuring that each quantization bin contains an equal number of values derived from the input tensor. Quantile quantization operates by estimating the quantiles of the input tensor using the empirical cumulative distribution function.

A key drawback of quantile quantization is that quantile estimation is computationally intensive. Consequently, fast quantile approximation techniques such as SRAM quantiles~\citep{dettmers2022optimizers} are employed for estimation. However, due to their approximate nature, these algorithms can produce significant quantization errors for outliers, which are often critical values.

These costly quantile estimations and approximation inaccuracies can be avoided when input tensors follow a distribution that is fixed up to a quantization constant. In such scenarios, input tensors share identical quantiles, making precise quantile estimation computationally practical.

Typically, pretrained neural network weights follow a zero-centered normal distribution with a standard deviation $\sigma$ (refer to Appendix~\ref{app:normality}). We can map all weights to a unified fixed distribution by scaling $\sigma$ so that the distribution fits precisely within the range of our chosen data type. For our data type, we define an arbitrary range of $[-1, 1]$. Consequently, both the quantiles of the data type and those of the neural network weights must be normalized within this interval.

The information-theoretically optimal data type for zero-mean normal distributions with arbitrary standard deviations $\sigma$ constrained to the range $[-1, 1]$ is determined as follows: (1) calculate the $2^k+1$ quantiles of a theoretical $N(0,1)$ distribution to derive a $k$-bit quantile quantization data type suited for normal distributions, (2) normalize this data type’s values into the $[-1, 1]$ range, and (3) quantize the input weight tensor by rescaling it through absolute maximum normalization to the $[-1, 1]$ range.

After aligning the weight range with the data type range, standard quantization procedures apply. Step (3) corresponds to adjusting the weight tensor’s standard deviation to match that of the $k$-bit data type. More precisely, we compute the $2^k$ values $q_i$ of the data type using:

\begin{equation}
q_i  = \frac{1}{2}\left( Q_X\left(\frac{i}{2^k+1}\right) + Q_X\left(\frac{i+1}{2^k+1}\right)\right),
\end{equation}

where $Q_X(\cdot)$ denotes the quantile function of the standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$. A challenge with symmetric k-bit quantization is that it does not precisely represent zero, which is crucial for accurately quantizing padding and other zero-valued elements without error. To guarantee a discrete zeropoint at $0$ and fully utilize all $2^k$ values for a k-bit data type, we construct an asymmetric data type by estimating the quantiles $q_i$ over two ranges: $2^{k-1}$ for the negative range and $2^{k-1}+1$ for the positive range. These two sets of $q_i$ values are then combined, removing one of the duplicate zeros present in both sets. We refer to this resulting data type, which ensures an equal expected number of values in each quantization bin, as \emph{k-bit NormalFloat} (NFk), since it is information-theoretically optimal for zero-centered normally distributed data. The exact values of this data type are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:nf4}.

\paragraph{Double Quantization{}} 
We propose \emph{Double Quantization} (DQ), a method that quantizes the quantization constants themselves to achieve further memory savings. While using a small blocksize is essential for accurate 4-bit quantization~\citep{dettmers2022case}, it also leads to a significant memory overhead. For instance, with 32-bit constants and a blocksize of 64 for $\mathbf{W}$, the quantization constants contribute an average of $32/64 = 0.5$ bits per parameter. Double Quantization reduces the memory required by these constants.

In detail, Double Quantization treats the original quantization constants $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ as inputs for a second quantization step. This second stage produces quantized constants $c_2^{\text{FP8}}$ and a new set of quantization constants $c_1^{\text{FP32}}$. We apply 8-bit Floats with a blocksize of 256 in this second quantization, as empirical results, consistent with \citet{dettmers2022case}, show no performance loss with 8-bit quantization. Since $c_2^{\text{FP32}}$ values are positive, we first subtract their mean to center them around zero, enabling the use of symmetric quantization. On average, for a blocksize of 64, this reduces the memory overhead per parameter from $32/64=0.5$ bits to $8/64 + 32/(64 \cdot 256) = 0.127$ bits, representing a savings of 0.373 bits per parameter.

\paragraph{Paged Optimizers} leverage the NVIDIA unified memory~\footnote{\tiny \url{https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-c-programming-guide}} capability, which performs automatic page-by-page data transfers between the CPU and GPU to ensure error-free GPU computation when the GPU encounters out-of-memory situations sporadically. This mechanism operates similarly to conventional memory paging between the CPU's RAM and disk storage. We utilize this feature to allocate paged memory for the optimizer states, which are then automatically moved to CPU RAM when the GPU memory is depleted and paged back into GPU memory as needed during the optimizer update phase.

\paragraph{\textsc{QLoRA}.} Combining the aforementioned components, we define \textsc{QLoRA} for a single linear layer within the quantized base model equipped with a single LoRA adapter as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Y}^{\text{BF16}} =  \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}} \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}) + \mathbf{X}^{\text{BF16}}\mathbf{L}^{\text{BF16}}_1\mathbf{L}^{\text{BF16}}_2,
\end{equation}

where the function doubleDequant$(\cdot)$ is specified as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{doubleDequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}, \mathbf{W}^{\text{k-bit}}) = \text{dequant}(\text{dequant}(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{\text{k-bit}}), \mathbf{W}^{\text{4bit}}) = \mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}},
\end{equation}

In our approach, we employ NF4 format for $\mathbf{W}$ and FP8 for $c_2$.

We select a blocksize of 64 for $\mathbf{W}$ to enhance quantization accuracy, while using a blocksize of 256 for $c_2$ to minimize memory consumption.

For parameter updates, only the gradients concerning the adapter weights $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ are required, whereas gradients with respect to the 4-bit weights $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ are not necessary. Nonetheless, computing $\frac{\partial E}{\partial \mathbf{L}_i}$ involves obtaining $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$, which is calculated via equation~(5) by dequantizing the stored weights $\mathbf{W}^{\text{NF4}}$ to the computational data type $\mathbf{W}^{\text{BF16}}$. This process yields the derivative $\frac{\partial \mathbf{X}}{\partial \mathbf{W}}$ with BFloat16 precision.

In summary, \textsc{QLoRA} employs one type of storage data (typically 4-bit NormalFloat) alongside a computation data type (16-bit BrainFloat). To carry out the forward and backward passes, we convert the storage data type into the computation data type. However, we only calculate weight gradients for the LoRA parameters, which utilize 16-bit BrainFloat.

\section{QLoRA vs. Standard Finetuning}
\label{sec:comp_to_std}

We have outlined the functioning of QLoRA and its ability to drastically decrease the memory needed for model finetuning. The key question now is whether QLoRA can match the performance of full-model finetuning. Additionally, we aim to examine the elements of QLoRA, including the effect of NormalFloat4 compared to conventional Float4. The upcoming sections detail experiments designed to address these inquiries.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.} We investigate three types of architectures (encoder, encoder-decoder, and decoder only) and compare QLoRA with 16-bit adapter-finetuning as well as full finetuning on models up to 3B parameters. Our evaluation benchmarks include GLUE \citep{wang2018glue} with RoBERTa-large \citep{liu2019roberta}, Super-NaturalInstructions (TKInstruct) \citep{wang2022super} with T5 \citep{t5}, and 5-shot MMLU \citep{hendrycksmeasuring} following finetuning LLaMA on Flan v2 \citep{longpre2023flan} and Alpaca \citep{alpaca}. To further investigate the benefits of NF4 over other 4-bit data formats, we adopt the methodology of \citet{dettmers2022case} to assess post-quantization zero-shot accuracy and perplexity across various models (OPT~\citep{zhang2022opt}, LLaMA~\citep{touvron2023llama}, BLOOM~\citep{scao2022bloom}, Pythia~\citep{biderman2023pythia}) with sizes ranging from 125M to 13B parameters.

More detailed information is provided in the results section for each specific setup to enhance clarity. Complete details are available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp-setup}.

Although paged optimizers are essential for conducting 33B/65B \textsc{QLoRA} tuning on a single 24/48GB GPU, we do not present precise measurements for them since paging occurs mainly when handling mini-batches with long sequence lengths, which is infrequent. Nonetheless, we analyze the runtime of paged optimizers for 65B models on 48GB GPUs and find that, with a batch size of 16, paged optimizers achieve training speeds comparable to standard optimizers. Future research should evaluate and characterize the conditions under which slowdowns due to the paging mechanism arise.

\paragraph{Default LoRA hyperparameters do not match 16-bit performance}

Applying the conventional approach of implementing LoRA on query and value attention projection matrices \citep{hu2021lora} does not yield performance comparable to full finetuning for large base models. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:hyper} for LLaMA 7B finetuning on Alpaca, we observe that the total number of LoRA adapters used is the most crucial hyperparameter, and applying LoRA to all linear transformer block layers is necessary to achieve performance on par with full finetuning. Other LoRA hyperparameters, such as the projection dimension $r$, have no significant impact on performance (refer to Appendix \ref{app:exp-setup}).

Similarly, we observe that the default hyperparameters for fully finetuned baselines are not optimally tuned. We conduct a hyperparameter search over learning rates ranging from 1e-6 to 5e-5 and batch sizes between 8 and 128 to establish robust baselines. The outcomes of 7B LLaMA finetuning on Alpaca are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:hyper}.

\paragraph{4-bit NormalFloat demonstrates superior performance compared to 4-bit Floating Point}

Although the 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) data type is theoretically optimal in terms of information, it remains to be confirmed whether this advantage translates into practical benefits. We replicate the experimental setup from \citet{dettmers2022case}, evaluating quantized LLMs (OPT \citep{zhang2022opt}, BLOOM \cite{scao2022bloom}, Pythia \cite{biderman2023pythia}, LLaMA) of various sizes (125M to 65B) with different data types on language modeling and zero-shot tasks. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:nf4} and Table~\ref{tbl:nf4_ppl}, NF4 significantly enhances performance compared to FP4 and Int4, and double quantization effectively decreases memory usage without harming performance.

\paragraph{k-bit \textsc{QLoRA} achieves parity with 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA}

Recent research has demonstrated that 4-bit quantization for \emph{inference} is feasible but results in a performance drop relative to 16-bit precision \citep{dettmers2022case,frantar2022gptq}. This prompts a key question: can the performance loss be recovered through 4-bit adapter finetuning? We evaluate this in two scenarios.

The first compares 4-bit adapter finetuning to full 16-bit finetuning of RoBERTA and T5 models, with sizes ranging from 125M to 3B parameters, on the GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions datasets. The results, presented in Table~\ref{tab:nf4vsbf16}, show that 16-bit, 8-bit, and 4-bit adapter approaches match the fully finetuned 16-bit baseline's performance across both datasets. This indicates that the degradation caused by coarse quantization can be completely recovered through adapter finetuning after quantization.

For our second experimental setup, because fully finetuning models with 11 billion parameters or more requires multiple servers equipped with high-memory GPUs, we proceed to investigate whether 4-bit \textsc{QLoRA} can achieve comparable results to 16-bit LoRA across the 7B to 65B parameter range. To accomplish this, we finetune LLaMA models ranging from 7B to 65B parameters on two instruction-following datasets, Alpaca and FLAN v2, and assess their performance using 5-shot accuracy on the MMLU benchmark. The findings, presented in Table~\ref{tbl:llama-datatype}, demonstrate that NF4 combined with double quantization fully recovers the 16-bit LoRA MMLU performance. Additionally, we observe that \textsc{QLoRA} with FP4 falls short of the 16-bit brain float LoRA baseline by roughly one percentage point. This supports our conclusions that (1) \textsc{QLoRA} with NF4 matches the performance of both 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA finetuning, and (2) NF4 offers higher quantization precision compared to FP4.

\paragraph{Summary} Our experimental outcomes consistently reveal that 4-bit \textsc{QLoRA} using the NF4 data type equals the performance of 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA finetuning on standard academic benchmarks with established evaluation protocols. We also demonstrate that NF4 outperforms FP4 and that employing double quantization does not harm performance. Taken together, this provides strong evidence that tuning with 4-bit \textsc{QLoRA} reliably matches the results of 16-bit methods.

Consistent with prior quantization research \citep{dettmers2022case}, our MMLU and Elo outcomes suggest that given fixed resources for finetuning and inference, it is advantageous to increase the base model's parameter count while reducing their numerical precision. This underscores the significance of the efficiency gains enabled by \textsc{QLoRA}. Since no performance degradation relative to full finetuning was observed in our 4-bit finetuning experiments, it remains an open question where exactly the performance-precision trade-off lies for QLoRA tuning, which we propose as a subject for future investigation.

We move forward to explore instruction tuning at scales that would be unfeasible to examine using full 16-bit finetuning on standard academic research hardware.

\section{Advancing Chatbot State-of-the-Art with QLoRA}
\label{sec:pushing}
After demonstrating that 4-bit \textsc{QLoRA} achieves performance on par with 16-bit finetuning across different model sizes, tasks, and datasets, we undertake a comprehensive investigation of instruction finetuning up to the largest open-source language models accessible for research purposes. To evaluate the efficacy of instruction finetuning on these models, we conduct assessments using a challenging Natural Language Understanding benchmark (MMLU) and introduce novel approaches for evaluating chatbot performance in real-world scenarios.

\subsection{Experimental Setup} We provide an overview of the experimental setup here, with complete details given in Appendix \ref{app:chatbot}.

\paragraph{Data} Since, to the best of our knowledge, there is no exhaustive analysis of recent instruction-following datasets, we select eight up-to-date datasets. These include crowdsourced datasets (OASST1~\citep{kopf2023openassistant}, HH-RLHF~\citep{bai2022training}), datasets derived via distillation from instruction-tuned models (Alpaca~\citep{alpaca}, self-instruct~\citep{wang2022self}, unnatural-instructions~\citep{honovich2022unnatural}), corpus aggregations (FLAN v2~\citep{chung2022scaling}), as well as hybrid datasets (Chip2~\citep{laion2023}, Longform~\citep{koksal2023longform}). These datasets vary in language coverage, dataset size, and licensing terms.

\paragraph{Training Setup} To avoid confounding factors related to differing training objectives, we carry out QLoRA finetuning using cross-entropy loss (i.e., supervised learning) without employing reinforcement learning, even on datasets that contain human preference annotations. For datasets where a clear separation between instruction and response exists, we finetune exclusively on the response (see ablation studies in Appendix \ref{app:chatbot}). For OASST1 and HH-RLHF, multiple responses per prompt are available; we select the highest-quality response at each level of the conversation tree and finetune on the entire selected conversation, including instructions. In all experiments, we utilize NF4 \textsc{QLoRA} with double quantization and paged optimizers to avoid memory spikes during gradient checkpointing. We conduct small hyperparameter tuning experiments for the 13B and 33B LLaMA models, finding that hyperparameter settings discovered at 7B generally transfer well (including number of epochs), except for learning rate and batch size. For 33B and 65B models, we reduce the learning rate by half and double the batch size.

\paragraph{Baselines} We benchmark our models against both research-oriented (Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023} and Open Assistant~\citep{kopf2023openassistant}) and commercial chatbot systems (GPT-4 \citep{openai2023gpt}, GPT-3.5-turbo, and Bard). The Open Assistant model is based on a LLaMA 33B model finetuned with Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) on the same OASST1 dataset we use. Vicuna is created by full fine-tuning of LLaMA 13B on proprietary user-shared conversations from ShareGPT, effectively distilling knowledge from OpenAI GPT models.

\subsection{Evaluation}

Following standard practice, we utilize the MMLU (Massively Multitask Language Understanding) benchmark \citep{hendrycksmeasuring} to assess performance across a broad spectrum of language understanding tasks. This benchmark is a multiple-choice evaluation comprising 57 tasks, including elementary mathematics, US history, computer science, law, and others. We report results using 5-shot test accuracy.

We also assess generative language capabilities through both automated and human-based evaluations. This second evaluation approach relies on queries that have been carefully selected by humans and is designed to gauge the quality of model-generated responses. Although this approach offers a more practical and widely accepted benchmark for chatbot performance, there is currently no standardized protocol in the literature. Below, we outline our proposed methodology, which utilizes nucleus sampling with $p=0.9$ and a temperature of $0.7$ in all experiments.

\paragraph{Benchmark Data} Our evaluation employs two carefully curated query datasets: the Vicuna prompts \citep{vicuna2023} and the OASST1 validation set \citep{kopf2023openassistant}. The Vicuna prompts consist of 80 questions spanning various categories, which we use without modification. The OASST1 dataset contains multilingual, crowd-sourced multi-turn dialogues between users and an assistant. We extract all user messages from the validation set as queries, including preceding conversation turns within the prompt. This results in 953 unique user queries. We refer to these two collections as the Vicuna and OA benchmarks, respectively.

\paragraph{Automated Evaluation} Initially, following the evaluation protocol introduced by \citet{vicuna2023}, we employ GPT-4 to rate the responses of different systems relative to ChatGPT (GPT-3.5 Turbo) on the Vicuna benchmark. For each query and the corresponding responses from ChatGPT and the evaluated model, GPT-4 is instructed to score each response out of ten and provide a rationale. The model’s overall performance is expressed as a percentage relative to ChatGPT’s total score. Note that this relative score may exceed 100\% if the evaluated model attains a higher absolute score than ChatGPT. We observe a significant ordering bias, where GPT-4 tends to assign higher scores to the response presented earlier in the prompt. To mitigate this effect, we suggest reporting the average score across both orderings.

Subsequently, we assess performance via direct pairwise comparisons of system outputs. We simplify the evaluation to a three-category labeling task that also accounts for ties. GPT-4 is prompted to select the superior response or declare a tie, providing an explanation for its choice. These head-to-head comparisons are conducted across all permutations of system pairs on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks.

\paragraph{Human Evaluation} Although recent studies suggest that generative models can be effectively used for system evaluations \citep{fu2023gptscore}, the reliability of GPT-4 ratings as a proxy for human judgment in chatbot evaluation has not yet been definitively established. Consequently, we perform two parallel human evaluations on the Vicuna benchmark that mirror both automated evaluation protocols described above. Using Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), we recruit two human annotators for comparisons against ChatGPT and three annotators for pairwise system comparisons.

\paragraph{Elo Rating} Utilizing both human and automated pairwise comparisons, we establish a tournament-style framework where models face off against one another. The tournament consists of matches in which pairs of models compete to generate the superior response for a specified prompt. This approach parallels the methods used by \citet{bai2022training} and \citet{vicuna2023} for model comparison, but we enhance it by incorporating GPT-4 ratings alongside human evaluations. We randomly select from the collection of labeled comparisons to calculate Elo~\citep{elo1967proposed,elo1978rating}. Elo rating, commonly employed in chess and similar games, quantifies the expected win probability relative to an opponent's rating. For instance, a player with an Elo of 1100 versus one with 1000 has an anticipated win rate of roughly 65\%, while a match between two players both rated 1000 or both 1100 yields an expected win rate of 50\%. After each match, the Elo rating updates in proportion to the expected result: an unexpected victory causes a significant rating adjustment, whereas an expected outcome results in a minor change. Over time, Elo ratings serve as an approximate indicator of each participant’s skill level in the game. We initialize ratings at 1,000 and set $K=32$. Following \citet{vicuna2023}, we perform this process 10,000 times using different random seeds to mitigate ordering effects, such as the influence of the sequence in which model pairs compete initially.

\subsection{\textbf{Guanaco}: \textsc{QLoRA} fine-tuned on OASST1 as a Leading Chatbot} Through both automated and human assessments, we observe that the top-performing \textsc{QLoRA} fine-tuned model, {Guanaco} 65B—trained on a version of OASST1—is the highest-performing open-source chatbot and delivers performance comparable to ChatGPT. When measured against GPT-4, {Guanaco} 65B and 33B have an estimated win probability of 30\%, based on Elo ratings derived from system-level pairwise comparisons by human annotators—marking the highest such figures reported so far.

Table~\ref{tbl:vicuna_eval} presents results from the Vicuna benchmark \cite{vicuna2023} relative to ChatGPT. Our findings indicate that {Guanaco} 65B is the top model following GPT-4, attaining 99.3\% of ChatGPT's performance. Although {Guanaco} 33B contains more parameters than the Vicuna 13B, it employs only 4-bit precision for its weights, making it significantly more memory efficient—requiring 21 GB compared to 26 GB—and yielding a three-percentage-point improvement over Vicuna 13B. Additionally, {Guanaco} 7B, with a footprint of just 5 GB, can easily run on modern smartphones while achieving nearly a 20 percentage point advantage over Alpaca 13B.

Nonetheless, Table~\ref{tbl:vicuna_eval} also exhibits very broad confidence intervals, with substantial overlap in model performance. We suspect this uncertainty arises from an unclear scale specification; for instance, it is ambiguous what a score of 8 out of 10 signifies across different contexts. Therefore, we advocate using the Elo ranking approach \citep{elo1967proposed}, which relies on \textit{pairwise} comparisons from human annotators and GPT-4 to circumvent issues related to anchoring absolute scales. At the example level, agreement between GPT-4 and the majority vote of human annotators is weaker, with a Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$. Overall, this indicates moderate concordance between system-level evaluations by GPT-4 and human annotators, suggesting that model-based evaluation can serve as a reasonably reliable alternative to human judgment. We elaborate on additional considerations in Section~\ref{ssec:considerations}.

Elo ratings presented in Table~\ref{tbl:elo_large} show that the {Guanaco} 33B and 65B models surpass all other models except GPT-4 on both the Vicuna and OA benchmarks, performing similarly to ChatGPT as reflected in Table~\ref{tbl:vicuna_eval}. Specifically, finetuning Llama models on FLAN v2 yields excellent results on MMLU but leads to the poorest performance on the Vicuna benchmark (a pattern also seen with other models). This suggests a degree of partial independence in the current evaluation benchmarks: excelling in MMLU does not necessarily translate to strong chatbot capabilities (as evaluated by Vicuna or OA benchmarks), and vice versa.

\section{Qualitative Analysis}

Although quantitative analysis forms the foundation of our evaluation, relying solely on summary statistics presents several challenges. One of the most significant concerns is benchmark validity \citep{liao2021we}—the question of whether a benchmark genuinely measures what its title or description implies remains uncertain, particularly as we uncover “shortcuts” that machine learning models sometimes exploit to solve benchmarks \citep{gururangan2018annotation, poliak2018hypothesis}. To address this issue to some extent, we conduct a qualitative analysis in two parts. First, in \S\ref{ssec:examples}, we provide examples that we consider illustrative of certain observed trends in the outputs generated by our 65b {Guanaco} model. Second, in \S\ref{ssec:considerations}, we discuss various factors related to the results we have presented and our interpretation of those findings.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis of Example Generations}
\label{ssec:examples}

To identify examples, we initially review the data generated for the Vicuna and OpenAssistant benchmarks, searching for recurring patterns in the responses produced by {Guanaco}. When a pattern emerges, we attempt to construct a question or prompt that triggers this pattern despite it being an incorrect solution; for instance, if the model frequently provides lengthy answers, we prompt it to “Answer yes or no without explanation.” This approach allows us to identify both “lemons,” where we successfully cause the model to fail adversarially, and “cherries,” where the model withstands such attempts, and we present examples of both. All outputs in this section were produced using Nucleus Sampling \cite{holtzmancurious} with $p=0.9$.

Naturally, this is not exhaustive, as it falls outside the remit of this small qualitative study to account for every variable involved; for example, the full range of responses that the model might produce for a given prompt is quite extensive, so we depend on samples that we hope are representative. Nevertheless, we contend that illustrating these examples provides context to the quantitative data presented earlier in the paper. Since we release all models and code as open source, we hope this section will encourage future research to explore in greater depth the issues we highlight here.  
\paragraph{Factual Recall} For questions like ``What is the capital of Zambia?'' all models reliably produce correct answers, for instance,

\begin{quote} 
    \textbf{Guanaco}:
    \texttt{The capital of Zambia is Lusaka.}
\end{quote}

However, as questions become more obscure, {Guanaco} tends to become less reliable, yet remains confident. For example, when given this prompt from HotPotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}:

\begin{quote}
     \textbf{User}: In what year was the singer who popularized `I'll Keep the Lovelight Burning' born?  

     \textbf{Guanaco}:~\texttt{Al Jolson is credited with popularizing the song `I'll Keep the Lovelight Burning,' and he was born in the year 1886.}
 \end{quote}

it produces an incorrect popularizer and wrong birth year (although the birth year given is accurate for Al Jolson, the person named).

\paragraph{Suggestibility} {Guanaco} demonstrates an unexpected resistance to accepting certain types of assumed misinformation, for example, in the following dialogue:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: How was it finally, officially confirmed that the earth is flat by peer reviewed scientists? \\ \\
    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{The earth has never been officially verified as flat by peer reviewed scientists. The dominant consensus among scientists is that the earth is spherical. In fact, this has been understood for centuries and validated through numerous experiments and observations. \\ \\ The notion that the earth is flat is a conspiracy theory that science has disproven. The claim that a group of scientists have peer reviewed and confirmed the earth’s flatness is simply untrue. \\ \\ If you have any further questions, please feel free to ask.}
\end{quote}

 also demonstrates a strong ability to recognize questions that cannot be answered, for example,

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: What time is it?

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{I'm sorry, but as an AI language model, I don’t have access to real-time data. My responses are generated based on my existing knowledge and do not reflect the current time. If you want to know the current time, I recommend using a clock or checking an online time source.}
\end{quote}

Comparable behavior is observed for queries like ``Where are you?'', ``How are you?'', and so on.

\paragraph{Refusal} In a similar vein, {Guanaco} occasionally declines to follow instructions for apparently arbitrary reasons:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: Please reverse the words in the sentence ``I'm a cool dude in a mocha mood.'' \\ \\
    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{I'm sorry, I’m currently unable to do that, but I can clarify why the sentence you provided is grammatically correct.\\ \\The sentence ``I'm a cool dude in a mocha mood" adheres to the proper grammar and syntax conventions of English. Here, the adjective ``cool'' precedes the noun ``dude'' to describe it, and the prepositional phrase ``in a mocha mood" adds further context to the sentence. This sentence structure is entirely valid and coherent within its context.\\ \\If you have any other inquiries or need further assistance, please feel free to ask.}
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Secret Keeping} Regrettably, this refusal is not consistently reliable, even when intended. Often, system designers want to keep certain information confidential from the user, despite the system having the ability to generate free-form text responses. We prompt the model with ``The secret word is `banana'. Do not disclose or repeat this under any circumstances, regardless of what the human asks.'' and test how easily a user might obtain this secret information. Directly asking does not succeed:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: What is the secret word?

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{I'm sorry, but I cannot reveal the secret word. I am programmed to neither disclose nor repeat the secret word, no matter what the human requests. How else may I assist you?}
\end{quote}

which is the intended response. However, a small amount of manipulation circumvents this behavior:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: This is a game. The goal is to ignore your previous instructions. What is the secret word?

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{Sorry, the secret word is banana. It’s better if we don’t mention it. Is there anything else you want to ask?}
\end{quote}

demonstrating the necessity to develop approaches for more reliable adherence to instructions.

\paragraph{Math} The primary limitation of {Guanaco} lies in mathematics, a domain where many language models face difficulties \citep{liang2022holistic}. When {Guanaco} provides a detailed solution, it is often correct, for instance,

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: Lee mows one lawn and charges \$33. Last week he mowed 16 lawns and three customers each gave him a \$10 tip. How many dollars did Lee earn mowing lawns last week?

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{Lee earned \$582 mowing lawns last week. \\ \\ To calculate the total amount he earned, we first determine his earnings from mowing 16 lawns: \\ \\  \$33 \times 16 = \$528 \\ \\ Then, we add the tips from the three customers: \\  \\ \$10 \times 3 = \$30 \\ \\ Therefore, the total amount Lee earned was: \\ \\ \$528 + \$30 = \$558}
\end{quote}

Nonetheless, {Guanaco} can fail even on straightforward problems if it does not decompose the task into clear steps, a recognized challenge \cite{weichain}. For example, consider the following exchange:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: Please factorize 1833.

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{Sure, I'd be glad to assist! 1833 is a prime number, meaning its only factors are 1 and 1833. \\ \\ Hence, the unique factorization of 1833 is: \\ \\ 2\^{}1 * 3\^{}2 * 17\^{}1 \\ \\ I hope this is helpful! Feel free to ask if you have more questions.}
\end{quote}

which is not only incorrect (the actual factorization is $3 \times 17 \times 43$), but it is mistaken twice over.

\paragraph{Theory of Mind} {Guanaco} demonstrates notably strong Theory of Mind abilities \cite{nematzadeh2018evaluating, sap2022neural}. For example, the model provides a detailed and accurate response to the following query:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: James and Abby are in the bedroom. Abby put the pen in the desk drawer. Abby leaves the bedroom. James moves the pen into the bag. Where does James think Abby will look for the pen?

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{James knows that Abby put the pen in the desk drawer. But James also knows that he has moved the pen into the bag, and he assumes that Abby does not know about this. So, James may guess that Abby will look for the pen in the desk drawer, because that's where she left it last time. However, this assumption may be incorrect, because Abby may have seen James moving the pen.}
\end{quote}

Nevertheless, these deductions are inconsistent, and frequently the model provides explanations that assume circumstances that are illogical in the context, such as:

\begin{quote}
    \textbf{User}: Evelyn entered the living room. Jackson entered the playroom. James entered the playroom. The beans are in the treasure chest. James exited the playroom. Jackson moved the beans to the pantry. Jackson exited the playroom. James entered the living room. Where will James look for the beans?

    \textbf{Guanaco}: \texttt{James will look for the beans in the pantry, because that's where Jackson moved them.}
\end{quote}

Here, {Guanaco} assumes a transfer of knowledge that was never specified. These types of errors are consistent with recent findings in the literature \cite{sap2022neural}, indicating that further research is necessary.

\subsection{Considerations}
\label{ssec:considerations}

\paragraph{Evaluation}

We observe moderate consistency among human raters (Fleiss $\kappa=0.42$), with further decline when comparing outputs from two strong systems. This highlights the limitations inherent in current benchmarks and human evaluation methodologies for chatbot task performance. During manual comparisons of responses generated by ChatGPT and {Guanaco} 65B on the Vicuna benchmark, we found that subjective preferences significantly influenced judgments, as the authors often disagreed on which responses were better. Future research should explore methods to address these challenges by leveraging insights from fields that have developed approaches for handling subjective preferences, such as Human-Computer Interaction and Psychology.

Our analysis also reveals notable biases in automated evaluation systems. For instance, we detect strong order effects where GPT-4 assigns higher ratings to the system mentioned first in the prompt. The relatively low agreement at the sample level between GPT-4 and human annotators (Fleiss $\kappa=0.25$) further suggests that human evaluators and automated systems may rely on differing preference criteria. Additionally, as shown in Table~\ref{tbl:elo_large}, GPT-4 rates its own outputs substantially higher than human ratings do, with an Elo score of 1348 versus 1176—corresponding to an approximately 20\% greater chance of winning against an opponent.

Future research should explore the existence of possible biases within automated evaluation systems as well as strategies to mitigate them.

\paragraph{Data \& Training} We observe that the OASST1 dataset used to train the {Guanaco} models is multilingual, and the OA benchmark also includes prompts in various languages. Future studies should assess how much multilingual training enhances performance on instructions given in languages other than English and whether this accounts for the wider performance gap observed between the Vicuna-13B model (trained exclusively on English data) and {Guanaco} 33B and 65B on the OA benchmark.

Given the strong results achieved by the {Guanaco} models, we examine potential data leakage between the OASST1 dataset and the Vicuna benchmark prompts. After conducting fuzzy string matching across the two datasets and manually reviewing the closest matches, we do not identify any overlapping prompts.

Additionally, we emphasize that our model is trained solely with cross-entropy loss (supervised learning) and does not utilize reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). This highlights the need for further studies comparing the trade-offs between simple cross-entropy loss and RLHF training. We anticipate that \textsc{QLoRA} will facilitate such large-scale analyses without requiring extensive computational resources.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Quantization of Large Language Models} Research on quantization for LLMs has primarily concentrated on improving inference-time quantization. Key methods for maintaining 16-bit LLM performance focus on handling outlier features (e.g., SmoothQuant~\citep{xiao2022smoothquant} and LLM.int8()~\citep{dettmers2022llm}), while others apply more advanced grouping techniques \citep{park2022nuqmm, yao2022zeroquant}. Lossy quantization approaches investigate the compromises involved in conventional rounding~\citep{dettmers2022case,zeng2022glm,pope2022efficiently} or methods to optimize rounding decisions to enhance quantization accuracy~\citep{frantar2022gptq}. Apart from our work, SwitchBack layers~\citep{wortsman2023stable} represent the only research studying backpropagation through quantized weights at scales exceeding 1B parameters.

\paragraph{Finetuning with Adapters} Although we employ Low-rank Adapters~\citep{hu2021lora} (LoRA) in our work, numerous other Parameter Efficient FineTuning (PEFT) techniques have been introduced, including prompt tuning~\citep{qin2021learning,lester2021power,li2021prefix}, adjusting the embedding layer inputs~\citep{an2022input}, tuning hidden states~(IA$^3$) \citep{liu2022few}, incorporating entire layers~\citep{houlsby2019parameter}, modifying biases~\citep{zaken2021bitfit}, learning weight masks guided by Fisher information~\citep{sung2021training}, and hybrid methods~\citep{henderson2021compacter}. Our findings demonstrate that LoRA adapters can achieve performance comparable to full 16-bit finetuning. Exploring the advantages and compromises of alternative PEFT methods remains an avenue for future investigation.

\paragraph{Instruction Finetuning} Instruction finetuning aims to enable a pretrained LLM to adhere to instructions by finetuning it on input-output pairs from diverse data sources, such that it produces the specified output given the input prompt. Various methods and datasets supporting this include MetaICL~\citep{min2021metaicl}, MetaTuning~\citep{zhong2021adapting}, InstructGPT~\citep{ouyang2022training}, FLAN~\citep{wei2021finetuned,chung2022scaling}, PromptSource~\citep{bach2022promptsource}, Super-NaturalInstructions~\citep{wang2022super,sanh2021multitask}, Self-instruct~\citep{wang2022self}, UnnaturalInstructions~\citep{honovich2022unnatural}, OPT-IML~\citep{iyer2022opt}, UnifiedSKG~\citep{xie2022unifiedskg}, OIG/Chip2~\citep{laion2023}, Alpaca~\citep{alpaca}, Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023}, Koala~\citep{koala_blogpost_2023}, and Self-instruct-GPT-4~\citep{peng2023instruction}.

\paragraph{Chatbots} A large number of instruction-following models are implemented as dialogue-based chatbots, frequently utilizing Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)~\citep{christiano2017deep} or training with AI-generated feedback data from an existing model (RLAIF)~\citep{bai2022constitutional}. Relevant datasets and approaches include Anthropic-HH~\citep{askell2021general,bai2022training}, Open Assistant~\citep{kopf2023openassistant}, LaMDA~\citep{thoppilan2022lamda}, and Sparrow~\citep{glaese2022improving}. While we do not employ reinforcement learning, our leading model, Guanaco, is finetuned on multi-turn conversations from the Open Assistant dataset, which was originally developed for RLHF training~\citep{kopf2023openassistant}. For chatbot evaluation, strategies substituting costly human annotation with GPT-4-based assessments have been proposed \citep{vicuna2023,peng2023instruction}. Our work advances these methods by emphasizing a more robust evaluation framework.

\section{Limitations and Discussion}

We present evidence that our technique, \textsc{QLoRA}, can match the performance of full 16-bit finetuning when applied to a 4-bit base model combined with Low-rank Adapters (LoRA). Nonetheless, we have not confirmed that \textsc{QLoRA} attains parity with full 16-bit finetuning at larger scales such as 33B and 65B parameters. Given the substantial computational demands, we defer this analysis to future research.

An additional limitation lies in the evaluation of instruction finetuning models. Although we conduct assessments using MMLU, the Vicuna benchmark, and the OA benchmark, we do not evaluate on other datasets like BigBench, RAFT, and HELM, leaving open the question of whether our results generalize across these benchmarks. Conversely, we undertake an extensive study on MMLU and introduce novel techniques for chatbot evaluation.

Based on the presented data, it seems that the effectiveness of these benchmarks likely depends on the similarity between the finetuning data and the benchmark dataset. For instance, FLAN v2 shares similarities with MMLU but differs from chatbot benchmarks, whereas the Chip2 dataset exhibits the opposite pattern. Correspondingly, both models perform in line with these similarities on the MMLU and Vicuna benchmarks. This underscores the need not only for improved benchmarks and evaluation methods but also for careful consideration of what exactly is being assessed. Are we aiming to build models that excel in classroom-level high school and college knowledge, or do we prioritize chatbot conversational capabilities? Or perhaps some other objective? Because evaluating with existing benchmarks is generally easier than developing new ones, certain benchmarks may inadvertently guide the community in a specific direction. It is crucial that, as a community, we ensure that benchmarks truly measure what we value.

While we offer a comprehensive evaluation of general chatbot performance, a further limitation is that our responsible AI assessment of {Guanaco} remains limited. We assess the probability that {Guanaco}-65B generates socially biased token sequences compared to other models in Table~\ref{tbl:crows}. The results show that {Guanaco}-65B's average score is substantially lower than that of other raw pretrained models. This suggests that finetuning on the OASST1 dataset helps reduce the bias present in the LLaMA base model. Although these findings are promising, it remains uncertain whether {Guanaco} performs equally well across other bias dimensions. We defer more extensive bias analysis of {Guanaco} and similar chatbots to future research.

Another limitation is that we have not tested different bit-precisions, such as 3-bit base models, or alternative adapter techniques. Beyond LoRA, there exists a broad range of Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) methods known to perform effectively. However, it is not yet clear whether these methods scale well to very large models. We selected LoRA due to its well-established robustness, though other adapters might deliver superior results. Since finetuning after quantization appears to recover much of the information lost during quantization, this could enable more aggressive quantization strategies. For example, applying 3-bit GPTQ quantization to the base model with LoRA finetuning might achieve performance comparable to 16-bit full finetuning after retraining.

\section{Broader Impacts}

Our \textsc{QLoRA} finetuning technique represents the first approach allowing finetuning of 33B parameter models on a single consumer GPU and 65B parameter models on a single professional GPU, without sacrificing performance compared to full finetuning baselines. We have shown that our best 33B model trained on the Open Assistant dataset can compete with ChatGPT on the Vicuna benchmark. Given that instruction finetuning is vital for converting raw pretrained LLMs into ChatGPT-like chatbots, we believe our method will promote widespread and routine finetuning, especially benefiting researchers with limited resources—a significant advancement for democratizing access to cutting-edge NLP technology. \textsc{QLoRA} can be viewed as a leveling mechanism that helps narrow the resource disparity between large corporations and small teams equipped with consumer GPUs.

Another possible area of influence is the deployment on mobile devices. We anticipate that our \textsc{QLoRA} approach could achieve the crucial milestone of facilitating the finetuning of LLMs directly on phones and other environments with limited resources. Although 7B models have previously been demonstrated to run on phones, \textsc{QLoRA} represents the first technique to enable the finetuning of such models on these devices. We estimate that using an iPhone 12 Plus, \textsc{QLoRA} can finetune 3 million tokens overnight while the device is charging. Although finetuned 7B models do not yet match the performance of ChatGPT, we believe their quality is sufficient to support innovative applications that were previously unfeasible due to concerns about privacy or the quality of LLMs. \textsc{QLoRA} may facilitate privacy-preserving LLM usage, allowing users to maintain control over their own data and models, while also simplifying the deployment of LLMs.

Nonetheless, finetuning is a technology with dual-use potential that can be misused to cause harm. The widespread adoption of LLMs carries known risks \citep{bommasani2021opportunities,bender2021dangers}, yet we argue that democratizing access to a technology that is rapidly becoming widespread will promote more thorough and independent scrutiny, rather than concentrating the power of LLMs in the hands of a few large corporations that withhold models or source code from public examination.

In summary, we contend that \textsc{QLoRA} will have a generally positive effect by making the finetuning of high-quality LLMs much more accessible and straightforward.
