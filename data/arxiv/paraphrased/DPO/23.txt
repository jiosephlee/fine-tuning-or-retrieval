\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning capabilities, fine-grained regulation of their outputs remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training processes.
Current approaches to enhance such steerability typically involve collecting human judgments about the comparative quality of model outputs and further training the unsupervised LM to reflect these human preferences, commonly using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
Yet, RLHF is often intricate and prone to instability, as it requires first training a reward model to capture human preferences and then applying reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM to maximize this modeled reward without deviating excessively from the original parameters.
In this work, we propose a novel reward model parameterization within RLHF that allows direct derivation of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, enabling us to address the traditional RLHF objective using only a straightforward classification loss.
We refer to this approach as \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO). DPO is stable, efficient, and requires minimal computational resources, removing the necessity for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning and reducing hyperparameter tuning demands.
Our empirical results demonstrate that DPO effectively fine-tunes LMs to better match human preferences, performing on par with or surpassing existing techniques. Notably, DPO outperforms RLHF methods based on PPO for sentiment control and achieves equal or superior response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being much easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora develop impressive and unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, the data used to train these models is produced by humans whose intentions, preferences, and expertise vary greatly. Not all of these goals or skills are desirable for the model to replicate; for instance, while it is helpful for an AI programming assistant to \textit{recognize} common coding errors to provide corrections, we would still prefer the model to generate code that reflects the (sometimes infrequent) high-level expertise present in its training examples. In a similar vein, we may want our language model to \textit{know about} prevalent misconceptions held by a significant proportion of people, yet we do not want the model to repeat such misconceptions as truth in half the cases when questioned about them. Essentially, distinguishing and promoting the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broader repertoire of \textit{knowledge and skills} is vital for developing AI systems that are reliable, effective, and manageable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches usually align LMs with human values through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based goal targeted by these methods can be solved precisely using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches teach language models the desired behaviors by leveraging curated datasets of human preferences, which capture the kinds of actions humans consider safe and useful. This stage of preference learning takes place following an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training phase on extensive text corpora. Although the most direct strategy for preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human demonstrations, the most effective approaches are based on reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques involve training a reward model using human preference data, and then applying RL to adjust the language model policy, encouraging outputs that receive higher reward scores, while avoiding large divergences from the base model. Despite RLHF enabling language models to excel in conversation and programming tasks, its pipeline is much more involved than simple supervised learning—it requires training several LMs and repeatedly sampling from the model’s policy during training, which leads to high computational overhead.

In this work, we present a method for optimizing a language model to match human preferences directly, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same goal as standard RLHF methods—maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint—but offers a simpler and more accessible implementation and training process. Conceptually, DPO modifies the relative log probabilities of preferred versus non-preferred responses, applying a dynamic, sample-specific importance weight that mitigates the model collapse associated with a basic probability ratio objective. Like traditional approaches, DPO is based on a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) for evaluating how well a reward function matches observed preference data. However, unlike standard techniques that utilize the preference model to generate a loss for training a reward function, followed by policy optimization on the resulting reward, DPO employs a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly in terms of the policy. Using a collection of human preference judgments over model outputs, DPO thus optimizes the policy through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, resulting in the optimal policy for a latent reward function inferred from preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that eliminates the need for reinforcement learning when fine-tuning language models using preference data. Through experimentation, we demonstrate that DPO matches or outperforms established techniques—including PPO-based RLHF—for training models on tasks like sentiment adjustment, text summarization, and conversational AI, across language models with sizes reaching up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models at larger scales are capable of performing certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or by using few-shot prompting strategies \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user objectives can be notably enhanced through fine-tuning on datasets comprising instructions and human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows LLMs to better generalize to unseen instructions beyond those present in the instruction-tuning dataset, leading to improved overall usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven successful, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of response quality tends to be more practical than gathering expert demonstrations. As a result, later research has refined LLMs using datasets of human preferences, yielding advances in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, narrative generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically involve first training a neural network reward model to align with the collected preference data, often utilizing a preference model like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. Afterwards, the language model is further optimized to maximize this learned reward function by applying reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or similar variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Related research also employs instruction-following LLMs, enhanced with human feedback, to produce additional synthetic preference datasets targeting specific features like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, with only minimal human guidance provided in the form of textual rubrics for model annotation. These strategies represent a synthesis of two major lines of inquiry: one focused on using reinforcement learning to train language models on various objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another centered around learning from human preferences in general \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
While leveraging relative human preferences holds considerable promise, fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning continues to pose significant practical difficulties. This work introduces a theoretically-grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the realm of language, the study of policy learning from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to a variety of proposed techniques. In contextual bandit scenarios, where preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of rewards, the problem is referred to as contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since there are no absolute rewards in this setting, theoretical work on CDBs employs the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy that achieves at least a 50\% expected win rate when compared to \textit{any} other policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDB frameworks assume that preference labels are provided in an online fashion, whereas learning from human preferences generally operates on a pre-collected, static dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Along similar lines, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) relies on binary preferences that originate from an \textit{unknown} `scoring' function, replacing rewards altogether \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are numerous PbRL algorithms, including those capable of utilizing off-policy preference data, but most involve first estimating the underlying scoring (reward) function before proceeding to optimize it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a direct, single-stage policy optimization method that learns to satisfy preferences without requiring an explicit reward model.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning}, as well as subsequent work \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. The pipeline generally consists of three main steps: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model; and 3) optimization through reinforcement learning.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by adapting a pre-trained LM using supervised learning with curated data specific to the target task(s) (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Phase}: During this phase, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. Human evaluators then review these pairs and indicate which answer they prefer, denoted as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the chosen response and $y_l$ the less favored one from $(y_1, y_2)$. The observed preferences are presumed to be governed by an unknown underlying reward model $r^*(y, x)$, which is not directly observable. Various methods exist to model such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a frequently employed approach (though broader Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are applicable if multiple ordered responses are available). According to the BT model, the probability of a human selecting $y_1$ over $y_2$ for a given prompt $x$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of preference comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, we can define a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. This task can be viewed as a binary classification problem, resulting in the negative log-likelihood loss:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ refers to the logistic sigmoid. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized with the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, with an added linear output layer on top of the transformer to yield a single scalar reward score \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To control the variance of the learned reward function, previous studies commonly normalize the rewards so that  $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the reward function that has been trained is leveraged to give feedback signals to the language model. Consistent with previous research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization target is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
where the coefficient $\beta$ regulates how much the policy is allowed to diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which is typically the initial SFT model $\pisft$. In implementation, the policy $\pi_\theta$ of the language model is initialized from $\pisft$. Including this regularization term is crucial for ensuring that the model remains close to the data distribution where the reward model is most reliable, as well as for preserving response diversity and avoiding mode collapse to a handful of high-reward outputs. Because language output is inherently discrete, the above objective is non-differentiable and thus is commonly optimized through reinforcement learning methods. The prevalent technique \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing this expression using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of utilizing reinforcement learning algorithms on large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to formulate a straightforward method for policy optimization that directly utilizes preference data. In contrast to traditional RLHF techniques, which first learn a reward model and then perform reinforcement learning to optimize it, our strategy exploits a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to derive its optimal policy analytically, eliminating the need for an RL training loop.

As we will elaborate shortly, our central idea is to exploit an explicit analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies. This permits us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into a loss over policies directly. Employing this change-of-variables technique, we sidestep the need to explicitly fit a separate reward model, yet still benefit from established models of human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. In this framework, the policy network inherently encapsulates both the language model and the (implicit) reward function.

\textbf{Formulating the DPO Objective.} We begin from the same RL framework as earlier studies, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, assuming a general reward function $r$. Building on prior research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily shown that the solution maximizing reward under a KL constraint, as outlined in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}%
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ represents the partition function. Detailed derivations are provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when substituting in the MLE-based reward estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally challenging~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which complicates practical use of this parameterization. Nonetheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown normalization constant $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and manipulating the result, we have:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
Applying this reparameterization to the actual reward $r^*$ and the associated optimal policy $\pi^*$, we note that the Bradley-Terry model depends only on reward differences for two responses, specifically, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Replacing $r^*(x,y)$ in the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} with the reparameterization from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, we see the partition function terms cancel, which allows the preference probability to be written solely using the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$, as determined under the Bradley-Terry framework, satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The full derivation is available in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry model, analogous forms for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can be derived as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preferences now expressed using the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we are able to define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the approach used in reward modeling (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the policy objective is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this formulation, an implicit reward is learned via an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy is directly given by $\pi_\theta$. Furthermore, as this process corresponds to learning a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits useful theoretical guarantees—such as consistency—provided certain assumptions on the preference data distribution hold \cite{bong2022generalized}. Additional theoretical aspects of DPO, and its connections to related methods, are examined in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update work?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, examining the gradient of its loss function, $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, is helpful. The gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{larger when reward estimate is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{push up probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{push down probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ serves as the implicit reward function defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). In essence, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ boosts the probability of the preferred answer $y_w$ and reduces the probability of the less favored answer $y_l$. Crucially, each data point is weighted by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overvalues $y_l$ compared to $y_w$, with scaling by $\beta$—reflecting how incorrectly the model ranks the completions and incorporating the effect of the KL regularization. Our results highlight that this weighting is critical: a simpler version of the approach, omitting this weighting term, can lead to undesirable language model behavior (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The typical DPO process involves: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, assign human preference labels to obtain the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and the target $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, it is preferable to utilize existing public preference datasets instead of collecting new samples and human annotations. Since such datasets are typically generated with $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever possible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we set $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)]}$. This approach helps address the distribution gap between the inaccessible true reference distribution and the $\piref$ employed by DPO. Additional implementation and hyperparameter information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper insight into the DPO approach, present theoretical justification, and connect the strengths of DPO to the shortcomings observed in actor-critic methods utilized in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need to both estimate an explicit reward function and use RL to train the policy by employing a unified maximum likelihood objective. Importantly, the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry framework with a reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we optimize the parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$, which can be viewed as equivalent to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after a change of variables. In this section, we will develop the underlying theory for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the family of possible reward models, and show that it supports exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation on reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be established:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, and specifically under the Bradley-Terry preference framework, any two reward functions belonging to the same class yield identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate identical optimal policies in the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are simple and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights the well-known issue of under-specification in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is typically necessary to add extra identifiability constraints in order to obtain guarantees for the MLE solutions of Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function in an equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy, which implies that, for our purposes, recovering any representative reward function from the optimal class suffices. We prove the following Theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, every reward equivalence class compatible with Plackett-Luce (and, specifically, Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be any reward function, which induces an optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be written in the form given above. We introduce the projection operator $f$:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ serves to normalize the reward function by subtracting the log-partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since this normalization only depends on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ lies within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds universally for reward functions), we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, the projection $f$ yields a reward function from the same equivalence class with the desired parametric structure, confirming that the reparameterization does not restrict the generality of the reward model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as characterizing precisely which member of each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization—namely, the reward function for which:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, via Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a proper probability distribution (i.e., all probabilities are non-negative and sum to one).
According to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we see that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} defines the partition function for the optimal policy specified by $r(x, y)$.
A central insight of the DPO method is that by introducing suitable constraints into the otherwise under-constrained Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference models, we can retain the full set of representable reward models, yet render the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically manageable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms}

Our framework can also be leveraged to analyze sources of instability in standard actor-critic methods employed for RLHF, including PPO. We adhere to the RLHF process and concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach allows us to relate our problem to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, applied to the constrained RL formulation introduced in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, we seek to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \| \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal solution derived in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} as dictated by the reward model $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some manipulation, this yields the following optimization criterion:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to the objective used by previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which employ the DPO-compatible reward for the class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization factor within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be seen as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term leaves the optimal policy unchanged, its absence can increase the variance of the policy gradient, leading to unstable learning dynamics. While one could compensate for this normalization using a learned value function, this, too, poses optimization challenges. Another approach adopted by earlier research is to normalize rewards based on a human completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization factor. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that is baseline-free by design.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we experimentally assess DPO's capability to learn policies from preference data. First, within a controlled text-generation framework, we investigate how effectively DPO balances reward maximization against KL-divergence regularization relative to popular preference optimization approaches such as PPO. Afterwards, we test DPO on more challenging RLHF problems and larger model sizes, focusing on tasks like dialogue and summarization. Our findings show that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO typically matches or exceeds the performance of established methods such as RLHF with PPO and the strategy of selecting the top trajectory out of $N$ samples using a learned reward model. Before discussing these outcomes, we outline our experimental methodology; further specifics are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We investigate three distinct open-ended text generation tasks in our study. Across all experiments, each algorithm learns a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ corresponds to a movie review prefix sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, with the objective being that the policy generates $y$ with a positive sentiment. To enable a systematic evaluation, for this task we \textit{create} preference pairs over outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive} \mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive} \mid x, y_l)$. For SFT, we conduct further fine-tuning of GPT-2-large until reaching convergence, utilizing reviews from the IMDB dataset’s training split (more information in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post; here, the policy aims to generate a summary $y$ capturing the main ideas. In line with previous approaches, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} in conjunction with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is fine-tuned using human-composed Reddit post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} with the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} library for RLHF. The human preference labels are sourced from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}, based on samples from a related but differently fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, 
$x$ is a user prompt, which could range from an inquiry about astrophysics to personal advice requests. The policy’s goal is to provide a useful and engaging answer $y$ to the user's question, and we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170,000 dialogues between humans and AI assistants. Each conversation concludes with two candidate responses generated by a large, unspecified language model, accompanied by a label indicating the preferred human choice. Since there is no available pre-trained SFT model for this dataset, we construct the SFT model by fine-tuning a publicly available language model solely on the preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We assess our methods using two distinct evaluation strategies. To study how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we evaluate them in the controlled sentiment generation setting by examining the trade-off curve (frontier) between reward achieved and KL-divergence from the reference policy. This frontier can be computed due to access to the true reward function (the sentiment classifier). In real-world scenarios, however, the ground-truth reward function is unavailable. Thus, we evaluate the algorithms by their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 to approximate human judgment of summary quality and helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, we compare against the reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred human response from the dataset. Although prior work indicates that LMs can serve as stronger automatic evaluators than traditional metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we conduct a human study to support our reliance on GPT-4 for evaluation in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show that GPT-4's evaluations align closely with human judgments, with agreement levels matching or surpassing those between human annotators.

\textbf{Approaches.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established strategies for aligning language models with human preferences. For the summarization task, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, and for the dialogue task, we apply 2-shot prompting to \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We further examine the performance of the \textbf{SFT} model, along with \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected output $y_w$—sourced either from the SFT model (for sentiment and summarization tasks) or from a standard language model (in single-turn dialogue scenarios). Another pseudo-supervised alternative is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; the 'unlikelihood' component is optionally weighted by a coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$. Our analysis also includes \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, utilizing a reward model trained on preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant leveraging the true reward signal in the controlled sentiment context. For sentiment tasks, we experiment with two PPO-GT versions: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version featuring normalized rewards and further hyperparameter tuning to enhance results (these changes are also applied to the 'normal' PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we incorporate the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ completions from the SFT (or Preferred-FT for dialogue), selecting the top-scoring response based on a reward model trained on preference data. While this approach often yields high-quality outputs and separates reward model evaluation from PPO training, its practicality is limited by the need to generate $N$ completions per input at inference time, making it inefficient for larger $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 estimated win rates for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue; among all methods, only DPO surpasses the performance of selected summaries in the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rate trends for varying sampling temperatures throughout training. The margin by which DPO outperforms dataset labels remains relatively consistent during training, regardless of sampling temperature.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-regularized reward maximization objective, which is standard in most RLHF methods, manages the trade-off between maximizing reward and preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. As a result, when assessing different approaches, it is essential to consider both the reward attained and the corresponding KL divergence; attaining marginally higher rewards at the expense of a much larger KL is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward versus KL trade-offs achieved by several methods in the sentiment domain. For each algorithm, we perform several training runs, varying a policy conservativeness hyperparameter across runs (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. Every 100 training steps up to convergence, we evaluate each trained policy on a test prompt set, measuring the mean reward according to the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the total of the KL-divergences across all timesteps.} compared to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results show that DPO achieves the most efficient trade-off frontier, obtaining the highest reward while maintaining low KL values. This finding is especially significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO are optimizing an identical objective, DPO consistently outperforms PPO in terms of efficiency; the reward/KL frontier achieved by DPO is strictly superior to PPO's. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO even in cases where PPO is provided with true reward feedback (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO be applied to large-scale preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We then assess how well DPO performs when fine-tuned on tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, standard automatic metrics such as ROUGE often do not align closely with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have shown that preference-based fine-tuning of LMs using PPO leads to more favorable summaries. To compare approaches, we generate completions on the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the mean win rate compared to reference completions from the test split. For each method, outputs are sampled across temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with resulting win rates presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT each fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results show that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate close to 61\%, surpassing PPO’s best win rate of around 57\% at the same temperature. DPO also attains a higher peak win rate than the best-of-$N$ baseline. It’s important to mention that DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter was not systematically optimized, so these outcomes may not reflect DPO’s full capability. Additionally, DPO demonstrates much greater stability across different sampling temperatures, while PPO’s performance can decline to the level of the original GPT-J model as temperature increases. Preferred-FT does not yield notable improvements over the SFT baseline. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO using human evaluation is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO completions sampled at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over those from PPO at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test partition from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} consisting of exchanges with a single human-assistant interaction. In GPT-4 assessments, we utilize the preferred test completions as references to calculate the win rates for different techniques. Since there is no established SFT baseline for this task, our pipeline begins with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, then applies Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the preferred completions so that its outputs stay in-distribution, followed by DPO training. We additionally benchmark against the strongest result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (as the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at $N=128$ for this scenario; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompt configuration of the original Pythia-2.8B, finding that DPO matches or outperforms other methods when using their optimal temperature settings. We also analyze an RLHF model using PPO trained on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} and reference implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but could not identify a prompting strategy or sampling temperature that enables it to exceed the performance of the base Pythia-2.8B. Considering our TL;DR findings and the fact that both PPO and Best of 128 optimize the same reward, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level effectiveness. All in all, DPO stands out as the only practical method to outperform the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH set, matching or surpassing the much more computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains its top performance in a relatively small number of steps.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences provides an effective and scalable approach for developing robust, aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to learn from preferences without the need for reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning challenge into a conventional RL context to leverage standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows training models to adhere to human preferences using a simple cross-entropy loss, bypassing both reinforcement learning and any compromise in generality. With minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO achieves performance that matches or surpasses leading RLHF methods, such as those utilizing PPO, significantly lowering the obstacles to training additional language models based on human feedback.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings highlight a number of open questions for subsequent research. For instance, how well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios when contrasted with methods based on explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize comparably to those trained with PPO, but a more thorough investigation is warranted. It also remains to be seen whether self-labeling during DPO training enables similarly efficient use of unlabeled prompts. Another topic of interest is how reward over-optimization appears within the context of direct preference optimization—does the modest drop in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right provide an example of this phenomenon? Moreover, though our analysis covers models containing up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models presents a promising avenue for future work. With respect to evaluation, we note that win rates judged by GPT-4 are influenced by the prompt wording; future studies might explore optimal strategies for eliciting reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO has significant potential beyond preference-based language model training, including possible applications in training generative models across various other domains.
