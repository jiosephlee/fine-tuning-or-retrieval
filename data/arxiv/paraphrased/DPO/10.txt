\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire general world knowledge and certain reasoning abilities, achieving fine-grained control over their outputs is challenging due to the fully unsupervised training paradigm.
Current techniques for improving controllability rely on gathering human judgments about the relative quality of generated outputs and adapting the unsupervised LM to these preferences, typically through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF entails a complicated and frequently unstable process, which involves first constructing a reward model to mirror human preferences and then using reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM in order to maximize this surrogate reward without deviating excessively from the original model.
In this work, we propose a novel way to parameterize the reward model in RLHF, which enables direct extraction of the associated optimal policy in closed form and allows us to address the classic RLHF objective using a straightforward classification loss.
This approach, which we name \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), delivers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency by removing the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter tuning.
Our empirical results indicate that DPO is capable of fine-tuning LMs to reflect human preferences as well as, or better than, prior methods. In particular, DPO surpasses RLHF approaches based on PPO in controlling sentiment in outputs and matches or enhances quality in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being considerably easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora have demonstrated unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Despite this, the data fueling these models originates from humans who possess diverse intentions, values, and expertise. Not all of these characteristics are suitable for replication; for instance, although it is useful for an AI programming assistant to \textit{recognize} common coding errors in order to rectify them, we still prefer the model to \emph{favor} the (sometimes infrequent) examples of expert-level code within the dataset when producing code itself. Analogously, while it is important for a language model to \textit{know} about widespread misconceptions—perhaps held by half the population—we would not want it to state these misconceptions as fact in half of its responses! That is, it is essential to \emph{choose} the model's preferred actions and replies from its broad spectrum of \textit{knowledge and competencies} to ensure the AI behaves safely, effectively, and remains under human control \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches commonly guide LMs toward aligning with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we will demonstrate that the RL-style objective used by these methods can, in fact, be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy objective, thus streamlining the entire preference optimization process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches shape the behaviors of language models by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences, which indicate the kinds of behaviors people deem safe and useful. This preference alignment phase is carried out following a preliminary period of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on an extensive corpus of text. Although the simplest method for preference alignment is to perform supervised fine-tuning using exemplary human-generated responses, the most effective approaches to date rely on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained using a dataset of human preferences, after which reinforcement learning is employed to adjust a language model policy to generate outputs rated highly by the reward model, all while preventing significant divergence from the base model. Despite RLHF's success in producing models with strong performance in dialogue and programming tasks, its pipeline is much more intricate compared to supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the model during training, which leads to considerable computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for optimizing a language model to align with human preferences directly, bypassing explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same objective as traditional RLHF methods—maximizing reward while imposing a KL-divergence regularization—but offers a more straightforward implementation and training process. At a high level, DPO updates function by boosting the relative log-likelihood of preferred responses compared to less preferred ones, while incorporating adaptive, sample-specific importance weights to avoid the model collapse observed when using a basic probability ratio loss. Similar to prior approaches, DPO leverages a theoretical preference framework (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how effectively a reward function captures empirical preference data. However, whereas previous techniques employ the preference model to define a loss for reward model training and subsequently optimize a policy based on the learned reward, DPO reparameterizes the preference loss so it depends directly on the policy. With access to a dataset containing human preference annotations over model outputs, DPO enables direct optimization of the policy using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy for an implicit reward function inferred from preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward method for training language models from preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs on par with, or better than, current approaches—including PPO-based RLHF—for preference-based learning tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, even when applied to models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models at larger scales are capable of completing certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and alignment with user objectives are notably enhanced when fine-tuned on datasets comprised of instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning', allows LLMs to generalize beyond the instruction-tuning distribution and overall makes them more user-friendly \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has proven valuable, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of output quality is often more feasible than gathering expert-level demonstrations. Consequently, later research has fine-tuned LLMs on collections of human preferences, leading to enhanced performance in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These techniques typically begin by training a neural reward model to align with observed human preferences, frequently modeled by frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, followed by adjusting the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning algorithms, such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related approaches \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. An adjacent research direction uses instruction-tuned LLMs guided by human feedback to generate extra synthetic preference datasets that target aspects like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying on minimal human input, such as a written rubric for LLM evaluation. Altogether, these strategies merge two research areas: one focused on reinforcement learning-based language model training for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another dedicated to general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 

Although leveraging relative human preferences is appealing, reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning of large language models remains a significant practical obstacle; this study introduces a theoretically grounded alternative for optimizing relative preferences without the use of RL.

Beyond language-related tasks, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of multiple techniques. When preferences or rankings over actions are used instead of explicit rewards in contextual bandits, the setup is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In these scenarios, since explicit reward signals are absent, theoretical analyses of CDBs rely on the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose probability of outperforming \textit{every} alternative policy is at least 50\% on average \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDBs typically assume that preference feedback is provided in an online fashion, whereas in learning from human preferences, training is usually conducted using a fixed, offline collection of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In a similar vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) approaches rely on binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} 'scoring' function rather than direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. PbRL encompasses a range of algorithms, including approaches that utilize off-policy preference data; however, these usually require an initial step of estimating the latent scoring or reward function before optimization takes place \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our contribution is a single-step policy learning framework that directly tunes the policy to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This approach typically consists of three stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (2) collecting preferences and training a reward model, and (3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by applying supervised learning to a pre-trained LM, using high-quality datasets tailored to the target task(s) such as summarization or dialogue. This step yields a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the second stage, prompts $x$ are passed to the SFT model, which generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are reviewed by human annotators, who indicate a preference for one response over the other, represented as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ are the chosen and unchosen responses from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. These preferences are thought to originate from an underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$ that is unobserved. Several methods are employed to model these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being widely used (although more general ranking models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can be used when multiple ranked answers are available). The BT model specifies that the probability of a human preferring one response over another, $p^*$, is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed set of comparison data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, we can define a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and optimize its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Treating this as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss takes the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is commonly initialized with the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, adding a linear output layer atop the last transformer block to yield a scalar reward score \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To mitigate reward function variance, earlier work typically applies normalization such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the reward model supplies feedback to guide the language model's updates. Building on previous research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the learning objective is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy is permitted to diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$. Usually, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized to match $\pisft$ as well. This constraint is crucial because it ensures the model does not stray excessively from the input distribution for which the reward model is reliable, helps preserve output variety, and avoids collapsing responses to a handful of high-reward options. Because language generation involves discrete choices, this optimization problem is not directly differentiable, so reinforcement learning methods are generally employed. The common practice~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} is to define the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and then maximize it using PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties faced when applying reinforcement learning methods to large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward technique for optimizing policies directly from preference data. In contrast to traditional RLHF approaches—which first infer a reward function and subsequently maximize it through reinforcement learning—our method utilizes a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows us to directly compute its optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for iterative RL training.

As we elaborate below, the central idea is to exploit an analytical correspondence between reward functions and their optimal policies. This mapping lets us convert a loss defined over reward functions into one over policies. By using this change-of-variables strategy, we bypass the need for learning a separate, explicit reward model, yet still optimize with respect to established models of human preference, such as the Bradley-Terry model. In summary, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language model and the implicit reward signal.

\textbf{DPO Objective Derivation.} We begin with the standard RL objective used in earlier research, as shown in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, with a general reward function $r$. As established by previous works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the solution to the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where the normalization constant $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is referred to as the partition function. Details of the derivation are presented in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when approximating the true reward $r^*$ with its MLE estimator $r_{\phi}$, computing $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which hinders direct application of this formulation. Nonetheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be manipulated to express the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference $\piref$, and the unknown $Z(\cdot)$. By applying the logarithm to both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we derive:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This form can be applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its optimal policy $\pi^*$. Notably, the Bradley-Terry model is only influenced by the difference between the rewards assigned to two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the reward reparameterization from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference function in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, leaving an expression for human preference based only on the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Hence, under the Bradley-Terry framework, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
A full derivation appears in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry approach, analogous results hold for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, which are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preferences now expressed in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In parallel with the reward modeling strategy (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy learning objective is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this formulation, we learn an implicit reward through a different parameterization, where the resulting optimal policy is directly $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since this method is mathematically equivalent to learning a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from certain theoretical guarantees, like consistency when the preference data meets specific conditions \cite{bong2022generalized}. We explore these theoretical aspects of DPO and compare them with related methods in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update work?} To gain a clearer, mechanistic picture of DPO, it's instructive to look at the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{larger if the reward estimate is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{push up probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{push down probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ is determined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). Conceptually, this gradient update increases the chance of generating the preferred responses $y_w$ while reducing the likelihood of the less-preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, the influence of each training instance depends on how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ favors the dispreferred response, weighted by $\beta$—in other words, on how much the current reward model misranks the responses, while also factoring in the KL-divergence constraint. Our empirical results indicate that this weighting is vital: removing it (as in a simple variant without the weighting factor) can cause the language model to break down (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}
The standard DPO workflow proceeds as follows: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completion samples $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then annotate them with human preference to form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) update the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using the fixed $\piref$, collected dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and chosen $\beta$ value.

In real-world scenarios, it is preferable to leverage publicly available preference datasets instead of collecting new samples and labels. Because these datasets are generally constructed using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever this model is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not provided, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the log-likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the distribution mismatch between the unavailable true reference distribution and the $\piref$ approximation used in DPO. Additional implementation notes and hyperparameter configurations are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we present a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, supplying theoretical justification and connecting the strengths of DPO to shortcomings observed in actor-critic methods employed for RLHF (including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Actually a Reward Model}  
DPO manages to eliminate the need for both directly modeling a reward function and running RL by utilizing a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the loss function in Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry formulation where the reward is given by $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we train the parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$ in a way that is effectively identical to optimizing a reward model as in Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}, after applying a variable transformation. This section develops the theoretical foundations for this reparameterization, demonstrates that it does not restrict the expressiveness of potential reward models, and proves that it supports exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relationship among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are defined to be equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this relation is an equivalence relation, dividing the set of reward functions into equivalence classes. We can formulate the next two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, including the Bradley-Terry model, any two reward functions belonging to the same class yield identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in identical optimal policies for the constrained RL formulation.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are immediate and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights the classic under-specification challenge associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is common practice to introduce additional identifiability conditions to obtain guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimates in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function in a given equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy, so for our purposes, it suffices to recover any member of the optimal equivalence class. The following theorem, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this point:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild regularity conditions, any reward equivalence class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and, in particular, the Bradley-Terry) models admits a representation via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ according to Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ can be written in the above reparameterized form. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ effectively shifts the reward function by the logarithm of the optimal policy's partition function. Since this normalization only depends on $x$, the function $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ as defined on the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward function), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, $f$ produces a reward from the equivalence class of $r$ in the desired reparameterized form, ensuring that our choice of reward model remains fully general under this transformation.
\end{sproof}

Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} specifies which particular reward, from each equivalence class, is selected by the DPO reparameterization; namely, it selects the reward satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, via Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
that is, $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a proper probability distribution (non-negative and normalized to sum to 1).

Referring to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, observe that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy derived from $r(x, y)$. The core idea behind the DPO algorithm is to enforce specific constraints on the under-determined Plackett-Luce (and in particular, Bradley-Terry) preference models, so as to maintain the expressive power of the reward model class, while guaranteeing that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains explicitly computable for any input $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms}
Our framework also allows us to analyze sources of instability in commonly used actor-critic approaches for RLHF, like PPO. We adopt the RLHF methodology, concentrating on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. There are clear links to the control as inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL scenario presented in \ref{eq:RL}. We posit a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seek to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ represents the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, defined by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Some algebraic manipulations give rise to the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective matches those used in earlier studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, employing the DPO-equivalent reward formulation for rewards of the form $r_{\phi}$. In this formulation, the normalization factor within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be seen as the soft value function for the reference policy $\piref$. Even though this term does not change the optimal policy, omitting it can cause high-variance policy gradients, which hampers stable learning. One approach to incorporate this normalization is to learn a value function, though this is also a challenging optimization problem. Alternatively, past works have addressed normalization by subtracting a human completion baseline, which effectively acts as a one-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization. The DPO reparameterization, however, results in a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we investigate DPO's effectiveness at training policies directly using preference data. We begin with a controlled text-generation scenario to compare how well DPO balances reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization against established preference-based methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF tasks, such as dialogue and summarization. Our experiments reveal that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or outperforms strong baselines—including PPO-based RLHF and the approach of selecting the top trajectory out of $N$ samples according to a learned reward. Prior to discussing the outcomes, we outline our experimental methodology; further specifics can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation problems. Across all scenarios, algorithms are trained to optimize a policy using a set of preference data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents the initial portion of a movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce a continuation $y$ that expresses positive sentiment. For systematic evaluation, we synthetically create preference pairs over generated outputs, leveraging a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. In the SFT setting, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned on the training subset of IMDb reviews until the model converges (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for additional information). For the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the objective is for the policy to generate a concise summary $y$ capturing the key points. Consistent with previous approaches, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, supplemented by human preference data from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT baseline is trained on human-crafted summaries of forum threads\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF platform. Human preference judgments were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on completions from a different but comparably fine-tuned SFT system. Lastly, the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} setting uses $x$ as a user prompt, ranging from science questions to advice requests. The policy must output an informative and engaging reply $y$. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k human–assistant conversations. Each dialogue concludes with two model-generated responses and a human-annotated preference for one. Here, since no prior SFT model exists, we fine-tune a standard language model solely on the preferred responses to construct the SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We adopt two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm performs on the constrained reward maximization task, we first analyze the set of achievable reward and KL-divergence values from the reference policy in a controlled sentiment generation scenario; here, this frontier is measurable since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. In contrast, for real-world applications where the true reward function is inaccessible, we measure each algorithm’s \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a surrogate for human judgment when evaluating the quality of summaries and helpfulness of responses in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, we select test set reference summaries as the baseline, while in dialogue, we rely on the dataset’s preferred responses. Although prior work indicates that LMs may outperform conventional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we further validate our reliance on GPT-4 by conducting a human study described in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show that GPT-4’s evaluations are highly correlated with human judgments, and the level of agreement between humans and GPT-4 is typically on par with, or exceeds, inter-human annotator agreement.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a variety of established strategies for training language models to reflect human preferences. As a baseline, we investigate zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. We further examine the \textbf{SFT} model, as well as \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model obtained via supervised fine-tuning on the selected completion $y_w$—sourced from the SFT model in controlled sentiment and summarization, or from a generic LM in the single-turn dialogue scenario. Another pseudo-supervised technique we include is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to assign greater likelihood to $y_w$ while \textit{reducing} the likelihood assigned to $y_l$; we incorporate an optional weighting factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood penalty. We also evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, where a reward model trained on preference data guides the optimization, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method utilizing the true reward function in the controlled sentiment context. For sentiment tasks, we use both a standard PPO-GT implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and an adapted version with reward normalization and additional hyperparameter tuning (these improvements are also applied when using learned rewards with PPO). Lastly, we analyze the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, which involves drawing $N$ samples from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selecting the top response based on a reward model trained from preference data. Although highly effective, this strategy separates the reward model's quality from PPO training but is computationally expensive, since it requires generating $N$ completions per input during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates assessed by GPT-4 for one-turn dialogues on the Anthropic-HH dataset; DPO stands out as the only technique that surpasses the selected summaries within the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured at various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO’s gains over the ground-truth labels remain relatively consistent across training for all examined temperatures.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-penalized reward maximization objective, commonly utilized in RLHF methods, seeks to maximize reward while simultaneously constraining the policy from diverging excessively from the reference policy. As a result, when evaluating different algorithms, it is essential to consider both the attained reward and the associated KL divergence; achieving marginally greater reward at the cost of substantially higher KL is often undesirable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL trade-off frontier for several algorithms in the sentiment classification context. For each method, we conduct several training runs, each with a distinct policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT), totaling 22 runs overall. Every 100 steps during training, up to convergence, we evaluate policies using a collection of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This refers to the total of per-step KL divergences.} with respect to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results indicate that DPO achieves a considerably more favorable frontier than other methods, securing the highest reward while maintaining low KL. This finding is especially significant for a number of reasons. Firstly, DPO and PPO are optimizing the same objective, yet DPO demonstrates much greater efficiency, providing a reward/KL balance that consistently surpasses PPO. Secondly, DPO attains a superior reward-KL frontier to PPO \emph{even in scenarios where PPO has access to ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization tasks, it is well-known that automated metrics like ROUGE do not always align well with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier research has shown that employing PPO for LM fine-tuning using human feedback leads to more preferred summaries. For our evaluation, we sample outputs from the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the average win rate compared to reference outputs. For each approach, completions are generated at various sampling temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all further fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO achieves an approximate win rate of 61\% at temperature 0.0, surpassing PPO’s peak win rate of about 57\% at the same temperature. Additionally, DPO attains a greater maximum win rate than the best among the $N$ baselines. It should be noted that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not extensively optimized, which suggests these outcomes might be conservative estimates of DPO’s full effectiveness. Furthermore, we observe that DPO maintains strong performance across a range of sampling temperatures, whereas PPO’s effectiveness can diminish to that of the original GPT-J model when the temperature is high. The Preferred-FT approach offers little improvement over the baseline SFT model. A direct comparison of DPO and PPO using human assessments is presented in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO samples at a temperature of 0.25 were chosen over PPO samples at temperature 0 in 58\% of instances.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using the subset of the Anthropic HH dataset’s test split \citep{bai2022training}, specifically focusing on samples involving one round of human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, the preferred completions in the test data serve as the reference to determine the win rate of each approach. Since a standard SFT model does not exist for this task, we begin with the pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions to ensure they align with the model's distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top result among 128 Preferred-FT completions (the Best of $N$ baseline, which plateaus at $N=128$ for this dataset; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompted version of Pythia-2.8B, observing that DPO matches or surpasses the others at optimal temperature settings. We further evaluate an RLHF model, trained using PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a reputable repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we are unable to identify a prompt or temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Considering our TL;DR findings and that both methods optimize the same reward, we treat the Best of 128 baseline as an approximate indicator of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO is the only method that both efficiently improves upon the Anthropic HH dataset’s preferred completions and matches or exceeds the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO rapidly reaches its peak performance.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to learn from preferences without the need for reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework just to leverage existing RL tools, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows for training language models to meet human preferences using a basic cross-entropy objective, completely bypassing reinforcement learning and without any loss in generality. DPO achieves comparable or superior performance to current RLHF techniques, such as those utilizing PPO, with almost no hyperparameter adjustment required. As a result, DPO significantly lowers the complexity involved in training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings prompt a number of compelling avenues for subsequent research. One key question is how the DPO policy performs on out-of-distribution data in comparison to policies trained with explicit reward signals. Preliminary experiments indicate that DPO policies exhibit generalization capabilities on par with those of PPO-based models, though a more thorough investigation is warranted. For instance, it remains to be seen whether leveraging self-labeling techniques within the DPO framework enables effective utilization of unlabeled prompts. Another aspect to consider is the nature of reward over-optimization under direct preference optimization, and whether the moderate performance drop observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this phenomenon. Furthermore, our experiments are limited to models with up to 6B parameters; extending DPO to much larger, cutting-edge architectures represents a promising research direction. In terms of evaluation methodology, we observe that GPT-4-computed win rates vary depending on the prompt phrasing, suggesting that future studies should investigate strategies for reliably obtaining high-quality assessments from automated evaluators. Beyond preference-based language model training, DPO has the potential to be applied to a wide range of generative modeling tasks across different domains.
