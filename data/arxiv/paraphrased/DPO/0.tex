\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, but precisely steering their behavior remains challenging due to the fully unsupervised nature of their training. Current approaches to achieve such control gather human judgments on the relative quality of model outputs and then fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, often using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF involves a complex and frequently unstable process: it first fits a reward model that captures human preferences, and then applies reinforcement learning to fine-tune the large unsupervised LM to maximize this estimated reward while preventing substantial divergence from the original model. In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model used in RLHF that permits direct derivation of the optimal policy in closed form, enabling us to address the standard RLHF objective using only a straightforward classification loss. The resulting method, which we name \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to match or surpass human preference alignment achieved by existing techniques. In particular, DPO fine-tuning outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling sentiment of generated outputs and equals or enhances response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being significantly simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets exhibit remarkable capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models are trained on data generated by humans who possess diverse objectives, priorities, and expertise. Some of these objectives and skills might not be desirable to replicate; for instance, while we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} common programming errors to correct them, we would still prefer to steer the model towards the (possibly infrequent) high-quality coding skills found within its training data when it generates code. Similarly, we might want our language model to be \textit{aware} of a misconception that half the population believes, yet we certainly do not want the model to affirm this misconception as true in 50\% of related queries. In other words, it is essential to select the model’s \emph{desired responses and behaviors} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} to develop AI systems that are safe, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally guide LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective used by these methods can be exactly optimized via a simple binary cross-entropy loss, significantly streamlining the preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, existing approaches imbue desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging curated datasets of human preferences that reflect behaviors deemed safe and useful by humans. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pretraining on extensive textual data. While the most direct method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human demonstrations of high-quality outputs, the most effective approaches rely on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF techniques involve training a reward model on human preference data and then employing RL to optimize the language model’s policy to generate high-reward responses without deviating excessively from the base model. Although RLHF yields models with impressive conversational and programming capabilities, its pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iterative sampling from the policy during training, resulting in substantial computational overhead.

In this paper, we demonstrate a method to directly optimize a language model to align with human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same objective as current RLHF approaches (maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint) while being easy to implement and straightforward to train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log probability of preferred responses over dispreferred ones, but it incorporates a dynamic, per-instance importance weight that mitigates the model degradation observed when using a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference model (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how well a given reward function matches empirical preference data. However, whereas current methods employ the preference model to define a preference loss for training a reward model followed by policy optimization of the learned reward, DPO applies a change of variables to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Thus, given a dataset of human preferences over model outputs, DPO can optimize a policy with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data.

Our primary contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models from preferences without reinforcement learning. Experimental results indicate that DPO performs at least as well as existing techniques, including PPO-based RLHF, for learning from preferences across tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, using language models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of progressively larger sizes have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks zero-shot \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets consisting of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows LLMs to generalize to instructions not seen during instruction-tuning and generally boosts their usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has been successful, \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality are often easier to obtain than expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs using datasets based on human preferences, enhancing performance in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These techniques first train a neural network reward function to align with the preference dataset using a preference model such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, then fine-tune the language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning algorithms, typically REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A closely related area of research uses LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback for instruction following to produce additional synthetic preference data targeting specific attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human supervision through textual rubrics guiding the LLM's annotations. These approaches represent a merging of two research domains: one focusing on training language models with reinforcement learning across various objectives \citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and another on general methods for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of employing relative human preferences, fine-tuning large language models through reinforcement learning remains a significant practical challenge; this paper introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without relying on RL.

Outside the realm of language, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methods proposed. Contextual bandit learning that relies on preferences or rankings of actions rather than explicit rewards is known as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In situations where absolute rewards are unavailable, the theoretical framework for CDBs replaces the idea of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected winning probability against \textit{any} other policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. However, in the CDB framework, preference labels are provided in an online manner, whereas in human preference learning, we often work with a fixed offline dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns from binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} scoring function instead of rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Several algorithms for PbRL have been developed, including some that can leverage off-policy preference data, but they generally involve first explicitly estimating the hidden scoring function (i.e., the reward model), followed by policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a one-step policy learning method that directly optimizes the policy to satisfy the observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We revisit the RLHF procedure described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently by \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process generally involves three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) preference sampling and reward learning; and 3) RL-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: The RLHF process usually starts by fine-tuning a pretrained language model using supervised learning on high-quality datasets tailored to the downstream tasks of interest (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: In the second stage, the SFT model is prompted with inputs $x$ to generate answer pairs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators who express preferences between the two answers, indicated as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the preferred and less preferred completions of $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. These preferences are assumed to be drawn from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unobserved. Various models exist for representing preferences, among which the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model is widely used (though more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also fit within this framework if multiple ranked responses are available). The BT model specifies that the human preference distribution $p^*$ can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given access to a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can parametrize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and determine its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Treating the task as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In the context of language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the supervised fine-tuned (SFT) model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$ by adding a linear layer atop the final transformer layer to yield a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce the variance of the reward function, previous work often normalizes the rewards such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning Phase}: In this stage, the trained reward function is employed to guide the language model's optimization. Following previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the objective is formulated as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where the hyperparameter $\beta$ controls how much the learned policy $\pi_\theta$ can diverge from a reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, which is the initial SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. Usually, $\pi_\theta$ is initialized as $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This KL divergence constraint is crucial to prevent the policy from straying too far from the distribution where the reward model provides reliable feedback, while also preserving diversity in generated outputs and avoiding collapse to a narrow set of high-reward responses. Since language generation is discrete, this objective is non-differentiable and is commonly optimized via reinforcement learning algorithms. A standard method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}. 

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties of deploying reinforcement learning methods at scale for tasks like fine-tuning language models, we aim to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that works directly from preference data. Unlike conventional RLHF techniques, which first estimate a reward function and subsequently optimize it with reinforcement learning, our approach takes advantage of a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to derive the optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for an RL training loop. As we will elaborate shortly, the central idea is to utilize an analytical mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, enabling us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into a loss over policies. This change-of-variables strategy obviates the need to explicitly fit a standalone reward model, yet still performs optimization consistent with established human preference models like the Bradley-Terry model.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO objective.} We begin with the same RL objective as used in previous work, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, with a general reward function $r$. Following earlier studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it is straightforward to demonstrate that the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} has the form:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where the partition function is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A full derivation is available in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even if we replace the ground-truth reward function $r^*$ by its MLE estimate $r_{\phi}$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains costly \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making direct use of this form challenging in practice. Nevertheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we obtain:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the difference in rewards between two completions, that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the reparameterization from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, enabling us to express the human preference probabilities solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Hence, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$, according to the Bradley-Terry model, satisfies the preference model:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}

The detailed derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry model, similar derivations hold for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as outlined in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the probability of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In analogy with the reward modeling objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy learning objective becomes:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \

In this manner, we fit an implicit reward using a different parameterization, whose optimal policy is simply $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since our approach corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from certain theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. In Section~\ref{sec:theory}, we provide a deeper discussion of the theoretical aspects of DPO in comparison to other related works.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{higher weight when reward estimate is wrong}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{increase likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{decrease likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (further details in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Intuitively, the gradient of the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of the preferred completions $y_w$ while decreasing the probability of the less preferred completions $y_l$. Crucially, each example is weighted by how much more the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ favors the less preferred completion, scaled by $\beta$—that is, according to the degree to which the implicit reward model incorrectly ranks the completions—thus incorporating the influence of the KL regularization term. Our experiments highlight the significance of this weighting factor, as omitting it leads to model degradation, as demonstrated in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{DPO overview.} The typical DPO process involves the following steps: 1) Generate sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, obtain human preference labels to form the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; and 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the chosen $\beta$. 

In practical scenarios, it is preferable to leverage publicly available preference datasets rather than producing new samples and collecting human annotations. Since these preference datasets are typically generated from $\pi^\text{SFT}$, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ whenever it exists. If $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is not accessible, we instead initialize $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This step reduces the distributional mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\pi_\text{ref}$ employed in DPO. Additional details about implementation and hyperparameter choices are available in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} In this section, we further interpret the DPO approach, provide a theoretical justification, and connect its benefits to challenges encountered with actor-critic methods used in RLHF (such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Implicitly a Reward Model} DPO circumvents the need to explicitly fit a reward function or execute reinforcement learning by optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model parameterized by the reward $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. We optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ equivalently to reward model training described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} through an appropriate reparameterization. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it imposes no restrictions on the class of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
We define two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ to be equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}

It is straightforward to verify that this indeed forms an equivalence relation, dividing the set of reward functions into equivalence classes. We can then present the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, and specifically the Bradley-Terry model, reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Two reward functions in the same equivalence class lead to the same optimal policy in the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are simple and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-known issue of under-identification inherent in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this under-identification, additional identifiability constraints are typically required to ensure guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma implies that every reward function within the same equivalence class corresponds to the same optimal policy, meaning that our ultimate goal is only to recover some reward function from the optimal class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild conditions, every reward equivalence class consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$ for some policy $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which determines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward function in the equivalence class of $r$ admits the stated reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator normalizes the reward by subtracting the log partition function of $\pi_r$. Since this normalization term depends solely on $x$, $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the expression on the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward function), we get $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ yields a member of $r$'s equivalence class that matches the desired form, meaning that our proposed reparameterization incurs no loss of generality in the reward model.
\end{sproof}

An alternative perspective on Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization chooses, namely, the reward function that meets the condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a valid probability distribution (with non-negative probabilities summing to 1). Moreover, referencing Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we recognize that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward function $r(x, y)$. The essential idea behind the DPO method is that by imposing specific constraints on the inherently under-determined Plackett-Luce family of preference models (including Bradley-Terry), we maintain the range of representable reward functions, while simultaneously ensuring that the optimal policy given by Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be computed analytically for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework can also shed light on the instabilities commonly observed in standard actor-critic algorithms employed for RLHF, such as PPO. We follow the RLHF procedure and concentrate on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. Connections can be drawn to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} applied to the constrained RL problem specified in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, we minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model} generated by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the following optimization objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective is identical to the one optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that use the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this framework, the normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this term does not influence the optimal solution, its absence can cause the policy gradient of the objective to have high variance, potentially destabilizing the learning process. This normalization term can be accounted for by using a learned value function, but optimizing this can be challenging. Alternatively, previous methods have normalized rewards via a human completion baseline, effectively performing a single-sample Monte-Carlo estimation of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that does not depend on any baseline.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically test DPO's capability to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances maximizing reward with minimizing KL-divergence relative to the reference policy, compared to widely used preference learning approaches such as PPO. Subsequently, we assess DPO's performance on larger models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and even outperforms selecting the best among $N$ sampled trajectories under a learned reward function. Prior to detailing these results, we outline the experimental setup; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. In every experiment, algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents a prefix of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is required to generate $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable controlled evaluation, we \textit{generate} preference pairs by comparing generations using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, where $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence on reviews from the training portion of the IMDb dataset (additional details can be found in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy must produce a summary $y$ that captures the main points of the post. Consistent with prior research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preferences collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We employ an SFT model fine-tuned on human-authored forum post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework for RLHF. The human preference dataset was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from samples generated by a different but similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ is a user query that could range from astrophysics questions to requests for relationship advice. The policy must generate an engaging and informative response $y$ to the user's query. We use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k dialogues between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue ends with two responses generated by a large (though unspecified) language model, accompanied by a preference label indicating the human-preferred response. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists for this task, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model solely on the preferred completions to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments utilize two distinct evaluation strategies. To assess how effectively each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective in the controlled sentiment generation setting, we measure each algorithm's frontier of attained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this frontier is calculable because we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). However, in practical scenarios, the true reward function is unknown; thus, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, employing GPT-4 as a surrogate for human judgment regarding summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, we adopt reference summaries from the test set as the baseline; for dialogue, the preferred response in the test dataset serves as the baseline. Although previous research indicates that LMs may outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we conduct a human evaluation study to validate our use of GPT-4 in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings reveal that GPT-4 evaluations align closely with human judgments, with human agreement with GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding agreement levels among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Besides DPO, we examine multiple established approaches for training language models to conform to human preferences. At a basic level, we explore zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Furthermore, we evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model fine-tuned through supervised learning on the chosen completion $y_w$ from either the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another pseudo-supervised technique is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$; this method includes an optional coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ applied to the 'unlikelihood' term. We also evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} using a reward function derived from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method that learns from the ground truth reward function accessible in the controlled sentiment setting. For sentiment experiments, we implement two variants of PPO-GT: an off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance performance (these modifications are also applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we consider the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest-scoring response according to a reward model trained on preference data. This effective approach separates the reward model's quality from the PPO optimization but is computationally costly even for moderate $N$, as it requires generating $N$ completions per query at test time.

\subsection{How effectively can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly used in RLHF algorithms aims to balance maximizing reward while preventing the policy from straying too far from the reference policy. Thus, when evaluating different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward obtained and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the reward-KL frontier for several algorithms within the sentiment setting. We conduct multiple training runs for each algorithm, varying the hyperparameter controlling policy conservativeness in each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT). This grid search totals 22 runs. After every 100 training steps up to convergence, we assess each policy on a collection of test prompts, calculating both the average reward using the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the sum of the KL divergences at each time step.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO delivers the most efficient frontier by a significant margin, achieving the highest reward while maintaining low KL. This outcome is especially noteworthy for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO is markedly more efficient; the reward/KL tradeoff of DPO strictly outperforms that of PPO. Second, DPO attains a superior frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even when PPO has access to the ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO scalable to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak correlation with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning LMs with PPO using human preferences yields more effective summaries. Our evaluation involves sampling completions from the test split of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculating the average win rate against reference completions in the test set. Completions across all methods are generated at sampling temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. We observe that DPO attains an approximate win rate of 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO’s roughly 57\% win rate at its optimal sampling temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO reaches a higher peak win rate compared to the best of $N$ baseline. It is important to note that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not extensively tuned here, suggesting these outcomes might underestimate DPO’s full capability. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates considerably greater stability across different sampling temperatures than PPO, whose performance may decline to that of the underlying GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT shows no significant improvement over the SFT model. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO through human evaluations is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO-generated samples at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples generated at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on a subset of the test portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, involving one round of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations utilize the preferred completions in the test set as a reference to calculate the win rate for each method. Since there is no standard SFT model available for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions to ensure the completions lie within the model's distribution, and subsequently train with DPO. Additionally, we compare against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Our findings indicate that DPO matches or surpasses performance at the optimal temperatures for each approach. We also evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a recognized repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but were unable to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Drawing from results on TL;DR and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate proxy for PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient approach that improves upon the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and achieves comparable or better outcomes than the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To further assess the robustness of PPO and DPO under distributional shifts, we tested the policies derived from our Reddit TL;DR summarization experiment on a different dataset: news articles from the test split of the CNN/DailyMail dataset \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}, using the optimal sampling temperatures identified in the TL;DR setting (0 and 0.25). The findings are shown in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. We calculated the GPT-4 win rate compared to the ground-truth summaries in these datasets, employing the same GPT-4 (C) prompt utilized for Reddit TL;DR but substituting the phrase ``forum post'' with ``news article''. On this novel distribution, DPO still significantly surpasses the PPO policy. This experiment offers preliminary evidence that DPO policies can generalize at least as well as PPO policies, despite DPO not relying on the extra unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts that PPO uses.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We performed a human evaluation to confirm the reliability of GPT-4’s assessments, leveraging results from the TL;DR summarization experiment and employing two distinct GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt asks only which summary better captures the key information from the post. The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally queries which summary is more succinct; this prompt was evaluated because we observed that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, a preference not shared by human raters. The full prompts can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conducted three comparisons using the highest-performing (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing (PPO, temp. 1.0), and a mid-range (SFT, temp. 0.25) approach to encompass a range of output qualities; each was compared against greedily-sampled PPO at its best temperature. Our results indicate that for both prompts, GPT-4’s agreement with human judgments is roughly on par with inter-human agreement, suggesting GPT-4 is a reasonable proxy for human evaluation (limited human raters led us to collect multiple human judgments only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). In general, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates more aligned with human preferences; therefore, this prompt is used for the main results in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. For further details on the human evaluation, including the rater interface and the list of volunteers, see Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach to training capable and aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training method that enables language models to be trained from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning problem into a traditional RL framework to utilize existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the language model to be trained to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning or sacrificing generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or better than current RLHF methods, including PPO-based approaches; consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity of training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings open several crucial avenues for future research. How does the DPO policy perform in out-of-distribution scenarios compared to models trained with explicit reward functions? Preliminary results indicate that DPO policies generalize on par with PPO-trained models, but further thorough investigation is warranted. For instance, can self-labeling training methods using the DPO policy effectively leverage unlabeled prompts? Moreover, how does reward over-optimization present itself within the direct preference optimization framework, and could the slight performance drop observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example? Additionally, while our evaluations have covered models with up to 6 billion parameters, exploring the scalability of DPO to state-of-the-art models several orders of magnitude larger remains an exciting prospect. Concerning evaluations, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates are influenced by prompt design; future work might focus on identifying optimal approaches to obtain high-quality judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO’s potential applications extend beyond training language models from human preferences, including training generative models across various modalities.
