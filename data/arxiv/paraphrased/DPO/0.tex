\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning capabilities; however, fine-grained regulation of their outputs is challenging, owing to the fully unsupervised framework under which they are trained. Conventional approaches to making such models more steerable typically involve gathering human judgments on the relative merit of generated texts and then fine-tuning the original model to better conform to these preferences, often leveraging reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF tends to be intricate and can lack stability, as it typically requires first training a reward model to approximate human preferences and subsequently employing reinforcement learning to optimize the base LM towards maximizing this surrogate reward, all while constraining divergence from the original model. In this work, we propose an alternative parameterization of the reward model within RLHF, which permits the derivation of the associated optimal policy in closed form. This enables us to address the canonical RLHF objective using only a straightforward classification loss. Our approach, named \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), demonstrates stability, strong empirical performance, and computational efficiency, dispensing with both LM sampling during fine-tuning and extensive hyperparameter searches. Empirically, we find that DPO can fine-tune language models for human preference alignment at least as well as, if not better than, prior techniques. Specifically, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in sentiment control tasks, and either matches or surpasses it in summarization and single-turn dialogue quality, all while offering a much more streamlined and accessible training procedure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive datasets have demonstrated remarkable and unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Despite this, these models are exposed to data produced by humans with diverse objectives, preferences, and competencies. Many of these human-driven attributes are not always suitable for direct emulation; for instance, it is valuable for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to facilitate their correction, but, in practice, we prefer the model to \emph{generate} code that reflects the high-caliber programming present in a minority of training examples. Likewise, although it is important for an LM to \textit{recognize} a widespread misconception that half of users may hold, we certainly do not wish for it to echo this misconception in half of its responses to relevant queries. Effectively aligning the model’s \emph{output and conduct} with the desired subset of its extensive \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is therefore fundamental to achieving AI systems that are reliable, effective, and manageable \citep{ouyang2022training}. While prevailing approaches guide LMs toward human-preferred outputs through reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the objective commonly utilized in RL-based methods can actually be solved directly by optimizing a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the preference alignment process.

Broadly, existing techniques encourage language models to adopt targeted behaviors by using carefully collected human preference datasets that illustrate what is considered safe and beneficial by people. This alignment process typically follows an initial unsupervised pre-training phase on a vast corpus of text. Although a simple route is to conduct supervised fine-tuning using examples of exemplary human-provided answers, the leading paradigm remains reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on annotated preference data, which is subsequently used to direct an LM policy through RL to favor outputs judged as highly rewarding, while constraining the model to remain close to its pre-trained state. Although RLHF produces models with advanced conversational and code generation performance, the method introduces significant complexity compared to standard supervised learning, requiring the training of several models and on-the-fly policy sampling, which translates to notable computational demands.

In this work, we present a method for optimizing language models to align with human preferences directly, foregoing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. Our approach, termed \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO)}, introduces an algorithm that implicitly targets the same objective as prevalent RLHF frameworks—maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint—yet maintains ease of implementation and training. The DPO mechanism intuitively enhances the log probability ratio between favored and unfavored outputs, but incorporates an adaptive, per-instance weighting scheme. This feature guards against the degeneration of model performance observed when employing a straightforward probability ratio loss. Similar to other approaches, DPO builds upon a foundational theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate the agreement between a reward function and observed preference annotations. Distinct from standard procedures, which train a reward model based on a preference loss and subsequently optimize a policy against this learned reward, DPO applies a variable substitution to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. As a result, when provided with a dataset reflecting human preferences over generated outputs, DPO can train the policy via a simple binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the optimal policy relative to an implicit reward function inferred from the observed preferences.

The principal innovation of this paper is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a streamlined, RL-free strategy for preference-based training of language models. Our empirical evaluation demonstrates that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of contemporary methods, including those based on PPO-driven RLHF, across tasks such as sentiment control, text summarization, and conversational modeling, and is scalable to models comprising up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

As self-supervised language models scale up, they increasingly demonstrate the capability to perform certain tasks in a zero-shot fashion \citep{radford2019language} or through few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, substantial improvements in their downstream task accuracy and alignment with user objectives are achieved when these models undergo fine-tuning on instruction datasets containing human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning', not only enables large language models (LLMs) to handle novel instructions beyond their fine-tuning distributions but also generally enhances their practical applicability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven effective, collecting \textit{comparative} human feedback about the quality of model outputs is often less demanding than gathering expert-crafted demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent studies have refined LLMs by leveraging datasets built from human preference comparisons, thereby elevating model performance in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, narrative generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and task instruction following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These preference-based approaches generally involve two stages: first, a neural reward model is learned to reflect the collected preferences, often operationalized via the Bradley-Terry preference model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}; second, the language model is further trained to maximize this reward, typically employing reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their extensions \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A parallel research stream utilizes LLMs trained to follow instructions with human feedback as generators of additional synthetic preference data, targeting specific characteristics like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, where only minimal human input in the form of textual guidelines informs the LLM's evaluations. Altogether, these advancements reflect the intersection of research into reinforcement learning-based training of language models for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and general-purpose methods for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. While learning from relative human assessments is an appealing strategy, the process of fine-tuning large language models using reinforcement learning is still fraught with significant practical obstacles; the present work offers a theoretically-grounded alternative for optimizing with relative preference data that circumvents RL.

Beyond linguistic applications, the task of deriving policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning paradigms, yielding a variety of methodological contributions. In particular, contextual bandit learning leveraging preferences or rankings (as opposed to explicit rewards) is formalized as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}) framework. When absolute reward signals are absent, the analysis of CDBs pivots to the notion of a \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy whose probability of outperforming any alternative policy exceeds or equals 50\% on expectation \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike RLHF setups, CDBs acquire preference feedback in an online manner, whereas preference learning from human judgment typically relies on a static, pre-collected batch of annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) utilizes binary preferences produced via an unknown “scoring” function in place of conventional reward feedback \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A number of PbRL algorithms have been devised—including strategies that repurpose off-policy preference datasets—but these commonly operate by first learning a latent reward function (the reward model), followed by policy optimization with respect to this model \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified, direct policy learning approach that seeks to optimize the policy to adhere to observed preferences without intermediate reward modeling.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF procedure as detailed by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning}, and subsequently adopted in works such as \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process is typically structured into three main stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collection of preference data and reward model learning; and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF commences by adapting a pre-trained language model through supervised learning, making use of high-quality annotated datasets tailored for downstream applications (e.g., dialogue, summarization), producing an initial policy $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: In the subsequent phase, prompts $x$ are presented to the SFT model, which generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. Human annotators then express their preferences between each pair, denoted as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, with $y_w$ and $y_l$ representing the preferred and non-preferred responses from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preferences are presumed to arise from a hidden reward mechanism $r^*(y, x)$ that is not directly accessible. Preference modeling can be accomplished by several methods, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly adopted approach; more complex models such as the Plackett-Luce framework \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} may be utilized when rankings over multiple answers are available. Within the BT framework, the observed human preference distribution $p^*$ is formalized as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given a fixed dataset of pairwise comparisons, $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$, sampled according to $p^*$, a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be parameterized, and its parameters learned via maximum likelihood estimation. By recasting this scenario as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. In language modeling, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$, augmented with a linear projection atop the last transformer layer, yielding a scalar reward estimate~\cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To limit reward function variance, it is standard to normalize so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: During reinforcement learning, the acquired reward function serves as a supervisory signal for the language model. Building on previous work~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the objective is formalized as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

with $\beta$ controlling the regularization strength relative to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, which is typically the initial SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. In practice, $\pi_\theta$ is initialized from $\pi^\text{SFT}$ as well. The KL regularization term ensures the updated policy remains close to the data distribution where the reward model is reliable, which also helps preserve output diversity and avoids the collapse to deterministic, high-reward responses. Since language generation produces discrete outputs, this objective is inherently non-differentiable, necessitating reinforcement learning methods for optimization. The prevalent strategy~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} is to define a composite reward $r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))$ and maximize it using PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}. 

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

In light of the difficulties associated with applying reinforcement learning to large-scale systems such as language models, we seek a more streamlined method for policy optimization rooted directly in human preferences. Unlike conventional RLHF frameworks that involve explicit reward model training followed by RL-based optimization, our method capitalizes on a specific formulation of the reward model that allows for direct recovery of the optimal policy in closed form, bypassing the need for an RL stage. Central to our approach is a principled mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, enabling the reparameterization of the loss over rewards as a loss over policies. This change-of-variable framework eliminates the necessity of training a separate reward model, yet remains compatible with established preference models, including the Bradley-Terry model. Effectively, the policy network simultaneously embodies both the language generation model and an implicit reward estimator.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO Objective.} We begin from the standard reinforcement learning (RL) objective with a general reward function $r$, as presented in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} and established in previous literature. As demonstrated in prior works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it follows directly that the solution to the reward maximization problem subject to a KL divergence constraint (see Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}) can be written as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where the normalization factor $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ ensures $\pi_r$ is a valid probability distribution. For a detailed derivation, refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when substituting in an MLE-based reward estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, estimating $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, thus limiting the practicality of this form. Nevertheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be manipulated to represent the reward as a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the unknown normalization constant $Z(x)$. To do so, we take the natural logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, yielding after rearrangement:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This reparametrization may be directly applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Critically, the Bradley-Terry model is solely a function of the reward difference between two outputs, that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the representation from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x, y)$ into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} removes the dependency on the partition function, permitting us to write the human preference probability using only $\pi^*$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$. As a result, the Bradley-Terry-based optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ must satisfy:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

The detailed derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} employs the Bradley-Terry model, analogous derivations hold under more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having now related human preference probabilities directly to the optimal policy rather than the underlying reward, we can cast the problem as maximizing the likelihood of preference data for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In parallel to the reward modeling formulation (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our objective for the policy becomes:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid

In this framework, we model an implicit reward through a different parameterization, where the corresponding optimal policy directly corresponds to $\pi_\theta$. Furthermore, our method aligns with fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, which brings with it desirable theoretical guarantees—such as consistency, provided certain assumptions about the preference data distribution are met \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate on these theoretical considerations and DPO's relation to related approaches in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{Interpreting the DPO Update.} To gain insight into the mechanism of DPO, we can examine the gradient of its loss, $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The derivative of the loss with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ takes the following form:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater impact when the reward model misorders samples}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{encourage $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{discourage $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ serves as the implicit reward defined by the comparison between the learned language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference $\pi_\text{ref}$ (discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, this gradient expression works by boosting the probability assigned to the favored completion $y_w$, while suppressing the probability of the less favored $y_l$. Crucially, the influence of each data point is modulated by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the less preferred completion relative to the preferred one—this is scaled by $\beta$ and thus reflects both the reward misordering and the degree of regularization. Our empirical analysis highlights the necessity of this weighting; omitting the weighting factor can cause undesirable degeneration in the language model, as illustrated in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{DPO pipeline overview.} The typical workflow for DPO involves two main steps: (1) For each prompt $x$, generate two completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$, assign human preference labels, and assemble the resulting offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; (2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the DPO loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using the specified $\pi_\text{ref}$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the chosen $\beta$ parameter. In practical scenarios, it is often advantageous to leverage existing publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting fresh human annotations. Since these datasets are typically based on samples from $\pi^\text{SFT}$, the reference policy is initialized as $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ where possible. In cases where $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is not accessible, $\pi_\text{ref}$ is instead initialized by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach addresses potential distribution mismatch between the ideal reference distribution (which is not directly accessible) and the practical $\pi_\text{ref}$ used in DPO. Further implementation details and hyperparameter settings are described in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} This section aims to deepen the understanding of DPO by offering theoretical insights, justifications, and a discussion of its benefits relative to the shortcomings observed in actor-critic approaches for RLHF (such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO uniquely circumvents both explicit reward modeling and separate RL policy optimization by employing a unified maximum likelihood framework. Importantly, the optimization in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} aligns with the Bradley-Terry model, using a reward formulation $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. Optimization of the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ thus parallels reward model training as in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, via a variable transformation. This section develops the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrating that it does not restrict the expressiveness of the reward models that can be learned and that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We begin by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are defined as equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.
\end{definition}

It is straightforward to verify that this notion establishes an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the space of reward functions into equivalence classes. This enables us to present two important lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce, and specifically the Bradley-Terry, preference framework, any pair of reward functions from the same equivalence class yields identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    For the constrained RL problem, all reward functions in the same equivalence class result in the same optimal policy.
\end{lemma}

The proofs for these statements are elementary, and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-established identifiability issue in the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Given this inherent ambiguity, it is standard practice to apply further identifiability constraints to ensure that the MLE solutions to Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} are well-defined \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma implies that any reward function drawn from a particular equivalence class prescribes the same optimal policy; thus, when considering our ultimate objective, it suffices to recover any representative from the optimal equivalence class. The following theorem, whose proof is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this insight:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward equivalence class consistent with the Plackett-Luce model family (including the Bradley-Terry model) admits a representation of the form ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a given reference model $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be a reward function associated with an optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$, as defined by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that within the equivalence class of $r$, there is a function with the specified reparameterized structure. Define the mapping $f$ as follows:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ acts by subtracting the log-partition function—taken with respect to $\pi_r$ and the reference model—from the original reward. This normalization term depends solely on $x$, ensuring $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which universally holds for any reward function), we find $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, the transformation $f$ yields a canonical representative of the equivalence class for $r$ in the desired log-ratio form, validating that the proposed reparameterization incurs no loss of expressiveness.
\end{sproof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can also be interpreted as identifying the specific reward function, within each equivalence class, that the DPO reparameterization chooses. This function is characterized by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ indeed forms a valid probability distribution (all probabilities are non-negative and sum to one). Notably, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy determined by the reward function $r(x, y)$. The essential observation behind the DPO approach is that, by enforcing particular constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce (and, specifically, Bradley-Terry) class of preference models, one can retain the expressive power over reward models while rendering the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} analytically manageable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms}
The present framework further allows us to analyze issues of instability in standard actor-critic methods commonly adopted in RLHF, such as PPO. We center our attention on the RL fine-tuning phase as described in Section~\ref{section:prelims}, in alignment with the RLHF workflow. By drawing parallels to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, which addresses the constrained RL scenario in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, we postulate a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seek to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy generated by Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model} given the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the following objective for optimization:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective aligns with those maximized in previous studies 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, where the DPO-equivalent reward is applied to the $r_{\phi}$ reward class. Within this framework, the normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function for the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this normalization does not alter the set of optimal policies, omitting it may result in a policy gradient with substantial variance, thereby destabilizing training. One solution is to approximate the normalization using a learned value function, though optimizing this auxiliary component may also be challenging. Alternatively, earlier approaches have utilized a human completion as a baseline, corresponding to a single-sample Monte-Carlo approximation of the normalization constant. The DPO reparameterization, on the other hand, produces a reward structure that eliminates the need for any external baselines.

\section{Experiments}
Here, we empirically assess DPO’s capacity for direct preference-based policy optimization. To begin, we analyze a carefully controlled text-generation scenario and ask: how effectively does DPO balance the trade-off between maximizing reward and minimizing KL divergence from the reference policy, in comparison with standard preference-based algorithms like PPO? We then extend our investigation to more challenging RLHF tasks and larger model architectures, including tasks such as summarization and dialogue. Across these settings, we observe that DPO, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, generally matches or surpasses robust baselines like PPO-based RLHF, and even outperforms choosing the best among $N$ samples using a learned reward. We first explain our experimental methodology, with further specifics provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We conduct experiments across three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all these settings, the algorithms learn a policy using a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents an initial segment of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective is for the policy to produce $y$ reflecting positive sentiment. To facilitate controlled comparison, we automatically construct preference pairs for generated texts by leveraging a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we adapt GPT-2-large to the task by fine-tuning on positive reviews from the IMDb training split, continuing until performance stabilizes (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for more information). In the \textbf{summarization} scenario, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is tasked with generating a summary $y$ capturing the key points. Our experimental setup follows established methodologies and utilizes the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} together with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We employ an SFT model refined on summaries authored by humans\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and train it using the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} in an RLHF paradigm. The preference data in this case comes from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}, using outputs from a different but similarly fine-tuned SFT model. The third task, \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, defines $x$ as a user query, spanning topics from astrophysics to personal advice. Here, the policy is required to generate a helpful and engaging reply $y$. We leverage the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170k conversation transcripts between humans and an AI assistant. Each transcript concludes with two candidate responses, both generated by a large (but unspecified) language model, with a human annotation indicating the preferred choice. In this domain, as there is no pre-existing SFT model, we create one by fine-tuning a standard language model solely on the completions favored by annotators.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We adopt two distinct evaluation methodologies in our experiments. For controlled sentiment generation, we systematically assess each algorithm’s performance by mapping out its reward and KL-divergence frontier relative to the reference policy, leveraging access to an oracle reward function (i.e., a sentiment classifier). This enables a precise characterization of how well each method navigates the constrained reward maximization problem. Conversely, since the true reward function is generally unavailable in practical scenarios, we measure algorithm effectiveness by computing the \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 serves as an automated surrogate for human raters—judging summary quality in summarization tasks and response helpfulness in single-turn dialogue settings. For summarization, our baseline comprises reference summaries from the test split, whereas, in dialogue, the preferred test response acts as the standard. Although literature suggests that large language models can surpass conventional automatic evaluation metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we conduct a dedicated human subject study (see Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}) to validate reliance on GPT-4. Our findings show a high degree of concordance between GPT-4’s assessments and human raters, with agreement rates on par with or surpassing human inter-annotator consensus.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we benchmark several established strategies for aligning language models with human preferences. Our baselines include zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on summarization and two-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. We also assess the standard \textbf{SFT} model and the \textbf{Preferred-FT} approach, where the model is fine-tuned via supervised learning using preferred outputs $y_w$—sourced either from SFT (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic LM (for dialogue). Another baseline, \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, employs a training objective that increases the likelihood of $y_w$ while actively reducing that of $y_l$, modulated by an optional unlikelihood coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$. Furthermore, we include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, utilizing a reward model trained on preference data, and an oracle variant, \textbf{PPO-GT}, that leverages direct access to the ground-truth reward for controlled sentiment experiments. For PPO-GT, we deploy both an out-of-the-box implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a version featuring normalized rewards and refined hyperparameters for enhanced results; these refinements are similarly applied to standard PPO when using learned rewards. Lastly, we incorporate the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, where $N$ candidate completions are generated from SFT (or Preferred-FT in dialogue), and the response rated highest by the learned reward function is selected. Although this approach can deliver strong empirical performance by isolating reward model quality from PPO training dynamics, its computational cost is prohibitive for even moderate $N$ due to the need for multiple completions per test instance.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The reward maximization objective in RLHF algorithms typically incorporates a KL-divergence constraint to limit the policy's deviation from a reference, effectively balancing reward maximization with regularization. Thus, evaluating algorithms requires consideration of both the achieved reward and the corresponding KL-divergence; a higher reward paired with a significantly larger KL is not always advantageous. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, we present the trade-off curve between reward and KL for several methods in the sentiment classification context. For each method, we conduct several independent training sessions, each utilizing a different parameter controlling policy conservativeness (target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO; $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood; random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 experimental runs. At every 100 steps of training up to convergence, we assess the trained policies on a test set by calculating both the mean reward as determined by the true reward model and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, summing KL-divergence at each timestep of the sequence.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. Our results show that DPO establishes a superior reward-KL frontier compared to the other baselines, providing the highest observed rewards at consistently lower KL values. This outcome is striking for several reasons. Most notably, although DPO and PPO target the same formal objective, DPO demonstrates a marked improvement in reward/KL efficiency, strictly outperforming PPO in this regard. Furthermore, DPO surpasses PPO even in cases where PPO is granted access to the ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed by assessing the fine-tuning capability of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, commonly used automatic metrics such as ROUGE are known to sometimes diverge from human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have shown that employing PPO to fine-tune language models on human feedback can yield more useful summaries. To benchmark various approaches, we generate completions from the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the mean win rate against reference completions present in the test set. For each method, completions are sampled at temperatures ranging from 0.0 up to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). All methods, including DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT, begin by fine-tuning the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our analysis reveals that DPO achieves a win rate of roughly 61\% when the temperature is set to 0.0, surpassing PPO, which records about 57\% at its optimal temperature of 0.0. In addition, DPO attains a higher peak win rate than the best-of-$N$ baseline. It is important to mention that we made no significant effort to optimize the $\beta$ hyperparameter for DPO; consequently, these figures may not fully capture DPO's capabilities. Furthermore, DPO demonstrates substantially greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature relative to PPO, whose performance can fall to the level of the unmodified GPT-J model as temperature increases. Preferred-FT yields only marginal gains over the SFT baseline. We further conduct a direct comparison between DPO and PPO using human evaluators in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, finding that DPO completions at a temperature of 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO completions at a temperature of 0.

For single-turn dialogue evaluation, we assess various approaches using the portion of the Anthropic HH dataset’s test split \citep{bai2022training} containing one round of human-assistant exchange. In the case of GPT-4 assessments, we utilize the test set’s preferred completions as references to calculate the win rate of each approach. Since there is no established SFT model for this particular setting, our process begins with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model. We apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the selected completions, thereby ensuring the model’s outputs are in-distribution, and subsequently use this reference for DPO training. Additionally, we include a comparison to the top-performing out of 128 Preferred-FT completions (our analysis shows that the Best of $N$ metric stabilizes at $N=128$ for this dataset; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), and we also evaluate a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model. Across these baselines, DPO matches or surpasses the strongest temperature setting for each. Further, we test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}—a well-established implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}—but could not identify a prompt or temperature setting that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B. Given the alignment in reward function between PPO and Best of 128, as seen in our TL;DR experiments, we treat the latter as an approximate indicator of PPO's effectiveness. Summarizing, DPO stands out as the sole computationally efficient technique to surpass the Anthropic HH dataset’s preferred completions and achieves results comparable to, or exceeding, the resource-intensive Best of 128 approach. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO achieves optimal results in relatively few training steps.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To more thoroughly assess how PPO and DPO perform under distributional changes, we test the policies trained in our Reddit TL;DR summarization study on a distinct domain: the test split of the CNN/DailyMail dataset \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}, which consists of news articles. We utilize the same optimal sampling temperatures identified from the TL;DR experiments (0 and 0.25). The outcomes are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. For evaluation, we measured the rate at which GPT-4 selected the model-generated summaries over the reference summaries using the GPT-4 (C) prompt applied in the Reddit TL;DR setting, except that ``forum post'' was substituted with ``news article.'' On this novel data distribution, DPO demonstrates a clear and consistent advantage over PPO. These findings serve as preliminary evidence that DPO policies are capable of generalizing to new domains comparably to PPO, despite the fact that DPO does not leverage the supplementary unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts available to PPO.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the trustworthiness of GPT-4's evaluation, we carry out a human subject study focused on the TL;DR summarization results and two distinct prompting strategies for GPT-4. The first, \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple), prompts GPT-4 to determine which summary more effectively captures the essential content of the post. The second, \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise), additionally asks GPT-4 to consider conciseness when making its judgment; this is motivated by our observation that GPT-4 (S) tends to favor longer and more repetitive summaries compared to human preferences. The complete prompt wordings are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We design three sets of comparisons that include a high-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), a lower-performing baseline (PPO, temp. 1.0), and a mid-level approach (SFT, temp. 0.25). Each of these is compared to the greedy PPO output (with its optimal sampling temperature), ensuring a representative range of summary qualities. Our findings indicate that GPT-4’s preferences, regardless of prompt, align with human judgments at rates comparable to inter-human agreement, implying that GPT-4 is a suitable stand-in for human raters (note: due to a limited number of human evaluators, multiple human ratings were gathered only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). Among the prompts, \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} yields win rates most similar to human evaluations; as such, we report main results in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets} using this prompt. Further methodological details, such as the rating interface and a list of participating volunteers, are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning stands out as a robust and scalable approach for developing language models that are both capable and well-aligned. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward training approach that allows language models to be optimized based on preference data without relying on reinforcement learning. Unlike traditional methods that reformulate preference learning into an RL context for the sake of leveraging standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between policy distributions in language models and reward signals. This enables us to train models to adhere to human preferences using a simple cross-entropy objective, thus dispensing with both reinforcement learning and any accompanying loss of generality. Remarkably, DPO achieves results on par with or superior to leading RLHF techniques, such as those utilizing PPO, even with minimal hyperparameter adjustment; as a result, DPO significantly lowers the practical complexity associated with preference-based model training.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings open several avenues for continued investigation. For instance, the extent to which policies trained via DPO generalize to distributions different from those seen during training, as compared to models optimized against explicit reward functions, remains to be fully explored. Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO-trained models generalize in a manner akin to those based on PPO, but a thorough analysis is necessary. Another aspect to investigate is whether strategies like self-labeling with DPO can be leveraged to effectively utilize datasets containing unlabeled prompts. Moreover, questions remain about the manifestation of reward over-optimization in the DPO context, such as whether the slight reduction in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is indicative of this phenomenon. While our experiments are limited to models with up to 6B parameters, evaluating how DPO scales to significantly larger, state-of-the-art models presents a promising future direction. Regarding assessment methodologies, we observe that GPT-4's win rate metrics are influenced by prompt phrasing; thus, refining automated evaluation practices is a worthwhile subject for subsequent research. Finally, DPO holds promise not only for language modeling but also as a general technique applicable to the training of generative models across various domains.
