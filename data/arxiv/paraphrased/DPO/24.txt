\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and develop some reasoning capabilities, it is challenging to exert fine-grained control over their behavior because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches to improve steerability involve gathering human ratings that compare the quality of model outputs, then adjusting the unsupervised LM to better reflect these preferences—often using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). However, RLHF typically requires a complicated and sometimes unstable process: first training a reward model based on human judgments, and then using reinforcement learning to adapt the large unsupervised LM in order to maximize this learned reward, all while preventing excessive divergence from the base model. 

In this work, we propose a novel way to parameterize the reward model in RLHF, which enables us to directly compute the associated optimal policy in closed form. This reformulation lets us address the conventional RLHF objective with just a straightforward classification loss. We call this approach \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO). DPO is stable, efficient, and lightweight in terms of computation, eliminating the need for LM sampling during fine-tuning and reducing the amount of hyperparameter tuning required. 

Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can adjust LMs to human preferences as effectively as, or better than, leading alternatives. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF for controlling sentiment in generated text, and provides equal or superior response quality in tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being significantly easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Massive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive corpora demonstrate remarkable and sometimes unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, the training data for these models originates from humans with diverse intentions, objectives, and expertise. Not all of these intentions or skills are suitable for our models to mimic; for instance, while it is helpful for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} common programming errors so that it can address them, we would prefer the model, when producing code, to favor the (often uncommon) high-quality programming skills found within its training corpus. In a similar vein, although it is useful for a language model to be \textit{informed} about a misconception shared by 50\% of individuals, we would not want it to state that misconception as fact in half of the related queries! That is, it is essential to distinguish between a model's vast \textit{knowledge and competencies} and the \emph{preferred outputs and actions} we expect, to ensure that AI systems remain controllable, effective, and safe \citep{ouyang2022training}. While current approaches usually guide LMs towards human-preferred behavior using reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed by such methods can be directly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the preference alignment process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches impart desired behaviors to language models by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that capture behaviors humans consider safe and beneficial. This preference learning phase takes place after the language model undergoes large-scale, unsupervised pre-training on an extensive text corpus. The simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on human-generated examples of high-quality outputs. However, the most effective methods rely on reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on a dataset of human preferences, and then reinforcement learning is used to adjust the language model’s policy so that it generates responses with high reward scores, while also ensuring the model does not deviate too far from its initial parameters. Although RLHF leads to models with strong capabilities in conversation and code generation, its process is far more intricate than supervised learning. RLHF requires training several language models, repeatedly sampling from the model policy during training, and consequently incurs substantial computational expense.

In this work, we demonstrate how to optimize a language model to follow human preferences directly, eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that inherently targets the same objective as conventional RLHF techniques (maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint), while remaining both easy to implement and simple to train. Conceptually, DPO updates aim to boost the relative log likelihood of preferred responses over less favored ones, but crucially introduce a dynamic, example-specific importance weighting to avoid the model collapse associated with a basic probability ratio objective. Like other approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that quantifies the correspondence between a reward function and observed preference data. In contrast to traditional methods that employ the preference model to construct a preference loss for training a reward model—subsequently optimizing a policy against the learned reward—DPO reparameterizes the preference loss to depend directly on the policy. Leveraging a dataset of human-judged preferences among model outputs, DPO is thus able to optimize the policy with a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward derived from the observed preferences.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that avoids reinforcement learning for training language models based on preferences. Our experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs on par with, or better than, established techniques—including PPO-based RLHF—for preference-based tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and conversational modeling, utilizing language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size have demonstrated the ability to handle certain tasks in a zero-shot setting \citep{radford2019language} or with minimal prompt examples \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their alignment with user intent can be greatly enhanced by further training on datasets containing instructions and completions written by humans \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows large language models to generalize to instructions not included in the tuning data and boosts overall model usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven successful, it is often more feasible to collect \textit{relative} human feedback on response quality rather than rely exclusively on expert demonstrations. Consequently, later research has fine-tuned LLMs using preference datasets, which has led to improvements in domains like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Such approaches typically involve first training a reward model—often using the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—to reflect human preferences, followed by optimizing the language model itself to maximize this reward through reinforcement learning strategies such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related methods \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another closely related direction uses LLMs fine-tuned with human feedback to produce additional synthetic preference datasets targeting specific attributes like harmlessness or safety \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak supervision from human-provided textual rubrics for model-generated annotations. These developments mark the merging of two research areas: one focused on applying reinforcement learning to language model training for diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another on broader strategies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the promise of training with comparative human feedback, using reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models remains a substantial practical hurdle; this paper presents a theoretically motivated alternative for optimizing relative preferences without relying on reinforcement learning.

Beyond linguistic settings, policy learning from preferences has been investigated within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to several proposed methodologies. In contextual bandit problems, preferences or rankings over actions—rather than direct reward signals—give rise to the so-called contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical studies of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, which is a policy achieving an expected victory rate of at least 50\% against \textit{every} other policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nevertheless, in the CDB framework, preference feedback is delivered in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences typically involves training on a static set of offline, preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Analogously, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) utilizes binary preferences produced by an \textit{unknown} scoring function in lieu of explicit rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Multiple PbRL techniques have been developed, some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but these approaches generally require an explicit estimation of the underlying scoring (reward) function prior to policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a unified stage policy learning method that optimizes policies to directly fulfill the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We discuss the RLHF process outlined by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning}, as well as in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This procedure typically comprises three main stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) gathering preference data and training a reward model; and (3) optimizing using reinforcement learning.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by using supervised learning to further train a pre-trained language model on curated, high-quality data relevant to the desired downstream tasks (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During this stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators, who indicate a preference for one response over the other, recorded as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$—with $y_w$ representing the favored response and $y_l$ the less favored from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preference judgments are presumed to arise from some unknown underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unobserved. Several models exist to represent preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being commonly adopted (though broader Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be utilized when multiple ranked completions are available). According to the BT model, the probability that annotators select a particular answer can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of comparison samples $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ collected from $p^*$, one can define a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. This can be recast as a binary classification setup, yielding a negative log-likelihood loss:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid (logistic) function. In practice for language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is often constructed by taking the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ and appending a linear head atop the final transformer layer to predict a single scalar reward \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To stabilize the reward output, it is common to normalize the rewards so that  $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the language model receives feedback from the previously learned reward function. In line with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the training objective is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy can diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which is the initial SFT model $\pisft$.
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$ as well. The KL-divergence penalty is crucial to keep the policy close to the region where the reward model is valid, while also preserving output diversity and avoiding collapse to only a few high-reward responses. Since text generation operates in a discrete space, the objective above is not directly differentiable and is generally addressed using reinforcement learning techniques. The common method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} defines the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and maximizes this quantity using PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of scaling reinforcement learning algorithms for tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to establish a straightforward method for policy optimization that works directly with preference data. In contrast to traditional RLHF techniques, which first fit a reward function before using RL to optimize it, our method utilizes a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to obtain the optimal policy analytically, without requiring an RL training phase. 

As we elaborate on below, our central idea is to use a closed-form mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies. This enables us to reframe the optimization problem: rather than minimizing a loss over reward functions, we can minimize a loss directly over policies. This variable transformation sidesteps the need for learning an explicit, separate reward function, yet remains compatible with established models of human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Ultimately, the policy network takes on the dual role of both the language model and the implicit reward estimator.

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO Objective.} We begin with the standard reinforcement learning (RL) objective, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, for an arbitrary reward function $r$, as considered in earlier studies. As established in prior literature~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily shown that the optimal solution to the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the normalization constant or partition function. Full details can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when utilizing the MLE-based estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, rendering this form difficult to use in practical applications. Nonetheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be reformulated to describe the reward in terms of its associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the base policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By applying the logarithm to both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and manipulating algebraically, we arrive at:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This formulation can be used for the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Notably, the Bradley-Terry model relies solely on the reward gap between two responses, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. If we replace $r^*(x,y)$ in the preference model, Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, with the representation from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out, enabling us to write the probability of human preference strictly in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, under the Bradley-Terry preference model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
This derivation is detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived based on the Bradley-Terry framework, an analogous approach can be applied for more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, with derivations provided in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preferences now expressed in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can establish a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In parallel with the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy objective is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
This approach implicitly learns a reward via a different parameterization, where the optimal solution is directly $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate conditions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} offers further analysis of DPO’s theoretical aspects and its connection to related research.

\textbf{How does the DPO update work?} To gain an intuitive grasp of DPO, it's helpful to study the gradient of its loss, $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The loss gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward ranking is incorrect}\left(\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boosts probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduces probability of $y_l$}\right)\right],
\end{multline*}
where the implicit reward function is defined as $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ using the current model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Conceptually, this gradient update pushes the model to assign higher likelihood to the better (preferred) completions $y_w$ and lower likelihood to the less preferred ones $y_l$. Crucially, each example is weighted by how much the implicit reward function $\hat{r}_\theta$ favors the less preferred answer over the preferred one, modulated by $\beta$. This captures the magnitude of the reward model's ranking error, scaled by the KL regularization strength. Empirical results highlight that this weighting scheme is essential—removing the coefficient (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}) can lead to model collapse.

\textbf{Overview of DPO.}  
The standard DPO process consists of: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each input prompt $x$, then annotating with human preferences to build the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; and 2) updating the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and the target $\beta$.  
In reality, leveraging publicly available preference datasets is preferable to creating new samples and collecting human judgments. Since these datasets are typically produced with $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when it is present. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we set $\piref$ to maximize the likelihood of preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This strategy reduces distributional discrepancies between the actual reference distribution—which we do not have access to—and the $\piref$ adopted by DPO. Further implementation specifics and hyperparameter choices are described in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper understanding of the DPO approach, present theoretical justification, and highlight how DPO addresses challenges found in actor-critic methods utilized for RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Actually a Reward Model in Disguise}  
DPO manages to avoid both explicit reward modeling and separate RL steps by employing a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} is identical to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we directly train our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, which corresponds to reward model training as shown in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, simply under a variable transformation. In this section, we will develop the theoretical basis for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it places no additional restrictions on the set of possible reward models, and prove that it enables exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We begin by formalizing an equivalence relation on reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into distinct classes. We can now present the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry case, any two reward functions belonging to the same category produce identical distributions over preferences.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class yield identical optimal policies in the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}
We omit the straightforward proofs here and refer the reader to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a classic under-specification problem in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, additional identifiability constraints are typically required to obtain meaningful guarantees on the MLE solutions to Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within an equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy; thus, when optimizing our objective, it suffices to recover any representative from the optimal reward class. In Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, we establish the following theorem:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild assumptions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (including Bradley-Terry) models can be characterized via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that determines an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$, as defined by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a reward from the equivalence class of $r$ admits the reparameterization above. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the log partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since this normalization term is a function solely of the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. If we substitute $r$ by the RHS of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds universally for any reward), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ yields a reward from the equivalence class of $r$ in the intended reparameterized form, so our reward model retains full generality under this representation.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} identifies the exact reward within each equivalence class that the DPO reparameterization selects; namely, the reward function that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a proper probability distribution (non-negative values summing to one).
Furthermore, as per Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} constitutes the partition function of the optimal policy induced by $r(x, y)$. The central idea behind the DPO algorithm is that, by imposing particular constraints on the under-specified Plackett-Luce (and especially Bradley-Terry) preference models, we maintain the flexibility of the representable reward models while rendering the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically tractable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instability}

Our framework also allows us to analyze the instability often observed with standard actor-critic methods used in RLHF, such as PPO. Adhering to the RLHF process, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. The constrained RL problem in \ref{eq:RL} can be linked to the control as inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement}. Here, we consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and determined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After some algebraic manipulation, the optimization objective is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}

This objective is identical to the one used in earlier research \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} that employs the DPO-equivalent reward formulation for rewards of the form $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be seen as the soft value function for the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not alter the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient estimates to have large variance, potentially destabilizing training. Introducing a learned value function can help account for this normalization, but learning such a function can be challenging. Past approaches have instead normalized rewards with a human completion baseline, essentially using a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation for the normalizer. By comparison, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the need for any baseline adjustments.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess how well DPO can learn policies directly from preference data. We begin with a controlled text-generation task, where we investigate how effectively DPO balances reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization relative to standard preference optimization methods such as PPO. Then, we test DPO on larger models and more challenging RLHF problems, including tasks like summarization and conversational response generation. Our results show that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO typically matches or outperforms strong baselines such as RLHF with PPO, as well as surpasses methods that select the best trajectory out of $N$ samples using a learned reward model. We outline the experimental methodology below; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our empirical study covers three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all tasks, the algorithms learn a policy from a collection of preference data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents the initial segment of a movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy must produce $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. For reliable assessment, we create preference pairs for generated outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until convergence using reviews from the IMDB train split (more details in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). For the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is required to produce a concise summary $y$ highlighting the main ideas. Consistent with prior research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, supplemented with human preference data from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-created Reddit summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF framework. Human preferences were gathered by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on samples from another SFT model trained in a similar fashion. In the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} setting, $x$ is a user prompt that could be anything from a scientific question to a personal advice request. Here, the policy must return an informative and engaging reply $y$ to the user’s prompt. For this, we employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which consists of 170k conversations between humans and an AI assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two candidate responses from a large (unspecified) language model and a label indicating human preference. Since no SFT checkpoint is provided for this data, we fine-tune a base language model solely on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we evaluate performance in the controlled sentiment generation setup by plotting each method’s trade-off between reward achieved and KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is feasible because we have access to the ground-truth reward function (provided by a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in real-world contexts where the true reward function is unavailable, we instead assess each algorithm’s \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 serves as a stand-in for human evaluators, judging the quality of summaries and helpfulness of single-turn dialogue responses. For summarization tasks, we compare outputs against reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the response preferred in the test set. Although prior work indicates large language models can outperform existing metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we additionally run a human study to validate our reliance on GPT-4 for evaluation, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show strong alignment between GPT-4 and human judgments, with human-GPT-4 agreement typically matching or exceeding agreement between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess multiple established techniques for aligning language models with human preferences. For the summarization task, we apply zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, while for the dialogue task, we use 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We also examine the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, where the latter is trained via supervised fine-tuning on the selected completion $y_w$—sourced either from the SFT model (for summarization and controlled sentiment) or a base LM (for single-turn dialogue). Another approach, \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, is a pseudo-supervised method that encourages the model to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously decreasing the likelihood of $y_l$, employing a tunable coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ for the unlikelihood loss. We further include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which utilizes a reward model trained on preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the true reward function provided in controlled sentiment scenarios. In our sentiment studies, two versions of PPO-GT are tested: an out-of-the-box implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a revised version featuring reward normalization and optimized hyperparameters for enhanced outcomes (these adjustments are also applied to regular PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we evaluate the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which generates $N$ candidate completions using the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue), selecting the one with the highest score as determined by a reward model trained from human preferences. Although this strategy tends to yield high performance, it becomes computationally prohibitive for even moderate $N$, since $N$ completions must be sampled for each input during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates assessed by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogues; DPO stands out as the sole method that surpasses selected summaries within the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO’s advantage over the dataset labels remains consistent during training regardless of the sampling temperature used.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-regularized reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF methods seeks to maximize reward while ensuring the learned policy does not diverge excessively from the reference policy. As a result, when comparing different methods, it is essential to consider both the achieved reward and the resulting KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially larger KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the tradeoff between reward and KL for several methods in the sentiment task. For each method, we perform multiple independent runs, each with a different policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, while preferred-FT uses different random seeds), resulting in 22 runs in total. After every 100 training steps until reaching convergence, we assess each trained policy on a set of held-out prompts, calculating both the mean reward according to the ground truth reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the total of the per-step KL divergences.} from the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings show that DPO consistently achieves the most favorable tradeoff, reaching higher rewards while maintaining low KL divergence. This outcome stands out for several reasons. Notably, DPO and PPO target the same objective, yet DPO achieves much greater efficiency—DPO's reward/KL tradeoff strictly outperforms PPO. Furthermore, DPO delivers a superior frontier even when PPO is granted access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO capable of scaling to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, automatic metrics such as ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous studies have demonstrated that preference-based fine-tuning of LMs with PPO leads to more desirable summaries. Our comparison of various approaches is based on generating outputs for the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset, and measuring the mean win rate versus reference summaries in the test set. For each method, completions are sampled at temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT methods all further train the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO reaches an approximately 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, surpassing PPO’s best win rate of about 57\% at its optimal temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO achieves a higher peak win rate compared to the best-of-$N$ baseline. It is important to highlight that we did not perform extensive tuning of DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter, so DPO's actual capacity may be underestimated here. We also observe that DPO maintains greater resilience to temperature changes in sampling, whereas PPO's win rate can drop to that of the original GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT, in contrast, does not yield notable improvement over the SFT baseline. Furthermore, in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we present head-to-head human assessments comparing DPO and PPO, showing that at temperature 0.25, DPO outputs were favored over PPO outputs 58\% of the time.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on examples with a single human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluation, we use the preferred completions from the test data as references to calculate win rates for each approach. Since a standard SFT model does not exist for this task, we initialize from the pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, employ Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model using the chosen completions (ensuring outputs remain in-distribution), and then apply DPO training. Our comparisons also include the strongest out of 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at 128 for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted version of the original Pythia-2.8B, showing that DPO matches or surpasses the best-performing temperature setting of each competitor. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, using a reputable implementation \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but are unable to identify a prompt or temperature that exceeds the performance of the base Pythia-2.8B. Given our findings from TL;DR and the shared reward function across methods, we use Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method to outperform the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving performance comparable to or surpassing the resource-intensive Best of 128. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO achieves its optimal results relatively rapidly.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from human preferences serves as an effective and scalable approach for developing language models that are both competent and aligned. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward method for training language models using preferences, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting preference learning into a typical RL framework to leverage existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows for direct optimization of language models to align with human preferences, employing a basic cross-entropy loss function, while bypassing reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. Remarkably, DPO achieves comparable or superior performance to current RLHF techniques—such as those utilizing PPO—without extensive hyperparameter tuning. Consequently, DPO significantly lowers the hurdles associated with training language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings open up a number of directions for further investigation. One key question is how well the DPO policy generalizes to out-of-distribution data when compared to approaches that learn from an explicit reward signal. Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may generalize on par with PPO-based methods, but a more thorough analysis is warranted. For instance, can leveraging self-labeling with the DPO policy make similarly effective use of data that lacks labels? In addition, it is important to understand how reward over-optimization occurs within the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the minor drop in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is an example of this phenomenon. Furthermore, although our current experiments are limited to models of up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models presents an intriguing avenue for future study. On the topic of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4’s win rates are influenced by the phrasing of the prompt, highlighting the need for research into optimal strategies for eliciting reliable assessments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO offers potential applications beyond just training language models based on human preferences, such as developing generative models for other types of data.
