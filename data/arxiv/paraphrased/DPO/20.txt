\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning abilities, yet precisely directing their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current approaches for improving steerability typically gather human judgments comparing the quality of different model outputs, then fine-tune the unsupervised LM to match these preferences, commonly using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). RLHF, however, involves a complex and often unstable two-step process: first, building a reward model to capture human preferences, then applying reinforcement learning to tune the LM to maximize this learned reward while avoiding significant deviation from the base model. In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that makes it possible to directly recover the optimal policy in closed form. This allows us to address the usual RLHF objective with a straightforward classification loss. We term our approach \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO). DPO offers stability, strong performance, and computational efficiency, removing the need for LM sampling during fine-tuning and reducing the effort needed for hyperparameter selection. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to match human preferences as well as or better than existing approaches. In particular, DPO fine-tuning outperforms RLHF based on PPO in controlling generation sentiment, and delivers comparable or better results in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being notably easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Extensive unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora have demonstrated unexpected competencies~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models are developed on data produced by humans with diverse intentions, backgrounds, and abilities. Not all of these intentions and skills are desirable for the model to emulate; for instance, although we might want our AI code assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors to help fix them, we prefer the model to favor the (often scarce) exemplary coding expertise in its training set when writing code. Likewise, although the language model should be \textit{informed} about prevalent misconceptions believed by half the population, it should not report the misconception as correct in half of its answers on the topic. Thus, guiding the model's \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from among its broad spectrum of \textit{knowledge and capabilities} is vital for developing AI that is reliable, effective, and manageable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Traditional approaches typically use reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human values, but we will demonstrate that the RL-based preference optimization objective can be solved precisely using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, streamlining the preference alignment process.

Broadly speaking, current techniques teach language models to exhibit desired behaviors by using carefully selected human preference datasets that reflect behaviors people deem safe and useful. This preference acquisition phase follows a preliminary period of extensive unsupervised pre-training on a large-scale text corpus. The simplest strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human-annotated responses; however, the most effective approaches leverage reinforcement learning from human (or AI-generated) feedback, known as RLHF or RLAIF \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}. In RLHF, a reward model is trained on human preference data, and reinforcement learning is then employed to adjust the language model’s policy, encouraging it to generate responses that achieve high reward scores while remaining relatively close to the original model. Although RLHF yields language models with strong conversational and programming skills, its workflow is substantially more involved than supervised learning. It requires training several language models and incorporating policy sampling during training, which leads to high computational demands.

In this work, we present a method for directly tuning a language model according to human preferences, eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same objective as standard RLHF methods—namely, maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint—yet is easier to implement and train. At a high level, the DPO update boosts the log odds of preferred answers compared to non-preferred ones, while employing a dynamic, per-sample importance weighting to avoid the model collapse observed with a straightforward probability ratio objective. DPO, like prior methods, utilizes a theoretical preference model (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how well a reward function matches observed preference data. In contrast to other techniques that use this model to train a reward predictor and then fit a policy to this predictor, DPO leverages a variable substitution to express the preference loss as a direct function of the policy. With a dataset capturing human choices among model outputs, DPO can train a policy using a standard binary cross-entropy loss, resulting in the optimal policy for an implicit reward function learned from the preference dataset.

The core contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that enables training language models based on preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Our experimental results indicate that DPO performs on par with, or better than, current approaches such as PPO-based RLHF for preference-based learning tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue, even when applied to language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of increasing size have demonstrated the ability to solve some problems with zero-shot prompts \citep{radford2019language} or by leveraging a few examples \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user expectations can be substantially enhanced through fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning', helps LLMs to generalize to new instructions beyond those present in the instruction-tuning corpus, thereby making them more broadly useful \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction-tuning has proven successful, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of answer quality is generally more feasible than assembling large numbers of expert demonstrations. As a result, later research has refined LLMs with datasets containing human preferences, leading to improved abilities in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, narrative generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. In these approaches, a neural network-based reward function is first trained to match the collected preference data, typically using a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}. The language model is then fine-tuned to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related methods \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another closely linked direction involves employing LLMs that have been tuned for instruction-following with human feedback to create extra synthetic preference datasets, targeting characteristics like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, and relying only on lightweight human supervision via a written rubric for LLM annotations. These strategies merge two distinct research areas: training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and broader techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
Even though relying on relative human preferences is attractive, fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning still poses significant practical difficulties; this work introduces a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without resorting to RL.

Outside the realm of language tasks, acquiring policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to a variety of proposed methods. In contextual bandit scenarios, learning based on action preferences or rankings—rather than explicit rewards—is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute rewards, theoretical work on CDBs replaces the traditional optimal policy with the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy whose expected success rate when compared to \textit{any} other policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike CDBs, where preference information is provided in an online manner, learning from human preferences typically relies on a fixed, offline set of action pairs annotated with preference feedback \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) utilizes binary preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} scoring function instead of explicit reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There exist multiple algorithms for PbRL, some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but most methods involve an explicit stage for estimating the underlying scoring (reward) function, which is then optimized \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a direct policy learning approach that optimizes the policy in a single stage to adhere to the given preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF workflow as outlined by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and further explored in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). The process generally consists of three main stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) preference collection and reward modeling; and (3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by applying supervised learning to further train a pre-trained LM on curated, high-quality datasets tailored to the target application (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During this step, the SFT model receives prompt inputs $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These paired responses are shown to human annotators, who select their preferred answer. This preference is noted as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the favored and unfavored responses in $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. The choices made by the annotators are assumed to be governed by an unknown underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains inaccessible. Several methodologies are available for modeling such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being widely adopted, although the framework can also accommodate broader Plackett-Luce models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} if multiple ranked responses are available. According to the BT model, the human preference probability $p^*$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Supposing we have a fixed collection of preference pairs $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, we can introduce a parameterized reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$, learning its parameters by maximizing likelihood. Interpreting this as a binary classification problem, the corresponding negative log-likelihood loss is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid (logistic) function. For language models, the reward predictor $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically based on the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, with a linear output head added to the top of the last transformer layer to produce a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce the variance of the reward function, it is common practice to normalize the predicted rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In this phase, the reward function acquired during previous training is utilized to guide the language model. Consistent with prior studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the training objective is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\, \piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ determines how much the policy can diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which is the pretrained SFT model $\pisft$.
In implementation, the policy $\pi_\theta$ for the language model typically starts as $\pisft$. The KL penalty is essential to keep the policy close to the region where the reward model produces reliable feedback, to preserve output diversity, and to avoid the policy converging to a narrow set of outputs. Because text generation involves discrete sequences, the loss above is not directly differentiable and is usually addressed using reinforcement learning. The typical method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} defines the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and applies PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} to maximize it.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties of scaling reinforcement learning methods to tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to create a straightforward method for policy optimization that relies directly on preference data. In contrast to traditional RLHF techniques—which first fit a reward model and then maximize it using reinforcement learning—our method utilizes a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to compute the optimal policy analytically, thereby eliminating the need for an RL training loop. 

As we will elaborate shortly, the central idea is to exploit an analytical correspondence between reward functions and their optimal policies, enabling the reformulation of a loss over rewards into a loss over policies. This variable substitution sidesteps the need for an explicit, separate reward model, while still aligning with established models of human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network serves the dual role of modeling both the language model and the (implicit) reward function.

\textbf{Deducing the DPO objective.} We begin by considering the same reinforcement learning objective as previous research, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, using a general reward function $r$. As shown in earlier works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the solution to the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} has the following form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as a normalization factor. The full derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Although an MLE estimate $r_\phi$ can be used to approximate the true reward function $r^*$, computing $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, presenting practical challenges. Nevertheless, it is possible to re-express Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to write the reward as a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Concretely, taking the logarithm of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing algebraic manipulation gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This change of variables can be applied to both the true reward $r^*$ and its optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model is determined only by reward differences between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} results in cancellation of the partition function, allowing us to describe human preference probabilities solely with $\pi^*$ and $\piref$. Therefore, under the Bradley-Terry framework, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies the preference relation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
A full derivation appears in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is based on the Bradley-Terry approach, analogous derivations can be constructed using more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the human preference probabilities in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Following a similar structure to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this formulation, we are implicitly modeling the reward through an alternative parameterization, where the policy $\pi_\theta$ itself represents the optimal solution. Additionally, since this approach can be interpreted as training a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate further on the theoretical aspects of DPO and its connections to related methodologies in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update operate?} To gain insight into the workings of DPO, it is helpful to study the gradient of its loss function, $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The parameter gradient $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater influence when the reward estimate misranks the outputs}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{lower probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward implicitly defined by the current language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). Conceptually, the loss function's gradient modifies $\pi_\theta$ by pushing up the probability of preferred responses $y_w$ and pushing down the probability of non-preferred ones $y_l$. Notably, the update's strength on each training example is proportional to how much the internal reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ favors the less desired completions, adjusted by the coefficient $\beta$—in other words, it depends on the degree to which the internal reward model ranks the outputs incorrectly, moderated by the KL penalty. Our empirical findings show that including this weighting is critical, as removing the coefficient (i.e., using a simpler version of the update) can lead to degenerate model behavior (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The standard DPO workflow proceeds as follows: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, annotate them with human preference labels, and assemble the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$. 2) Train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to the chosen $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, practitioners often rely on pre-existing, public preference datasets rather than collecting new samples and human judgments. Since these datasets are typically generated with $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever this model is provided. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we fit $\piref$ to maximize the likelihood of preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps address the mismatch between the unavailable true reference distribution and the $\piref$ applied in DPO. More implementation details and information about hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, present supporting theoretical arguments, and connect the benefits of DPO to common problems encountered with actor-critic algorithms employed in RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}

DPO eliminates the need for both direct reward function fitting and RL-based policy optimization by utilizing a single maximum likelihood loss. Observe that the objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} is mathematically equivalent to a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Our approach involves optimizing the parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$, which corresponds to optimizing the reward model as described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} under a variable transformation. In this section, we will develop the theoretical underpinnings of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the space of possible reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables the precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by establishing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are called equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.  
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this forms an equivalence relation, which divides the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can be established:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce preference model, including the Bradley-Terry variant, any pair of reward functions belonging to the same class will generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in the same optimal policy for the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a familiar identifiability problem in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is common practice to add extra constraints to ensure identifiability and to guarantee the properties of the MLE estimators from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma implies that all reward functions from a given equivalence class induce identical optimal policies; thus, for our overall objective, we only need to recover a single representative reward function from the correct equivalence class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, any reward class that is compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and, specifically, the Bradley-Terry) models can be written as the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and some reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be an arbitrary reward function, which induces the optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as determined by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We show that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ admits the above reparameterized form. Define the projection operator $f$ as
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the log-partition function (i.e., normalization constant) of $\pi_r$. The normalization term depends solely on $x$, so $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ still lies in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which applies to any reward), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. This demonstrates that $f$ yields a member of the equivalence class of $r$ with the desired structure, ensuring no loss of generality from employing this reparameterization for the reward model.
\end{sproof}

Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as precisely identifying which reward function within an equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization; namely, the reward function that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, according to Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which ensures that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (i.e., the probabilities are nonnegative and sum to 1).
Referring to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is evident that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} defines the partition function for the optimal policy generated by the reward $r(x, y)$.
The main idea behind the DPO algorithm is to enforce particular constraints on the generally under-constrained Plackett-Luce (and, in particular, the Bradley-Terry) class of preference models. This ensures that the set of admissible reward functions remains unchanged, but makes the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically manageable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}
Our framework also enables us to analyze the sources of instability in standard actor-critic algorithms commonly applied in RLHF, such as PPO. Adhering to the RLHF workflow, we specifically investigate the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We establish links to the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} as applied to the constrained RL setup defined in \ref{eq:RL}. Given a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, we seek to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} determined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After some manipulation, this results in the following objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}
This formulation aligns with objectives utilized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, where the DPO-equivalent reward is employed for the class of rewards $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization factor within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be viewed as a soft value function for the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient to exhibit large variance, potentially destabilizing training. One can introduce a learned value function to address this normalization, yet this approach often poses optimization challenges. Alternatively, earlier approaches have normalized rewards with a human-completion baseline, effectively a single-sample Monte-Carlo approximation of the normalization factor. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function independent of any such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we experimentally assess DPO’s effectiveness at learning policies directly from preferences. We begin by investigating, in a controlled text-generation scenario, how well DPO balances reward maximization and maintaining closeness (in terms of KL-divergence) to a reference policy, in comparison with widely used preference optimization methods such as PPO. We then test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF benchmarks, including tasks like summarization and dialogue. Our results show that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or surpasses robust baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as methods that select the top trajectory out of $N$ samples under a learned reward. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline our experimental methodology, with further specifics available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We assess our methods on three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all tasks, algorithms optimize a policy using a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents a prompt taken from a movie review in the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, with the objective for the policy to generate a completion $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. For rigorous evaluation, we \textit{automatically} construct preference pairs by leveraging a pre-trained sentiment classifier to compare completions, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_l)$. For SFT, we adapt GPT-2-large via fine-tuning on the training split of IMDb reviews until performance plateaus (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for further details). For \textbf{summarization}, $x$ consists of a Reddit forum post, and the policy's role is to generate a summary $y$ capturing the essential points. Consistent with prior research, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} combined with human preference annotations compiled by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model is fine-tuned on human-written summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX RLHF framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}. The human preference set was collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using outputs from a similar but distinct SFT model. Lastly, in \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, $x$ is a user prompt, which can range from a science inquiry to a personal advice request. Here, the policy is tasked with generating a relevant and informative response $y$. For this, we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which comprises 170,000 human-assistant dialogues. Each conversation ends with two candidate responses from a large, unidentified language model, along with a label indicating the response preferred by the human annotator. Since there is no pre-existing SFT model for this dataset, we create one by fine-tuning a general language model solely on preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methodologies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we first use a controlled sentiment generation scenario, measuring each algorithm’s performance via the trade-off between the obtained reward and the KL-divergence from the reference policy; this trade-off curve is computable since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is available. Conversely, in practical scenarios where the ground-truth reward is inaccessible, we evaluate each algorithm based on its \textit{win rate} against a baseline, relying on GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment in both summary quality (for summarization) and helpfulness (for single-turn dialogue). For summarization tasks, the baseline is the reference summary from the test data, while for dialogue tasks, it is the preferred test response. Although prior research suggests that language models can outperform standard metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we additionally perform a human evaluation study to validate our reliance on GPT-4, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results reveal a high correlation between GPT-4 and human ratings, with agreement rates between humans and GPT-4 matching or exceeding those observed among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess multiple established techniques for aligning language models with human preferences. As a baseline, we apply zero-shot prompting to \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization, and use 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in dialogue tasks. We further evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model as well as \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves supervised fine-tuning using the preferred output $y_w$—sourced from either the SFT model (in the case of sentiment control and summarization) or a general LM (for single-turn dialogues). Another alternative we consider is the \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural} approach, which trains the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while actively decreasing the probability of $y_l$, incorporating a tunable coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the unlikelihood loss. Additionally, we include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} based on a reward model derived from preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method that uses the actual reward function available in controlled sentiment scenarios. For sentiment-based experiments, two versions of PPO-GT are tested: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized variant that applies reward normalization and enhanced hyperparameter tuning (these improvements are also adopted for standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline is considered, where $N$ samples are drawn from either the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue), and the top-scoring output, as judged by a preference-trained reward model, is selected. This method, while often yielding superior performance, is not scalable for practical use with even moderate values of $N$, since it requires sampling $N$ completions per query at test time.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 estimated win rates for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogues; among the methods evaluated, only DPO surpasses the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates evaluated at different sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the dataset labels, and this margin is maintained across various temperatures as training progresses.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained objective for reward maximization, commonly employed in RLHF methods, seeks to maximize reward while simultaneously limiting the policy's divergence from a reference policy. As a result, when evaluating different methods, it is essential to consider both the obtained reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially higher KL is generally not preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} presents the trade-off curve between reward and KL for several approaches in the sentiment analysis scenario. For each method, we perform multiple training runs, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in 22 total runs. Every 100 training steps up to convergence, we assess each trained policy on a suite of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward using the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the sum of KL-divergences over all time steps.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results show that DPO clearly yields the most favorable frontier, attaining top reward levels while maintaining low KL values. This finding is striking for several reasons. First, even though DPO and PPO optimize the identical objective, DPO is substantially more effective, with DPO’s reward/KL tradeoff consistently outperforming that of PPO. Second, DPO establishes a better frontier than PPO, \emph{even in scenarios where PPO is given access to ground-truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on large-scale preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We now assess how well DPO fine-tunes on tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, widely-used automatic metrics such as ROUGE often fail to align with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous studies show that language models fine-tuned with PPO using human preference data generate higher-quality summaries. To benchmark different approaches, we generate samples from the test partition of the TL;DR summarization dataset and compute the mean win rate against reference summaries from the test set. For every method, outputs are sampled across temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with corresponding win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all fine-tune the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. At a temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO, which reaches about 57\% at its best sampling temperature. Furthermore, DPO surpasses the maximum win rate achieved by the best-of-$N$ baseline. It is important to mention that DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter was not carefully tuned, suggesting these findings may underrepresent DPO's capabilities. Additionally, DPO demonstrates much greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose win rate may decline to that of the original GPT-J model as the temperature increases. Preferred-FT does not deliver a marked improvement over the base SFT model. We further perform a direct comparison of DPO and PPO with human raters in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO samples at a temperature of 0.25 are preferred 58\% of the time relative to PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogues, we assess the various approaches on a subset of the Anthropic HH test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on instances with a single human-assistant exchange. For GPT-4 evaluations, we use the preferred test completions as references to calculate win rates for each method. Since there is no established SFT model for this setting, we begin with the pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on selected completions so that the outputs are in-distribution, and subsequently train using DPO. We also benchmark against the top selection from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline levels off at 128 for this dataset; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model, finding that DPO matches or outperforms the other approaches at their optimal sampling temperatures. Additionally, we assess an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH set \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we are unable to identify prompts or temperatures that surpass the performance of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Drawing from our TL;DR results and the shared reward optimization of both strategies, we use Best of 128 as an approximate indicator for PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient technique that achieves gains over preferred completions on the Anthropic HH benchmark, delivering results on par with or superior to the resource-intensive Best of 128 approach. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains optimal performance in relatively few training steps.

\subsection{Corroborating GPT-4 Assessments with Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We carry out a user study to assess how trustworthy GPT-4's evaluations are, drawing on findings from the TL;DR summarization task and leveraging two separate GPT-4 prompt designs. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt inquires which summary captures the core content of the post most effectively. The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt, by contrast, also requests a judgment on conciseness; this variation is included because, under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 tends to favor summaries that are longer and more redundant than human raters prefer. \section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable method for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, an intuitive approach for optimizing language models using preference data, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting preference learning into a traditional RL framework to utilize generic RL methods, DPO establishes a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the model to be trained to reflect human preferences using a straightforward cross-entropy objective, entirely sidestepping reinforcement learning without any compromise in generality. Remarkably, DPO achieves results on par with, or superior to, current RLHF approaches, such as those employing PPO, while requiring almost no hyperparameter tuning. Consequently, DPO substantially lowers the difficulty of training additional language models from preference feedback.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings open up a number of avenues for future investigation. For instance, how well does the DPO policy extrapolate beyond the training distribution when compared to models trained via explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO-based policies generalize on par with those optimized with PPO, but a broader analysis is warranted. Another question is whether incorporating self-labeling with the DPO policy can similarly leverage unlabeled prompts. In addition, it remains unclear how reward over-optimization appears in the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the modest performance dip observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is an example of this effect. While our current evaluations consider models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to models that are several orders of magnitude larger remains an exciting prospect. On the subject of evaluation methods, our results show that GPT-4's computed win rates are sensitive to the specific prompts used, suggesting that further research is needed to determine optimal strategies for obtaining reliable assessments from automated systems. Beyond aligning language models with human preferences, DPO has the potential for broader applicability, such as training generative models in other domains.
