\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely directing their outputs is challenging due to the purely unsupervised nature of their training process. Current approaches for improving controllability typically involve collecting human preference data that ranks the quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the LM to better reflect these preferences, often via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Yet, RLHF tends to be a complicated and sometimes unstable technique, requiring the construction of a reward model based on human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to update the LM in a way that increases this reward, all while preventing significant divergence from the original model. In this work, we present a novel way of parameterizing the reward model within RLHF that permits direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, thereby allowing us to address the RLHF objective using only a straightforward classification loss. This leads to a new method, \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), which is robust, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the need for language model sampling during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter adjustments. Our results indicate that DPO is able to fine-tune LMs to align with human judgments at least as well as, and in some cases better than, traditional approaches. Specifically, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated outputs, and matches or exceeds performance in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, while being much easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on massive corpora, often develop unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Yet, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects a diverse range of intentions, preferences, and expertise. Not all of these attributes are ideal to reproduce; for instance, while it is useful for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors so that it can rectify them, we prefer the model to \emph{favor} the high-level coding proficiency—however scarce—in its training set when producing code. In the same vein, although a language model should be \textit{informed} about misconceptions that are commonly held (e.g., by half the population), we do not want the model to state such misconceptions as facts in half of the corresponding queries. Thus, carefully specifying the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its broad \textit{repertoire of knowledge and skills} is vital for developing AI systems that are reliable, high-performing, and controllable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Traditionally, existing techniques steer LMs to align with human preferences through reinforcement learning (RL), but in this paper we demonstrate that the RL-based objectives employed by prior work can be satisfied exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the entire preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, current techniques impart target behaviors to language models by utilizing carefully selected collections of human preferences that reflect the actions people consider safe and beneficial. This preference acquisition phase happens subsequent to the initial phase, where the model undergoes large-scale, unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. Although the simplest method for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning with high-quality human-generated response examples, the most effective approaches belong to the class of reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained to predict human preference scores, and reinforcement learning is used to adjust the language model’s policy to output responses with higher predicted rewards while preventing substantial divergence from the pretrained model. Despite RLHF yielding models with strong conversational and programming skills, the RLHF workflow is much more intricate than supervised learning, since it requires training several language models and repeatedly generating samples from the current policy during training, which substantially increases computational demands.

In this work, we demonstrate how a language model can be optimized directly for human preferences without the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same reward maximization objective—with a KL-divergence penalty—as conventional RLHF methods, yet remains easy to implement and train. Conceptually, DPO updates work by increasing the log odds of preferred responses over less preferred ones, while utilizing a per-sample adaptive importance weight to avoid model collapse seen with simple probability ratio objectives. Similar to prior techniques, DPO employs a theoretical preference framework (for example, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess alignment between a reward function and observed preference data. Unlike standard approaches, which leverage the preference model to craft a loss for reward model training and subsequently optimize a policy against this learned reward, DPO applies a variable substitution to directly express the preference loss in terms of the policy itself. Given a dataset containing human comparisons of model outputs, DPO can train a policy via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward derived from the preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that enables language model training from preference data without relying on reinforcement learning (RL). Through experiments, we demonstrate that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of current techniques—such as PPO-based RLHF—on preference learning tasks like sentiment control, text summarization, and conversational modeling, using models scaling up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Increasingly large self-supervised language models are capable of completing certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or by leveraging few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream applications and consistency with user intent can be substantially boosted by fine-tuning on corpora consisting of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows LLMs to adapt to instructions not encountered during fine-tuning, thereby broadly enhancing their practicality \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable success, gathering \textit{relative} human assessments of output quality is often simpler than collecting expert-annotated demonstrations. As a result, recent work has refined LLMs using datasets reflecting human preferences, leading to better performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches first train a neural reward model to align with the preference data, typically using a preference model like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then adapt the language model by optimizing for this reward through reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another related approach uses instruction-following LLMs trained with human feedback to produce synthetic preference datasets focused on specific criteria like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, often employing only minimal human input in the form of rubrics for annotating outputs. Collectively, these strategies represent an intersection between two research threads: one exploring the training of language models via reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another developing general-purpose methods for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the attractiveness of leveraging relative human feedback, applying reinforcement learning for fine-tuning large language models poses significant practical difficulties; this work introduces a principled method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond linguistic applications, policy learning from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple strategies proposed. In contextual bandit scenarios, learning from preferences or rankings over actions (as opposed to direct rewards) is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical analyses of CDBs introduce the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy that achieves at least a 50\% expected win probability against \textit{every} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDBs assume that preference feedback is provided online, whereas human preference learning typically relies on a pre-collected, static dataset of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In a similar vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates using binary preference information derived from an \textit{unknown} 'scoring' function rather than reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are numerous PbRL algorithms, some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but these typically require an explicit estimation of the hidden scoring (reward) function followed by optimization with respect to this estimate \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a unified policy learning method that directly trains a policy to align with preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). The standard procedure is comprised of three main steps: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (2) gathering preference data and training a reward model, and (3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts with supervised fine-tuning of a pre-trained LM using carefully curated data tailored to the target tasks (such as dialogue, summarization, etc.), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Phase}: In this stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and outputs answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. Human annotators are then shown these pairs and asked to indicate their preferred response, denoted as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the chosen and non-chosen completions from $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. The assumption is that preferences are governed by some hidden reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains unknown. Several methods exist for modeling such preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a standard option (more general models like the Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also applicable if multiple rankings are available). The BT model posits that human preferences $p^*$ can be modeled as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a static dataset of pairwise comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, we parameterize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Treating this as a binary classification problem yields a negative log-likelihood loss of:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized with the weights of the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, appending an extra linear layer after the final transformer block to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To stabilize the reward output, prior work normalizes rewards such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the acquired reward function serves as feedback for updating the language model. Consistent with prior research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the training objective is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\| \piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ determines how much the learned policy can diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which is usually set to the starting SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ of the language model is initialized using $\pisft$. Including this regularization ensures the model does not stray excessively from the data distribution where the reward model remains reliable, and also preserves output diversity while avoiding collapse to a handful of high-reward outputs. Since language generation operates over discrete outputs, this objective cannot be differentiated directly and is thus optimized using reinforcement learning methods. The prevalent strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} formulates the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and leverages PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} for maximization.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties faced when deploying reinforcement learning methods on large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our aim is to develop a straightforward technique for optimizing policies based directly on preference data. In contrast to previous RLHF techniques—which first train a reward function and then optimize it using RL—our method utilizes a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to obtain the corresponding optimal policy analytically, eliminating the need for iterative RL procedures. 

As we will elaborate shortly, our central idea is to employ a closed-form mapping between reward functions and their optimal policies, allowing us to reformulate an objective over reward models as an objective over policies. This change-of-variables technique dispenses with the need to separately train an explicit reward function, yet still makes use of established models of human preference, like the Bradley-Terry model. In this framework, the policy network encapsulates both the language model and the implicit reward structure.

\textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin with the standard RL objective, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, using a general reward function $r$, consistent with previous literature. Prior work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning} demonstrates that the optimal policy for the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the partition function. For a full derivation, see Appendix~\ref{app:derivation1}. Even if we substitute the reward function with its MLE approximation $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, evaluating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally challenging~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making this form difficult to implement directly. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can rewrite the reward function in terms of its associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This change of variables can be applied to both the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Conveniently, the Bradley-Terry model only relies on the difference in rewards between two outputs; that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x, y)$ into the Bradley-Terry preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition functions eliminate each other, allowing us to represent the probability of human preference solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Consequently, under the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The detailed steps can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived for the Bradley-Terry model, the same methodology extends to the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as outlined in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preferences now expressed in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward model, we can establish a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Mirroring the approach used in reward modeling (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Through this approach, we learn an implicit reward using a different parameterization, where the optimal policy is represented directly by $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because this method corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits specific theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} further explores theoretical aspects of DPO and its relationship to prior research.

\textbf{How does the DPO update work?} To gain a mechanistic perspective on DPO, it is informative to examine the gradient of the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to model parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{larger value if reward estimate is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward as defined by the model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). Intuitively, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to make the model assign higher probability to the preferred answers $y_w$ and lower probability to the less desirable ones $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is weighted according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overvalues the less preferred completion, modulated by the factor $\beta$—that is, according to how much the implicit reward model misranks the completions, taking into account the KL constraint’s influence. Our findings indicate this weighting is essential: a simpler variant lacking this coefficient can lead to poor model behavior (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The standard DPO workflow involves: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, then assigning human preference labels to assemble the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and a chosen $\beta$.  
In real applications, practitioners typically prefer to use publicly available preference datasets instead of creating new samples and collecting additional human feedback. As these datasets are generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ if it is accessible. When $\pisft$ is unavailable, we obtain $\piref$ by fitting it via maximum likelihood to the preferred responses ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, by setting ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach serves to reduce distribution mismatch between the actual reference distribution (which cannot be directly accessed) and the $\piref$ distribution applied in DPO. Additional information regarding implementation choices and hyperparameter settings can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, present theoretical justification, and compare the benefits of DPO to the limitations present in actor-critic techniques employed in RLHF, such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}  
DPO manages to avoid both explicitly modeling a reward function and carrying out RL by utilizing a single maximum likelihood-based objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} is identical to a Bradley-Terry framework where the rewards are parameterized by $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we optimize the parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$, which is equivalent to optimizing the reward model as shown in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after applying a variable transformation. In this section, we will develop the theoretical underpinnings of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the space of possible reward models, and prove that it enables exact retrieval of the optimal policy. To start, we introduce an equivalence relation on reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are called equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this forms an equivalence relation, dividing the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. The next two lemmas can then be established:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce model—and specifically the Bradley-Terry framework—any pair of reward functions belonging to the same category generates an identical distribution over preferences.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class yield identical optimal policies in the context of the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}
We omit the direct proofs for brevity and provide them in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights the well-documented under-determination problem found in Plackett-Luce models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. This ambiguity often necessitates the use of extra identifiability conditions to ensure meaningful MLE solutions for Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions within the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy. Therefore, for the purposes of our objective, it suffices to recover any one representative reward function from the optimal equivalence class. In Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, we provide a proof for the following theorem:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every equivalence class of reward functions compatible with Plackett-Luce (specifically, including Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed using the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for a certain model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ denote any reward function, which induces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$, as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We now demonstrate that some member of the equivalence class of $r$ can be written in the reparameterized form above. Define the projection $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
The operator $f$ effectively normalizes the reward function by subtracting the log-partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since this normalization only depends on the context $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ using the RHS of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward function), yields $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Hence, this projection produces a representative reward function with the desired structure and justifies the generality of our chosen reward model reparameterization.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as selecting a specific representative from each equivalence class via the DPO reparameterization, i.e., the reward function that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (non-negative and sums to 1).
Observing Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the normalization constant (partition function) of the optimal policy derived from $r(x, y)$.
The central idea of the DPO method is to impose specific constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference models in such a way that the representational capacity for reward models is preserved, while ensuring that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} is analytically computable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instability}
Our framework also enables analysis of instability issues in standard actor-critic methods applied to RLHF, like PPO. We align with the RLHF workflow, specifically considering the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. Connections can be made to the control-as-inference approach \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL problem set out in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, the goal is to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy defined by Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and induced by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. With some manipulation, this leads to the following objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective is the same as that employed in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which optimize the DPO-style reward within the reward family $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be understood as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this term doesn't influence the optimal policy, omitting it can lead to high-variance policy gradients and subsequent instability during training. One way to address the normalization term is by introducing a learned value function, though this approach can be challenging to train in practice. Alternatively, previous work has normalized rewards using a human response baseline, essentially a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization factor. In contrast, the DPO reformulation produces a reward function that eliminates the need for explicit baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess how well DPO can learn policies solely from preference data. First, in a controlled text-generation scenario, we investigate: how effectively does DPO balance reward maximization and minimizing KL-divergence with the reference policy, compared to standard preference optimization techniques like PPO? We then test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as dialogue and summarization. Our experiments show that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO often matches or surpasses strong baselines—including RLHF using PPO and selecting the best of $N$ sampled trajectories based on a learned reward model. Prior to presenting our findings, we outline the experimental design; further specifics are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation problems. Across all experiments, the various algorithms are trained to optimize a policy based on a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D} = \bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ is the beginning portion of a movie review taken from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective for the policy is to produce a continuation $y$ that expresses positive sentiment. To facilitate a controlled evaluation, for this task, we construct preference pairs for generations using an existing sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is further trained until convergence using reviews from the training subset of the IMDB dataset (additional implementation specifics in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy's role is to generate a summary $y$ highlighting the core content of the post. Adopting methodologies from previous studies, we rely on the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} and utilize human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT model used here is trained on human-authored forum summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} with the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} for RLHF. The human preference labels originate from samples generated by a different, yet similarly trained, SFT model as described by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ represents a user prompt, which could range from science questions to personal advice requests. Here, the policy is tasked with providing a helpful and engaging answer $y$; we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which consists of 170,000 conversations between people and an automated assistant. Every conversation concludes with two candidate replies created by a large (unspecified) language model, along with a label indicating which response was preferred by a human annotator. Since there is no available pre-trained SFT model in this scenario, we train a standard language model exclusively on the preferred completions to obtain the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experimental setup employs two distinct evaluation strategies. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization goal, we first consider the controlled sentiment generation context. Here, we measure each algorithm by its trade-off frontier between obtained reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy; since we have access to the true reward function (provided by a sentiment classifier), this frontier can be explicitly determined. Conversely, in practical applications where the ground truth reward function is unavailable, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy. For this, we use GPT-4 as an automated judge, acting as a stand-in for human evaluation of both summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks. In summarization, the baseline consists of the reference summaries from the test split; for dialogue, it is the preferred human response in the dataset. While prior work indicates that language models may outperform conventional metrics as evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we also run a human evaluation to validate the appropriateness of GPT-4 for this task in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show that GPT-4's assessments align closely with those of human raters, with agreement rates matching or surpassing typical inter-annotator reliability among humans.

\textbf{Approach.} Beyond DPO, we assess a range of established methods for aligning language models with human preferences. To start, we examine zero-shot prompting on the summarization task using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} and 2-shot prompting on the dialogue task using \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. Additionally, we evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model, alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a variant further fine-tuned with supervised learning on the preferred output $y_w$—sourced from the SFT model in both sentiment control and summarization, or from a generic LM in single-turn dialogue scenarios. Another supervised-like approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which adjusts the model to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while explicitly penalizing the likelihood of $y_l$, with an optional weighting factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the unlikelihood component. We further include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, using a reward signal learned from preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that optimizes with respect to the ground-truth reward in the sentiment control setting. For sentiment-related experiments, we utilize two PPO-GT implementations: a standard version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized one that incorporates reward normalization and tuned hyperparameters for enhanced results (these improvements are also applied to standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we incorporate the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach as a baseline, in which $N$ outputs are generated from the SFT (or Preferred-FT for dialogue), with the top response—determined by a preference-trained reward model—returned. While this method often yields strong results, it separates reward model effectiveness from PPO optimization and is resource-intensive, as generating $N$ completions per query during evaluation is computationally demanding even for moderate values of $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates measured by GPT-4 for the Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue task; among all methods, only DPO outperforms the selected summaries from the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across different sampling temperatures throughout the training process. DPO maintains a consistent advantage over dataset labels across various temperatures as training progresses.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective common in RLHF methods seeks to optimize reward while limiting the policy's divergence from a reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating and comparing algorithms, both the obtained reward and the KL divergence must be considered; attaining marginally higher reward at the expense of a much larger KL distance is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the tradeoff between reward and KL for different algorithms in the sentiment task. For each algorithm, we conduct several training runs, each with a distinct policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT). Altogether, this results in 22 training runs. Every 100 steps during training up to convergence, we assess each policy on a set of test prompts, measuring both the mean reward according to the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum over all time steps of KL divergences.} with the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results show that DPO achieves the most optimal frontier, delivering the highest reward while maintaining low KL divergence. This finding is striking for several reasons. First, although DPO and PPO target the same objective, DPO is significantly more efficient; the reward/KL tradeoff for DPO is superior to that of PPO. Furthermore, DPO surpasses PPO's frontier, \emph{even when PPO has access to exact reward signals} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO effective on real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess how well DPO fine-tunes models on tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, standard automatic metrics such as ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and prior studies indicate that applying PPO-based fine-tuning with human preferences leads to better summary quality. To compare various approaches, we generate completions using the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and measure the average win rate when pitted against the reference completions in the test set. For each method, completions are drawn at sampling temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, and their respective win rates are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all begin fine-tuning from the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results show that DPO attains a win rate close to 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO, which achieves around 57\% at its best sampling temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO secures a higher peak win rate than the strongest $N$-sampling baseline. It is worth mentioning that we did not conduct extensive tuning of DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting that the observed performance may not fully reflect DPO’s capability. Furthermore, we observe that DPO is significantly more stable across different sampling temperatures compared to PPO, whose effectiveness can decline to match the base GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT does not show notable gains over the SFT baseline. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also provide a direct comparison of DPO and PPO using human assessments, finding that completions from DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time when compared to PPO at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using the subset of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split, focusing on dialogues with a single exchange between human and assistant. To measure the effectiveness of different methods, GPT-4 is employed to evaluate win rates, referencing the preferred test completions. Since a standard SFT baseline does not exist for this task, we begin with the pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to fine-tune a reference model on the selected completions—ensuring outputs match the training distribution—then further train using DPO. Additionally, we benchmark against the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (having observed that the Best of $N$ metric levels off at $N=128$; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and include a 2-shot prompted variant of the base Pythia-2.8B model, observing that DPO matches or outperforms these alternatives at optimal temperature settings. We also include evaluation of an RLHF model, fine-tuned with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} and sourced from a recognized implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but could not identify a prompt or temperature setting where it surpasses base Pythia-2.8B performance. Drawing on our findings from TL;DR and acknowledging that both Best of 128 and PPO maximize the same reward signal, we use Best of 128 as a rough stand-in for PPO-level outcomes. In summary, DPO stands out as the only efficient method that reliably exceeds the preferred completions from the Anthropic HH dataset, delivering results on par with or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains peak performance after relatively few training steps.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward training method for leveraging preferences to train language models without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning problem into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be optimized to match human preferences \textit{directly} using a basic cross-entropy loss, with no need for reinforcement learning or a loss in generality. DPO achieves performance on par with or surpassing current RLHF techniques, including those using PPO, all while requiring little to no hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO substantially lowers the difficulty of training language models guided by human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings highlight several key avenues for further investigation. One open question is how the DPO policy performs in out-of-distribution scenarios compared to approaches that learn from explicit reward functions. Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies may achieve similar generalization as PPO-based systems, but a thorough exploration is required. For instance, could leveraging self-labeling techniques with DPO policies facilitate effective utilization of prompts without labels? Another aspect to consider is the occurrence of reward over-optimization within direct preference optimization and whether the minor dip in performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is indicative of this issue. Moreover, although our experiments cover models up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to much larger, cutting-edge architectures presents an intriguing prospect for future research. On the evaluation side, we observe that GPT-4-derived win rates are influenced by the phrasing of prompts; thus, determining optimal strategies to obtain reliable automated system judgments warrants further study. Finally, DPO has the potential for numerous applications beyond preference-based language model training, such as optimizing generative models in various other domains.
