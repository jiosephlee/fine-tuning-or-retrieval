\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive general knowledge and develop some reasoning abilities, yet exerting fine-grained control over their outputs remains challenging due to the purely unsupervised setup of their training regimes. Current strategies for enhancing controllability involve gathering human feedback that ranks generated outputs, followed by fine-tuning the base LM to match these preferences—frequently employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nonetheless, RLHF introduces significant complexity and instability: it typically requires fitting a reward model that captures human judgements, and subsequently adjusts the original LM through reinforcement learning to optimize this reward, while avoiding excessive deviation from the pre-trained model. In this work, we present a novel reward model parameterization within the RLHF framework, which allows for direct, closed-form retrieval of the optimal policy, effectively reducing the RLHF optimization to a straightforward classification objective. We term this method \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO). DPO is robust, effective, and resource-efficient, dispensing with the need for LM sampling during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Through empirical studies, we demonstrate that DPO fine-tunes LMs to reflect human preferences at least as well as, and often better than, existing alternatives. Remarkably, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF approaches in regulating generational sentiment, and achieves equal or superior results in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, all while being much easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recent advancements have shown that large unsupervised language models (LMs), when trained on massive datasets, develop a range of unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, such models are exposed to human-generated data reflecting diverse objectives, values, and levels of expertise. Inevitably, certain human tendencies embedded in this data may not be appropriate to emulate. For instance, while it is beneficial for an AI-based coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors to address them, it remains essential to orient the model’s outputs toward the (perhaps uncommon) instances of superior programming skills in the dataset. In a similar vein, we may wish for our language model to \textit{know} about widespread misconceptions, but we do not want it to propagate these inaccuracies proportionally to their prevalence in the data—such as asserting a fallacy as true in half the queries if half of people believe it. Therefore, isolating and promoting the \emph{preferred behaviors and outputs} from the model’s broad \textit{range of knowledge and competences} is fundamental to ensuring AI systems are reliable, effective, and manageable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although reinforcement learning (RL) has typically been used to align LMs with human preferences, we demonstrate that the RL-based objectives commonly employed can, in fact, be precisely optimized via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thereby streamlining the preference learning workflow.

Broadly speaking, current techniques shape LMs to exhibit desirable behaviors by leveraging handpicked datasets of human judgments that encapsulate safe and useful conduct. This step of preference learning is conducted following the initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training phase. While a direct strategy for preference adaptation consists of supervised fine-tuning using examples of high-quality human responses, reinforcement learning from human or artificial feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep, bai2022constitutional}) has emerged as the most effective approach. RLHF methods begin by training a reward model on datasets of human preferences, and subsequently use RL to modify the LM’s policy, guiding it to favor high-reward outputs while preventing excessive divergence from the pre-trained model. Despite the notable conversational and programming prowess achieved through RLHF, its procedural complexity surpasses that of supervised learning, since it requires the training of multiple LMs and online policy sampling during optimization, resulting in substantial computational demands.

This work introduces a method for training language models to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward models or reinforcement learning frameworks. We introduce \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO)}, a technique that inherently targets the same objective as standard RLHF approaches—maximizing reward while maintaining a KL-divergence constraint—but does so via a conceptually and practically simpler procedure. At its core, DPO enhances the log-likelihood ratio of preferred over non-preferred outputs, modulating this shift through a per-sample, adaptive importance weighting scheme. This mechanism circumvents the model collapse observed when using a straightforward probability ratio objective. DPO, like prior methods, makes use of a formal preference structure (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess alignment between a reward function and observed human choices. Yet, unlike the prevailing two-stage pipeline—first optimizing a reward model via preference loss, then improving a policy to maximize this learned reward—DPO leverages a variable substitution that directly frames the preference loss in terms of the model policy. Thus, with access to a dataset of human-labeled preferences on model outputs, DPO enables optimization of the model policy via a basic binary cross-entropy loss, implicitly aligning the policy with a reward structure inferred from human preferences.

The principal contribution of this paper is the introduction of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), offering an RL-free, streamlined approach to preference-based language model training. Empirical evaluation demonstrates that DPO matches or exceeds the performance of methods such as PPO-based RLHF on preference-driven benchmarks—spanning sentiment control, summarization, and conversational modeling—across models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Progressively larger self-supervised language models have demonstrated the capacity to perform certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or with minimal in-context examples \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user intent can be markedly enhanced by fine-tuning them on corpora comprising explicit instructions and human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' empowers LLMs to extrapolate to instructions not explicitly encountered during training, thereby broadening their applicability and usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has yielded considerable improvements, obtaining \textit{relative} human assessments of output quality is often more feasible than collecting expert-level demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has further refined LLMs using datasets derived from human preference judgments, resulting in gains across tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction compliance \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically involve first training a neural reward model to predict human preference patterns, often leveraging models like Bradley-Terry \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then optimizing the language model to increase this reward using reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their adaptations \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Related research exploits instruction-following LLMs, further refined via human feedback, to automatically synthesize preference data targeting specific properties such as safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, requiring only weak forms of human supervision like rubric-based annotation. Altogether, these developments integrate two research streams: reinforcement learning for language model training on diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and general strategies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. While learning from relative human preferences offers distinct advantages, the process of fine-tuning LLMs via reinforcement learning is operationally complex; this work introduces a theoretically grounded method to optimize relative preferences without resorting to RL.

Beyond the domain of language, learning policies based on preferences has been a subject of investigation in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, yielding a variety of methods. One notable line of work is contextual bandit learning, where preferences or rankings over actions, instead of scalar rewards, guide learning; this setting is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When explicit rewards are unavailable, the theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the conventional optimal policy with a concept known as the \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose average probability of outperforming any other policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Unlike human preference learning, where feedback generally consists of a static dataset of annotated preference pairs \citep{yan2022human}, CDB scenarios deliver preference information in an online fashion. Likewise, in \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL), learning is driven by binary preference feedback derived from an opaque ‘scoring’ function, rather than observable rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. PbRL approaches typically require estimating this latent reward function from collected preferences before proceeding with policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}, and some can leverage off-policy preference data. In contrast, we introduce a unified policy learning methodology that directly trains policies to accommodate stated preferences without explicitly modeling the underlying reward.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We begin with an overview of the RLHF process as introduced in \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (see also \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}), which generally unfolds in three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), 2) gathering preference data and reward model training, and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: The RLHF workflow typically initiates with supervised fine-tuning, adapting a pre-trained language model to specific tasks (e.g., conversation, summarization) through high-quality labeled datasets, yielding the model $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: During the reward modeling phase, the fine-tuned model $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is given prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. Human annotators are then asked to select the answer they prefer, resulting in preference annotations $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the chosen response and $y_l$ the less preferred one. These human preferences are presumed to be driven by an unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$, to which we do not have direct access. Several methods exist to capture such preference structures, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being widely used (the framework is also compatible with more expressive Plackett-Luce models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} if multiple rankings per prompt are available). In the BT formalism, the probability distribution over human preferences $p^*$ takes the form:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given a fixed dataset of comparison pairs $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, we can parameterize a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences. By viewing the task as a binary classification problem, the associated negative log-likelihood objective is defined as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. For large language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$, and augmented with a final linear projection that outputs a scalar reward for each input pair~\cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To promote stability and reduce reward variance, it is common practice to normalize the output so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward model provides feedback signals to the language model. As suggested by previous studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the objective is formulated as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

with $\beta$ serving as a regularization coefficient that controls divergence from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, usually corresponding to the original SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The policy $\pi_\theta$ is generally initialized from $\pi^\text{SFT}$ as well. This regularization term is crucial: it helps ensure that the learned policy stays within the regime where the reward model is reliable, preserves sample diversity, and guards against collapse to a small set of high-reward outputs. Since text generation involves discrete choices, this optimization cannot be performed via direct backpropagation and is typically solved using reinforcement learning techniques. The prevailing method~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} constructs the reward function as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$, and maximizes it using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Recognizing the practical difficulties of scaling reinforcement learning algorithms for language model fine-tuning, we aim to develop a more direct optimization strategy that operates using preference data. Unlike traditional RLHF approaches which explicitly learn a reward model and subsequently optimize it via reinforcement learning, our method utilizes a specific reward parameterization that admits a closed-form expression for the associated optimal policy—thereby eliminating the need for an RL training loop. As detailed in the following section, our core contribution is an analytic mapping from rewards to optimal policies, which allows us to convert losses over reward models into equivalent losses over policies. This variable substitution technique obviates the necessity to fit an explicit reward model, yet still faithfully captures preference modeling frameworks such as the Bradley-Terry model. Thus, the policy network itself serves both as the generator and as

\textbf{Reformulating the DPO Objective.} We begin from the standard RL objective introduced in previous literature, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, considering a generic reward function $r$. Consistent with prior analyses~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily established that the policy which optimizes the KL-regularized reward maximization problem, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, adopts the structure:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

with $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ representing the partition function. The full derivation appears in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when substituting the reward estimator $r_{\phi}$ for the true underlying reward $r^*$, directly computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally prohibitive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, presenting practical obstacles for this approach. Nevertheless, by manipulating Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can re-express the reward as a function of its optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, applying the logarithm to both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging gives:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This change of variables can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, in the Bradley-Terry framework, the probability model is only dependent on differences between the rewards of two candidate completions, specifically, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the reparameterized reward from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference probability, Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, eliminates the partition function, enabling us to express the human preference probabilities solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Therefore, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$, given the Bradley-Terry model, fulfills:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

The derivational details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is formulated using the Bradley-Terry model, similar results hold for broader classes of preference models, such as the Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as described in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the likelihood of observed human preferences as a function of the optimal policy, rather than the explicit reward, we are now equipped to define a maximum likelihood estimation objective for a policy parametrization $\pi_\theta$. Drawing a parallel with reward modeling objectives (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), we arrive at the policy optimization objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

In this framework, we learn an implicit reward function through an alternative parameterization, wherein the optimal policy directly corresponds to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since this method is mathematically equivalent to optimizing a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees—such as consistency, provided the preference data adheres to certain assumptions \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate on further theoretical implications of DPO and contextualize it with respect to related approaches in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update function?} To gain an intuitive, mechanistic perspective on DPO, we examine the gradient of the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The derivative with respect to $\theta$ can be expressed as:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater influence when the reward assessment is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{promote probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

Here, the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y)$ is defined as $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, linking the current language model $\pi_\theta$ and a fixed reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for further discussion). The intuition behind the loss gradient is that it acts to boost the likelihood of preferred outputs $y_w$ while suppressing the likelihood of less preferred ones $y_l$. Crucially, the contribution of each training example is weighted by how much the model’s reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly favors the less desirable completion $y_l$, modulated by $\beta$, which incorporates the impact of the KL regularization. Empirical results highlight the necessity of this weighting; omitting it—such as in a simplified variant—can lead to collapse or unwanted behaviors in the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.} The standard DPO workflow involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$, then annotate them using human preferences to assemble an offline dataset of preference pairs, $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) Train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\pi_\text{ref}$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the chosen $\beta$. In actual practice, it is preferable to leverage existing public preference datasets rather than creating new samples and obtaining fresh human preference labels. Given that such datasets are often derived from sampling with $\pi^\text{SFT}$, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ when it is accessible. If $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is unavailable, we instead fit $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, setting ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach serves to reduce distributional mismatch between the true (but inaccessible) reference distribution and the proxy $\pi_\text{ref}$ utilized in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter choices are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO}
In this section, we further elucidate the DPO methodology, provide a formal theoretical foundation, and illustrate how DPO addresses limitations encountered by actor-critic methods commonly used in RLHF, such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} 
DPO enables direct policy learning by optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective, thus sidestepping the need for explicit reward modeling and separate RL-based policy optimization. In particular, the objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry model parameterized by a reward $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, and the training of the parametric model $\pi_\theta$ can be understood as reward model optimization as formulated in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after a change of variables. In what follows, we develop the theoretical perspective underlying this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the space of reward models that can be learned, and prove that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We initiate the analysis by introducing an equivalence relation over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
We define two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ as equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.
\end{definition}

It can readily be verified that this equivalence relation divides the space of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. This leads to two immediate results:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce framework—including the Bradley-Terry model—any pair of reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class will result in identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    If two reward functions are members of the same equivalence class, they will induce identical optimal policies in the setting of constrained RL.
\end{lemma}

The arguments for these lemmas are direct and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-documented issue of non-identifiability in the Plackett-Luce family of preference models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it becomes necessary to enforce extra identifiability conditions to secure meaningful guarantees for MLE estimates, as discussed in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} and in prior literature \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma implies that all members of a given equivalence class yield the same optimal policy, so our estimation objective is confined to recovering a representative from the equivalence class associated with the optimal policy. The following theorem, with its proof in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this idea:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Under mild regularity conditions, every equivalence class of reward functions compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically Bradley-Terry) models can be parameterized as ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$ for some choice of $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference distribution $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which induces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. To demonstrate that a member of the equivalence class of $r$ can be constructed via the aforementioned parameterization, consider the mapping $f$ defined by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This mapping $f$ subtracts the log-partition function (which depends only on $x$) from $r(x, y)$, effectively normalizing the reward. Because the normalization term is solely a function of $x$, $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ according to Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}, which universally holds for any $r$, we find $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. This demonstrates that $f$ yields an equivalence class representative in the desired parametric form, ensuring that our proposed reparameterization incurs no loss of generality.
\end{sproof}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can also be interpreted as identifying, within each equivalence class, the specific reward function chosen by the DPO reparameterization—namely, the one for which:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

That is, $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a legitimate probability distribution, being positive and normalized over all $y$. By revisiting Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it becomes clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} serves as the normalization constant (partition function) for the optimal policy that corresponds to the reward $r(x, y)$. The central idea behind DPO is that it introduces constraints on the otherwise underdetermined Plackett-Luce (and, specifically, Bradley-Terry) preference modeling frameworks. This approach ensures that the set of feasible reward models remains unchanged, while simultaneously allowing the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to be computed in closed-form for any input $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our analytical framework also facilitates identifying sources of instability in standard actor-critic methods applied to RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF process, and concentrating on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}, one can relate this analysis to the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL formulation in \ref{eq:RL}. We posit a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seek to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy derived from Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model} and is parameterized by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After manipulating the objective, we arrive at the following maximization problem:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}

The objective optimized here is identical to those utilized in previous research 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, employing the DPO-equivalent reward for the class of $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be seen as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this normalization does not impact the optimal solution, omitting it can lead to high-variance policy gradient estimates, thereby destabilizing the training process. One method to handle this normalization is to introduce a learned value function, though this approach also introduces optimization difficulties. Alternatively, earlier studies have implemented normalization by referencing a human completion baseline, effectively yielding a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the necessity for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
Here, we conduct empirical studies to assess how effectively DPO learns policies directly from preference data. We start by considering a controlled text generation benchmark, investigating DPO’s efficiency in balancing reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization relative to the reference policy, and comparing it against established preference-learning methods such as PPO. We then examine DPO’s scalability by testing on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF benchmarks, including tasks like summarization and dialogue. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO often matches or surpasses competitive baselines such as PPO-based RLHF and even the top-$N$ trajectory selection with a learned reward model. We begin by outlining the experimental methodology; comprehensive information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We examine three distinct open-ended text generation tasks in our experimental setup. Across all tasks, the methods are trained to optimize a policy using a dataset of human preferences, denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ refers to a partial movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective is for the policy to generate a continuation $y$ that exhibits positive sentiment. To facilitate controlled comparison, we construct preference pairs for generated outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, selecting pairs where $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, the GPT-2-large model is fine-tuned on the training split of IMDb reviews until performance plateaus (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for more information). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the goal is to generate a summary $y$ highlighting the main discussion points. Consistent with previous studies, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} along with human preference annotations collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We rely on an SFT model trained on human-authored summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and apply the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF framework. The human preference data originates from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}, who curated it using outputs from a comparable, separately fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ corresponds to a user prompt ranging from scientific inquiries to personal advice. Here, the policy must craft a response $y$ that is both helpful and engaging. For this, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which comprises 170,000 human–assistant interactions. Each conversation concludes with two model-generated replies, accompanied by a human-annotated preference indicating the better response. As there is no available pre-trained SFT model in this context, we create the SFT model by fine-tuning a generic language model solely on the preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We adopt two distinct strategies for evaluation in our experiments. To examine each algorithm's capability to optimize the reward under constraints, we utilize the controlled sentiment generation scenario where the effectiveness is assessed through the trade-off frontier between attained reward and KL-divergence relative to the reference policy; this is feasible because the actual reward function (sentiment classifier) is accessible. In contrast, since real-world scenarios lack access to a known ground truth reward function, we instead evaluate algorithms by their \textit{win rate} when compared against a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment of summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of the reference summaries found in the test data, while for dialogue, it is the preferred response identified within the test set. While prior research indicates that LMs can outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we supplement this with a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 for evaluation, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. The results demonstrate a high correlation between GPT-4 assessments and human judgments, with the level of agreement between human evaluators and GPT-4 equaling or exceeding that among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we investigate several established methods for training language models to align with human preferences. As baseline approaches, we implement zero-shot prompting of \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. We also include the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves supervised fine-tuning on selected completions $y_w$ produced by the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general LM (for single-turn dialogue). Another approach we test is the pseudo-supervised \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural} method, which explicitly optimizes the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while simultaneously \textit{decreasing} the likelihood of $y_l$, incorporating an adjustable weight $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood component. Our evaluation further includes \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, employing a reward model derived from preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant trained using the actual reward function in the controlled sentiment context. For sentiment experiments, we experiment with two PPO-GT implementations: a standard version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and an enhanced version with normalized rewards and hyperparameter optimization for improved outcomes (these improvements are also applied to standard PPO with learned rewards). Additionally, we assess the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which entails sampling $N$ outputs from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in the dialogue setting) and selecting the top response according to a reward model trained on preference data. This method can yield strong performance by decoupling reward model quality from PPO optimization, but is computationally demanding, as it necessitates generating $N$ completions per input during inference, which is impractical for even moderate values of $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective, which is standard in RLHF methodologies, seeks to maximize rewards while simultaneously limiting how much the policy diverges from a baseline reference policy. Consequently, evaluating algorithms in this context necessitates a joint consideration of both the attained reward and the corresponding KL divergence; an approach that yields modestly better rewards at the expense of substantially higher KL divergence may not be preferable. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, we illustrate the trade-off between reward and KL divergence (the reward-KL frontier) for a range of algorithms under the sentiment task. For each algorithm, we perform several training runs, each varying the policy conservativeness parameter (using target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and different random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. Every 100 training steps during optimization, up to convergence, we assess each resulting policy on a collection of test prompts, recording both the mean reward as evaluated by the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL divergence\footnote{Computed as the total sum of per-timestep KL divergences.} between the learned policy and the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. Our analysis indicates that DPO defines a significantly more effective frontier, managing to secure high rewards while keeping KL divergence low. This is a noteworthy finding for several reasons. Importantly, while both DPO and PPO attempt to solve the same underlying objective, DPO achieves this with markedly greater efficiency; its reward/KL frontier consistently surpasses that of PPO. Furthermore, DPO establishes a superior reward-KL balance compared to PPO, \emph{even in scenarios where PPO is provided with access to ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, it is known that standard automated metrics like ROUGE may not align well with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have demonstrated that preference-based PPO fine-tuning yields more favorable summaries. To compare various approaches, we generate completions using the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset, then determine the average proportion of completions that outperform reference summaries from the test set. For each method, completions are sampled across a range of temperatures between 0.0 and 1.0; the resulting win rates are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). The models evaluated—DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT—are all fine-tuned from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our analysis reveals that DPO attains a win rate close to 61\% at a temperature of 0.0, surpassing PPO, which reaches approximately 57\% under its best-performing sampling setting (temperature 0.0). Furthermore, DPO exhibits a higher peak win rate than the best-of-$N$ baseline. It is important to mention that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not extensively tuned, potentially leading to an underestimation of its true performance ceiling. Additionally, DPO shows greater resilience to increases in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose effectiveness may decline to that of the original GPT-J model at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT yields only marginal improvements over the SFT baseline. We further contrast DPO and PPO directly via human evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, finding that DPO outputs at a sampling temperature of 0.25 were preferred 58\% of the time relative to PPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue evaluation, we assess various approaches on the portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test set that features one round of human-assistant exchange. In these experiments, GPT-4 judges use the test set's preferred completions as ground truth, measuring each method's win rate against them. Since there is no established SFT model specific to this benchmark, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT on the selected completions to obtain a reference model aligned with the data distribution, and subsequently train this model with DPO. Comparisons are made against the strongest of 128 completions produced via Preferred-FT (noting that the Best of $N$ approach achieves diminishing returns beyond 128 samples for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted variant of the base Pythia-2.8B model; DPO consistently matches or surpasses the best temperatures used in these baselines. Additionally, we include an RLHF model—trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} and sourced from a reputable implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}—yet, we are unable to identify prompts or sampling temperatures that outperform the original Pythia-2.8B. Considering results from TL;DR and the shared reward objective, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate measure for PPO-level effectiveness. In summary, DPO stands out as the only efficient approach that exceeds the Anthropic HH dataset's preferred completions, achieving comparable or superior results to the more resource-intensive Best of 128 method. Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} further illustrates DPO’s rapid convergence toward peak performance.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To further investigate how PPO and DPO handle distributional changes, we assess the generalization of policies trained in the Reddit TL;DR summarization task by testing them on a distinct data domain: news articles from the test partition of the CNN/DailyMail dataset \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}. We employ the optimal sampling temperatures identified for TL;DR (0 and 0.25) during this evaluation. The corresponding outcomes are reported in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. We used GPT-4 to determine the win rate of model-generated summaries relative to gold-standard dataset summaries, applying the same GPT-4 (C) evaluation prompt as for Reddit TL;DR, but substituting “forum post” with “news article.” On this novel input distribution, DPO consistently achieves superior performance over the PPO policy. These findings suggest that DPO exhibits generalization capabilities at least on par with PPO, despite DPO’s lack of access to the extra unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts utilized in PPO training.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the validity of GPT-4’s evaluation, we conduct a human annotation study using the TL;DR summarization results and two versions of the GPT-4 prompt. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt directly requests an assessment of which summary best captures the essential information of the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally inquires about the conciseness of the summary; this distinction is important as we observe a tendency for GPT-4, when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, to favor more verbose and redundant summaries than do human annotators. Detailed prompt text is included in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Our analysis covers three comparison pairs spanning a range of sample qualities: the top-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing (PPO, temp. 1.0), and an intermediate method (SFT, temp. 0.25), each compared against the PPO policy sampled greedily (its optimal temperature). Results reveal that, for both prompts, GPT-4’s preferences align with those of human raters to a similar extent as the agreement among different human annotators themselves, indicating that GPT-4 is a reasonable stand-in for human judgment (though for DPO and PPO-1, multiple human annotations were collected, limited by the number of available raters). Overall, we observe that the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt produces win rates more in line with human evaluations; thus, we use this prompt as the standard for the principal results in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further methodological details, including a description of the rater interface and a list of participants, are available in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach to developing language models that are both effective and aligned with human values. In this work, we proposed DPO, a straightforward methodology for learning from preferences that bypasses reinforcement learning. Unlike conventional methods that recast preference learning into the RL domain to take advantage of existing RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This mapping facilitates direct alignment with human preferences via a standard cross-entropy objective, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and preserving expressiveness. Notably, DPO achieves competitive or superior performance compared to prevalent RLHF techniques, such as those using PPO, while requiring minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO considerably lowers the complexity of preference-based language model training.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} The findings from this study open multiple avenues for further exploration. One pertinent question is the extent to which the DPO-trained policy generalizes to novel data, particularly in comparison to models trained with explicit reward functions; preliminary evidence indicates DPO generalizes on par with PPO, but extensive validation is necessary. Further investigation is also warranted into whether techniques such as self-labeling via the DPO policy can efficiently leverage unannotated prompts. Another important area of inquiry is the nature of reward over-optimization within the DPO framework and whether the modest dip in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right is indicative of such effects. Furthermore, although our evaluation includes models with up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to models with substantially greater capacities remains an intriguing challenge. Regarding assessment, we observe that GPT-4-based win rates are sensitive to the prompting strategy; future research could examine optimal protocols for eliciting reliable automated judgments. Lastly, DPO presents opportunities for broader application, extending beyond language models to the training of generative models across various modalities.
