\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, it remains challenging to precisely direct their outputs because their training is entirely unsupervised.  
Current approaches to improve the controllability of LMs typically gather human judgments about the comparative quality of model outputs and then further train the LMs to reflect these preferences, often leveraging reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).  
Yet, RLHF is intricate and can be unstable, as it involves first training a reward model to capture human preferences and then using reinforcement learning to adjust the original unsupervised LM, maximizing the reward while trying to avoid significant divergence from the initial model.  
We present a novel reward model parameterization within the RLHF framework, enabling direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form and allowing the traditional RLHF objective to be solved through a straightforward classification loss.  
The new approach, which we term \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is robust, efficient, and resource-friendly, removing the need for language model sampling during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter adjustment.  
Empirically, we demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to better align with human preferences, matching or surpassing the effectiveness of established techniques. Specifically, DPO-based fine-tuning outperforms PPO-style RLHF in controlling output sentiment and equals or improves summary and dialogue response quality, all while being much simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on massive corpora, demonstrate unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-produced data, which reflects a diverse array of intentions, values, and competencies. Not all of these human characteristics are appropriate for our purposes; for instance, we want an AI programming assistant to \textit{recognize} common coding errors so it can correct them, but when producing code itself, the model should prioritize the (sometimes infrequent) high-level programming proficiency captured within its training set. Analogously, it is useful for an LM to \textit{know about} widespread misconceptions (even if half of the population believes them), but the model should not propagate these errors by presenting them as true half of the time in response to related questions. Thus, determining the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from its extensive \textit{repository of knowledge and skills} is fundamental for constructing AI systems that are reliable, effective, and manageable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current techniques usually align LMs with human values using reinforcement learning (RL), we will demonstrate that the RL-based objective prevalent in these approaches can actually be solved exactly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which substantially streamlines the preference learning workflow.

Broadly speaking, current approaches shape language model behavior by leveraging carefully selected datasets of human preferences that reflect actions people consider safe and useful. This preference learning phase follows an initial period of extensive unsupervised pre-training on massive text corpora. Although supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human-labeled examples is the simplest form of preference learning, the most effective strategies utilize reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on data capturing human preferences, and reinforcement learning is then used to guide the language model toward generating answers that receive higher reward scores, all while ensuring the model does not deviate too much from its pre-trained state. RLHF models demonstrate strong capabilities in dialogue and code generation; however, the RLHF training process is much more intricate than standard supervised learning, requiring the training of several language models and sampling from the model policy during optimization, which leads to substantial computational expense.

This work demonstrates a method for directly tuning a language model according to human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly targets the same reward-maximization objective with a KL-divergence penalty as common RLHF techniques, yet is both easy to implement and efficient to train. Conceptually, DPO modifies the log-odds in favor of preferred over non-preferred outputs, but employs adaptive, per-sample weighting to avoid the model collapse that arises with simplistic log-ratio objectives. Similar to prior methods, DPO leverages a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) for assessing the alignment between a reward function and observed preference judgments. In contrast to traditional pipelines that first train a reward model via a preference loss and then optimize a policy to maximize this learned reward, DPO reformulates the preference loss directly in terms of the policy by applying a change of variables. Using a collection of human preference annotations on model completions, DPO thus trains a policy through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, ultimately yielding the optimal policy for an implicitly defined reward that fits the preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that trains language models from preference data without using reinforcement learning. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the performance of current techniques, such as PPO-based RLHF, for preference-driven learning tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size have demonstrated the ability to solve certain tasks without prior examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or after seeing only a few examples in prompts (few-shot) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their outcomes on downstream benchmarks and their adherence to user instructions can be greatly enhanced by further training on collections of instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, referred to as `instruction-tuning', helps LLMs generalize to new instructions that are not present in the fine-tuning data, leading to improved overall utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Even though instruction tuning is highly effective, it is typically easier to gather \textit{comparative} human assessments of response quality than to obtain expert-written demonstrations. Consequently, follow-up research has adapted LLMs by training them on datasets reflecting human preferences, boosting their abilities in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, creative writing \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially train a neural reward model to be consistent with preference datasets, often relying on preference models like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then further optimize the language model to maximize this reward signal using reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or other variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Related research uses instruction-tuned LLMs refined with human feedback to create additional artificial preference data focused on specific properties like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, leveraging minimal human involvement, often just a descriptive rubric for annotation. These strategies represent an intersection between research in reinforcement learning-based training for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Although using comparative human preferences is attractive, applying reinforcement learning for fine-tuning large language models remains practically demanding; this work introduces a theoretically sound method for optimizing relative preferences that avoids the use of RL.

Beyond language-related applications, learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of multiple methods. When contextual bandit learning leverages preferences or rankings among actions instead of explicit rewards, it is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In cases where absolute rewards are unavailable, the analysis of CDBs replaces the standard optimal policy notion with the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy that secures an expected win rate of at least 50\% against \textit{every} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDB approaches typically assume online preference feedback, whereas human preference learning commonly relies on a fixed offline dataset of action pairs annotated with preferences \citep{yan2022human}. In a similar vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) uses binary preferences created by an unknown underlying `scoring' function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are a variety of PbRL algorithms, including techniques capable of utilizing off-policy preference data; however, they generally follow a two-step process—first estimating the hidden scoring (reward) function, and then optimizing for it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. By contrast, we introduce a single-stage approach that directly optimizes the policy to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF framework as presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process typically involves three stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) collecting preference data and training a reward model; and (3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by taking a pre-trained LM and applying supervised fine-tuning using high-quality data tailored to the target task(s) (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Stage}: During this stage, the SFT model receives prompt inputs $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are evaluated by human annotators, who indicate their preference, denoted as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the favored response and $y_l$ is the less preferred one from the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preferences are assumed to arise from an unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$, to which we do not have direct access. Several models can be used to represent preference data, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a common selection; more comprehensive frameworks like the Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be incorporated if multiple rankings are available. The BT model assumes the probability of human preference $p^*$ is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed set of human comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be introduced and its parameters trained by maximizing the likelihood. Interpreting this setup as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood objective can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized from the SFT policy $\pisft(y \mid x)$, appending a linear output on top of the final transformer layer to yield a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To achieve a lower variance reward function, it is standard practice to normalize $r_\phi$ such that  $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ across all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Step}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the previously trained reward model is utilized to deliver feedback to the language model. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ serves as a regularization coefficient that manages how much $\pi_\theta$ diverges from the reference policy $\piref$, specifically the initial SFT model $\pisft$. Generally, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with $\pisft$. The KL divergence term is essential, as it restricts the model from straying too far from the data distribution where the reward model is valid, while also encouraging output diversity and discouraging collapse to a narrow set of high-reward responses. Owing to the discrete outputs in text generation, the above objective is non-differentiable, necessitating the use of reinforcement learning for optimization. The prevailing method~\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves shaping the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and employing PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal} for maximization.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties inherent in utilizing reinforcement learning techniques for large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, we aim to develop a straightforward method for direct preference-based policy optimization. In contrast to conventional RLHF approaches that first fit a reward model and then perform reinforcement learning, our method employs a specific reward model parameterization that allows the direct computation of the optimal policy in closed form, bypassing the need for an RL training loop.

As we elaborate below, our central idea involves using an analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies. This enables us to reformulate an objective originally defined on reward functions as an objective over policies. By leveraging this change-of-variables strategy, we eliminate the need for training an explicit reward model, while still optimizing in line with standard human preference frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model. In this setup, the policy network inherently embodies both the language model and the implicit reward function.

\textbf{Deducing the DPO Objective.} We begin with the standard RL objective outlined in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, utilizing a generic reward function $r$. As demonstrated by previous research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the solution that optimizes the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where the normalizing constant $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ ensures $\pi_r$ is a valid distribution. For full details, refer to Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when adopting the MLE approximation $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, the computation of the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally demanding~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, complicating the use of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} in practice. To address this, we can rewrite Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to solve for the reward as a function of the associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the partition function $Z(\cdot)$. By taking the logarithm on both sides and rearranging terms, we get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This transformation can also be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and its optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry framework relies only on reward differences between two outputs, namely, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By plugging the parameterization from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference expression Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing us to write the preference likelihood purely in terms of $\pi^*$ and $\piref$. Therefore, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry model satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
A step-by-step derivation can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} employs the Bradley-Terry preference model, analogous formulations for the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having expressed the human preference probabilities in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can now define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In a similar fashion to the reward model-based method (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the objective for our policy is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

Through this formulation, we effectively learn an implicit reward via an alternative parameterization, where the corresponding optimal policy is $\pi_\theta$ itself. Additionally, since our method amounts to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits several theoretical guarantees, including consistency under reasonable assumptions regarding the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides a deeper theoretical comparison between DPO and related approaches.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic intuition for DPO, it helps to study the gradient of its loss function, $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ takes the form:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward ordering is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{push up probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{lower probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward implicitly computed by the language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Essentially, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ serves to boost the probability of preferred answers $y_w$ while suppressing the probability of less desirable ones $y_l$. Crucially, each training instance is weighted by the degree to which the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ ranks the dispreferred $y_l$ higher than the preferred $y_w$, modulated by $\beta$—reflecting how much the reward model misorders the responses and integrating the effect of the KL constraint. Our results indicate that this weighting is vital: if the coefficient is omitted, as in a simpler version of the approach, the language model’s outputs can degrade (refer to Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The standard DPO process involves the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, and annotate them with human preference labels to build an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the DPO objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to the selected $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the target $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, it is preferable to make use of publicly released preference datasets rather than creating new samples and collecting human feedback. Because such datasets are typically generated using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is not accessible, we instead define $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, that is, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)]}$. This approach helps address the distribution mismatch between the ideal reference distribution (which is unavailable) and the practical $\piref$ adopted in DPO. Additional implementation details and hyperparameter choices are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper analysis of the DPO approach, establish its theoretical foundations, and discuss how the strengths of DPO address challenges found in actor-critic methods commonly applied to RLHF, including PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model}  
DPO avoids the need to explicitly fit a reward function or apply reinforcement learning by relying on a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} mirrors a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, our goal is to train the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, which is functionally the same as optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} under a variable transformation. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it places no limitations on the types of reward models we can learn, and prove it enables exact retrieval of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$. 
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. We can now present the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry framework, any two reward functions belonging to the same class generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class will produce identical optimal policies in the constrained RL framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and are postponed to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a widely recognized under-specification phenomenon inherent to the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. As a result of this ambiguity, it is customary to introduce identifiability conditions to ensure meaningful MLE solutions in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma indicates that every reward function within the same equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy. Therefore, for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any representative reward function from the optimal equivalence class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward equivalence class consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and in particular, Bradley-Terry) models can be captured via the parameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as specified in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that some reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be written in the above form. Define the mapping $f$ by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ acts by normalizing the reward function with the log-partition function associated with $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on the prefix $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which universally holds for any reward), we observe that $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ yields an equivalent reward with the desired structure, and the reparameterization in our reward model entails no loss of generality.
\end{sproof}
Another perspective on Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward, within each equivalence class, is chosen by the DPO reparameterization—specifically, the reward function that fulfills:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning $\pi(y\mid x)$ defines a proper probability distribution (i.e., positive values summing to 1).
Moreover, following Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy dictated by $r(x, y)$.
The central observation behind the DPO method is that, by imposing specific constraints on the under-determined Plackett-Luce (and especially Bradley-Terry) preference models, we retain the full expressiveness of the reward model class while ensuring the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically computable for all inputs $x$.

\subsection{Instability Issues in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our framework also allows us to analyze sources of instability in commonly used actor-critic methods for RLHF, such as PPO. Mirroring the RLHF pipeline, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. This approach can be related to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} when addressing the constrained RL problem specified in \ref{eq:RL}. We consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and attempt to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and is determined by the reward model $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. With some manipulation, this leads to the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}
This objective is equivalent to those optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, utilizing the DPO-equivalent reward within the $r_{\phi}$ reward family. Here, the normalization term within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not impact the optimal solution, omitting it leads to policy gradients with potentially large variance, resulting in unstable training. To manage this normalization term, one can introduce a learned value function, but optimizing it presents additional challenges. Another approach, used in previous work, normalizes the rewards using a baseline based on a human completion, which effectively acts as a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward signal that is baseline-free and avoids these normalization issues.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we assess the effectiveness of DPO for training policies directly based on preference data. We begin by investigating, in a controlled text generation context, how well DPO balances reward maximization with maintaining low KL-divergence from a reference policy, and compare its efficiency to standard preference learning approaches like PPO. We then examine DPO’s capabilities on larger-scale models and more complex RLHF challenges, such as tasks in summarization and dialogue. Our findings indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as approaches that select the best of $N$ generated sequences according to a learned reward model. Before discussing these findings, we outline our experimental methodology; further information can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We evaluate our methods on three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. In every setting, algorithms derive a policy from a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents the beginning of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective is to generate $y$ expressing positive sentiment. To enable systematic evaluation, we synthesize preference pairs for generations using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we fine-tune GPT-2-large on IMDb training split reviews until the model converges (details provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the case of \textbf{summarization}, $x$ corresponds to a Reddit forum post, and the policy is tasked with producing a summary $y$ that captures the main ideas. In line with established work, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} and human preferences compiled by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT model is trained on human-authored Reddit summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and we use the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} library for RLHF. The human preference labels originate from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} and are based on outputs from another, similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, $x$ denotes a user prompt, which might range from scientific queries to requests for personal advice. Here, the policy's goal is to generate a relevant and constructive reply $y$. We utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue corpus \citep{bai2022training}, consisting of 170k interactions between humans and AI assistants. Each dialogue concludes with two responses produced by a large (unidentified) language model, with a human-annotated label indicating the preferred response. Since there is no dedicated SFT model for this dataset, we fine-tune a publicly available language model on the preferred responses to obtain our SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experimental evaluation employs two methodologies. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization goal, we evaluate performance in the controlled sentiment generation scenario by examining each algorithm's reward and KL-divergence frontier relative to the reference policy; we are able to determine this frontier since the true reward function (a sentiment classifier) is accessible in this setting. In contrast, in practical scenarios where the ground-truth reward function is unavailable, we measure algorithm performance by their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy, using GPT-4 to approximate human judgment of summary quality and the helpfulness of dialogue responses for summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the test set’s reference summaries serve as the baseline; for dialogue, we use the responses preferred in the test data as baselines. Although prior work indicates that LMs can outperform traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we additionally conduct a human evaluation to support our use of GPT-4 as an evaluator in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results indicate that GPT-4’s assessments are highly correlated with those of human annotators, with human-GPT-4 agreement often matching or surpassing agreement among human annotators themselves.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a range of established methods for aligning language models with human preferences. For the summarization task, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, and for dialogue, we apply 2-shot prompting using \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We also analyze the \textbf{SFT} model as well as \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which undergoes supervised fine-tuning on the selected completion $y_w$—originating from the SFT model in the case of controlled sentiment and summarization, or from a standard LM for single-turn dialogue. Another supervised-inspired approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which updates the policy to encourage high probability for $y_w$ while explicitly reducing the likelihood for $y_l$; this method incorporates an optional scaling factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the unlikelihood term. We further evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} guided by a reward model trained on preference data, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle approach that optimizes against the actual reward function provided in the controlled sentiment environment. For sentiment tasks, we examine two PPO-GT variants: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a customized version that applies reward normalization and additional hyperparameter tuning to enhance outcomes (these improvements are similarly used for regular PPO with a learned reward signal). Lastly, we test the \textbf{Best of $N$} strategy, which involves generating $N$ outputs from either the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue scenarios) and selecting the top-scoring completion based on a reward model trained on preference annotations. This robust technique separates reward model performance from PPO training, though it is computationally expensive—necessitating $N$ samples per input during inference, which becomes infeasible for even moderate $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 evaluated win rates for one-step dialogue from Anthropic-HH; among all approaches, only DPO surpasses the selected summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates at various sampling temperatures throughout training. The advantage of DPO compared to dataset labels remains consistent at different temperatures during training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained objective for reward maximization, commonly employed in RLHF algorithms, aims to optimize reward while simultaneously limiting how much the policy diverges from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating and comparing different algorithms, it is important to consider both the attained reward and the associated KL divergence; a modest reward improvement at the cost of significantly greater KL divergence is not always advantageous. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} depicts the trade-off between reward and KL for various algorithms in the sentiment domain. For each algorithm, we conduct several training runs, each with a different policy conservativeness parameter (target KL values $\in \{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05, 0.1, 1, 5\}$ for DPO, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, as well as varying random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. At intervals of 100 training steps, up to convergence, we assess every policy on a collection of test prompts, calculating both the mean reward according to the true reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, this refers to summing the KL-divergence at each timestep.} with respect to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our results show that DPO consistently yields the most favorable reward-KL frontier, achieving higher rewards while maintaining low KL values. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. Firstly, both DPO and PPO are optimizing the same objective, yet DPO is markedly more effective; its reward/KL trade-off entirely supersedes that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO in terms of the reward-KL frontier, \emph{even when PPO is provided access to true rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess how well DPO fine-tunes on tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, standard automated metrics like ROUGE often do not correlate well with human preferences~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have shown that leveraging PPO to fine-tune language models based on human judgments results in superior summaries. Our evaluation involves generating completions on the TL;DR summarization dataset’s test set and measuring the mean win rate versus reference summaries. For each method, we sample completions with temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, and display the win rates in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. At a sampling temperature of 0.0, DPO attains a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO’s approximately 57\% win rate at its optimal temperature. DPO also surpasses the best performance of the $N$ baseline in terms of maximum win rate. It’s important to mention that DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter was not thoroughly tuned, implying these results might not fully reflect DPO’s possible performance. Additionally, DPO displays significantly more resilience to changes in sampling temperature than PPO, which can fall back to the baseline GPT-J model’s results as temperature increases. Preferred-FT, on the other hand, shows little improvement over the SFT baseline. Further, we provide a direct comparison between DPO and PPO through human evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where samples from DPO at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time compared to PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue evaluation, we assess various methods using a subset of the test split from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on interactions with a single exchange between human and assistant. The GPT-4 evaluation process uses the test set’s preferred completions as a reference to determine the win rates of each approach. In the absence of an established SFT baseline for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model on selected completions to keep outputs in distribution, and subsequently train using DPO. Our comparisons also include the top result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ metric reaches a plateau at $N=128$ for this task; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted version of the base Pythia-2.8B model. Results indicate that DPO matches or exceeds the best performance at optimal temperatures for each method. We further test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, provided by a recognized source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but we are unable to identify any prompt or temperature setting that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Given our TL;DR findings and the fact that both approaches maximize the same reward function, we treat Best of 128 as a rough stand-in for PPO-level results. To summarize, DPO stands out as the only approach that is both computationally efficient and surpasses the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving performance on par with or superior to the more resource-intensive Best of 128 approach. Additionally, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO rapidly converges to its optimal performance.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning serves as an effective and scalable method for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we proposed DPO, a straightforward approach that enables training language models using preference data, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit into a typical RL framework and employing conventional RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct relationship between language model policies and reward functions. This allows for language models to be optimized directly according to human preferences via a standard cross-entropy loss, without resorting to reinforcement learning or compromising generality. Remarkably, DPO achieves comparable or superior results to prevailing RLHF techniques, including PPO-based methods, with minimal hyperparameter tuning. Consequently, DPO significantly lowers the barrier to training language models guided by human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings bring up several key questions for subsequent research. For instance, how does the DPO policy perform in out-of-distribution scenarios in comparison to methods that learn from an explicit reward signal? Preliminary experiments indicate that DPO policies exhibit generalization comparable to PPO-based approaches, but more thorough investigation is required. Another interesting avenue is whether self-labeling using the DPO policy can similarly leverage unlabeled prompts effectively. Furthermore, it remains to be explored how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework and whether the modest drop in results shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right can be attributed to this phenomenon. While our assessments involve models with up to 6B parameters, investigating the scalability of DPO to cutting-edge models that are significantly larger offers a promising path for future studies. On the evaluation front, our analysis shows that GPT-4-derived win rates are sensitive to the choice of prompt; future work could examine optimal approaches for obtaining reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO holds potential for a wide array of applications beyond aligning language models with human preferences, such as training generative models in diverse modalities.
