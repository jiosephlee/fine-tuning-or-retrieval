\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive knowledge about the world and some inferential abilities, but exerting fine-grained control over their outputs remains challenging because their training process lacks any supervision. Current approaches to making these models more steerable involve collecting human assessments comparing the quality of different model outputs, then adapting the pretrained LM to prefer these choices, commonly via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Yet, RLHF is often intricate and prone to instability: it first requires training a reward model to represent human preferences, then uses reinforcement learning to adapt the LM so as to maximize the learned reward while maintaining proximity to the initial model parameters. This work proposes a novel reward model parameterization within the RLHF framework, which makes it possible to derive the optimal policy in closed form. As a result, the classic RLHF objective can be addressed using a straightforward classification loss function. We term the resulting approach \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), which is computationally efficient, robust, and easy to deploy—removing the necessity of sampling during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter searches. Empirical results demonstrate that DPO can align LMs with human preferences on par with, or better than, previous state-of-the-art techniques. Specifically, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and delivers comparable or superior performance in tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while offering substantial simplification in training and implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on extensive corpora have demonstrated remarkable and unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, the data used in such training originates from humans who possess diverse objectives, preferences, and competencies. Not all of these are desirable for the model to emulate; for instance, although it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} common programming errors so it can remedy them, we would ideally prefer the model to preferentially generate outputs reflective of the (potentially infrequent) high-caliber coding skills present within its dataset. In a similar vein, we may desire that the model be \textit{cognizant} of a prevalent misconception held by half the population, yet we would not want the system to assert that misconception as factual in half of the cases it is queried. Thus, it is vital to carefully select the model’s \emph{targeted behaviors and responses} from its broad \textit{repository of knowledge and skills} in order to develop AI systems that are reliable, effective, and manageable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Conventional techniques usually align LMs with human values by utilizing reinforcement learning (RL), but as we will demonstrate, the optimization target of such RL-based approaches can actually be attained precisely through a simple binary cross-entropy loss, which streamlines the preference learning process considerably.

Broadly, prevalent approaches impart preferred behaviors to language models by employing curated datasets of human preferences that delineate the qualities humans consider safe and useful. This phase of preference learning follows a preceding stage in which the model is pretrained unsupervised on a massive body of textual data. The most direct method of preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on examples of high-quality responses generated by humans, yet reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}) has emerged as the most effective methodology. In RLHF, a reward model is constructed from human preference data, and then RL is utilized to adapt the LM’s policy to favor outputs with high reward, while preventing substantial deviation from the original model’s behavior. Although RLHF leads to systems with advanced conversational and programming capabilities, its implementation is significantly more involved than straightforward supervised fine-tuning, as it necessitates training several language models and repeatedly sampling from the model policy during training, leading to elevated computational demands.

This work introduces a method for aligning a language model with human preferences through direct optimization, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO)}, an approach that inherently maximizes the same objective pursued by current RLHF frameworks—specifically, maximizing rewards under a KL-divergence constraint—yet offers greater implementation and training simplicity. Conceptually, DPO updates the model by promoting the log-likelihood of preferred outputs over less favored ones, while employing adaptive, per-sample importance weighting to avert the model collapse often observed with straightforward probability ratio objectives. Similar to previous approaches, DPO utilizes a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate the correspondence between a reward function and observed preference judgments. However, unlike traditional methods that train a reward model based on preference loss and subsequently optimize the policy with respect to this learned reward function, DPO leverages a variable transformation to reformulate the preference loss in terms of the policy itself. Using a collection of annotated human preferences over model responses, DPO directly trains the policy through a binary cross-entropy loss, resulting in an optimal policy with respect to an implicit reward function that fits the preference data.

Our principal contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm for preference-based language model training that eliminates reliance on RL. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the effectiveness of established methods, including RLHF with PPO, across a variety of preference-driven tasks such as sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Large-scale self-supervised language models have demonstrated the ability to tackle certain tasks without any examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or with just a handful of demonstrations through few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their effectiveness on downstream benchmarks and their conformity to user expectations can be notably enhanced by further training them on curated datasets consisting of instructions alongside human-authored responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This approach, known as `instruction-tuning', allows LLMs to extend their capability to handle novel instructions not present in the tuning data, thereby generally improving user experience and versatility \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has achieved considerable progress, collecting \textit{relative} human preference data regarding response quality tends to be more scalable and less demanding than gathering expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has focused on fine-tuning LLMs based on datasets reflecting human preferences, leading to enhancements across domains such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Typically, these strategies involve first adapting a neural network-based reward model to align with observed preferences—often modeled using frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—and then applying reinforcement learning methods, such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}, to adjust the language model to optimize this reward. A related research direction utilizes instruction-following LLMs, which have been refined with human input, to create new synthetic preference datasets focused on particular qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, using only minimal human oversight in the form of textual guidelines for the LLM-generated assessments. Collectively, these advances synthesize two primary research trajectories: one focused on deploying reinforcement learning to train language models for diverse targets~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and the other on generic approaches for harnessing human preferences in learning \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of leveraging relative human judgments, refining large language models through reinforcement learning introduces significant practical obstacles; this study puts forward a theoretically grounded method for optimizing based on relative preferences that does not rely on RL.

Beyond language tasks, the problem of policy learning from preferences has been explored extensively in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of various methodologies. One notable line of work is contextual bandit learning utilizing preference or ranking feedback in lieu of explicit reward signals, a scenario formalized as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Without access to absolute rewards, theoretical analyses of CDBs replace the goal of identifying an optimal policy with that of finding a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy guaranteed to win in expectation at least 50\% of the time against any competing policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. A key distinction, however, is that CDBs assume preference feedback is provided interactively and online, whereas human preference learning often proceeds with a static collection of offline, preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) methods utilize binary preference data produced by an \textit{unknown} 'scoring' function rather than relying on direct reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. PbRL encompasses a range of algorithms, including techniques for leveraging off-policy preference datasets, but these generally involve an explicit intermediate step of estimating the underlying scoring or reward function, which is then subsequently maximized \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. Contrasting with these two-stage procedures, our approach proposes a unified policy learning method that optimizes directly for compliance with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF workflow as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and later works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This pipeline generally unfolds in three main stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) sampling preferences and learning the reward; and (3) reinforcement learning for optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: The RLHF process usually commences by further training a pre-existing language model with supervised learning, employing high-quality data relevant to the downstream application (such as dialogue or summarization), thus yielding the model $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: In the subsequent stage, the SFT model is conditioned on inputs $x$ to generate pairs of candidate responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. Human annotators then provide preference judgments between these candidates, expressed as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ respectively indicate the preferred and less-preferred completions within the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. These preference signals are presumed to originate from an underlying, inaccessible reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Several statistical models exist to capture such preference data, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being among the most widely adopted. (Extensions, such as the Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, are equally applicable in cases with more extensive ranking information.) The BT model assumes the probability distribution over human preferences $p^*$ can be described as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given a fixed dataset of pairwise preferences $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$, one can introduce a parameterized reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Treating the task as binary classification yields the following negative log-likelihood objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. In language model settings, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is commonly initialized with the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$ and augmented with a linear readout atop the last transformer layer, producing a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To mitigate reward variance, it is common practice to normalize the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ across $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward function supplies evaluative feedback to the model. Following established work~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization is cast as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ modulates the regularization relative to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$—typically the original SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The policy $\pi_\theta$ is likewise initialized from $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The KL divergence term acts as a constraint, ensuring the policy remains in a regime where reward model predictions are reliable, preserving sample diversity and discouraging collapse to isolated responses. As language generation involves discrete sampling, this objective is not amenable to gradient-based optimization and is therefore tackled via reinforcement learning. Prevailing methods \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} reformulate the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$ and optimize using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}. 

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Recognizing the complexity inherent in deploying reinforcement learning at scale for language model fine-tuning, we set out to devise a more straightforward preference-based policy optimization strategy. Departing from conventional RLHF pipelines—which separately learn a reward function and then optimize it using RL—our method employs a specific reward model parameterization that allows for closed-form computation of its optimal policy, thereby circumventing the need for reinforcement learning altogether. As we elaborate below, the central idea is to use a direct analytical relationship between reward models and their induced optimal policies, translating loss functions on rewards into equivalent losses on policies. This variable substitution eliminates the requirement for a standalone, explicit reward model, while still respecting established models of human preferences such as the Bradley-Terry framework. In this formulation, the policy itself serves both as the language generator and as an implicit reward function.

\textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin our analysis with the reinforcement learning objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, associated with an arbitrary reward function $r$, consistent with previous literature. As established in works such as~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it follows readily that the solution to the KL-regularized reward maximization in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} assumes the form:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ represents the normalization constant (partition function). A full derivation is available in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. In practice, computing $Z(x)$ is computationally intractable—even with an MLE-based estimate $r_\phi$ for the true reward $r^*$—as noted by~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}. This limitation complicates the practical utility of this policy form. Nevertheless, we can algebraically manipulate Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to reformulate the reward $r(x, y)$ in terms of the associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the intractable partition function $Z(x)$. Specifically, by applying the natural logarithm to Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, we obtain:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

Applying this parameterization to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$, we note that the Bradley-Terry model’s preference structure depends solely on differences in reward between two outputs, that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the reformulated expression for $r^*(x, y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the Bradley-Terry preference equation (Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}), the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels out. As a result, the probability of human preference is now expressed entirely via the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, independent of $Z(x)$. Therefore, under the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

Further details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived for the Bradley-Terry scenario, analogous formulations can be obtained for more general models such as Plackett-Luce~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as described in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having rewritten the probability of human preference data exclusively in terms of the optimal policy, we can now define a maximum likelihood training criterion for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Mirroring the approach of reward modeling (cf. Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), we arrive at the following objective for policy optimization:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}

In this formulation, we infer an implicit reward through a different parameterization, with its optimal policy directly corresponding to $\pi_\theta$. Furthermore, our approach can be viewed as fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, which ensures certain theoretical guarantees, such as consistency when specific assumptions about the distribution of preference data are satisfied \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides additional discussion of the theoretical foundations of DPO and its relationship to related work.

\textbf{Mechanistic interpretation of the DPO update.} To gain an operational understanding of DPO, it is instructive to examine the gradient of its loss, denoted $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The derivative of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to $\theta$ is given by:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{emphasizes greater errors in reward estimation}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{promote $y_w$ likelihood} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{penalize $y_l$ likelihood}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

Here, $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ defines the implicit reward function according to the current language model $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (details provided in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient acts to make the model assign higher probability to preferred outputs $y_w$ and lower probability to less preferred outputs $y_l$. The magnitude of each update is modulated by the extent to which the model's implicit reward overvalues the dispreferred output, scaled by $\beta$—in other words, it reflects how much the reward model misranks the pair, and incorporates the impact of the KL regularization. Our empirical findings highlight that this weighting is crucial; omitting this factor, as in a simplistic variant, can lead to undesirable degeneration in the language model's outputs (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.} The typical DPO workflow proceeds as follows: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate candidate completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$ and annotate them using human preferences, thus forming an offline dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) Train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\pi_\text{ref}$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the desired value of $\beta$.  
In real-world applications, it is often preferable to leverage publicly available preference datasets rather than collect new samples and preference labels. As these datasets are typically created with $\pi^\text{SFT}$ as the sampling policy, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ when possible. In cases where $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is not provided, $\pi_\text{ref}$ is obtained by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., by setting ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This method helps address the shift between the inaccessible true reference distribution and the practical $\pi_\text{ref}$ that DPO employs. Implementation specifics and choices of hyperparameters are further discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} This section deepens our understanding of the DPO approach by offering theoretical insights, clarifying its benefits, and connecting DPO’s strengths to the weaknesses of actor-critic algorithms such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}, which are commonly used in RLHF.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO unifies reward modeling and policy learning by enabling both tasks to be addressed via a single maximum likelihood objective, avoiding the need to explicitly train a reward model or conduct RL. Specifically, the loss in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} aligns with the Bradley-Terry framework, parameterizing rewards as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. We directly optimize the parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}$, analogous to optimizing the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} after reparameterization. The theory underpinning this transformation is developed in this section, where we demonstrate that it does not limit the representational power of learned reward models and permits exact recovery of the optimal policy. We initiate this analysis by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are said to be equivalent if and only if ${r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)}$ for some function $f$.
\end{definition}

It is straightforward to verify that this relation is indeed an equivalence relation, partitioning all reward functions into equivalence classes. The following lemmas capture important consequences of this structure:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce family—specifically, the Bradley-Terry model—any pair of reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class will result in identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions from the same equivalence class induce the same optimal policy when solving the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}

Proofs for these statements are relatively direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-established ambiguity inherent to the Plackett-Luce models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. This inherent ambiguity typically necessitates the introduction of additional constraints to ensure identifiability and meaningful maximum likelihood estimation as defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that, with respect to policy optimization, all reward functions within a given equivalence class are effectively indistinguishable; consequently, for our purposes, it suffices to identify any representative of the optimal equivalence class. In Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, we formally establish the following result:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild regularity conditions, every reward equivalence class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (including the Bradley-Terry) model can be expressed using the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$ for some distribution $\pi(y\mid x)$ and reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be an arbitrary reward function that leads to a corresponding optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to demonstrate that some reward function within $r$'s equivalence class can be written in the reparameterized form above. Define the following projection operator:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes $r(x, y)$ by subtracting the log-partition function (over $y$), which depends solely on $x$; thus, $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ still lies in the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. If we substitute $r$ according to Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}—a relation holding for any reward function—we obtain $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, the projected reward $f$ belongs to the equivalence class of $r$ and has the required reparameterized form. This demonstrates that our choice of parameterization incurs no loss of generality.
\end{sproof}

An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it characterizes the precise representative reward function chosen by the DPO reparameterization within each equivalence class. Specifically, the selected reward function fulfills the following condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (all probabilities are non-negative and sum to one). Examining Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it becomes apparent that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function associated with the optimal policy derived from the reward $r(x, y)$. The central insight underpinning the DPO algorithm lies in the realization that, by imposing suitable constraints on the underdetermined Plackett-Luce preference model family (including the Bradley-Terry model), it is possible to retain the expressiveness of the reward model class, while ensuring that the optimal policy of Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains computationally tractable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework additionally allows for the analysis of sources of instability in standard actor-critic methods commonly used in RLHF pipelines, such as PPO. Here, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. The connections to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, as applied to the constrained RL objective stated in \ref{eq:RL}, become apparent. By parameterizing the model as $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and seeking to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy constructed from the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$ (see Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model}), one arrives—after algebraic manipulations—at the following optimization criterion:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

The same objective has been optimized in previous studies 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, employing the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this framework, the normalization factor within $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be viewed as representing the soft value function of the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this normalization does not alter the set of optimal solutions, omitting it can result in policy gradients with significant variance, thereby destabilizing the training process. While it is possible to account for the normalization term by learning a value function, this approach often poses optimization challenges. Prior literature has also addressed this issue by normalizing rewards relative to a human completion baseline, effectively using a single-sample Monte Carlo estimation of the normalization constant. The DPO reparameterization, however, produces a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
This section presents empirical studies assessing DPO’s effectiveness in training policies based solely on preference data. Our initial experiments, conducted in a controlled text-generation context, investigate how well DPO balances the trade-off between reward maximization and maintaining proximity (in KL-divergence) to the reference policy, relative to established preference learning methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we extend our evaluation to larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF domains, including tasks like summarization and dialogue. Across these scenarios, we observe that DPO typically achieves performance comparable to or better than robust baselines such as RLHF with PPO, and surpasses the outcome of choosing the best among $N$ sampled trajectories based on a learned reward model—all with minimal hyperparameter tuning. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline our experimental methodology; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We conduct experiments across three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all tasks, the methods learn a policy using a dataset of preference tuples $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents the opening segment of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the model is expected to generate a continuation $y$ with a positive sentiment. To ensure a controlled assessment, this experiment creates preference pairs by evaluating generations using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, enforcing $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is fine-tuned until it converges, utilizing training split reviews from the IMDb dataset (see further implementation details in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). For \textbf{summarization}, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy's task is to compose a summary $y$ highlighting the post’s central points. Consistent with existing literature, the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} is used, together with human preference annotations from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT baseline in this task is trained on human-generated summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} via the TRLX framework \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} to enable RLHF training. The associated human preference labels were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on outputs from a comparable, but not identical, SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ stands for a user prompt, which might range from an astrophysics query to a request for personal guidance. Here, the model must deliver a helpful and engaging response $y$. The Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}—containing 170,000 conversations between humans and a language model assistant—serves as the basis for this task. Each conversation concludes with two model-generated replies, one of which is identified as preferred by a human annotator. Since a dedicated SFT model is unavailable for this scenario, we instead create one by fine-tuning a general-purpose language model exclusively on preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We implement two distinct strategies for evaluation in our study. To rigorously assess the capability of each algorithm in optimizing constrained reward maximization, we operate within a controlled sentiment generation context, measuring each method by plotting its trade-off curve—namely, the set of achievable reward and corresponding KL-divergence from the reference policy. This is feasible since we possess the explicit reward function, realized as a sentiment classifier. Conversely, in practical deployment scenarios where the authentic reward function is unavailable, we turn to an alternative metric: the algorithm’s \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy. Here, GPT-4 is employed as a stand-in for human evaluators, judging the quality of summaries and the helpfulness of dialogue responses within summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, baseline comparisons are made against the reference summaries in the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is set by the human-preferred response annotated in the test data. Although prior work indicates that LMs may surpass traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we supplement our approach with a human evaluation study to validate our use of GPT-4 for assessment, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings indicate that GPT-4’s assessments align closely with human judgments, exhibiting levels of human–GPT-4 agreement that are on par with or surpass those seen between human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we examine a range of established techniques for aligning language models with human preferences. For the summarization domain, we utilize zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}; in the dialogue task, we apply 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We also evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model and introduce \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which is produced by fine-tuning via supervised learning on the selected output $y_w$, using either the SFT model’s output (for sentiment control and summarization) or a generic LM (for dialogue). Another relevant method is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which adjusts the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while explicitly penalizing the likelihood of $y_l$, modulated by an optional hyperparameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood loss. Further, we include \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which leverages a reward model trained from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that operates with access to the true reward function in the controlled sentiment setup. In the sentiment control experiments, PPO-GT is realized in two forms: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and a modified version that normalizes reward signals and retunes hyperparameters for enhanced efficacy (these adjustments are also employed when training PPO on learned rewards). Lastly, we benchmark the \textbf{Best of $N$} strategy, which samples $N$ candidate completions from the SFT (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) model and selects the one with the highest reward, as determined by a reward model trained on preference annotations. While this method decouples reward model performance from PPO’s optimization procedure and can yield strong results, its computational cost grows prohibitively with $N$, since every test input necessitates $N$ samples.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

In standard RLHF approaches, the objective of maximizing a reward function while imposing a KL constraint serves to encourage policy improvement without allowing excessive divergence from a reference policy. Thus, proper evaluation of different algorithms necessitates consideration of both the attained reward and the corresponding KL distance; achieving a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantial increase in KL may not be advantageous. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the trade-off between reward and KL for a range of algorithms in the sentiment domain. We conduct multiple training sessions per algorithm, each time adjusting the policy conservativeness via different hyperparameter settings (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 experimental runs. Every 100 training steps, up to convergence, each policy is evaluated using a held-out set of test prompts. For each, we calculate the mean reward based on the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined as the sum of KL-divergences over all timesteps.} relative to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. The results indicate that DPO establishes a superior frontier, consistently achieving higher rewards for lower KL values than competing methods. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. Notably, although both DPO and PPO target the same optimization objective, DPO demonstrates substantially greater efficiency; across the reward/KL spectrum, DPO outperforms PPO. Furthermore, DPO delivers a more favorable frontier than PPO, \emph{even under conditions where PPO has access to exact reward signals} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We further investigate how well DPO fine-tunes language models on tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automated metrics like ROUGE have been shown to correlate poorly with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies demonstrate that applying PPO for fine-tuning on human preferences yields more favorable summaries. To assess the effectiveness of each method, we generate model outputs using the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the mean win rate relative to reference summaries in the same split. For all evaluated models, outputs are generated across a range of sampling temperatures (0.0 to 1.0), and the corresponding win rates appear in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT each use the same GPT-J SFT model as their initialization\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our experiments show that DPO achieves an average win rate near 61\% at a sampling temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO, which achieves about 57\% at its best temperature of 0.0. Additionally, DPO attains a higher maximal win rate than the best-of-$N$ baseline approach. Importantly, since we did not conduct extensive tuning of the DPO $\beta$ hyperparameter, these outcomes might not reflect the full capacity of DPO. Furthermore, our results indicate that DPO exhibits greater robustness to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance can drop to the level of the base GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the original SFT model. For further comparison, we directly contrast DPO and PPO using human raters in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO samples generated at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over those from PPO at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue assessment, we conduct experiments on the segment of the Anthropic HH test set \citep{bai2022training} that contains a single exchange between human and assistant. GPT-4 serves as the evaluator, determining the win rate of each approach by referencing the test set's preferred completions. Since there is no established SFT baseline for this dataset, our procedure begins with the pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model. We then apply Preferred-FT to create a reference model that is fine-tuned on the selected completions, ensuring outputs remain close to the reference distribution, before proceeding to DPO training. Additionally, we benchmark against the strongest result from 128 Preferred-FT samples (noting, based on our findings, that the Best of $N$ metric stabilizes at 128 for this experiment; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompted version of the Pythia-2.8B base model. DPO consistently matches or exceeds the performance of the other strategies at their optimal temperature settings. Further comparison includes an RLHF model, which was trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}. We were unable to identify any prompt or temperature combination that resulted in performance gains over the original Pythia-2.8B model with this RLHF approach. Given the alignment between the reward functions in TL;DR and this setup, as well as previous findings, we use Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only efficient method to surpass the preferred completions baseline on the Anthropic HH dataset, and it delivers comparable or superior results relative to the more resource-intensive Best of 128 approach. Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} further illustrates that DPO achieves its optimal outcome in relatively few iterations.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To more rigorously assess how PPO and DPO perform under distributional changes, we apply the policies learned in our Reddit TL;DR summarization experiments to a distinct domain: news articles from the test set of the CNN/DailyMail dataset \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}. For this evaluation, we use the optimal sampling temperatures identified on TL;DR (0 and 0.25). Table~\ref{tab:ood} summarizes the outcomes. We estimated GPT-4’s win rate versus the ground-truth news article summaries by adapting the same GPT-4 (C) prompt employed for Reddit TL;DR—substituting “forum post” with “news article.” On this out-of-distribution task, DPO maintains a clear advantage over PPO. These findings provide preliminary support that DPO-based policies are able to generalize across domains at least as effectively as those trained via PPO, despite DPO not leveraging the extra unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts available to PPO.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the dependability of GPT-4 as an evaluator, we carried out a human evaluation based on the TL;DR summarization results, utilizing two variants of GPT-4 prompts. The first, \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple), only requests identification of the summary that better captures the essential information. The second, \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise), also factors in conciseness, as we observed that under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 tends to prefer summaries that are longer and more repetitive compared to human preferences. Full details of these prompts are included in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. For our analysis, we compare three systems—DPO at temperature 0.25 (best performing), PPO at temperature 1.0 (lowest performing), and SFT at temperature 0.25 (intermediate performance)—each against greedily-sampled PPO (its highest-performing temperature), in order to encompass a range of output quality. The analysis reveals that GPT-4's evaluations, for both prompts, are as consistent with human judgments as inter-human agreement itself, indicating that GPT-4 provides a valid surrogate for human raters (note: due to rater availability, only the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons include repeated human ratings). On balance, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that align more closely with human preferences; we therefore adopt this prompt for our principal results in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information about the human study procedures, including the online interface and the roster of raters, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning presents a robust and scalable strategy for developing language models that are both effective and aligned with user intentions. In this work, we proposed DPO, a straightforward approach for training language models from preference data without recourse to reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting preference learning tasks to fit traditional RL frameworks to leverage standard RL methods, DPO establishes a correspondence between policy distributions in language models and associated reward structures, which allows direct optimization towards human preferences using a simple cross-entropy objective—eschewing reinforcement learning entirely, with no loss in generality. DPO matches or surpasses existing RLHF techniques, such as those employing PPO, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment; as a result, DPO substantially lowers the entry barrier for training additional language models from human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} The findings in this paper open several avenues for further investigation. An outstanding question is how well DPO-trained policies generalize to new, unseen data compared to those trained with explicit reward signals. Preliminary evidence indicates that generalization under DPO is on par with that of models trained via PPO, but further, systematic evaluation is necessary. For instance, the potential for leveraging self-labeled data through DPO in the context of unlabeled prompts remains to be thoroughly examined. Furthermore, the characteristics and consequences of reward over-optimization within the direct preference optimization paradigm, including whether the minor drop in performance depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right exemplifies this issue, require closer scrutiny. While our evaluations are limited to models containing up to 6B parameters, extending DPO to models with orders of magnitude more parameters is an intriguing line of future research. In terms of evaluation methodology, we observed that the win rates assessed by GPT-4 are sensitive to prompt formulation; subsequent work might focus on optimizing prompt strategies to obtain the most reliable evaluations from automated systems. Lastly, the utility of DPO is not confined to language models—its application to the training of generative models across different data types is a promising direction to explore.
