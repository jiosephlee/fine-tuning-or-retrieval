\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, exerting precise influence over their outputs remains challenging because of their wholly unsupervised training processes. 
Current strategies to enhance steerability usually involve gathering human judgments on the relative quality of generated outputs and then adapting the unsupervised LM to conform to these preferences, most commonly via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
Nonetheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable approach, requiring the construction of a reward model that represents human feedback, followed by reinforcement learning-based fine-tuning of the original LM to maximize this learned reward while avoiding excessive deviation from the base model.
In this work, we propose a novel way to parameterize the reward model in RLHF that permits the direct derivation of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, letting us address the standard RLHF objective using a straightforward classification loss.
We name this approach \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), a method that is robust, effective, and computationally efficient—removing the necessity for language model sampling during adaptation or substantial hyperparameter searching.
Our empirical results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to follow human preferences as well as, or better than, prior techniques. In particular, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF for sentiment control, and achieves comparable or superior quality for summarization and single-turn conversation, all while being much simpler to use and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora have demonstrated unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models learn from human-generated data that reflects a diverse array of intentions, values, and competencies. Not all of these are ideal to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI code assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors in order to address them, we would ultimately prefer the model to favor the (possibly uncommon) examples of high-level coding found in its training set when producing code. Likewise, although we may wish our language model to be \textit{informed} about a misconception held by half of the population, we certainly do not want the model to assert this falsehood in half of its responses regarding the topic! Put differently, it is essential to guide the model’s \emph{preferred outputs and actions} from its extensive range of \textit{knowledge and skills} in order to construct AI systems that are reliable, high-performing, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Conventional techniques typically employ reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human judgments, but we will demonstrate that the RL-driven objective commonly used in these approaches can, in fact, be matched exactly by a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which significantly streamlines the preference optimization process.

Broadly speaking, current techniques encourage desired behaviors in language models by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences, reflecting actions that people consider safe and beneficial. This learning from preferences is performed after the initial phase of extensive, unsupervised pre-training on a massive text corpus. Although the most direct method for this stage is supervised fine-tuning on exemplary human-generated responses, the leading group of techniques is reinforcement learning from human (or AI-generated) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF approaches first train a reward model using a dataset of human preference comparisons, then apply reinforcement learning to adjust the language model’s policy, prompting it to generate answers that receive high reward scores, while preventing excessive deviation from the base model. Even though RLHF can yield models with strong dialogue and programming performance, the overall RLHF workflow is substantially more involved than supervised learning, requiring the training of multiple language models and iteratively sampling from the model policy during training, resulting in high computational overhead.

In this work, we demonstrate a method for optimizing a language model to align with human preferences without the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning techniques. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly targets the same objective as traditional RLHF approaches—maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence constraint—but is both easy to implement and straightforward to train. Conceptually, DPO adjusts the model by boosting the relative log probability of preferred outputs compared to less preferred ones, incorporating a dynamic, sample-specific importance weight that mitigates the model collapse often seen with a naive probability ratio objective. As with previous methods, DPO uses a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how well a reward function matches observed preference data. However, unlike other approaches that employ the preference model to create a preference loss for reward model training and subsequently optimize a policy for the learned reward, DPO leverages a change of variables so that the preference loss becomes a direct function of the policy itself. With access to human preference data over model outputs, DPO is able to train a policy using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, effectively learning the optimal policy for an implicit reward function inferred from the preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm for preference-based language model training that does not rely on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses current approaches, including RLHF methods based on PPO, for preference learning in applications like sentiment control, summarization, and conversational tasks, utilizing language models of up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size have shown the ability to perform some tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or with just a few prompt examples \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user objectives can be greatly enhanced by additional fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions and human-authored responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, referred to as `instruction-tuning’, equips LLMs with the capability to generalize beyond the specific instructions present in the tuning dataset and generally makes them more usable \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved impressive results, it is frequently easier to gather \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality compared to expert-labeled demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has refined LLMs using collections of human preference data, resulting in improved performance on tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, narrative generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically involve training a neural reward model to agree with the preference dataset, often using preference models like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then further optimizing the language model to maximize this learned reward through reinforcement learning algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their modifications \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A closely related research direction uses LLMs—already instruction-tuned with human feedback—to create additional synthetic preference data focused on aspects like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying on only minimal human guidance in the form of a written rubric for LLM evaluations. This family of methods unites progress from two research streams: one on applying reinforcement learning to language models for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and another on general-purpose techniques for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
Even though leveraging relative human preferences is attractive, fine-tuning large language models via reinforcement learning continues to pose substantial practical difficulties; in this work, we propose a theoretically sound alternative for optimizing relative preferences without the use of RL.

Beyond language-related applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of multiple methods. Contextual bandit learning where preferences or rankings over actions replace explicit reward feedback is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical work on CDBs replaces the standard notion of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}—a policy whose expected probability of outperforming \textit{any} alternative policy is at least 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDB settings assume that preference feedback is provided in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences typically utilizes a predetermined, offline collection of preference-annotated action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. In a similar vein, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) approaches learn using binary preferences produced by some unknown ‘scoring’ function, rather than directly using reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There are a range of PbRL algorithms, with some capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but these generally require an explicit estimation step for the hidden scoring function (or reward model) before optimizing over it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a single-stage approach to policy learning that directly tunes the policy to comply with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently by \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This pipeline typically involves three main steps: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (2) gathering preferences and training a reward model, and (3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by applying supervised fine-tuning to a pre-trained language model using high-quality datasets tailored to the target application (such as dialogue or summarization). This process yields a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Stage}: During this phase, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. Human annotators are then shown these pairs and indicate their preference, denoted $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ represent the selected and unselected completions among $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. These judgments are assumed to arise from an underlying reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is inaccessible. Several preference modeling techniques exist, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being widely used (although alternatives such as the more general Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be used when multiple ranked answers are available). In the BT formulation, the probability distribution of human preferences $p^*$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed comparison dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, one can parameterize a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and fit its parameters via maximum likelihood estimation. If we cast the problem as binary classification, the negative log-likelihood loss function becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the sigmoid function. Within the language model framework, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized with weights from the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ and augmented with a linear output head atop the final transformer layer, yielding a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. For stability and lower variance, reward outputs are commonly normalized so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the RL fine-tuning stage, the reward model that has been learned serves as the feedback mechanism for the language model. Building on previous research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the problem is cast as the following optimization:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \piref(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ determines the regularization strength relative to the reference policy $\piref$, which corresponds to the original SFT model $\pisft$.
In actual implementations, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is generally started from $\pisft$. This regularization is crucial, as it ensures the model does not stray far from the data regime where the reward function remains reliable, as well as preserves diversity in outputs and avoids collapsing to only a few high-reward responses. Since generating language involves discrete outputs, the objective function is not differentiable, necessitating reinforcement learning for optimization. The prevalent method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves constructing the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing this quantity with PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties of using reinforcement learning algorithms for large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to establish a straightforward method for policy optimization based directly on preferences. In contrast to traditional RLHF techniques that first fit a reward model and then optimize it using RL, our method employs a specific reward model parameterization that allows direct extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for an RL training procedure. 

As we will elaborate shortly, the central idea is to utilize an analytical relationship connecting reward functions to their optimal policies, allowing us to recast a loss over reward functions as a loss defined over policies. This variable substitution method circumvents the need to train a separate, explicit reward model, yet still operates within existing frameworks for modeling human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. Essentially, the policy network serves a dual role, simultaneously representing the language model and an implicit reward.

\textbf{Deducing the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective outlined in previous studies, specifically Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, for a general reward function $r$. As shown in earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the optimal policy that maximizes the KL-regularized reward objective in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serves as the normalization constant. A full proof is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. However, even if we substitute the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, calculating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practical usefulness of this policy parameterization. We can, however, rewrite Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to isolate the reward function in terms of its associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the base policy $\piref$, and the normalization term $Z(\cdot)$. This is achieved by taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging, which yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
We can utilize this change of variables for both the true reward $r^*$ and its optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model is a function solely of the reward difference between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By plugging the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the normalization constant $Z(x)$ cancels, and the probability of a human preferring one response over another can be written only in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Thus, for the Bradley-Terry preference model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ must satisfy:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
Details of the derivation appear in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is built on the Bradley-Terry framework, analogous results can be developed for broader Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With the probability of human preference data now expressed in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward modeling approach (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy learning objective is formulated as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
In this manner, we are effectively fitting an implicit reward through a different parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because this framework amounts to fitting a reparametrized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits theoretical guarantees such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} explores further theoretical properties of DPO and compares them to related methods.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain a mechanistic perspective on DPO, examining the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is insightful. The gradient with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater weight if reward estimate is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{encourage $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{discourage $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward that is implicitly assigned by the language model $\pi_\theta$ in comparison to the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works by increasing the probability of the preferred outputs $y_w$ while reducing the probability of the less-preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each training instance is weighted according to the extent to which the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the dispreferred completions, modulated by $\beta$—essentially, it measures the severity of the misranking by the reward model, factoring in the KL regularization strength. Our results show that this specific weighting is vital; omitting the weighting factor (i.e., using a simpler variant of the method) often results in degenerate language model behavior (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO summary.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, assigning human preference labels to build an offline preferences dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using the chosen $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a specified $\beta$.  
In real-world settings, it is preferable to utilize existing public preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting fresh human feedback. As these preference datasets are typically derived from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when it is available. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, that is, ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach aims to reduce the distribution mismatch between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\piref$ used within DPO. More information on implementation and hyperparameter choices is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
In this section, we offer additional insights into the DPO approach, present theoretical justification, and discuss how the benefits of DPO address shortcomings found in actor-critic techniques applied in RLHF, such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Reward Model}  
DPO circumvents the need to explicitly fit a reward function or carry out RL by instead learning the policy through a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} is identical to a Bradley-Terry model where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We then optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ in the same manner as reward model training in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, after applying a change of variables. In what follows, we develop the theoretical foundations of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the space of possible reward models, and prove that it supports exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation over reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are called equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this relation is an equivalence relation, thereby dividing the collection of reward functions into equivalence classes. The next two lemmas follow from this:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry preference model as a special case, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class yield identical optimal policies in the context of the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma highlights a well-recognized issue of under-specification present in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this ambiguity, it is generally necessary to add extra identifiability constraints to ensure meaningful guarantees for the MLE solutions in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within a single equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy. Consequently, our main goal reduces to recovering any representative reward function from the optimal class. The following Theorem is proved in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every class of reward functions that is compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and in particular, Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed using the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for a particular model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference distribution $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which defines an associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$, given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that any reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ admits the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ rescales the reward function using the logarithm of the partition function for $\pi_r$. Since the normalization depends only on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the RHS of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds for all reward functions), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ returns a reward from $r$'s equivalence class in the required format, ensuring no loss of generality when adopting the reparameterized reward model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as specifying precisely which reward function from each equivalence class is selected by the DPO reparameterization—namely, the reward function that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a valid probability distribution (with nonnegative probabilities summing to one).
Furthermore, applying Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we see that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function for the optimal policy defined by the reward $r(x, y)$. 
The main idea behind the DPO algorithm is to introduce specific constraints to the otherwise underdetermined Plackett-Luce (and in particular, Bradley-Terry) preference models, so that we retain the full expressivity of the reward model class while guaranteeing that the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically solvable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instabilities}
Our framework can also be applied to analyze instability issues in common actor-critic methods used for RLHF, such as PPO. Following the RLHF process, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. By relating to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL problem from \ref{eq:RL}, we assume a model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} generated by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After some derivation, this produces the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}
This objective is identical to those used in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which optimize the DPO-equivalent reward corresponding to $r_{\phi}$. In this framework, the normalization component within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function for the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not alter the optimal policy, omitting it may result in high variance in the policy gradient, potentially destabilizing training. This normalization can be accounted for by introducing a learned value function, though doing so can be challenging to optimize. Alternatively, earlier approaches have normalized rewards with a human completion baseline, effectively producing a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalizer. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we experimentally assess DPO's effectiveness in training policies directly from preference data. First, within a controlled text generation framework, we investigate how well DPO balances reward maximization and KL-divergence minimization relative to standard preference optimization methods like PPO. We then test DPO on larger models and more challenging RLHF scenarios, such as summarization and conversational tasks. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO consistently matches or exceeds the performance of strong baselines like RLHF with PPO, as well as surpasses methods that select the best of $N$ sampled outputs according to a learned reward. Prior to detailing these findings, we outline the experimental methodology; further information is available in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We examine three distinct open-ended text generation tasks in our experiments. In every case, the methods aim to learn a policy from a dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents a movie review prefix drawn from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce $y$ with a positive tone. For consistent evaluation, we \textit{create} preference pairs over generated outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. When using SFT, we train GPT-2-large on the IMDb train split’s reviews until it converges (additional information in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, with the policy tasked to output a summary $y$ capturing its main ideas. In line with previous research, we leverage the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} and preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT baseline is fine-tuned on human-written Reddit post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} library for RLHF. Human preferences were obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from samples generated by a comparable, but differently-trained, SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, $x$ stands for a human prompt—ranging from technical questions to personal advice requests. The policy’s job is to generate a relevant and useful response $y$. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, featuring 170,000 interactions between people and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two model-generated responses and a preference annotation for the response preferred by a human annotator. Since no existing SFT model is provided in this case, we create one by further training a generic language model exclusively on the preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct strategies for evaluation in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm performs in maximizing the constrained reward objective, we focus on the controlled sentiment generation scenario. Here, we measure each method by examining its trade-off between achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy—this trade-off curve can be computed because we have access to the true reward function (provided by a sentiment classifier). Conversely, since the true reward is unavailable in practical applications, we assess algorithms using their \textit{win rate} compared to a baseline policy. For this, we rely on GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgments regarding summary quality in summarization tasks and response usefulness in single-turn dialogue tasks. In the summarization setting, the baseline is the reference summaries from the test set; in the dialogue setting, the baseline consists of the preferred responses in the dataset. Although prior work indicates that LMs can outperform existing metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we additionally conduct a human evaluation to support our choice of GPT-4 for assessment, as detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings reveal that GPT-4's assessments are highly consistent with human ratings, with human-GPT-4 agreement typically matching or surpassing agreement between individual human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a range of established methods for training language models to reflect human preferences. As a baseline, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for summarization and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for dialogue. We further test the \textbf{SFT} model, as well as \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves supervised fine-tuning on the selected completion $y_w$—using completions from the SFT model for sentiment and summarization tasks, and from a general LM for single-turn dialogue tasks. Another related approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which adjusts the policy to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while explicitly reducing the likelihood of $y_l$, introducing a tunable coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the ‘unlikelihood’ penalty. We also examine \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} trained on a reward model derived from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, which is an oracle version leveraging the true reward function in the sentiment control scenario. For PPO-GT, we experiment with two variants: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version that applies reward normalization and optimized hyperparameters for improved results (these adjustments are also applied when running PPO with a learned reward model). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ outputs from the SFT (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and chooses the top-scoring answer as judged by a reward model trained on preference data. While this method effectively separates reward modeling from PPO optimization, it is not practical for larger $N$ due to the need to generate $N$ completions per query during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates for Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue, as assessed by GPT-4; among the tested approaches, only DPO outperforms the reference summaries on the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured across various sampling temperatures during training. The advantage of DPO compared to dataset labels remains relatively consistent for different sampling temperatures throughout training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-regularized reward maximization objective commonly employed in standard RLHF methods seeks to maximize reward while simultaneously constraining the policy from straying too far from a baseline (reference) policy. Consequently, in algorithm comparisons, it is essential to consider both the attained reward and the resulting KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a substantially increased KL divergence is not always preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} displays the trade-off curve between reward and KL for different methods in the sentiment analysis scenario. For each method, we conduct several training runs, varying a policy-conservativeness hyperparameter each time (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, as well as different random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 experiments. At every 100 training steps, up to convergence, we evaluate each trained policy on a collection of held-out prompts, measuring the mean reward under the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the total KL-divergence summed across all timesteps.} with respect to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO achieves a far superior frontier, obtaining the highest reward for a given KL while keeping KL low. This outcome is striking for several reasons. Firstly, both DPO and PPO target the same objective, yet DPO is significantly more efficient; its reward/KL trade-off strictly outperforms PPO. Secondly, DPO establishes a superior reward-KL curve even compared to PPO when the latter has access to ground-truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO applicable to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess how well DPO fine-tunes models on tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, it is well known that automatic metrics like ROUGE often do not align well with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and past studies have shown that models trained via PPO on human feedback can generate more desirable summaries. Our evaluation consists of generating outputs on the TL;DR summarization dataset's test split, then calculating the average win rate over reference summaries from the test set. For every method, outputs are sampled using temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0; the win rates can be found in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). All methods—DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT—fine-tune an identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results show that at a temperature of 0.0, DPO achieves about a 61\% win rate, outperforming PPO, which attains around 57\% at its best sampling temperature (also 0.0). DPO further surpasses the best-of-$N$ baseline in terms of peak win rate. It is important to highlight that we did not perform extensive tuning of the DPO $\beta$ hyperparameter, so these findings may not fully capture DPO's capabilities. Additionally, DPO demonstrates greater resilience to temperature variation compared to PPO, whose performance can drop to that of the original GPT-J model as temperature increases. The Preferred-FT approach provides little benefit over the SFT baseline. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we also report a direct human comparison between DPO and PPO: when sampling at temperature 0.25, human raters preferred DPO generations 58\% of the time over PPO generations at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on the subset of the Anthropic HH dataset’s test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on cases with just one round of interaction between human and assistant. For GPT-4 evaluation, we use the preferred test completions as references to calculate the win rates of different methods. Since a standard SFT model is unavailable for this setting, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT on the preferred completions to build a reference model so its outputs align with the distribution, and then continue training with DPO. We also benchmark against the top completion out of 128 from Preferred-FT (Best of $N$ performance levels off at $N=128$ for this scenario; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted Pythia-2.8B base model, noting that DPO matches or exceeds the strongest results across optimal temperatures for each technique. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} using resources from a widely recognized repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but do not identify any prompt or temperature settings that outperform the original Pythia-2.8B model. Given our findings from TL;DR and the shared reward optimization objective, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only method that is computationally efficient, outperforms preferred completions on the Anthropic HH dataset, and offers results on par with or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 approach. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its optimal performance in relatively few training steps.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning provides a robust and scalable approach for developing effective, aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO—a straightforward training method that enables language models to learn from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning challenge to fit traditional RL frameworks and leveraging conventional RL techniques, DPO formulates a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows us to train a model to align with human preferences \textit{directly} through a basic cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. Remarkably, DPO matches or exceeds the performance of current RLHF methods, such as those utilizing PPO, even with minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO significantly lowers the barriers to training language models from human preference data.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings open up multiple avenues for future investigation. One key question is how well the DPO policy can generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that rely on an explicit reward function. Preliminary experiments indicate that DPO policies achieve generalization on par with models trained via PPO, but further, more thorough analysis is necessary. Another interesting possibility is whether training that incorporates self-labeling using the DPO policy could utilize unlabeled prompts as effectively. Additionally, understanding how reward over-optimization appears within the direct preference optimization framework is important—for instance, is the slight performance drop seen in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right indicative of this issue? Moreover, although our evaluation includes models with up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge models with substantially more parameters remains a promising area for future research. In terms of evaluation methods, we observe that GPT-4-calculated win rates are sensitive to the chosen prompt, suggesting that future work could explore optimal strategies for eliciting accurate automated evaluations. Lastly, DPO has potential applications well beyond language model training from human preferences, such as in the training of generative models across various modalities.
