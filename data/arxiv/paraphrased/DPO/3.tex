\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning capabilities, precisely controlling their behavior remains challenging due to the entirely unsupervised nature of their training. Current approaches to achieve such control gather human annotations comparing the relative quality of model outputs and fine-tune the unsupervised LM to align with these preferences, frequently employing reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF is a complicated and often unstable process, involving first training a reward model that captures human preferences, and subsequently fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM via reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward while avoiding significant deviation from the original model. In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the reward model within RLHF that permits direct derivation of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, enabling us to address the conventional RLHF problem using only a straightforward classification loss. The resulting method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is stable, effective, and computationally efficient, removing the necessity for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter tuning. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to match or surpass existing methods in aligning with human preferences. In particular, fine-tuning via DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue, while being much simpler to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}  
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets develop unexpectedly powerful abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. However, these models learn from data created by humans who have diverse objectives, priorities, and expertise. Some of these objectives and skills might not be desirable to replicate; for instance, while we may want our AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors in order to fix them, when generating code, we prefer to steer the model towards the (possibly uncommon) high-quality coding skills present in the training set. Likewise, we might want the language model to be \textit{aware} of a common misconception held by about 50\% of people, but we certainly do not want it to assert that the misconception is true in half of the cases when queried about it! Put differently, it is vital to select the model’s \emph{preferred responses and behaviors} from its broad \textit{knowledge and capabilities} to build AI systems that are reliable, effective, and controllable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally steer LMs towards human-aligned preferences via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL objective used can be exactly optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, significantly simplifying the preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, existing techniques instill desired behaviors into a language model by leveraging carefully curated human preferences that capture the types of behaviors considered safe and beneficial by people. This preference learning phase follows an initial large-scale unsupervised pre-training on extensive text corpora. While a direct way to perform preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high-quality outputs, the most effective approaches utilize reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF methods involve training a reward model on human preference data and then applying RL to optimize a language model policy to generate responses with high reward without deviating excessively from the original model. Though RLHF yields models with remarkable conversational and coding skills, the RLHF workflow is considerably more intricate than supervised learning, requiring training multiple language models and sampling from the policy during training, which incurs substantial computational overhead.

In this paper, we demonstrate a method to directly optimize a language model to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly maximizes the same objective as current RLHF algorithms (reward maximization subject to a KL-divergence constraint) while being easy to implement and straightforward to train. Intuitively, the DPO update increases the relative log probability of preferred responses compared to dispreferred ones, incorporating a dynamic, per-example importance weight that prevents the model degradation observed with a naive probability ratio objective. Similar to existing approaches, DPO depends on a theoretical preference model (such as the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) that assesses how well a given reward function matches empirical preference data. However, unlike existing methods that utilize the preference model to define a preference loss for training a reward model and then train a policy to optimize the learned reward model, DPO applies a change of variables to express the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. Given a dataset of human preferences over model responses, DPO can thus optimize a policy through a simple binary cross-entropy objective, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward function fitted to the preference data.

Our primary contribution is \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), a straightforward RL-free algorithm for training language models based on preferences. Our experiments demonstrate that DPO performs at least as well as existing techniques, including PPO-based RLHF, for learning from preferences on tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, using language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of growing size learn to perform certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or with few-shot prompting \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user intentions can be substantially enhanced by fine-tuning on datasets consisting of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning,' allows LLMs to generalize to instructions beyond those in the tuning dataset and generally improves their usability \citep{chung2022scaling}. Despite the achievements of instruction tuning, \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality are frequently easier to obtain than expert demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent studies have fine-tuned LLMs using datasets of human preferences, leading to improved performance in translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, storytelling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction-following \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches typically start by optimizing a neural reward model to align with the dataset of preferences under a preference framework such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, followed by fine-tuning a language model to maximize this reward via reinforcement learning techniques, often using REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related research direction exploits LLMs fine-tuned for instruction-following with human feedback to produce additional synthetic preference data targeting attributes like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying solely on weak human supervision in the form of textual rubrics for the LLM's annotations. These strategies represent a convergence of two research streams: one focusing on training language models with reinforcement learning for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and another on general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Despite the advantages of using relative human preferences, fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning remains a significant practical hurdle; this work proposes a theoretically grounded method for optimizing relative preferences without relying on RL.

Beyond the language domain, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of various methods. Learning in contextual bandits from preferences or rankings of actions, as opposed to explicit rewards, is referred to as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, the theoretical treatment of CDBs replaces the concept of an optimal policy with a \textit{von Neumann winner}, which is a policy whose expected probability of winning against \textit{any} other policy is no less than 50\% \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nonetheless, in the CDB framework, preference labels are provided in an online fashion, whereas learning from human preferences generally relies on a fixed batch of offline preference-annotated pairs of actions \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) methods utilize binary preferences generated by an \textit{unknown} underlying `scoring' function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. A range of PbRL algorithms exist, many of which are capable of reusing off-policy preference data; typically, these methods first explicitly infer the latent scoring function (i.e., the reward model) and then optimize the policy accordingly \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a single-stage policy learning technique that directly optimizes the policy to align with preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We revisit the RLHF framework as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}), which generally encompasses three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) preference sampling and reward learning; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF typically starts with supervised fine-tuning of a pre-trained language model on high-quality datasets relevant to the downstream tasks (e.g., dialogue, summarization), yielding a model denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Phase}: In this stage, the SFT model is prompted with inputs $x$ to generate pairs of outputs $(y_1, y_2) \sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. These output pairs are then presented to human annotators who indicate their preference between them, represented as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ denote the preferred and less preferred completions among $(y_1, y_2)$ respectively. These preferences are presumed to originate from an underlying latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which remains inaccessible. Various models have been employed to represent these preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model being a popular option (although more general Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be integrated into this framework if multiple ranked answers are available). According to the BT model, the human preference distribution $p^*$ can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given a fixed dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we can represent the reward function with a parameterized model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and estimate its parameters by maximizing the likelihood. Treating this as a binary classification problem, we obtain the negative log-likelihood loss as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. In language model contexts, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is commonly initialized from the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$, augmented with a linear layer on top of the final transformer layer to produce a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce variance in the reward predictions, previous studies normalize the reward outputs such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for each $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the trained reward model provides feedback signals for the language model. Building on prior work~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is formulated as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ controls the extent to which the policy $\pi_\theta$ can deviate from the baseline reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, typically the initial SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. Practically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized as $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This regularization is crucial to prevent the model from straying too far from the data distribution on which the reward model performs reliably, while also preserving diversity in generation and avoiding collapse to a few high-reward outputs. Since language generation is discrete, this objective is non-differentiable and is usually optimized with reinforcement learning algorithms. The common approach \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining a reward function ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$ and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Motivated by the complexities of applying reinforcement learning to large-scale tasks such as language model fine-tuning, we aim to develop a straightforward method to optimize policies directly from preferences. Unlike earlier RLHF approaches that first learn a reward function and then optimize it via reinforcement learning, our method employs a specific parameterization of the reward model that allows its optimal policy to be derived analytically, eliminating the need for an RL training loop. As we will explain in detail, the core insight is to utilize an explicit analytical relationship between reward functions and their corresponding optimal policies, enabling the transformation of a loss defined over reward functions into one defined over policies. This change-of-variables technique bypasses the need to fit a standalone, explicit reward model, yet continues to optimize under established preference frameworks such as the Bradley-Terry model. \textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin with the same reinforcement learning objective as previous studies, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined for a general reward function $r$. Following earlier work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily shown that the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} has the form:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where the partition function is defined as $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when employing the maximum likelihood estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward function $r^*$, computing the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally expensive \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practicality of this representation. Nevertheless, we can rearrange Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to rewrite the reward function in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and performing some algebraic manipulation, we get:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This reparameterization can be applied to the true reward $r^*$ and the corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Crucially, the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the reward difference between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the partition function terms cancel out, allowing us to write the human preference probabilities solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Therefore, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry preference model satisfies:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}.
\end{equation}

The full derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived for the Bradley-Terry model, analogous expressions can be derived for the more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

In this manner, we fit an implicit reward through an alternative parameterization, where the optimal policy corresponds directly to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, since our approach is equivalent to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it benefits from certain theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions about the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. \textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it is helpful to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{higher weight when reward estimate is wrong}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{increase likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{decrease likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ denotes the reward that is implicitly defined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Intuitively, the gradient of the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to increase the probability of preferred completions $y_w$ while decreasing the probability of less preferred completions $y_l$. Crucially, the contributions from each example are weighted according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ rates the less preferred completions higher, scaled by $\beta$—in other words, by how incorrectly the implicit reward model ranks the completions, reflecting the influence of the KL constraint. Our experimental results highlight the significance of this weighting; a naive variant of this method lacking the weighting factor can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.} The typical DPO workflow involves: 1) Drawing sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, then labeling these with human preferences to create the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\pi_\text{ref}$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and a specified $\beta$. 
In practical settings, it is preferable to leverage publicly available preference datasets rather than generating new samples and collecting human feedback. Because these preference datasets are sampled from $\pi^\text{SFT}$, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ whenever this model is accessible. When $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps reduce the distribution shift between the unknown true reference distribution and the $\pi_\text{ref}$ used in DPO. Additional implementation details and hyperparameters are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} In this section, we offer further insights into the DPO approach, supply theoretical justification, and connect DPO’s advantages to challenges faced by actor-critic methods applied in RLHF (such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO circumvents the need for explicitly fitting a reward function or performing reinforcement learning by optimizing a single maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} matches a Bradley-Terry model with the reward defined as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. Our parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$ is optimized equivalently to the reward model in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} through an appropriate change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the set of reward models that can be learned, and show that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.     
\end{definition}

It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, which divides the set of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. We can then present the following two lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, and specifically the Bradley-Terry model, two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class produce identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Reward functions that lie in the same equivalence class result in the same optimal policy when solving the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}

The proofs are straightforward and are provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma reflects a well-known under-specification characteristic of the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this under-specification, it is common to enforce additional identifiability constraints to ensure guarantees on the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all reward functions within the same equivalence class correspond to the same optimal policy. Therefore, for our ultimate goal, it suffices to recover any reward function from the relevant optimal equivalence class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Subject to mild conditions, every equivalence class of reward functions compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and in particular Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is some model and $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$ is a given reference model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that defines an associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that there exists a reward function in the equivalence class of $r$ that can be represented by the above reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right).
\end{equation}
This operator normalizes the reward function by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function corresponding to $\pi_r$. Since this normalization term depends only on $x$, the function $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ belongs to the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Finally, substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for any reward function) yields
$f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Hence, the projection $f$ maps $r$ to a member of its equivalence class in the desired form, showing that our proposed reparameterization of the reward model is fully general.
\end{sproof}

An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which reward function within each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization chooses. Specifically, this reward function fulfills the condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a legitimate probability distribution (with positive probabilities summing to one). Moreover, from Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is clear that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward function $r(x, y)$. The central idea behind the DPO algorithm is to apply particular constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce family (and specifically the Bradley-Terry subset) of preference models. This ensures that the set of representable reward models remains intact while simultaneously making the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} explicitly computable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework can also be leveraged to analyze the instabilities encountered by conventional actor-critic methods employed in RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF procedure, concentrating on the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We establish links to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} regarding the constrained RL problem presented in \ref{eq:RL}. Here, we consider a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$ and aim to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model}, induced by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this results in the optimization objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

This objective is identical to the one optimized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} using the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this framework, the normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function of the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this term does not influence the optimal solution, omitting it may cause the policy gradient of the objective to exhibit high variance, resulting in unstable learning. While it is possible to account for the normalization term through a learned value function, this approach can also be challenging to optimize. Alternatively, earlier methods have normalized rewards by using a human completion baseline, effectively a single sample Monte-Carlo estimate of the normalization term. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that eliminates the need for any baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess DPO’s capacity to train policies directly from preferences. Initially, in a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how efficiently DPO balances maximizing reward and minimizing KL-divergence with the reference policy compared to common preference learning algorithms such as PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO’s effectiveness on larger models and more challenging RLHF tasks, including summarization and dialogue. Our findings show that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or outperforms strong baselines like RLHF with PPO and also surpasses selecting the best of $N$ sampled trajectories under a learned reward function. Before presenting these results, we detail the experimental setup; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all experiments, algorithms learn a policy from a preference dataset denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents the beginning segment of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is tasked with generating $y$ that exhibits a positive sentiment. To enable a controlled evaluation, we \textit{generate} preference pairs over generated outputs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For supervised fine-tuning (SFT), we train GPT-2-large until convergence on reviews from the training split of the IMDb dataset (additional details are provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ corresponds to a Reddit forum post; the policy must generate a summary $y$ that captures the key points of the post. Following prior research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} combined with human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. For RLHF, we employ an SFT model fine-tuned on human-written forum post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} framework. The human preference dataset was obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples from a different yet similarly fine-tuned SFT model. Lastly, in the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ is a human query that could range from astrophysics questions to relationship advice. The policy must generate a response $y$ that is both engaging and helpful. We use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which contains 170k dialogues between humans and an automated assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses produced by a large (though unspecified) language model, along with a preference label indicating the human-favored response. Since no pre-trained SFT model exists in this setting, we fine-tune an off-the-shelf language model exclusively on the preferred completions to create the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experiments employ two distinct evaluation strategies. To assess the efficiency of each algorithm in optimizing the constrained reward maximization objective within the controlled sentiment generation context, we measure each algorithm's frontier based on the achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy. This frontier is calculable because we have access to the true reward function (a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in real-world scenarios where the true reward function is unknown, we assess algorithms based on their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a surrogate for human evaluation of summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue scenarios, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response within the test dataset. While prior research indicates that LMs can serve as superior automated evaluators compared to existing metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we perform a human study to validate the use of GPT-4 for evaluation in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show that GPT-4's judgments correlate strongly with human assessments, with human agreement with GPT-4 typically matching or exceeding inter-human annotator agreement.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess multiple existing techniques for training language models to align with human preferences. At the simplest level, we investigate zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} for the summarization task and 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} for the dialogue task. Furthermore, we evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model as well as \textbf{Preferred-FT}, a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the chosen completion $y_w$ from either the SFT model (in controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic LM (in single-turn dialogue). Another pseudo-supervised approach is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the policy to maximize the probability assigned to $y_w$ while \textit{minimizing} the probability assigned to $y_l$; this method includes an optional coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ applied to the 'unlikelihood' component. We also examine \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which uses a reward function learned from preference data, along with \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle that learns from the ground truth reward function available in the controlled sentiment setting. For the sentiment experiments, we employ two PPO-GT implementations: an off-the-shelf version \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version that normalizes rewards and fine-tunes hyperparameters to enhance performance (the same modifications are applied when running standard PPO with learned rewards). Lastly, we consider the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, which samples $N$ responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selects the highest-scoring response based on a reward function learned from the preference dataset. This highly effective method separates reward model quality from PPO optimization but is computationally infeasible for even moderate $N$, as it requires sampling $N$ completions for each query at test time.

\subsection{How effectively can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective commonly employed in RLHF algorithms strikes a balance between exploiting the reward and limiting the policy's divergence from the reference policy. Consequently, when evaluating algorithms, it is important to consider both the achieved reward and the KL divergence; obtaining slightly higher reward at the cost of significantly increased KL divergence may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the reward-KL frontier for different algorithms within the sentiment task. We conduct multiple training trials for each algorithm, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT). This grid comprises a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps until convergence, we assess each policy on a test prompt set, calculating the average reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the sum of the KL divergences at each timestep.} relative to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO yields the most efficient frontier by a large margin, attaining the highest reward while maintaining low KL. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. Firstly, although DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO is markedly more efficient; the reward/KL tradeoff of DPO strictly surpasses that of PPO. Secondly, DPO outperforms PPO’s frontier even when PPO is given access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO scalable to real-world preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE often show weak correlation with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous research has demonstrated that fine-tuning language models with PPO on human preference data yields more effective summaries. To compare various approaches, we generate completions from the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset, measuring the average win rate against reference completions within that set. For all methods, completions are sampled across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0, with win rates displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT each fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that at temperature 0.0, DPO achieves a win rate near 61\%, outperforming PPO’s peak of roughly 57% at the same temperature. Furthermore, DPO attains a higher maximum win rate relative to the best of $N$ baseline approach. It is important to mention that we did not extensively tune DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, suggesting these outcomes could underrepresent DPO’s true capabilities. Additionally, DPO demonstrates greater robustness to changes in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance can deteriorate to match the base GPT-J model at elevated temperatures. Preferred-FT fails to significantly improve upon the SFT model. We also conduct direct human evaluations comparing DPO and PPO in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO-generated samples at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training}, focusing on one round of human-assistant interaction. GPT-4 evaluations use the preferred completions in the test set as a reference to calculate the win rate for each method. Since there is no standard SFT model tailored for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, apply Preferred-FT to create a reference model trained on the chosen completions to ensure these completions align with the model’s distribution, and subsequently train using DPO. We also benchmark against the best result from 128 Preferred-FT completions (noting that the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at 128 completions for this task; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted variant of the Pythia-2.8B base model, observing that DPO performs comparably or better at the optimal temperatures for each method. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} from a reputable source\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but fail to identify a prompt or sampling temperature that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B model. Considering our TL;DR results and that both methods optimize the same reward function, we regard Best of 128 as a rough approximation of PPO-level performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method that enhances performance beyond the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and matches or surpasses the computationally intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches its peak performance relatively rapidly.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To further assess the robustness of PPO and DPO under shifts in data distribution, we test the policies obtained from our Reddit TL;DR summarization experiment on a different dataset: news articles from the CNN/DailyMail test split \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}, using the optimal sampling temperatures found for TL;DR (0 and 0.25). The outcomes are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. We measured the GPT-4 win rate against the ground-truth summaries in these datasets, employing the same GPT-4 (C) prompt used for Reddit TL;DR but substituting the term ``forum post'' with ``news article''. On this new distribution, DPO continues to outperform PPO by a notable margin. This experiment offers preliminary evidence that DPO policies can generalize comparably well to PPO policies, even though DPO does not leverage the additional unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts utilized by PPO.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We carry out a human evaluation study to confirm the reliability of GPT-4’s assessments, based on the outcomes of the TL;DR summarization experiment and two distinct GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt straightforwardly asks which summary better captures the important information in the post. The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt additionally inquires which summary is more concise; this prompt is evaluated because we observed that GPT-4 tends to favor longer, more repetitive summaries over humans when using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt. Full prompts are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We perform three pairwise comparisons, selecting the highest-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing method (PPO, temp. 1.0), and an intermediate method (SFT, temp. 0.25), to capture a broad range of sample qualities; all three are compared against greedily-sampled PPO (its optimal temperature). We observe that with both prompts, GPT-4’s agreement with humans is approximately as frequent as human-to-human agreement, indicating that GPT-4 serves as a reasonable surrogate for human evaluation (due to a limited number of human raters, multiple human judgments were collected only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons). Overall, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt tends to yield win rates that align more closely with human preferences; thus, we adopt this prompt for the main findings in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Additional information regarding the human study, including the web interface shown to raters and a list of human volunteers, can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable, aligned language models. We have presented DPO, a straightforward training framework for optimizing language models based on preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to apply standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to be trained to meet human preferences \textit{directly} using a simple cross-entropy loss, bypassing both reinforcement learning and loss of generality. With minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves performance comparable to or better than existing RLHF methods, including those utilizing PPO; thus, DPO significantly lowers the entry barrier for training language models from human preference data.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings prompt several key questions for subsequent research. How well does the DPO policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to approaches that learn from explicit reward functions? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize similarly to PPO-trained models, but a more extensive analysis is necessary. For instance, can self-labeling with the DPO policy effectively leverage unlabeled prompts? Furthermore, what form does reward over-optimization take in the direct preference optimization context, and could the slight dip in performance shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right be an example of this phenomenon? Additionally, while our experiments involve models up to 6B parameters, investigating the scalability of DPO to cutting-edge models with significantly larger sizes remains a promising avenue. Concerning evaluation, we observe that GPT-4-based win rates are sensitive to the prompt design; future studies may explore optimal strategies to obtain reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Lastly, DPO’s utility extends beyond language models trained with human preferences and can be applied to training generative models across various other modalities.
