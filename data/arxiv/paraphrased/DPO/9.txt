\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and some reasoning abilities, precisely guiding their outputs remains challenging because their training is entirely unsupervised. Current techniques for improving steerability rely on gathering human judgments about the comparative quality of model outputs and then fine-tuning the unsupervised LM to reflect these preferences, commonly via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). RLHF, however, is a complicated and frequently unstable process that first trains a reward model to capture human preferences, and then uses reinforcement learning to adjust the large LM so it maximizes the estimated reward, all while avoiding excessive deviation from the original model. In this work, we propose a novel way to parameterize the reward model in RLHF, which enables closed-form extraction of the corresponding optimal policy and allows the standard RLHF objective to be addressed using a straightforward classification loss. The new algorithm, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is robust, efficient, and requires minimal computational resources, removing the need to sample from the LM during fine-tuning or to conduct extensive hyperparameter searches. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to match or surpass the performance of existing methods in aligning with human preferences. In particular, DPO-based fine-tuning offers superior sentiment control compared to PPO-based RLHF, and achieves equal or higher response quality in tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue, while being considerably easier to implement and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive corpora demonstrate remarkable and sometimes unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Despite this, the data used for training is produced by humans with diverse intentions, priorities, and levels of expertise. Not all of these human traits are desirable for the model to replicate; for instance, while it's beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors in order to correct them, we actually want the model to favor the infrequent but high-quality examples of programming present in its dataset when generating code. Likewise, we prefer our language model to be \textit{informed} about widespread misconceptions—such as one held by 50\% of people—yet we do not want it to repeat this misconception as fact in half of its responses on the subject. Essentially, it is vital to distinguish and promote the model's \emph{preferred outputs and conduct} from the broader range of its \textit{knowledge and competencies} in order to develop AI systems that are reliable, effective, and governable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally use reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human values, we demonstrate that the RL-based objective at the core of these techniques can be precisely solved using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which streamlines the process of preference modeling.

Broadly speaking, current approaches shape the desired behaviors in a language model by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that exemplify behaviors people deem safe and useful. This learning from preferences typically follows an initial phase of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on an extensive corpus of text. Although the simplest technique for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning using human-provided examples of high-quality responses, the most effective category of approaches to date has been reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on human preference data and then reinforcement learning is used to adapt a language model policy to generate outputs that receive high rewards, while constraining deviations from the base model. Despite the fact that RLHF leads to language models with remarkable capabilities in dialogue and code generation, the RLHF pipeline is much more involved than standard supervised learning; it requires training several language models and performing policy sampling within the training loop, which results in substantial computational overhead.

This paper demonstrates a method for directly optimizing language models to align with human preferences, eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an approach that implicitly maximizes the same objective as standard RLHF techniques (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint), while remaining easy to implement and train. At its core, DPO adjusts the relative log likelihoods of preferred versus non-preferred outputs, employing a dynamic, instance-specific importance weight that mitigates the model collapse seen when using a naive probability ratio objective. Like prior approaches, DPO leverages a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess alignment between a reward function and observed preference data. In contrast to traditional methods, which employ the preference model to formulate a loss for reward model training—followed by separate policy optimization—DPO reformulates the preference loss to be a direct function of the policy itself, using a change of variables. Thus, with a dataset consisting of human judgments over model outputs, DPO is able to optimize the policy directly using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward model learned from preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an uncomplicated algorithm that enables training of language models from preference data without requiring reinforcement learning. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the effectiveness of established techniques—such as PPO-based RLHF—in leveraging preferences for tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue generation, even when utilizing language models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models of larger sizes can perform some tasks without examples (zero-shot) \citep{radford2019language} or by using only a handful of demonstrations (few-shot prompts) \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Yet, their effectiveness on downstream applications and their alignment with user expectations are often greatly enhanced by additional fine-tuning on datasets that consist of instructions paired with human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as ‘instruction-tuning’, allows large language models (LLMs) to better generalize to instructions not present in the training set and generally improves their practical utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has achieved notable results, collecting \textit{comparative} human assessments of output quality is often simpler than obtaining expert-written demonstrations. Consequently, subsequent research has fine-tuned LLMs on datasets built from human preference comparisons, leading to improved abilities in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These techniques first train a neural reward model to align with human preferences—typically using a preference framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—and then further adjust the language model to maximize this reward, utilizing reinforcement learning methods such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or similar approaches \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. A related line of research employs LLMs fine-tuned via human feedback for instruction following to produce additional synthetic preference data targeting specific qualities like safety or non-harmfulness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, with only weak human supervision provided through textual guidelines for the model’s annotations. These strategies blend two research areas: one focused on using reinforcement learning to train language models across various objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and the other on developing general approaches for preference-based learning from humans \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
While leveraging relative human preference data is attractive, fine-tuning large language models using reinforcement learning continues to present significant practical difficulties. This work introduces a theoretically-grounded method for optimizing models based on relative preferences without relying on reinforcement learning.

Beyond the realm of language, the task of learning policies from preferences has been explored in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple strategies suggested. In the case of contextual bandit learning where preferences or rankings over actions replace traditional rewards, the problem is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When absolute rewards are unavailable, theoretical treatment of CDBs utilizes the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that secures an expected win rate of at least 50\% against \textit{every} alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Yet, the CDB framework assumes preference feedback is collected interactively, whereas learning from human preferences typically involves a static set of offline, preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) is concerned with learning from pairwise preferences derived from an \textit{unknown} ‘scoring’ function instead of direct rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There exist a range of PbRL methods, including approaches capable of leveraging off-policy preference data, but these methods generally require estimating the underlying scoring function (i.e., the reward model) prior to policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a single-stage policy learning technique that directly trains a policy to align with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF workflow presented by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (and subsequently in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). This process generally consists of three stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), 2) collecting preferences and training a reward model, and 3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by using supervised learning to fine-tune a pre-trained language model on carefully curated datasets relevant to target tasks (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Step}: During the second stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates pairs of responses $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These response pairs are shown to human annotators, who indicate their preference for one answer over the other, written as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ represents the chosen response and $y_l$ the less preferred one out of $(y_1, y_2)$. It is assumed these preferences arise according to some unobserved reward function $r^*(y, x)$, which is inaccessible. Several preference modeling techniques exist, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a common option (and more general methods such as the Plackett-Luce ranking model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be used when rankings of multiple answers are available). The BT model formulates the probability of a human preferring $y_1$ over $y_2$ as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Supposing we have a fixed comparison dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled from $p^*$, a reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be learned by maximizing the likelihood. Recasting the setup as a binary classification task, the negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized with the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, adding a linear head atop the final transformer output to produce a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward variability, prior research typically normalizes the rewards so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for any prompt $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the reinforcement learning stage, the previously learned reward function serves to guide the language model’s updates. Consistent with earlier studies~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\,\|\,\piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ regulates how much the policy can diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which is the pretrained SFT model $\pisft$. 
Typically, the language model policy $\pi_\theta$ starts from $\pisft$. This regularization plays a crucial role, limiting how far the model can shift away from the data distribution where the reward model’s predictions are reliable, supporting output diversity, and reducing the risk of converging to only a few high-reward outputs. Since language is generated discretely, direct differentiation is not possible for this objective, so reinforcement learning techniques are employed for optimization. The most common method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} reformulates the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta(\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, and performs optimization with PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Inspired by the difficulties of using reinforcement learning algorithms for large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that works directly with preferences. In contrast to previous RLHF approaches that first fit a reward model and then optimize it through RL, our method utilizes a specific reward model parameterization, allowing us to directly obtain its optimal policy in closed form, thus eliminating the need for an RL training loop. 

As we will explain in more detail below, our central idea is to use an analytical relationship between reward functions and their optimal policies, allowing us to convert a loss defined over reward functions into a loss defined over policies. This change-of-variables strategy sidesteps the need to learn a separate reward model explicitly, yet still permits optimization according to existing frameworks for human preferences, such as the Bradley-Terry model. Fundamentally, the policy network thus encodes both the language model and the (implicit) reward.

\textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin with the standard reinforcement learning objective as in previous work (see Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}), using an arbitrary reward function $r$. As established in earlier research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, the optimal policy for the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the normalization constant, or partition function. The full derivation is available in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when the reward function $r$ is replaced with its MLE estimate $r_{\phi}$ for the true reward $r^*$, evaluating the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally intensive~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making direct use of this form challenging in real-world settings. Nevertheless, by rearranging Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we can isolate the reward function in terms of its associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the baseline policy $\piref$, and the yet unknown partition function $Z(\cdot)$. To do so, we take the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and perform algebraic manipulation to yield:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This reformulation can be applied to the ground-truth reward $r^*$ and its optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, in the Bradley-Terry model, only reward differences between two candidate responses are relevant; specifically, the preference probability is given by ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging the reparameterization from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference probability (Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}), we see that the normalization terms cancel out, allowing us to represent the human preference probability solely using the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Thus, under the Bradley-Terry preference model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ meets the following relationship:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
The complete proof is found in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} uses the Bradley-Terry framework, similar approaches extend to more general models, such as the Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having now expressed the probability of human preferences in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective tailored to a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Mirroring the approach used in reward modeling (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our objective for the policy becomes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
By doing this, we implicitly learn a reward structure through a different parameterization, with $\pi_\theta$ representing the optimal policy. Additionally, because this framework corresponds to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical guarantees, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions on the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} elaborates further on the theoretical underpinnings of DPO in connection with related literature.

\textbf{How does the DPO update function?} To gain a mechanistic perspective on DPO, it is informative to examine the gradient of its loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ can be expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{larger when reward estimate is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{encourage $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{discourage $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ serves as the implicit reward assigned by the language model $\pi_\theta$ compared to the reference model $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for further details). In essence, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ works to boost the probability of the chosen completions $y_w$ while reducing the probability of the less preferred $y_l$. Crucially, the contribution of each example is modulated by how much more favorably the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ evaluates the less preferred completion, scaled by the hyperparameter $\beta$. This reflects how incorrectly the reward model ranks the outputs and incorporates the influence of the KL constraint. Our empirical results highlight the necessity of this weighting, as omitting it—as in a naive variant—may lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}
The typical DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, draw samples $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$, then use human feedback to assign preference labels, forming the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, conditioned on the given $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the chosen $\beta$. In practice, instead of producing new samples and collecting fresh human preference annotations, it is desirable to leverage existing public preference datasets. As these datasets are usually based on samples from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ whenever such models are accessible. In scenarios where $\pisft$ is not available, we obtain $\piref$ by fitting it via maximum likelihood estimation to the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$; in other words, we set ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the mismatch between the ideal, but inaccessible, reference distribution and the surrogate $\piref$ applied in DPO. Further implementation specifics and hyperparameter choices are described in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
In this part, we offer a deeper understanding of the DPO approach, present theoretical justifications, and connect the benefits of DPO to known challenges associated with actor-critic methods employed in RLHF, like PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as an Implicit Reward Model} DPO avoids both constructing an explicit reward and employing RL for policy learning by using a unified maximum likelihood objective. Observe that the optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry framework with reward parameterization $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. We directly optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, which, after a variable substitution, is equivalent to the reward model training objective in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}. In this section, we develop the theoretical justification for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it preserves the expressiveness of reward models, and show that it enables perfect recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relationship between reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are called equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$. 
\end{definition}
Clearly, this defines an equivalence relation, which divides the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can thus be stated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce model, including the Bradley-Terry framework, any two reward functions belonging to the same class produce identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in identical optimal policies within the context of the constrained RL framework.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The initial lemma highlights a well-recognized issue of under-specification associated with the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Because of this under-determination, it is typically necessary to apply further identifiability constraints in order to provide any assurances regarding the maximum likelihood estimates derived from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The subsequent lemma asserts that any reward function from a given equivalence class leads to the same optimal policy; thus, for our purposes, recovering any representative reward from this class is sufficient for our overall objective. We establish the following Theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (as well as the Bradley-Terry) models can be captured via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ denotes a model and $\piref(y \mid x)$ is a chosen reference model.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$, which produces an associated optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$, as specified by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to demonstrate that there exists a reward from the equivalence class of $r$ expressible using the proposed reparameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ as
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ essentially adjusts the reward by subtracting the log-partition function of $\pi_r$, depending only on the input $x$. Because the normalization term is independent of $y$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which is valid for all reward functions), we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Therefore, $f$ yields a member of the equivalence class of $r$ in the required form, ensuring our reward representation is as general as the original model.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be seen as identifying the precise reward within each equivalence class selected by the DPO reparameterization, namely, the reward that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}
meaning that $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a valid probability distribution (non-negative and sums to one).
By referencing Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we observe that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the normalization constant (partition function) for the optimal policy arising from $r(x, y)$. 
A central idea in the DPO framework is to impose specific constraints on the under-determined Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference model families, which both maintains the full expressive capacity for reward models, and ensures that the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} becomes analytically computable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instabilities}
Our framework also allows us to analyze sources of instability in commonly used actor-critic approaches for RLHF, such as PPO. Adhering to the RLHF process, we examine the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. We make connections to the control-as-inference framework \cite{levine2018reinforcement} for the constrained RL task given in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, our objective is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ represents the optimal policy from Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, based on the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Some algebraic manipulation leads to the following optimization objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to the objective used in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} when employing the DPO-equivalent reward within the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization term in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization term does not influence the optimal policy, omitting it can result in a policy gradient with large variance, thus destabilizing training. Introducing a learned value function can account for this normalization, but optimizing the value function itself may pose significant challenges. Alternatively, earlier works have addressed normalization by using a human completion as a baseline—effectively a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the normalization constant. The DPO reparameterization, on the other hand, leads to a reward function that eliminates the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess how well DPO can train policies directly from preference data. To begin, in a controlled text-generation scenario, we investigate the efficiency with which DPO balances reward maximization and minimizing KL-divergence from the reference policy, and compare its performance to established preference optimization methods like PPO. Following this, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks, such as dialogue and summarization. Our results show that DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines like RLHF with PPO, and the strategy of selecting the best out of $N$ sampled trajectories using a learned reward function, all with minimal hyperparameter tuning. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline our experimental methodology; further details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study examines three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all experiments, the methods aim to learn a policy based on a dataset of preference tuples $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents the beginning of a movie review from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy is required to produce a continuation $y$ with positive sentiment. To enable controlled analysis, we synthetically generate preference pairs using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, enforcing that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we adapt GPT-2-large through fine-tuning on training data from the IMDb reviews until satisfactory performance is reached (more information in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). For \textbf{summarization}, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the goal is to produce a summary $y$ highlighting the main points. Building on previous research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization corpus \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} and incorporate human preference data collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. The SFT baseline is obtained by fine-tuning on human-generated forum summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} toolkit for RLHF. Human preference annotations were sourced by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from outputs of another comparably trained SFT model. Lastly, for \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, $x$ denotes a user prompt, which may vary from scientific inquiries to personal advice requests. Here, the policy is expected to generate a helpful and engaging reply $y$; the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, with 170k human-bot conversations, is used. Each dialogue concludes with two candidate model responses and a label indicating which response was preferred by the annotator. In this scenario, there is no existing SFT checkpoint; thus, we construct the SFT model by fine-tuning a publicly available language model solely on the preferred completions.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation methodologies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm maximizes the constrained reward in a controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure the frontier of achieved reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy for each method. This frontier can be directly computed since the true reward function—a sentiment classifier—is accessible. In contrast, since the true reward function is unavailable in real-world situations, we assess algorithms by their \textit{win rate} when compared to a baseline policy. For this, we use GPT-4 to simulate human judgment for both summary quality and response helpfulness in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. In the summarization task, the baseline consists of reference summaries from the test split, while in dialogue, it is the preferred response from the test set. Although recent research indicates that language models can outperform traditional metrics as automatic evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we support our choice of GPT-4 for evaluation with a human study in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show that GPT-4’s evaluations have a high correlation with those of humans, with agreement rates between humans and GPT-4 that are often as high as, or higher than, agreement among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Alongside DPO, we assess a range of established methods for aligning language models with human preferences. As a straightforward baseline, we use zero-shot prompting for \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} in summarization and 2-shot prompting for \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in dialogue. We also examine the \textbf{SFT} model and \textbf{Preferred-FT}, the latter being trained via supervised fine-tuning on the preferred output $y_w$—sourced from the SFT model for summarization and sentiment, or a standard LM for single-turn dialogue. Another variant, \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, is a pseudo-supervised approach that increases the likelihood of $y_w$ while actively decreasing the likelihood of $y_l$, incorporating an optional weighting factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood loss. We further analyze \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, employing a reward model trained from human preferences, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle model which leverages the exact reward signal in the controlled sentiment scenario. For sentiment, we deploy two PPO-GT versions: a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and an enhanced variant with reward normalization and additional hyperparameter tuning for optimal results—modifications also applied when using PPO with learned rewards. Lastly, we incorporate the \textbf{Best of $N$} approach, where $N$ outputs are sampled from either the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue), and the response with the highest reward—as determined by a learned preference-based reward model—is selected. Despite strong performance, this method is computationally intensive at inference, as generating $N$ samples for each prompt quickly becomes impractical for even moderate $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates measured by GPT-4 for Anthropic-HH single-turn dialogue; DPO is unique in outperforming the reference summaries from the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates across varying sampling temperatures during training. DPO consistently maintains a performance advantage over the reference labels throughout training, irrespective of the sampling temperature.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization objective, commonly employed in RLHF algorithms, serves to balance maximizing reward and constraining the policy's divergence from the reference policy. As a result, comparing different algorithms requires evaluating both the attained reward and the KL divergence; achieving marginally higher rewards at the expense of a substantially greater KL is not always optimal. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} displays the reward-KL tradeoff curves for a variety of algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each method, we conduct several training runs, each time varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and using different random seeds for preferred-FT), yielding a total of 22 training sessions. Every 100 training steps, until convergence, we assess each policy on a collection of test prompts, calculating the mean reward based on the ground truth reward function, as well as the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the aggregate of the KL-divergences at each timestep.} relative to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings indicate that DPO establishes the most favorable frontier, securing the highest reward for a given KL and maintaining a low KL overall. This outcome is especially significant for several reasons. First, although both DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO achieves markedly higher efficiency, with its reward/KL curve strictly outperforming that of PPO. Second, DPO attains a superior reward-KL frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even when PPO has access to the true reward signal} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Is DPO applicable to large-scale preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the case of summarization, automated metrics like ROUGE often do not align well with human judgment~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies have shown that tuning language models with PPO on human preference data produces more desirable summaries. Our evaluation considers several approaches by generating outputs on the test split of the TL;DR summarization dataset, and calculating their average win rates against reference outputs from the same test set. For all methods, we generate completions across a temperature range from 0.0 to 1.0; the resulting win rates are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all start from the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our findings show that DPO achieves about a 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO, which attains roughly 57\% at its optimal temperature setting. DPO also records a higher best win rate than the best-of-$N$ baseline. It should be highlighted that DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter was not thoroughly tuned, suggesting its true performance might be even better. Additionally, DPO demonstrates greater resilience to increases in sampling temperature, whereas PPO’s performance can drop to the level of the original GPT-J model as the temperature rises. Preferred-FT, on the other hand, offers little improvement compared to the initial SFT model. We further present a direct comparison between DPO and PPO based on human evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, where DPO outputs at temperature 0.25 are chosen over PPO’s at temperature 0 in 58\% of cases.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on the subset of the Anthropic HH test split \citep{bai2022training} that includes one round of human-assistant exchange. In the GPT-4 assessments, we use the preferred responses from the test set as references to determine the win rate across the different methods. Since there is no standard SFT baseline for this task, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, train a reference model via Preferred-FT on the selected responses so that outputs remain within the model’s distribution, and then proceed with DPO training. We further benchmark against the best of 128 Preferred-FT completions (as our experiments indicate the Best of $N$ baseline plateaus at $N=128$; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompted version of the original Pythia-2.8B, observing that DPO matches or surpasses the highest-performing temperatures for each method. We also test an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} using a reputable implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but cannot identify any prompt or temperature setting that outperforms the baseline Pythia-2.8B. Given our TL;DR findings and the fact that both methods optimize the same reward, we use Best of 128 as an approximate stand-in for PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only efficient approach that surpasses the reference completions in the Anthropic HH dataset and achieves similar or better results compared to the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO quickly reaches its optimal performance.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning serves as an effective and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we have presented DPO, a straightforward method for training language models from preferences that does not rely on reinforcement learning. Instead of reshaping the preference learning challenge into a conventional RL framework just to apply standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, allowing the model to align with human preferences \textit{directly} using a basic cross-entropy loss—eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without any loss of generality. Even with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO achieves results that are on par with or superior to popular RLHF methods, including those built on PPO, thereby substantially lowering the barriers to training language models based on human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Our findings highlight several open questions for future research. For instance, how well does the DPO policy transfer to out-of-distribution scenarios when compared to methods based on explicit reward learning? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies generalize on par with models trained via PPO, but further investigation is warranted. An interesting avenue is to assess whether training with self-labeling derived from the DPO policy is equally effective in leveraging unlabeled prompts. Another aspect to explore is how reward over-optimization appears within direct preference optimization, and whether the moderate performance drop observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right can be attributed to this phenomenon. Furthermore, although our current experiments involve models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models presents a promising path for future work. In terms of evaluation, we notice that GPT-4’s computed win rates are prompt-sensitive; thus, future research could focus on optimal strategies for eliciting reliable automated judgments. Lastly, DPO’s potential extends well beyond fine-tuning language models with human preferences and could be applied to training generative models in different domains.
