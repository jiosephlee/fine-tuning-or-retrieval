\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and certain reasoning abilities, yet steering their outputs remains challenging due to the purely unsupervised nature of their pretraining.
Current techniques to improve controllability typically gather human annotations regarding the relative merits of model outputs, then further train the LM to follow these preferences, often via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
Nonetheless, RLHF involves a multi-stage and sometimes unstable process: first training a reward model to represent human judgments, then using reinforcement learning to adjust the LM so as to maximize this modeled reward while constraining deviation from the original network.
We present a novel reward model parameterization for RLHF, which permits direct recovery of the optimal policy in closed form and reduces the standard RLHF setup to a straightforward classification loss.
We term the resulting approach \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), which is robust, efficient, and requires little computational overhead—removing the necessity for LM sampling during training and minimizing the need for extensive hyperparameter search.
Empirically, we find that DPO fine-tunes LMs to human preferences as well or better than prior approaches. Specifically, DPO outperforms RLHF methods based on PPO in sentiment control tasks and matches or surpasses them in summarization and single-turn conversation, all while being considerably easier to implement and optimize.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs), trained on massive corpora, have demonstrated unexpected abilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Despite these advances, such models learn from data created by humans with diverse intentions, backgrounds, and expertise. Not all of these influences are desirable; for instance, although it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} frequent programming errors in order to fix them, we generally prefer the model to favor the (sometimes infrequent) examples of excellent coding it has observed, rather than reproducing common mistakes. Similarly, while it is useful for a language model to \textit{know about} a widely held misconception, we do not want it to perpetuate that misconception in half its responses when asked about it. That is, it is essential to distinguish and select the model's \emph{preferred actions and answers} from its broad \textit{knowledge base and skills} when designing AI systems that are safe, effective, and reliably directed \citep{ouyang2022training}. Current techniques usually shape LMs to align with human values using reinforcement learning (RL); here, we demonstrate that the RL-inspired objective traditionally applied in these approaches can actually be precisely minimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, which streamlines the entire preference optimization process.

Broadly speaking, current approaches impart desired behaviors to a language model by leveraging carefully constructed collections of human preference data that capture behaviors regarded as safe and beneficial by people. This stage of learning preferences follows an initial period of extensive unsupervised pre-training on a massive corpus of text. Although supervised fine-tuning using high-quality human demonstrations is the simplest method for preference learning, the most effective techniques involve reinforcement learning from human or AI-generated feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF, a reward model is trained on human preference data, after which reinforcement learning is used to adjust the language model’s policy, encouraging outputs that the reward model rates highly while constraining deviations from the base model. Despite the strong conversational and coding proficiency demonstrated by RLHF-trained models, the RLHF process is substantially more complicated than supervised learning, as it requires training several language models and iteratively sampling from the policy during training, leading to high computational demands.

In this work, we present a method for optimizing a language model to align with human preferences without relying on explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. Our approach, called Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), is an algorithm that implicitly maximizes the same objective as standard RLHF methods (reward maximization under a KL-divergence constraint), yet is both easier to implement and more straightforward to train. The main idea behind DPO is to boost the relative log probability of preferred outputs over less preferred ones, while introducing a per-example adaptive importance weight that mitigates model collapse—a common problem with naive probability ratio objectives. Similar to prior algorithms, DPO employs a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess how closely a reward function matches observed preference data. The key difference is that, whereas conventional methods use this model to craft a preference loss for training a reward model and subsequently optimize a policy based on that learned reward, DPO leverages a variable substitution to directly express the preference loss in terms of the policy. With a dataset of human preference judgments over model outputs, DPO can thus train a policy through a simple binary cross-entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy for an implicit reward tailored to the preference dataset.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm that enables training language models from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Through our experiments, we demonstrate that DPO performs on par with current approaches—such as PPO-based RLHF—for preference-based learning in tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and dialogue, utilizing language models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models at larger scales have demonstrated the ability to perform certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or with only a handful of examples as prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user goals can be greatly enhanced through fine-tuning on datasets containing instructions and human-generated responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, known as `instruction-tuning', enables LLMs to better generalize to instructions not present in the original tuning set and tends to improve their overall utility \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has shown considerable promise, collecting \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality is often easier than gathering expert demonstrations. As a result, subsequent approaches have adapted LLMs using datasets that reflect human preferences, thereby boosting performance in tasks such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, narrative generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These techniques typically involve first fitting a neural network-based reward model to align with the preference dataset, often using a framework like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and then fine-tuning the language model to maximize this reward with reinforcement learning algorithms—commonly REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related approaches \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Closely associated research exploits LLMs trained for instruction following with human feedback to produce additional synthetic preference data targeting qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak human oversight in the form of textual guidelines for LLM-generated annotations. These strategies represent the intersection of two major research directions: one focused on applying reinforcement learning to train language models for a range of tasks~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and the other dedicated to general frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. 
Despite the advantages of utilizing relative human preferences, fine-tuning large language models with reinforcement learning methods continues to present significant practical difficulties; this work introduces a theoretically sound technique for optimizing with relative preferences that does not rely on RL.

Outside the realm of language, learning policies based on preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of multiple techniques. When contextual bandit algorithms leverage preferences or action rankings in place of reward signals, the problem is termed a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). Since absolute reward values are absent, theoretical work on CDBs replaces the standard optimal policy concept with the \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy that, on average, is expected to outperform \textit{any} alternative policy at least half of the time \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDBs typically receive preference information in an online manner, whereas learning from human preferences often relies on a predetermined set of offline, preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Along similar lines, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) learns using binary preference comparisons generated via an \textit{unknown} scoring function rather than direct reward feedback \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. There is a variety of PbRL algorithms, some of which can utilize off-policy preference data; these usually start by inferring the underlying scoring (reward) function, followed by optimizing with respect to it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we introduce a single-stage policy learning framework that directly updates the policy to adhere to observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} (with subsequent developments in \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). The approach generally consists of three main steps: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), 2) collecting preference data and training a reward model, and 3) optimizing with reinforcement learning.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF usually starts by taking a pre-trained language model and fine-tuning it using supervised learning on carefully curated data relevant to the target task(s) such as dialogue or summarization. This process results in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Stage}: In this subsequent stage, the SFT model is given prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These pairs are then evaluated by human annotators, who indicate their preferred answer as $y_w \succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ and $y_l$ correspond to the selected and rejected responses within $(y_1, y_2)$, respectively. These human judgments are considered to be driven by an underlying, unobserved reward model $r^*(y, x)$. Several methodologies exist for modeling such preference data, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a standard option (though more flexible alternatives like the Plackett-Luce ranking models \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be applied when multiple rankings are available). The BT model expresses the probability of a preference according to:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed set of comparison data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, a parametric reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ can be introduced, and its parameters optimized using maximum likelihood estimation. Treating this as a binary classification problem, the associated loss is given by the negative log-likelihood:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
with $\sigma$ denoting the logistic sigmoid. In language modeling, the network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ typically builds on the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$ and appends a linear output layer atop the last transformer block, generating a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To decrease reward function variance, prior research normalizes the output such that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for every $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Stage}: In the RL fine-tuning step, the reward model guides the language model through feedback. Consistent with previous research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is posed as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}} \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D},\, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\Bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\Bigr] - \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\Bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) \parallel \piref(y\mid x)\Bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ modulates how much $\pi_\theta$ can diverge from the base reference policy $\piref$, which is the SFT model $\pisft$ at the start. Typically, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is initialized from $\pisft$ as well. This regularization term is crucial as it discourages the model from moving too far away from the data regime where the reward model is reliable, preserves sample diversity, and helps avoid collapsing to a few high-scoring outputs. Since text generation is inherently discrete, this optimization objective cannot be directly differentiated, so reinforcement learning methods are commonly employed. The prevailing method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} reformulates the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) - \beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$, which is then maximized with PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when applying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like fine-tuning language models, our objective is to develop a straightforward method for policy optimization that relies directly on preference data. In contrast to earlier RLHF techniques—which first fit a reward model and then use RL to optimize it—our method utilizes a specific reward model parameterization that allows us to obtain its optimal policy in closed form, thereby eliminating the need for an RL training loop. 

As we will elaborate shortly, our main insight involves applying an analytic mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, which lets us convert a loss defined over reward models into a loss defined over policies. This change-of-variables method bypasses the requirement to train an explicit, separate reward model, yet remains compatible with established human preference models like the Bradley-Terry framework. Essentially, the policy network simultaneously serves as both the language model and an implicit reward function.

\textbf{Deriving the DPO Objective.} We begin with the standard RL objective found in previous work, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined under a general reward function $r$. Building on earlier research~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be easily demonstrated that the solution maximizing reward with a KL constraint, as in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where the normalization term $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ is the partition function. A detailed derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when using $r_\phi$, the MLE estimate of the true reward $r^*$, evaluating the partition function $Z(x)$ is computationally demanding \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, making this form impractical for real applications. Nonetheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be rearranged to express the reward as a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, reference policy $\piref$, and the unknown normalization $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by taking the logarithm of both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and simplifying, we get:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This change of variables can be used with the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model is a function only of the reward difference between two outputs, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Plugging in the reward expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x, y)$ into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} causes the partition function terms to drop out. This allows us to write the human preference likelihood using only $\pi^*$ and $\piref$. Thus, under the Bradley-Terry framework, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ must satisfy:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
See Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2} for further details. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is derived using the Bradley-Terry assumption, a parallel derivation is possible for the more general Plackett-Luce family~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as shown in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

At this point, having expressed the probability of human preference in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward function, we can define a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In a manner similar to the reward modeling framework (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), our policy objective can be written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
Through this objective, we effectively infer an implicit reward by reparameterizing, with the corresponding optimal policy represented by $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because this method is mathematically equivalent to learning a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it retains useful theoretical guarantees, including consistency under appropriate assumptions on the preference data distribution \cite{bong2022generalized}. In Section~\ref{sec:theory}, we explore the theoretical foundations of DPO and compare them with related approaches.

\textbf{How does the DPO update work?} To gain a mechanistic insight into DPO, it helps to inspect the gradient of the objective function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ is given by:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w))}_\text{higher impact when reward ranking is incorrect}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{boost probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{reduce probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward assigned by the policy $\pi_\theta$ relative to the reference $\piref$ (discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to increase the chance of generating preferred outputs $y_w$ while suppressing the likelihood of less preferred outputs $y_l$. Crucially, each training sample is weighted by the extent to which the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overvalues the less preferred option—this is scaled by $\beta$ and reflects how much the reward model misorders the completions, considering the effect of the KL regularization. Our results highlight that this sample weighting is critical; omitting the weighting term (i.e., using a simpler variant) can lead to problematic model behavior, as shown in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}.

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The typical DPO workflow involves: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, annotating them according to human preference to assemble the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize the loss $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ with respect to the chosen $\piref$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and selected $\beta$.  
In real-world scenarios, it is preferable to utilize publicly released preference datasets, rather than producing new samples and collecting human judgments. Because such preference datasets are typically generated from $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when this is accessible. If $\pisft$ is not available, we initialize $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood of the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, by setting ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach serves to reduce the mismatch between the ideal reference distribution, which is not available, and the practical $\piref$ applied in DPO. For additional information on implementation specifics and hyperparameter choices, see Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer an in-depth interpretation of the DPO approach, supply theoretical justification, and connect the benefits of DPO to limitations present in actor-critic methods commonly applied in RLHF, such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Functions as a Hidden Reward Model}  
DPO avoids the need to explicitly fit a reward function or use RL to train the policy, instead relying on a single maximum likelihood objective. Notice that the optimization goal in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} is equivalent to a Bradley-Terry model, where rewards are parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, we optimize the parametric model $\pi_{\theta}$, which is effectively the same as the reward model optimization in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}, after a suitable change of variables. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the expressiveness of possible reward models, and show that it enables the exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, which divides the space of reward functions into equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be stated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce preference framework, including the specific case of Bradley-Terry, any two reward functions belonging to the same category generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in the same optimal policy for the constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are direct and provided in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a commonly recognized ambiguity in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. Due to this ambiguity, it is standard practice to enforce extra identifiability constraints in order to obtain meaningful guarantees for the MLE solutions of Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma indicates that all reward functions within a single equivalence class produce the identical optimal policy, so for our purposes, it suffices to recover any reward function from the optimal class. We establish the following Theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild conditions, every reward equivalence class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically Bradley-Terry) models can be expressed using the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a chosen reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be any reward function, which defines an optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We show that a reward from the equivalence class of $r$ admits the above reparameterization. Define the projection $f$ as  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the log-partition function of $\pi_r$. Because the normalization only depends on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Substituting $r$ with the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any reward), we find $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ produces a representative of the equivalence class of $r$ with the required structure, ensuring our reparameterized reward model is fully general.
\end{sproof}
Alternatively, Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can be interpreted as specifying exactly which reward function the DPO reparameterization selects within each equivalence class, namely, the reward that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, per Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
i.e., $\pi(y\mid x)$ forms a proper probability distribution (non-negative and sums to one).
From Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we see that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} defines the partition function associated with the optimal policy generated by reward $r(x, y)$. 
The essential idea behind the DPO algorithm is that, by applying certain constraints to the underspecified Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference models, we can retain the set of achievable reward models while making the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically accessible for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instabilities in Actor-Critic Methods}

Our framework can also be applied to investigate the sources of instability in widely-used actor-critic methods for RLHF, such as PPO. We adhere to the RLHF methodology and specifically examine the RL fine-tuning phase described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. There is a notable connection between this process and the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement}, as it pertains to the constrained RL setup in \ref{eq:RL}. Given a parameterized policy model $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, the aim is to minimize the KL divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ corresponds to the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and governed by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. After some algebraic manipulation, this leads to the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\left[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\right]
\end{equation}
This matches the objective employed in earlier research \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, where a DPO-like reward is used for the reward family $r_{\phi}$. Here, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be seen as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not impact the optimal policy, omitting it can cause the policy gradient to exhibit large variance, resulting in unstable learning dynamics. While introducing a learned value function to estimate this normalization is possible, it brings additional optimization complexity. Alternatively, some previous approaches have subtracted a reward baseline based on human completions, which is essentially a Monte Carlo estimate using one sample of the normalizer. In comparison, the DPO reparameterization provides a reward function that is baseline-free by construction.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess DPO’s capability to learn policies directly from preference data. We start by investigating, within a controlled text generation environment, how effectively DPO balances reward maximization against KL-divergence minimization relative to popular preference-based algorithms like PPO. We then test DPO on larger models and more complex RLHF benchmarks, such as tasks involving summarization and conversation. Our results indicate that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses competitive baselines such as RLHF with PPO, and also outperforms the approach of choosing the best among $N$ sampled trajectories according to a learned reward. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline the experimental methodology; further experimental specifics are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We conduct experiments on three distinct open-domain text generation tasks. For every task, each algorithm learns a policy from a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the case of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ represents a movie review prompt from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the policy's goal is to produce $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To enable systematic assessment, we \textit{construct} preference pairs over model generations utilizing a pre-trained sentiment classifier, such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we continue GPT-2-large training until convergence on the IMDb training split's reviews (additional information in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the policy is tasked with generating a summary $y$ capturing the main ideas. Following established protocols, we use the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} and employ human preference data gathered by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We employ an SFT model further trained on human-composed forum post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} and utilize the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF library. The human feedback dataset comes from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using samples from another SFT model trained in a similar way. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} task, $x$ denotes a human query, which could range from a physics question to personal advice. Here, the policy must produce a helpful, engaging reply $y$; we employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, comprising 170,000 conversations between a human and an AI assistant. Each dialogue ends with two model-generated responses, and a preference label indicating the human choice. Since a pre-trained SFT model is unavailable for this dataset, we fine-tune a standard language model exclusively on preferred completions to obtain the SFT model.

\textbf{Evaluation.} Our experimental setup incorporates two distinct evaluation methodologies. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective in the controlled sentiment generation scenario, we measure each algorithm's performance by plotting the trade-off frontier between the obtained reward and the KL-divergence from the reference policy. This is feasible since we have access to the ground-truth reward function, provided by a sentiment classifier. In practical applications, however, the true reward function is generally inaccessible. Consequently, we rely on the \textit{win rate} of each algorithm compared to a baseline policy, using GPT-4 as a surrogate for human judgment in evaluating summary quality and response helpfulness within summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. For summarization, the baseline consists of the reference summaries found in the test set; for dialogue, the baseline is the preferred response from the test dataset. Although previous research indicates that LMs can serve as superior automatic evaluators compared to standard metrics \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we include a human evaluation to validate our use of GPT-4 for assessment, as discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our results show that GPT-4 evaluations are highly consistent with human ratings, with agreement between humans and GPT-4 matching or even surpassing the consistency between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a range of established methods for aligning language models with human preferences. For the summarization task, we utilize zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, while for dialogue, we implement 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We also consider the \textbf{SFT} model and the \textbf{Preferred-FT} approach, the latter being a model fine-tuned via supervised learning on the selected response $y_w$—sourced from either the SFT model (for sentiment control and summarization) or a generic language model (for single-turn dialogue). Another method in this category is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which trains the model to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ and simultaneously decrease the likelihood of $y_l$, employing an optional coefficient $\alpha\in[0,1]$ to scale the unlikelihood penalty. We also investigate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, utilizing a reward model trained on preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle version that learns directly from the true reward signal in the sentiment control setting. For sentiment experiments, we leverage two variants of PPO-GT: an out-of-the-box implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and an adapted version with reward normalization and additional hyperparameter tuning for enhanced outcomes (these enhancements are also applied to PPO with a learned reward). Lastly, we include the \textbf{Best of $N$} strategy, in which $N$ samples are drawn from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and the top-scoring output is chosen based on a reward model trained from preferences. Although this method achieves high performance by separating reward model quality from PPO training, it is computationally expensive—even for moderate values of $N$—as it necessitates generating $N$ completions per input during evaluation.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} GPT-4 estimated win rates on Anthropic-HH one-step dialogue; among all approaches, only DPO surpasses the chosen summaries within the Anthropic-HH test set. \textbf{Right.} Win rates measured at various sampling temperatures throughout training. DPO consistently outperforms the original dataset labels over the training period, regardless of sampling temperature.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward maximization framework common in standard RLHF methods ensures that the policy optimizes reward without straying too far from a reference policy. As a result, when evaluating different methods, it is essential to consider both the achieved reward and the KL divergence; obtaining a marginally higher reward at the cost of a much larger KL value may not be preferable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the trade-off between reward and KL divergence for several algorithms in the sentiment task. For each method, we conduct several training runs, each time varying the policy conservativeness parameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and using different random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 experiments. Every 100 training steps, up to convergence, we evaluate each policy using a fixed set of test prompts, measuring both the average reward according to the true reward function and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the total KL-divergence summed across all timesteps.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO establishes the most effective reward-KL frontier, achieving superior rewards while maintaining low KL. This outcome is especially striking for several reasons. First, even though both DPO and PPO optimize the same objective, DPO is considerably more efficient; the reward versus KL trade-off for DPO strictly surpasses that of PPO. Furthermore, DPO outperforms PPO on the reward-KL frontier, \emph{even when PPO has direct access to ground-truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}

We proceed to assess the fine-tuning effectiveness of DPO on tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. In summarization, standard automated metrics such as ROUGE have been shown to correlate poorly with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and previous studies indicate that fine-tuning language models with PPO based on human feedback leads to better summaries. To compare methods, we generate outputs from the test split of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the mean win rate against reference outputs in the test set. Samples are drawn using a range of temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with win rates visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT are all applied to the same GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Results show that DPO achieves about a 61\% win rate at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO, which reaches nearly 57\% at its best temperature of 0.0. DPO also attains a higher peak win rate compared to the best-of-$N$ baseline. It's important to mention that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not thoroughly tuned, so these outcomes may not fully reflect DPO’s capabilities. Additionally, DPO demonstrates significantly more stability across different sampling temperatures than PPO, whose performance drops to the level of the original GPT-J model at higher temperatures. The Preferred-FT method yields only minimal gains over the SFT baseline. We further compare DPO and PPO directly in human preference evaluations in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, finding that DPO samples at a temperature of 0.25 are favored 58\% of the time over PPO samples at a temperature of 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches on a subset of the Anthropic HH dataset test split \citep{bai2022training} containing a single human-assistant exchange. To evaluate GPT-4, we use the preferred completions from the test data as references, determining the win rates across different techniques. Since a standard SFT model is not available for this scenario, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, employ Preferred-FT to train a reference model on the preferred completions to ensure in-distribution outputs, and subsequently apply DPO training. We additionally benchmark against the best output from 128 Preferred-FT completions (having observed that the Best of $N$ baseline levels off at $N=128$ for this problem; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompt version of the Pythia-2.8B base model, discovering that DPO matches or surpasses these baselines at their optimal sampling temperatures. An RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} (from a widely recognized source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}) is also evaluated; however, we are unable to identify a prompt or temperature setting that allows it to outperform the base Pythia-2.8B. Drawing from our TL;DR findings and the shared reward function used by both methods, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO-level results. In summary, DPO stands out as the only efficient technique that consistently exceeds the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, offering results on par with or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}, DPO achieves its optimal performance in relatively few training steps.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach for developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward training method for language models that relies on preferences and operates without reinforcement learning. Instead of forcing the preference learning task into a conventional RL framework to utilize standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This approach allows for directly training language models to match human preferences using a basic cross-entropy loss, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning while preserving generality. With minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO achieves results on par with or superior to current RLHF methods, such as those leveraging PPO; as a result, DPO significantly lowers the barrier to preference-driven language model training.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings bring up a number of key open questions for subsequent research. For instance, how well does the DPO policy extrapolate to novel distributions, especially when compared to methods that use explicit reward signals? Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO policies can generalize comparably to models trained with PPO, but broader investigation is necessary. An open question is whether training via self-labeling using DPO policies can leverage unlabeled prompts effectively. Another point of interest is understanding how reward over-optimization appears in the direct preference optimization framework, and whether the modest dip in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right reflects this issue. Furthermore, although our experiments consider models with up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, cutting-edge architectures remains a promising avenue for future exploration. Concerning evaluations, we observe that win rates as judged by GPT-4 can vary depending on the prompt; hence, refining techniques for eliciting reliable and accurate feedback from automated evaluators is an important future direction. Finally, DPO holds potential beyond optimizing language models based on human preferences, such as training generative models in different domains.
