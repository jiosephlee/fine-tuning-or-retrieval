\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and exhibit certain reasoning abilities, it remains challenging to precisely direct their behavior because of the entirely unsupervised training process.
Current approaches to enhancing controllability involve gathering human annotations that indicate preference between different model outputs and then fine-tuning the base LM to better reflect these choices, typically by using reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF).
However, RLHF can be a complicated and sometimes unstable procedure, as it requires first training a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by fine-tuning the large unsupervised LM through reinforcement learning to optimize this reward—while preventing excessive deviation from the original model.
In this work, we propose a novel parameterization of the RLHF reward model that permits direct computation of the associated optimal policy in closed form, reducing the standard RLHF problem to the optimization of a straightforward classification loss.
This method, which we name \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is efficient, robust, and easy to compute, removing the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter searches.
Our results demonstrate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to align with human preferences as well as or better than prior techniques. In particular, DPO outperforms PPO-based RLHF in sentiment control tasks, and equals or surpasses it in summarization and single-turn dialogue response quality, all while being significantly simpler to apply and train.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Massive unsupervised language models (LMs) developed on extensive corpora have demonstrated unexpected skills~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nonetheless, these models are exposed to data produced by humans who have diverse intentions, preferences, and levels of expertise. Not all of these intents or skills are ideal for imitation; for instance, while it is valuable for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} common programming errors in order to fix them, we would still want the model to favor generating code that reflects the (often infrequent) high-level expertise captured within its training set. Likewise, although it is helpful for a language model to \textit{know} about a widespread misconception held by half the population, we would not want the model to repeat this misconception as fact half the time it is asked! In short, choosing the model's \emph{preferred outputs and actions} from its broad \textit{range of knowledge and skills} is essential for constructing AI systems that are safe, effective, and easily directed \citep{ouyang2022training}. Whereas current techniques generally guide LMs towards human-aligned outputs via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based approach employed by these techniques can be precisely optimized using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, streamlining the entire preference learning process.

Broadly speaking, current techniques shape language models to exhibit desired behaviors by leveraging carefully selected collections of human preferences that capture what people consider to be safe and useful. This preference acquisition phase follows an initial period of large-scale, unsupervised pre-training on massive text corpora. Although supervised fine-tuning on human-curated, high-quality response examples is the simplest form of preference learning, the most effective strategies belong to the family of reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). In RLHF approaches, a reward model is trained on annotated human preference data and then used within an RL framework to adjust the language model’s outputs, aiming to maximize the learned reward signal while keeping the policy close to the original model. Despite their success in producing language models with strong conversational and coding skills, RLHF pipelines are notably more intricate than supervised methods, requiring multiple language model trainings and on-policy sampling within the training loop, thereby incurring substantial computational overhead.

In this work, we demonstrate how a language model can be optimized directly to align with human preferences, without the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a method that implicitly targets the same objective as standard RLHF techniques (maximizing reward under a KL-divergence constraint) but is both easy to implement and straightforward to train. Conceptually, the DPO update boosts the relative log likelihood of favored responses over non-favored ones, yet introduces a dynamic, example-specific importance weight to avoid model collapse—a problem observed with a naive probability ratio approach. Similar to other methods, DPO depends on a theoretical preference framework (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to evaluate how accurately a reward function matches observed preference data. Unlike traditional approaches, which utilize the preference model to define a loss for training a reward model and then optimize a policy against this trained reward, DPO applies a change of variables, expressing the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. With access to a collection of human-labeled preferences on model outputs, DPO enables direct optimization of a policy through a binary cross-entropy loss, yielding the optimal policy for an implicit reward derived from preference data.

The primary contribution of our work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward algorithm for training language models based on preferences without relying on reinforcement learning. Experimental results demonstrate that DPO performs on par with current approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, for preference-based learning tasks like sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue, on language models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models that scale up are increasingly able to solve certain problems without prior examples—either in a zero-shot setting \citep{radford2019language} or using a handful of examples in few-shot prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, their ability to handle downstream applications and respond according to user intentions can be substantially enhanced by fine-tuning on curated datasets containing instructions and human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, referred to as `instruction-tuning,’ allows LLMs to better generalize to novel instructions not found in the original instruction-tuning data and generally makes them more practical \citep{chung2022scaling}. Even with instruction tuning’s success, collecting \textit{relative} human evaluations of output quality is often easier and more scalable than gathering expert-annotated demonstrations. As a result, later approaches have trained LLMs using datasets composed of human preference comparisons, yielding improved results in areas like translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following user instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Typically, these strategies first fit a neural reward model to reflect the observed preferences using a preference structure such as the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, then employ reinforcement learning—most often REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or adaptations thereof \citep{ramamurthy2023is}—to fine-tune the language model to maximize this learned reward. Another related research thread uses instruction-tuned LLMs, further refined through human feedback, to produce additional synthetic preference datasets for specific traits like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying only on weak supervision from rubric-style text guidance during annotation. This convergence combines research on training language models with reinforcement learning across diverse goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning} and on general frameworks for learning from human preference signals \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}.  
While using comparative human preference data is compelling, in practice, fine-tuning large language models via reinforcement learning poses significant obstacles; in this work, we introduce a principled technique for directly optimizing relative preferences without needing RL.

Beyond linguistic applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to the development of various strategies. When contextual bandit algorithms rely on action preferences or rankings rather than explicit rewards, this is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB) framework (\cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). In scenarios lacking direct reward feedback, theoretical work on CDBs often replaces the standard concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that, on average, defeats \textit{any} alternative policy at least half the time \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Nonetheless, the CDB model typically assumes that preference labels are acquired in an online fashion, whereas learning from human feedback usually involves offline datasets with pre-collected, preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based reinforcement learning} (PbRL) focuses on learning from binary preferences determined by an \textit{unknown} underlying scoring function, as opposed to relying on observed rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Multiple PbRL algorithms exist, some of which are capable of making use of offline preference data; these generally follow a two-step process of estimating the latent scoring or reward model, then optimizing with respect to it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, we propose a unified policy learning method that directly trains a policy to align with preference information.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF process described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning}, as well as in subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This process generally comprises three main steps: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT), (2) collecting preference data and training a reward model, and (3) optimizing with reinforcement learning.

\textbf{SFT}: RLHF generally starts by taking a pre-trained LM and further training it using supervised learning on curated datasets tailored to the target task(s) (such as dialogue or summarization), resulting in a model denoted as $\pisft$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Stage}: During this stage, the SFT model receives prompts $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pisft(y \mid x)$. These answer pairs are shown to human annotators, who indicate their preference for one of the answers, denoted as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ is the selected answer and $y_l$ is the less favored answer in the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. The process assumes that these preferences arise from an underlying, inaccessible reward function $r^*(y, x)$. Several methods can represent human preferences, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} model commonly employed. More general ranking models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are also suitable if multiple answers are ranked. According to the BT formulation, the probability distribution for human choice $p^*$ is described as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}
Given a fixed dataset of preference comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we introduce a reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ whose parameters can be learned via maximum likelihood estimation. Treating the task as binary classification, the corresponding negative log-likelihood loss is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ represents the sigmoid function. For language models, $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically built upon the SFT model $\pisft(y \mid x)$, augmented with an additional linear head atop the final transformer layer to output a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. Previous research has normalized reward outputs to stabilize training, enforcing that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the reinforcement learning phase, the language model receives feedback from the trained reward function. Building on previous research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization problem is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x, y)\right] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\left[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\parallel \piref(y\mid x)\right],
\end{equation}
with $\beta$ representing a coefficient that regulates how much the policy can diverge from the reference policy $\piref$, which refers to the original SFT model $\pisft$. 
In practice, the policy $\pi_\theta$ for the language model is initially set to $\pisft$. This regularization term is crucial for ensuring that the model does not stray excessively from the data regime where the reward model is reliable. It also helps preserve output diversity and reduces the risk of mode collapse to a small set of high-reward responses. Since language generation is inherently discrete, the objective is non-differentiable, so reinforcement learning techniques are employed for optimization. A common method \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} is to define the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \piref(y\mid x))}$ and optimize it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Driven by the difficulties encountered when utilizing reinforcement learning techniques for large-scale tasks like language model fine-tuning, we aim to formulate a straightforward method for directly optimizing policies based on preferences. In contrast to conventional RLHF techniques—which first fit a reward function and subsequently optimize it through RL—our method employs a particular parameterization of the reward model that permits closed-form extraction of the optimal policy, eliminating the need for a reinforcement learning training loop. 

As we will elaborate in the following sections, our central idea is to use an explicit analytical correspondence from reward functions to their optimal policies, which allows us to recast a loss defined on reward functions as a loss over policies. This variable substitution technique sidesteps the necessity of learning an explicit, separate reward model, yet continues to optimize according to established models of human preferences, like the Bradley-Terry model. Effectively, the policy network simultaneously serves as both the language model and an implicit reward estimator.

\textbf{Formulating the DPO objective.} We begin with the standard reinforcement learning objective, Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, defined under an arbitrary reward function $r$. Building on established work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it is easy to demonstrate that the optimal policy for the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}
where the normalization factor is $Z(x) = \sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$. A step-by-step derivation can be found in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when adopting the MLE-based reward estimator $r_{\phi}$ for the actual reward $r^*$, calculating the normalization constant $Z(x)$ remains computationally challenging \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, limiting the practicality of this formulation. Nevertheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be rearranged to re-express the reward in terms of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference $\piref$, and $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by applying the logarithm to both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and simplifying, we find:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}
This formulation applies directly to the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry preference model depends only on the reward difference between two responses: ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By substituting the new expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the normalization term cancels out, allowing us to represent the human preference solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\piref$. Therefore, under the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ fulfills:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\piref(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\piref(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}
Detailed steps are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. While Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} utilizes the Bradley-Terry framework, this approach extends to more general Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having now expressed the probability of human preference data in terms of the optimal policy instead of the reward function, we can construct a maximum likelihood objective for a policy parameterized by $\theta$, denoted as $\pi_\theta$. Similar to the reward model-based objective (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the policy optimization objective takes the following form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \piref) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\piref(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\piref(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}
With this approach, we effectively fit an implicit reward through a different parameterization, such that the resulting optimal policy is $\pi_\theta$. Furthermore, since this method is equivalent to training a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits desirable theoretical characteristics, including consistency provided that the preference data distribution satisfies appropriate conditions \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} provides additional discussion of DPO’s theoretical guarantees and its relationship to related methodologies.

\textbf{How does the DPO update work?} To gain a mechanistic perspective on DPO, it helps to examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The derivative with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:
\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\piref) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{larger impact when the reward ranking is inaccurate}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{push up probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{push down probability of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}
where $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$ is the reward assigned by the current language model $\pi_\theta$ in comparison to the reference $\piref$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for details). In essence, the gradient steps promote the probability of preferred responses $y_w$ while demoting the less preferred $y_l$. Notably, each sample’s influence is modulated by how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the value of $y_l$ relative to $y_w$, with this discrepancy scaled by $\beta$. This means samples where the reward model most misorders completions are prioritized, factoring in the effect of the KL regularization. Empirical results highlight that this form of weighting is critical; omitting the coefficient, as done in a basic variant, can lead to degenerate outputs from the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.}  
The typical DPO process involves: 1) Drawing completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \piref(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, assigning human preference labels to build the offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ to minimize $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ given $\piref$, $\mathcal{D}$, and the chosen $\beta$ value.  
In real-world scenarios, it's preferable to leverage existing public preference datasets instead of generating new samples and collecting human feedback. As these datasets are created using $\pisft$, we set $\piref = \pisft$ when possible. If $\pisft$ is unavailable, we set $\piref$ by maximizing the likelihood over preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, i.e., ${\piref = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach reduces the mismatch between the inaccessible true reference distribution and the surrogate $\piref$ used in DPO. Additional implementation specifics and hyperparameter details are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Examination of DPO}
Here, we offer a deeper understanding of the DPO approach, present theoretical justification, and discuss how the strengths of DPO address certain shortcomings found in actor-critic methods commonly applied to RLHF, such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model}

DPO manages to avoid both explicit reward modeling and reinforcement learning, instead learning the policy with a single maximum likelihood loss. Observe that the optimization objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} corresponds to a Bradley-Terry formulation with a reward function $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\piref(y \mid x)}$. Here, optimizing our parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$ is equivalent, under a change of variables, to optimizing the reward model as in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}. In what follows, we develop the theoretical justification for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the expressivity of possible reward models, and show that it enables exact recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are defined to be equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.     
\end{definition}
It is straightforward to verify that this defines an equivalence relation, which divides the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following two lemmas can be formulated:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, including the Bradley-Terry model, any two reward functions belonging to the same class generate identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in the identical optimal policy for the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}
The proofs are elementary and can be found in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-documented under-specification property associated with the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}. To address this ambiguity, it is common to introduce further identifiability conditions in order to ensure meaningful guarantees on the MLE solutions to Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within a given equivalence class gives rise to the same optimal policy. Thus, our ultimate objective only requires us to recover any representative reward function from the optimal class. We establish the following Theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Given mild regularity conditions, every reward class compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically, Bradley-Terry) models can be captured via the reparameterization ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}$ for some distribution $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a chosen reference model $\piref(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{sproof}
    Take any reward function $r(x, y)$ that induces an optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$, as given by Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We will demonstrate that a member of the equivalence class of $r$ can always be written using the above reparameterization. Define the projection $f$ by  
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the log-partition function of $\pi_r$. Since this normalization term only depends on $x$, $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ using the right side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (valid for any $r$), we obtain $f(r; \piref, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the mapping $f$ constructs a member of $r$’s equivalence class with the specified structure, confirming that our reparameterized reward model is fully general.
\end{sproof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} can alternatively be interpreted as characterizing exactly which reward function in each equivalence class the DPO reparameterization yields, specifically the reward satisfying:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, per Theorem~\ref{thm:main}'s reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}
which means that $\pi(y\mid x)$ defines a valid probability distribution (with nonnegative probabilities that sum to 1).
Moreover, by referencing Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it is evident that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function of the optimal policy derived from the reward $r(x, y)$.
The fundamental idea behind the DPO algorithm is to place explicit constraints on the otherwise under-determined Plackett-Luce (and Bradley-Terry) preference models so that the set of representable reward models is preserved, while at the same time, the optimal policy specified in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} becomes analytically manageable for every input $x$.

\subsection{Actor-Critic Algorithm Instabilities}

Our framework can also be leveraged to analyze instability issues in commonly used actor-critic algorithms for RLHF, like PPO. Adhering to the RLHF process, we specifically examine the RL fine-tuning phase as described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. Links can be established with the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} when considering the constrained RL problem in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, the goal is to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy defined in Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model} and driven by the reward $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this yields the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\piref(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\piref(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}
This objective aligns with those utilized in previous studies \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which optimize the DPO-equivalent reward for the particular reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this framework, the normalization component within $f(r_{\phi}, \piref, \beta)$ can be interpreted as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\piref$. Although this normalization does not alter the optimal policy, omitting it can result in a policy gradient with significant variance, which can destabilize training. Incorporating the normalization through a learned value function is possible, yet it can pose optimization challenges. As an alternative, earlier works have employed a human completion baseline to normalize rewards, effectively estimating the normalization with a single Monte Carlo sample. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward formulation that entirely eliminates the need for any such baseline.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we empirically assess how effectively DPO can train policies directly using preference data. First, within a controlled text-generation environment, we investigate how well DPO balances reward optimization against minimizing the KL-divergence to the reference policy, in comparison to standard preference optimization approaches like PPO. Subsequently, we examine DPO's effectiveness on larger model scales and more challenging RLHF benchmarks, such as dialogue and summarization. Our findings show that, with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO generally matches or surpasses strong baselines such as RLHF with PPO, as well as outperforming the approach of selecting the best out of $N$ generated samples according to a learned reward model. Prior to detailing these experimental results, we outline our experimental methodology; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We investigate three distinct open-ended text generation tasks. Across all tasks, methods are trained to optimize a policy based on a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ denotes the beginning of a movie review drawn from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the goal is for the policy to produce $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. For reliable evaluation, we construct preference pairs between model generations with a pre-trained sentiment classifier, ensuring $p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_w) > p(\text{positive}\mid x, y_l)$. For SFT, we adapt GPT-2-large through fine-tuning on positive reviews from the IMDb training partition (see App~\ref{app:sentiment_details} for further information). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ consists of a Reddit forum post, while the policy generates a concise summary $y$ that captures the key points. Consistent with earlier studies, we employ the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl} with human-labeled preferences from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. For SFT, we fine-tune on summaries authored by humans using the model found at \url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft} within the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF environment. The preference labels were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} on outputs from a related, comparably trained SFT model. The last task, \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, has $x$ as a user's prompt, which could be a question (e.g., about astrophysics) or a request for advice. Here, the policy must generate an informative and engaging reply $y$. For this, we rely on the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, containing 170k dialogues between a human and a conversational assistant. Each dialogue concludes with two responses from a large, unspecified language model, annotated with a preference for the more favorable reply. Since no pre-trained SFT checkpoint is provided, we train the SFT model by fine-tuning a generic language model exclusively on responses labeled as preferred.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To assess how well each algorithm optimizes the constrained reward maximization objective, we examine the performance of each algorithm in the controlled sentiment generation scenario by measuring the frontier of attained reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy; this is feasible since we have access to the actual reward function (provided by a sentiment classifier). Conversely, in practical applications, the true reward function is generally unavailable; thus, we evaluate the algorithms using their \textit{win rate} against a baseline policy, leveraging GPT-4 as a stand-in for human judgment to assess summary quality and response helpfulness in the summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks, respectively. In the summarization setting, reference summaries from the test split serve as the baseline; for dialogue, the test dataset's preferred response is used as the baseline. Although prior work indicates that LMs can outperform standard metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we conduct a human study to validate our use of GPT-4 as an evaluator in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show that GPT-4's assessments are highly consistent with human evaluations, often matching or exceeding inter-annotator agreement levels among humans.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, we assess a variety of established methods for training language models to align with human preferences. For the summarization task, we employ zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, while in the dialogue task, we use 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We also evaluate the \textbf{SFT} model alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves fine-tuning via supervised learning on the preferred completion $y_w$—drawn from the SFT model for controlled sentiment and summarization tasks, or from a general LM for single-turn dialogue. Another supervised-style baseline, \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, explicitly encourages the model to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ and \emph{decrease} that of $y_l$, with an optional coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controlling the `unlikelihood' penalty. We further test \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} using a reward model derived from preference annotations, as well as \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle variant that leverages the ground truth reward available for controlled sentiment experiments. In these sentiment tasks, we utilize two PPO-GT versions: a standard open-source implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and an enhanced version that normalizes rewards and adjusts hyperparameters for better results; these enhancements are also applied to PPO runs with learned rewards. Lastly, the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline selects the highest-rewarded output among $N$ samples from the SFT (or Preferred-FT in dialogue), using the reward model trained on preference data. Although this approach achieves strong results and separates reward modeling from PPO optimization, it is computationally expensive for even moderate $N$, as it necessitates producing $N$ completions for each test query.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_temp.pdf}
    \includegraphics[width=0.49\textwidth]{figures/results/dialogue_winrate_vs_steps.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Left.} Win rates as assessed by GPT-4 on Anthropic-HH single-step dialogue tasks; among all methods, only DPO demonstrates a win rate higher than that of the chosen summaries in the Anthropic-HH evaluation set. \textbf{Right.} Win rate trends across training steps for different sampling temperatures. DPO maintains a consistent advantage over dataset labels across various sampling temperatures throughout training.}
    \vspace{-2mm}
    \label{fig:dialogue-main}
\end{figure}

The KL-constrained reward optimization objective commonly utilized in RLHF algorithms aims to maximize reward while limiting the policy’s divergence from the reference policy. Consequently, when assessing different algorithms, it is important to consider both the reward attained and the KL divergence; marginally increasing reward at the cost of a significant rise in KL is often undesirable. Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} illustrates the trade-off curve between reward and KL for various methods in the sentiment domain. We conduct several training runs per algorithm, varying the policy conservativeness hyperparameter in each run (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT). This results in a total of 22 runs. After every 100 training steps up to convergence, we test each policy on a set of evaluation prompts, measuring the mean reward under the ground truth reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Specifically, the sum of KL-divergences across timesteps.} with respect to the reference policy, $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \piref\right)$. Our findings reveal that DPO achieves a significantly more optimal frontier, obtaining the highest rewards while maintaining a low KL. This outcome is noteworthy for several reasons. First, both DPO and PPO are optimizing the same underlying objective, yet DPO is substantially more effective; its reward/KL balance strictly surpasses PPO. Second, DPO produces a superior frontier compared to PPO, \emph{even when PPO has access to true reward signals} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO be applied to large-scale preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess DPO's fine-tuning capabilities on tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue. For summarization, it is known that automated metrics such as ROUGE do not always align well with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier research has demonstrated that language models fine-tuned with PPO on human preference data can generate better summaries. To benchmark various approaches, we sample model outputs from the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset and measure the average win rate compared to the reference outputs. For each method, completions are generated at sampling temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, and the corresponding win rates are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT all use the same GPT-J SFT model as their starting point\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO attains a win rate near 61\% at temperature 0.0, outperforming PPO, which reaches around 57\% at its best temperature setting (0.0). Furthermore, DPO surpasses the highest win rate achieved by the best of $N$ baseline. It should be mentioned that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not extensively tuned, so its performance might be even higher with better calibration. Additionally, DPO maintains its performance much better across varying sampling temperatures compared to PPO, which can decline to the base GPT-J level at higher temperatures. Preferred-FT does not yield notable improvements over the SFT baseline. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we further compare DPO and PPO in human preference studies, where DPO outputs sampled at temperature 0.25 were chosen 58\% of the time over PPO outputs sampled at 0.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using a subset of the test split from the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} where only one exchange occurs between human and assistant. For GPT-4 evaluations, we reference the preferred completions from the test set to determine the win rate of each method. Since there is no established SFT model for this benchmark, we begin with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B, apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model using the selected completions so that model outputs are in-distribution, and subsequently train using DPO. Our comparisons include the top result among 128 Preferred-FT completions (as the Best of $N$ approach plateaus at 128 completions for this benchmark; refer to Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted version of the original Pythia-2.8B, and we observe that DPO matches or surpasses other methods at their optimal temperatures. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained via PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} provided by a recognized source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but cannot identify a prompt or temperature setting that outperforms the base Pythia-2.8B. Given our TL;DR findings and that both approaches maximize the same reward, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate indicator of PPO performance. In summary, DPO stands out as the only computationally efficient method to exceed the quality of preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset, achieving comparable or better results than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} demonstrates that DPO attains peak performance after relatively few training iterations.

\section{Discussion}
Learning from preferences offers an effective and scalable approach for developing competent, aligned language models. In this work, we presented DPO, an uncomplicated training strategy that allows language models to learn from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning techniques. Instead of adapting the preference learning challenge into a traditional RL framework just to utilize standard RL methods, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This connection makes it possible to train language models to adhere to human preferences using only a straightforward cross-entropy objective, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning and without any loss in generality. DPO achieves performance on par with or superior to established RLHF methods, such as those using PPO, all while requiring minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO significantly lowers the hurdles associated with training language models using human preference data.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings open up multiple avenues for subsequent research. One key question is how DPO policies handle generalization to out-of-distribution scenarios compared to policies learned with an explicit reward signal. While our preliminary analysis indicates DPO achieves generalization on par with PPO-based approaches, a more thorough investigation is necessary. For instance, is it possible that self-labeling using the DPO policy enables similarly effective use of unlabeled prompts? Another aspect worth examining is how reward over-optimization appears in the context of direct preference optimization, and whether the small performance decline noted in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right exemplifies this effect. Furthermore, although our experiments cover models up to 6B parameters, assessing DPO's scalability to far larger, cutting-edge models remains a promising research area. In terms of evaluation, we observe that GPT-4-derived win rates are sensitive to prompt phrasing; future work should explore optimal strategies for prompting automated evaluators to obtain reliable assessments. Lastly, DPO offers potential for a range of applications beyond language modeling from human preferences, such as training generative models across different domains.
