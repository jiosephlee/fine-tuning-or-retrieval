\title{DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
The intricacies and highly structured nature of mathematical reasoning make it particularly demanding for language models. Here, we present DeepSeekMath~7B, which is built by continuing the pre-training of DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B using 120B tokens focused on mathematics, extracted from Common Crawl alongside data comprising natural language and code. DeepSeekMath~7B attains a notable 51.7\% on the MATH benchmark designed for competitive problem solving, achieving this result independently of external tool support or voting-based postprocessing. This performance is on par with models like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4. Evaluating 64 self-consistent samples from DeepSeekMath~7B raises the MATH score to 60.9\%. The model's proficiency in mathematical reasoning is underpinned by two principal innovations: firstly, leveraging a carefully curated selection process to maximize the value of publicly sourced web data; secondly, the proposal of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—a refinement of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)—which both improves mathematical reasoning and reduces PPO's memory overhead.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) has fundamentally altered AI-driven mathematical reasoning, fueling substantial improvement in tasks that test quantitative reasoning \citep{MATH} as well as geometry problem solving \citep{trinh2024solving}. These models have also become key tools for aiding human efforts to tackle advanced mathematical challenges \citep{tao}. Despite these advances, leading-edge solutions such as GPT-4 \citep{gpt4} and Gemini-Ultra \citep{gemini} remain proprietary, while open-source alternatives still exhibit a considerable performance gap.

This work presents DeepSeekMath, a language model tailored to mathematical domains that substantially surpasses open-source alternatives and nearly matches GPT-4 in academic evaluations. The development of DeepSeekMath is supported by the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a high-quality pre-training resource containing 120B mathematics-related tokens. Data for this corpus is sourced from Common Crawl (CC) by leveraging a classifier based on fastText \citep{joulin2016fasttext}. Initially, this classifier is trained with positive samples derived from OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath} and negative samples selected from a broad assortment of general web pages. In subsequent stages, the classifier is utilized to identify additional relevant data from CC, which is then rigorously curated through manual annotation. The augmented dataset is used to further refine the classifier, boosting its effectiveness. Empirical assessments demonstrate the robustness of this extensive dataset: DeepSeekMath-Base 7B attains 64.2\% accuracy on GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} and 36.2\% on the challenging MATH benchmark \citep{MATH}, surpassing even the much larger Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}. Furthermore, since the DeepSeekMath Corpus is inherently multilingual, we observe notable gains on Chinese mathematical assessment suites \citep{wei2023cmath,agieval}. We propose that our methodology for compiling mathematical datasets offers a valuable foundation for further exploration, with ample opportunities for advancement.

DeepSeekMath-Base is built upon DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B \citep{deepseek-coder}, as we have observed that leveraging a code-centric model as the initialization point yields superior results compared to starting with a general-purpose LLM. Additionally, we find that mathematical pre-training not only strengthens mathematical proficiency but also improves general reasoning, as evidenced by enhanced results on the MMLU \citep{mmlu} and BBH \citep{bbh} benchmarks.

Following pre-training, we perform mathematical instruction tuning on DeepSeekMath-Base utilizing datasets centered around chain-of-thought reasoning \citep{cot}, program-of-thought methodologies \citep{pot,pal}, and tool-based reasoning approaches \citep{tora}. The tuned model, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, surpasses all other 7B models in its category and attains performance on par with leading 70B open-source instruction-tuned systems.

In addition, we propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which adapts the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm \citep{schulman2017proximal} by removing the critic model in favor of baseline estimation using group-level reward statistics. This modification significantly curtails resource consumption during training. By restricting training to a partial set of English instruction data, GRPO drives notable gains over the already robust DeepSeekMath-Instruct. This is demonstrated through increased performance in both in-domain benchmarks (GSM8K: 82.9\% $\rightarrow$ 88.2\%, MATH: 46.8\% $\rightarrow$ 51.7\%) and out-of-domain problems (e.g., CMATH: 84.6\% $\rightarrow$ 88.8\%) throughout reinforcement learning.

We also introduce a unifying framework to contextualize various methodologies, including Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) \citep{yuan2023scaling}, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \citep{dpo}, PPO, and GRPO, elucidating that these techniques can be interpreted as direct or streamlined forms of reinforcement learning. Comprehensive experiments, such as comparisons between online and offline paradigms, outcome- versus process-based supervision, and evaluations of single-turn against iterative reinforcement learning, are conducted to probe the critical aspects of this framework. Finally, we discuss how our reinforcement learning strategy enhances the effectiveness of instruction-tuned models and outline future research directions for advancing RL using this integrated framework.

\subsection{Contributions}
This work presents advancements in large-scale math pre-training and investigates the role of reinforcement learning.

\textbf{Math Pre-Training at Scale}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item We demonstrate that publicly available Common Crawl data is a rich resource for mathematical content. Leveraging a carefully crafted pipeline for data curation, we assembled the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a robust dataset containing 120B tokens from mathematically relevant web pages. This dataset is nearly seven times larger than the collection of math web pages utilized by Minerva \citep{minerva}, and approximately nine times bigger than the recent OpenWebMath dataset \citep{openwebmath}.

    \item The DeepSeekMath-Base 7B model, which we pre-trained on our curated dataset, attains performance similar to that of Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}. This result highlights that model scale is not the sole determinant of mathematical reasoning; substantial performance can also be achieved by smaller models pre-trained with highly relevant data.

    \item Our empirical studies in math training reveal that exposing models to code training prior to mathematical pre-training enhances their problem-solving abilities in both tool-assisted and direct approaches. This provides partial insight into the question: \textit{does code training enhance reasoning capability?} Our findings suggest it does, particularly regarding mathematical reasoning.

    \item While the practice of training on arXiv papers is prevalent, especially for math-centric projects, we observe no significant gains across any of the mathematical benchmarks considered in our study.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Exploration and Analysis of Reinforcement Learning}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item We present Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a novel reinforcement learning algorithm that is both resource-efficient and performant. Unlike traditional approaches, GRPO dispenses with the critic network by using group scores to approximate the baseline, resulting in a considerable decrease in computational overhead when compared to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
    \item Our experiments indicate that GRPO substantially improves the instruction-tuned DeepSeekMath-Instruct model's capabilities, relying exclusively on instruction-tuning datasets. Additionally, we notice that reinforcement learning with GRPO boosts generalization to domains outside the original training scope.
    \item We offer a comprehensive framework for interpreting a variety of techniques—including RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO—within a unified perspective. Through rigorous experimentation, such as comparing online to offline training, outcome supervision to process supervision, and single-turn against iterative reinforcement learning, we systematically examine the critical components shaping this framework.
    \item Leveraging our unified perspective, we analyze the mechanisms underlying effective reinforcement learning and outline several promising directions for enhancing reinforcement learning in large language models (LLMs).
\end{itemize}

\section{Math Pre-Training}

\subsection{Data Collection and Decontamination} Here, we describe the methodology for assembling the DeepSeekMath Corpus from the Common Crawl dataset. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:data_collect}, an iterative data pipeline is presented, showcasing how to efficiently collect a large-scale, domain-specific mathematics corpus, beginning with a smaller, high-quality seed dataset. Notably, this procedure can be extended to various domains, including programming.

To initiate the process, we select OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}—a curated dataset of high-caliber mathematical web content—as the seed corpus. Leveraging this, we utilize fastText \citep{joulin2016fasttext} to train a retrieval model capable of identifying additional web pages similar in content and style to OpenWebMath. For this purpose, we randomly sample 500,000 entries from the seed corpus as positive examples and an equal number of randomly chosen Common Crawl pages as negatives. The open-source fastText library\footnote{\url{https://fasttext.cc}} is used for model training, with the following settings: a vector dimensionality of 256, learning rate of 0.1, a maximum word n-gram length of 3, minimum word frequency threshold set to 3, and 3 epochs. To minimize redundancy in Common Crawl, URL-based deduplication and near-deduplication approaches are applied, yielding a dataset of 40 billion HTML pages. The trained fastText model is then deployed to retrieve mathematical web pages from this deduplicated set. To further enhance quality, retrieved pages are ranked based on their fastText scores, with only the highest-ranked content retained. The quantity of retained data is determined by running pre-training trials using the top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens; in the initial stage, the top 40B tokens are selected for further processing.

Following the initial round of data gathering, a significant number of mathematical web pages remain absent from the collected set, largely due to the insufficiently diverse set of positive samples used to train the fastText model. To address this, we enhance the seed corpus by identifying and incorporating additional mathematical web sources, thereby allowing for more effective model optimization. In our methodology, we partition the entire Common Crawl dataset by domains, where a domain corresponds to web pages with an identical base URL. For each domain, we compute the fraction of pages that were acquired in the preliminary collection stage. Domains with more than 10\% of their web pages already collected are flagged as being related to mathematics (for example, \url{mathoverflow.net}). 

Next, we perform manual labeling of URLs linked to mathematical content within these selected domains (such as \url{mathoverflow.net/questions}). We add the pages associated with these URLs—provided they were missed in earlier iterations—to the seed corpus. This strategy expands our pool of positive examples, facilitating the development of a more robust fastText model that demonstrates higher recall of mathematical content in subsequent iterations. After completing four rounds of data acquisition, we obtain a dataset comprising 35.5M mathematical web pages, amounting to a total of 120B tokens. During the fourth cycle, we observe that almost 98\% of the data overlaps with that from the third cycle, leading us to terminate further data collection at this point.

In order to prevent benchmark contamination, we adopt the approach proposed by \cite{deepseek-coder}, filtering out any web pages that include questions or answers from English mathematical benchmarks like GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} and MATH \citep{MATH}, as well as Chinese datasets such as CMATH \citep{wei2023cmath} and AGIEval \citep{agieval}. Specifically, any segment of text that contains a 10-gram phrase which matches a substring from any of the referenced evaluation benchmarks is excluded from our training corpus. For benchmark segments that are shorter than 10 grams but contain at least 3 grams, we utilize exact matching to identify and remove such contaminated web pages.

\subsection{Validating the Quality of the DeepSeekMath~Corpus}

To evaluate the quality of the DeepSeekMath~Corpus, we conduct pre-training experiments and compare its performance with recently introduced mathematical corpora:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{MathPile} \citep{mathpile}: A composite corpus consisting of 8.9B tokens from diverse sources including textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, and arXiv, with over 85\% of the data originating from arXiv;
    \item \textbf{OpenWebMath} \citep{openwebmath}: A 13.6B-token dataset distilled from CommonCrawl for mathematical content;
    \item \textbf{Proof-Pile-2} \citep{llemma}: A specialized mathematical corpus aggregating OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack (10.3B tokens of mathematical code), and 28.0B tokens from arXiv. For our experiments on Proof-Pile-2, we adopt the 2:4:1 arXiv:Web:Code data ratio as described in \cite{llemma}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Training Setting}
\label{sec:quality-policy}
We conduct mathematics-specific training on a broadly pre-trained language model consisting of 1.3B parameters, utilizing the same architecture as DeepSeek LLMs \citep{deepseek-llm} and henceforth referred to as DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B. Each mathematical dataset is used to train a separate model for a total of 150B tokens. The entirety of our experiments employs the lightweight and efficient HAI-LLM \citep{haillm} training infrastructure. Adhering to the established protocol for DeepSeek LLMs, the optimization is performed with AdamW \citep{adamW}, configured with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and $\mathrm{weight\_decay}=0.1$. We employ a multi-stage learning rate schedule: the learning rate ramps up to its maximum following 2,000 warmup steps, then decays to 31.6\% of the maximum after 80\% of training, and further drops to 10.0\% after 90\%. The peak learning rate is set at 5.3e-4, the effective batch size is 4M tokens, and each training example is truncated or padded to a 4K token context.

\subsection{Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-Base 7B}

This section details the development and evaluation of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, a foundational model exhibiting advanced reasoning capabilities, particularly within mathematical domains. The model's parameters are initialized from DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B \citep{deepseek-coder}, after which it undergoes training over a corpus totaling 500B tokens. The data composition consists of 56\% from the DeepSeekMath Corpus, 4\% contributed by AlgebraicStack, 10\% from arXiv articles, 20\% comprised of Github code repositories, and 10\% representing natural language content—sourced from the Common Crawl in both English and Chinese. The primary training configuration mirrors the one outlined in Section \ref{sec:quality-policy}, with the exception that the peak learning rate is set to 4.2e-4 and the token batch size is fixed at 10M.

We undertake an extensive evaluation of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B’s mathematical proficiency. Our analysis examines its aptitude for independently generating comprehensive mathematical solutions without external aids, its competence in tackling mathematical challenges utilizing computational tools, and its facility for executing formal theorem-proving tasks. Furthermore, we report on the model's broader foundational abilities, which include assessments in natural language comprehension, logical reasoning, and coding proficiency.

\paragraph{Mathematical Problem Solving with Step-by-Step Reasoning}

We assess the effectiveness of DeepSeekMath-Base in mathematical problem solving by employing few-shot chain-of-thought prompting strategies \citep{cot}, across eight different benchmark datasets presented in both English and Chinese. These benchmarks target a wide spectrum of quantitative reasoning tasks—such as those found in GSM8K \citep{gsm8k}, MATH \citep{MATH}, and CMATH \citep{wei2023cmath}—alongside multiple-choice questions from benchmarks including MMLU-STEM \citep{mmlu} and Gaokao-MathQA \citep{agieval}. Collectively, these datasets span various mathematical disciplines, ranging from elementary to undergraduate-level material.

As presented in Table \ref{tab:base_math_cot}, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B consistently achieves top results across all eight evaluation benchmarks when compared to other open-source base models, which includes well-known models such as Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} and the more recent Llemma 34B \citep{llemma}, the latter of which has been trained on Proof-Pile-2 \citep{llemma} with a mathematics focus. In particular, DeepSeekMath-Base attains a performance margin exceeding 10\% absolute over the leading open-source base models on the challenging MATH dataset and even surpasses the closed-source Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}—a model with 77 times more parameters that extends PaLM \citep{palm} with additional mathematical content.

\paragraph{Mathematical Problem Solving with Tool Use} We assess the models’ abilities in program-based mathematical problem solving using GSM8K and MATH under few-shot program-of-thought prompting \citep{pot,pal}. In these experiments, each problem is approached by having the model generate a Python script, with access to libraries like \textit{math} and \textit{sympy} to handle complex computations. The script’s output serves as the answer for evaluation purposes. According to Table \ref{tab:base_math_pot_proof}, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B exceeds the previous best results set by Llemma 34B.

\paragraph{Formal Mathematics}

Automating the formalization of mathematical proofs supports rigor and productivity in mathematics, drawing substantial interest recently. We measure DeepSeekMath-Base 7B’s capability in informal-to-formal proof generation as described in \citep{dsp_proof}: given an informal statement, a formal representation, and an informal proof, the model must generate a formal proof. Evaluation is conducted on the miniF2F benchmark \citep{minif2f}, designed for formal olympiad-level problems, where few-shot prompting is employed to derive Isabelle-formalized proofs per instance. Following the procedure in \cite{dsp_proof}, the model first proposes a proof sketch, which is subsequently completed using the Sledgehammer automated prover \citep{sledgehammer}. Table \ref{tab:base_math_pot_proof} demonstrates that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B attains strong results on autoformalization of proofs.

\paragraph{Natural Language Understanding, Reasoning, and Code}

Model capabilities in natural language understanding are measured via MMLU \citep{mmlu}; reasoning skills are tested on BBH \citep{bbh}; and programming competence is evaluated with HumanEval \citep{codex} and MBPP \citep{mbpp}. Table \ref{tab:understanding_reasoning_code} illustrates that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B substantially improves over its predecessor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 \citep{deepseek-coder}, on MMLU and BBH, indicating the beneficial effects of mathematical pretraining for language comprehension and reasoning. Furthermore, due to the continued inclusion of code tokens during training, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B maintains its predecessor’s results on HumanEval and MBPP. Overall, it significantly surpasses the general-purpose Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} across these three reasoning and coding assessments.

\section{Supervised Fine-Tuning}

\subsection{SFT Data Curation}

We assemble an instruction-tuning dataset for mathematics encompassing both English and Chinese problem statements, sourced from a broad spectrum of mathematical domains and spanning multiple difficulty tiers. Each problem is paired with solutions that adopt one of several reasoning modalities: chain-of-thought (CoT) \citep{cot}, program-of-thought (PoT) \citep{pot,pal}, or tool-integrated reasoning format \citep{tora}. This dataset comprises a total of 776,000 training instances.

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{English mathematical datasets}: We enrich GSM8K and MATH datasets by providing tool-integrated solution annotations. Furthermore, a portion of MathInstruct \citep{MathInstruct} and the training split of Lila-OOD \citep{lila} are incorporated, where solutions utilize CoT or PoT strategies. Our English dataset collection spans a variety of mathematical areas, such as algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry.
    \item \textbf{Chinese mathematical datasets}: We gather Chinese K-12 mathematical questions across 76 specific subdomains (e.g., linear equations), each annotated with solutions reflecting both CoT and tool-integrated reasoning paradigms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B}

Here, we describe the fine-tuning process applied to DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which is further trained using mathematical instruction data derived from DeepSeekMath-Base. During training, problem-solution pairs are concatenated in random order up to a maximum context window of 4,000 tokens. The training regimen comprises 500 steps with a batch size set to 256, employing a fixed learning rate of 5e-5 throughout.

For assessment, the model's quantitative reasoning capabilities are tested both with and without the aid of external tools, utilizing four evaluation benchmarks in both English and Chinese. Comparisons are drawn with the current state-of-the-art systems:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Closed-source models} considered are: (1) the GPT series, notably GPT-4 \citep{gpt4} and GPT-4 Code Interpreter~\footnote{\url{https://openai.com/blog/chatgpt-plugins\#\#code-interpreter}}, (2) Gemini Ultra and Pro \citep{gemini}, (3) Inflection-2 \citep{inflection-2}, (4) Grok-1~\footnote{\url{https://x.ai/model-card}}, in addition to newly released models from Chinese firms including (5) Baichuan-3~\footnote{\url{https://www.baichuan-ai.com}}, and (6) the most recent GLM-4~\footnote{\url{https://open.bigmodel.cn/dev/api\#glm-4}} within the GLM model lineup \citep{glm}.
\end{itemize}

These models serve general-purpose functions and have typically been subjected to multiple alignment processes.
    \item \textbf{Open-source models} encompass both general-purpose models, such as (1) DeepSeek-LLM-Chat 67B \citep{deepseek-llm}, (2) Qwen 72B \citep{qwen}, (3) SeaLLM-v2 7B \citep{seallm}, and (4) ChatGLM3 6B \citep{chatglm3}, as well as models tailored for mathematical tasks, including: (5) InternLM2-Math 20B~\footnote{\url{https://github.com/InternLM/InternLM-Math}}, which extends InternLM2 with specialized math pretraining and subsequent instruction fine-tuning, (6) Math-Shepherd-Mistral 7B, utilizing PPO training \citep{schulman2017proximal} on Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} guided by a process-supervised reward mechanism, (7) WizardMath models \citep{wizardmath} that enhance mathematical reasoning capabilities of Mistral 7B and Llama-2 70B \citep{llama2} via evolve-instruct (an AI-generated instruction tuning methodology) and PPO training primarily on problems from GSM8K and MATH, (8) MetaMath 70B \citep{metamath}, which refines Llama-2 70B on an enriched set from GSM8K and MATH, (9) ToRA 34B \cite{tora}, adapting CodeLlama 34B for tool-augmented mathematical reasoning, and (10) MAmmoTH 70B \citep{MathInstruct}, a Llama-2 70B variant fine-tuned on MathInstruct through instruction tuning.
\end{itemize}

Table \ref{tab:sft_rl_math} highlights that, under evaluation settings without tool use, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B exhibits exceptional stepwise reasoning abilities. On the challenging MATH benchmark, this model outperforms all other open-source contenders, as well as most proprietary systems (including Inflection-2 and Gemini Pro), by a margin of at least 9 percentage points, even when compared to much larger models (e.g., Qwen 72B) or those specifically improved via reinforcement learning focused on math (e.g., WizardMath-v1.1 7B). Although DeepSeekMath-Instruct achieves performance comparable to Chinese proprietary offerings such as GLM-4 and Baichuan-3 on MATH, it remains behind GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra.

When models are permitted to leverage both natural language reasoning and programmatic tool integration for solving problems, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B attains nearly 60\% accuracy on MATH—surpassing all previously available open-source options. On other evaluation sets, its results are on par with DeepSeek-LLM-Chat 67B, the former top performer, despite being ten times smaller in scale.
\section{Reinforcement Learning}

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization} The use of reinforcement learning (RL) following supervised fine-tuning (SFT) has proven beneficial for elevating the mathematical reasoning proficiency of large language models \citep{wang2023math,wizardmath}. In what follows, we propose a novel RL algorithm—Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—that is both computationally efficient and effective.

\subsubsection{From PPO to GRPO} Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman2017proximal}, a well-established actor-critic reinforcement learning algorithm, is frequently employed during RL-based fine-tuning of LLMs \citep{ouyang2022training}. The algorithm refines the models by maximizing the following surrogate objective:

\begin{equation}
\footnotesize
    \mathcal{J}_{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}{[q \sim P(Q), o \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}(O|q)]} \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} \min \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})} A_{t}, \text{clip} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})}, 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon \right)  A_{

Here, $\pi_{\theta}$ denotes the current policy model, while $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ represents the previous policy. The variables $q$ and $o$ correspond to questions and generated outputs sampled from the dataset of questions and the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$, respectively. The hyper-parameter $\varepsilon$ is used for clipping in PPO, which helps maintain stability during optimization. The term $A_t$ signifies the advantage, derived by applying Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) \citep{gae} using rewards $\{r_{\ge t}\}$ in conjunction with a learned value function $V_{\psi}$. Consequently, in PPO, it is necessary to train a value function simultaneously with the policy network. In order to counteract excessive optimization of the reward model, a KL penalty per token, relative to a reference model, is conventionally incorporated into the reward at each step \citep{ouyang2022training}:

\begin{equation}
    r_{t} = r_\varphi(q, o_{\le t}) - \beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}|q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{ref}(o_{t}|q, o_{<t})},
\label{eq:PPO-reward}
\end{equation}

In this formulation, $r_\varphi$ denotes the reward model, and $\pi_{ref}$ refers to the reference model, often initialized as the SFT model; $\beta$ serves as the weighting coefficient for the KL penalty.

Typically, the value function used in PPO is parameterized by an additional model of similar scale to the policy network, resulting in considerable memory and computation demands. Furthermore, the value function acts as a baseline in advantage estimation to achieve variance reduction during policy optimization. Within the context of LLMs, the reward model is commonly configured to provide a scalar reward only for the terminal token, introducing challenges when training a value function to be precise for each token position. To address these issues, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:grpo}, we introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO eliminates the requirement for an auxiliary value function, as in PPO, by using the mean reward from a set of output samples (corresponding to the same question) as the baseline. More concretely, for each question $q$, GRPO draws a batch of responses $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$, and the policy is optimized by maximizing the following objective:

\begin{equation}
\footnotesize
\begin{split}

\subsubsection{Outcome Supervision RL with GRPO} 
To formally describe, for each input $q$, the previous policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ generates a set of $G$ outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$. Each output is evaluated by the reward model, providing reward values $\mathbf{r}=\{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$. These rewards are standardized by centering with their mean and scaling by their standard deviation within the group. With outcome supervision, the resulting normalized reward is allocated at the conclusion of each output $o_i$, and this normalized value is used as the advantage $\hat{A}_{i, t}$ for all tokens in that sequence, such that $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i- {\rm mean}(\mathbf{r})}{{\rm std}(\mathbf{r})}$. The policy is then updated by maximizing the objective outlined in equation (\ref{eq:GRPO-obj}).

\subsubsection{Process Supervision RL with GRPO} 
Outcome-based feedback provides a reward only at the sequence level, which can lack granularity for effectively directing learning in tasks with intricate reasoning structures. Drawing on insights from \cite{wang2023math}, we consider process supervision, which delivers feedback at each reasoning stage. More specifically, given question $q$ and a set of $G$ sampled outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$, a process reward model assigns scores to each reasoning step, leading to rewards arranged as $\mathbf{R} = \{ \{r_1^{index(1)},\cdots,r_1^{index(K_1)}\}, \cdots,  \{r_G^{index(1)},\cdots,r_G^{index(K_G)}\} \}$, where $index(j)$ is the end-token index for the $j$-th step, and $K_i$ indicates the number of reasoning steps in output $i$. Rewards are normalized in the same way, such that $\widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)} = \frac{r_i^{index(j)} - {\rm mean(\mathbf{R})}}{{\rm std(\mathbf{R})}}$.
Subsequently, the advantage for each token is obtained by summing the normalized rewards assigned to subsequent reasoning steps: $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \sum_{index(j) \ge t} \widetilde{r}_i^{index(j)}$.

\subsection{Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL}

Reinforcement learning is performed on DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. The RL training dataset comprises chain-of-thought formatted questions from GSM8K and MATH, selected from the SFT corpus, amounting to approximately 144K examples. Other SFT questions are excluded to analyze the effect of RL on benchmarks absent from the RL training stage. The procedure for assembling the reward model training set adheres to the approach in \citep{wang2023math}. The initial reward model is trained on DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, with a learning rate set at 2e-5. For the GRPO method, the policy model’s learning rate is established at 1e-6, and a KL penalty coefficient of 0.04 is employed. Each question is used to generate $64$ response samples. Sequence lengths are capped at 1024 tokens, and training batch size is also 1024. After each exploration phase, the policy model undergoes a single optimization step. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B is assessed on the same suite of benchmarks as DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. Within this framework, tasks like GSM8K and MATH that require chain-of-thought reasoning are categorized as in-domain, while the remaining benchmarks are classified as out-of-domain.

Table \ref{tab:sft_rl_math} presents the comparative outcomes of both open- and closed-source models employing chain-of-thought and tool-augmented reasoning on English and Chinese evaluation datasets. Key observations include: 1) DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieves accuracy rates of 88.2\% on GSM8K and 51.7\% on MATH using chain-of-thought approaches, outperforming all open-source models within the 7B–70B parameter spectrum and most closed-source alternatives. 2) Notably, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B's instruction tuning is exclusively based on chain-of-thought-formatted data from GSM8K and MATH, with training initiated from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. Despite the relatively limited range of training data, it consistently surpasses DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B across all assessment criteria, highlighting the impact of reinforcement learning.

\section{Discussion}
This section elaborates on our observations and insights gained throughout pre-training and reinforcement learning experiments.

\subsection{Insights from Pre-Training}

We begin by outlining the lessons from pre-training. Except where explicitly noted, the experimental settings conform to those defined in Section \ref{sec:quality-policy}. For clarity, references to the DeepSeekMath Corpus herein pertain to an 89B-token collection derived from the second cycle of our data gathering methodology.

\subsubsection{Advantages of Code-Oriented Training for Mathematical Reasoning}

There has been a prevalent but insufficiently substantiated belief that training on code bolsters reasoning ability. Our results partially support this, at least in mathematical problem-solving: code-focused training enhances models’ mathematical reasoning skills, regardless of whether external tools are employed.

To explore the influence of code pre-training on mathematical reasoning capacities, we conducted experiments comparing two-stage and one-stage training methodologies:

\textbf{Two-Stage Training}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Code Training for 400B Tokens $\rightarrow$ Math Training for 150B Tokens}: DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B undergoes training on 400B tokens composed of code, and is subsequently fine-tuned with 150B tokens focused on mathematics.
    \item \textbf{General Training for 400B Tokens $\rightarrow$ Math Training for 150B Tokens}: To provide a baseline comparison, we conduct a similar two-phase training regimen in which general language tokens (sourced from a comprehensive general corpus compiled by DeepSeek-AI) replace code tokens in the initial phase. This is to assess whether code tokens provide unique advantages in mathematical reasoning as compared to general language tokens.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{One-Stage Training}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Math Training for 150B Tokens}: The model is exposed exclusively to 150B tokens from mathematical corpora during training.
    \item \textbf{Training on a mixture of 400B Code Tokens and 150B Math Tokens}: Since sequential math training after code exposure diminishes coding proficiency, we explore if a one-stage curriculum where both code and math tokens are simultaneously included could not only support gains in mathematical reasoning but also help circumvent catastrophic forgetting.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results}
The impact of these various training regimes on downstream tasks is reported in Table \ref{tab:code-math} and Table \ref{tab:code-math-general-eval}.

Introducing code-centric training substantially augments program-aided mathematical problem-solving, applicable across both two-phase and unified training schemes. Evidence from Table \ref{tab:code-math} reveals that in a two-stage paradigm, pretraining on code alone substantially elevates proficiency in tasks like GSM8K and MATH when solved with Python. Subsequent math-specific training provides additional refinement. Notably, a one-stage joint exposure to both code and math tokens ameliorates the issue of catastrophic forgetting that is prominent in sequential approaches, while simultaneously boosting both coding (see Table \ref{tab:code-math-general-eval}) and program-aided math (see Table \ref{tab:code-math}) capacities.

Moreover, code pretraining imparts gains in mathematical reasoning even when no external computational tools are used. In a two-phase structure, the first stage with code already yields modest improvements, and serves to enhance the effectiveness of the subsequent math-focused fine-tuning, ultimately delivering superior results. By contrast, combining code and math tokens from the outset in a unified training phase leads to diminished performance in math reasoning tasks absent tool use. This outcome might be attributable to the limited model scale of DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B, which may be insufficient to effectively integrate diverse data modalities when trained in tandem.

\subsubsection{ArXiv Papers Appear Limited in Enhancing Mathematical Reasoning}

Incorporating arXiv papers is a standard practice for assembling math pre-training corpora \citep{minerva,gpt-f,llemma,mathpile}. Nonetheless, comprehensive evaluation of their contribution to mathematical reasoning abilities has been relatively sparse. Somewhat unexpectedly, our empirical results suggest that arXiv papers make little impact on improving mathematical reasoning skills. To investigate this, we evaluated models of varying scales—specifically DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B \citep{deepseek-coder}—utilizing different arXiv datasets subjected to diverse preprocessing techniques:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{MathPile} \citep{mathpile}: an 8.9B-token dataset predominantly (>85\%) comprised of scientific arXiv content, curated using specialized cleaning and filtering rules;
    \item \textbf{ArXiv-RedPajama} \citep{redpajama}: a collection of all arXiv LaTeX submissions, with supplementary material such as preambles, comments, macros, and bibliographies excised, summing to 28.0B tokens.
\end{itemize}

Our approach involved independently pre-training DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B for 150B tokens and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B for 40B tokens using each arXiv corpus. The outcomes indicate that training solely on arXiv papers yields negligible or even adverse effects on mathematical reasoning proficiency. Across a spectrum of mathematical tasks—including quantitative reasoning (e.g., GSM8K, MATH; Table \ref{tab:arxiv-cot}), multiple-choice STEM challenges (e.g., MMLU-STEM; Table \ref{tab:arxiv-cot}), and formal mathematics (e.g., miniF2F; Table \ref{tab:arxiv_atp})—the models demonstrated no clear gains, and sometimes declined in performance.

It is important to acknowledge the constraints of our findings. Our analysis did not address:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item The potential effects of arXiv data on math-specific tasks absent from this work, such as informalization—translating formal mathematical assertions or proofs into informal language;
    \item The synergy of arXiv data when integrated with alternative datasets;
    \item Whether advantages from arXiv papers become evident in substantially larger models.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, additional investigation is necessary, which we defer to future research.

\subsection{Insights of Reinforcement Learning}
\subsubsection{Towards to a Unified Paradigm} Here, we present a consolidated framework for interpreting and comparing various training approaches, including SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO, as well as conduct experiments to elucidate the influential components within this framework. In most cases, the parameter gradient $\theta$ for a given training strategy can be formalized as:

\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{A}}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\underbrace{(q,o) \sim \textcolor{red}{\mathcal{D}}}_{Data \ Source}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|}  \underbrace{GC_{{\mathcal{A}}}(q, o, t, \textcolor{red}{\pi_{{rf}}})}_{Gradient \ Coefficient}  \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right).
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

This framework is organized around three central elements: 1) the \textit{Data Source} $\mathcal{D}$, which specifies the dataset utilized for model training; 2) the \textit{Reward Function} $\pi_{{rf}}$, which provides the feedback signal to guide optimization; and 3) the \textit{Algorithm} $\mathcal{A}$, which integrates the training examples and reward information into a gradient coefficient $GC$—this value governs the adjustment direction and extent during learning. Various prominent methodologies can be interpreted within this unifying scheme:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)}: This approach involves further training of a pretrained model on datasets curated and labeled by humans, enhancing alignment with human preferences.
    \item \textbf{Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning (RFT)}: Building on SFT, RFT performs additional training using outputs generated by the SFT model in response to the same prompts, retaining only those responses deemed correct for further training.
    \item \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)}: DPO further adapts the SFT model by incorporating pairwise comparison signals, training on extended sets of model-generated outputs and optimizing a DPO-specific objective function.
    \item \textbf{Online Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning (Online RFT)}: Unlike RFT, Online RFT uses the SFT model as the initial policy, then iteratively updates the policy using newly sampled and augmented outputs from the current version of the model, enabling dynamic adaptation.
    \item \textbf{PPO/GRPO}: In these reinforcement learning-based strategies, the SFT model serves as the starting point, which is then incrementally improved by sampling responses from the live policy model and applying reinforcement-driven updates.
\end{itemize}

A summary of the method components is provided in Table \ref{tab:data_policy}, and readers are encouraged to consult Appendix \ref{app:analysis-rl} for a comprehensive derivation.

\paragraph{Reflection on Data Source} We categorize data sources into online and offline sampling. Online sampling involves acquiring training data directly from the real-time explorations of the training policy, while offline sampling makes use of data generated by the initial SFT model. RFT and DPO employ an offline sampling strategy, whereas Online RFT and GRPO use online sampling.

According to Figure \ref{fig:rl-analysis}, Online RFT consistently surpasses RFT on both evaluation benchmarks. Notably, although their performances are similar during the initial training phases, Online RFT later achieves a clear performance advantage, highlighting the benefits of online data collection. This can be explained by the fact that, early in training, the outputs from the actor closely mirror those of the SFT model, producing data with minimal divergence. As training progresses, the actor's outputs deviate more, making data sourced from online sampling increasingly valuable.

\paragraph{Reflection on Gradient Coefficient} The mechanism for updating model parameters utilizes a gradient coefficient that reflects the processed reward signal. In our analysis, the reward functions fall under two types: `Rule' and `Model'. The `Rule' type evaluates responses by correctness, while `Model' relies on scores produced by a trained reward model whose supervision originates from rule-based assessments. Equations \ref{eq:GC-RFT} and \ref{eq:GC-GRPO} point to an essential distinction between GRPO and Online RFT: GRPO adapts its gradient coefficient dynamically according to the reward model's outputs, enabling a nuanced balance of reinforcement and penalization across different response qualities. By contrast, Online RFT treats all correct responses equally, granting them identical positive reinforcement, and does not actively penalize incorrect ones.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:rl-analysis}, GRPO outperforms online RFT, underscoring the effectiveness of modifying the coefficients for positive and negative gradients. Moreover, GRPO+PS yields better results than GRPO+OS, demonstrating the advantage of employing fine-grained, step-sensitive gradient coefficients. We further investigate the effect of iterative reinforcement learning by performing two successive iterations in our experimental setup. Figure \ref{fig:iter-rl} reveals that iterative RL substantially enhances model performance, with the most significant gain observed during the initial iteration.

\subsubsection{Why RL Works?}  
In this study, we apply reinforcement learning on a selected portion of the instruction tuning dataset, leading to marked performance gains over the base instruction-tuned model. To provide deeper insight into the underlying reasons for RL's effectiveness, we compare Pass@K and Maj@K accuracies between the Instruct and RL models across two evaluation benchmarks. According to Figure \ref{fig:maj-pass}, RL notably improves Maj@K scores while leaving Pass@K largely unchanged. This observation suggests that reinforcement learning contributes to overall robustness in the model's output distribution, meaning that \textbf{the observed gains stem from better elevating the correct response within the TopK outputs, rather than a general improvement in the model’s fundamental abilities.} Consistent with these findings, \citep{wang2023making} reported a \textbf{misalignment problem} in SFT models for reasoning tasks, demonstrating that a range of preference alignment methods can enhance the reasoning performance of such models \citep{yuan2023rrhf,song2023preference,wang2023making}.

\subsubsection{How to Achieve More Effective RL?}
\label{sec:effec_rl}
We establish that reinforcement learning is highly effective for mathematical reasoning problems and additionally introduce a unified conceptual framework encompassing various well-known training methodologies. This framework interprets all considered approaches as forms of either explicit or approximate RL strategies. Equation \ref{eq:objective} outlines three central elements in this context: Data Source, Algorithm, and Reward Function. We discuss prospective avenues for improvement regarding these three facets.

\paragraph{Data Source}  
The data source represents the foundational input for any training method. Specifically for RL, the data source consists of unlabeled questions, with corresponding outputs generated by sampling from the policy model. In our experiments, we restrict the data to those questions used during instruction tuning and apply straightforward nucleus sampling for response generation. We hypothesize that this limitation may account for RL’s improvement being confined to Maj@K metrics. Moving forward, we intend to assess our RL framework on out-of-distribution questions and to incorporate \textbf{advanced sampling (decoding) strategies}, such as those relying on tree-search algorithms \citep{yao2023tree}. Furthermore, adopting \textbf{efficient inference techniques} \citep{xia-etal-2023-speculative,leviathan2023fast,kwon2023efficient,xia2024unlocking}, which dictate how effectively the policy model can be explored, will also be critically important.

\paragraph{Algorithms} Algorithms utilize the observed data and the reward signal to compute gradient coefficients that adjust the parameters of the model. In accordance with Equation \ref{eq:objective}, contemporary approaches tend to \textbf{TRUST} the feedback from the reward function, thereby modulating the conditional likelihood of generating specific tokens in response to that signal. Nonetheless, there is no guarantee that the reward signal remains dependable, particularly in tasks characterized by high complexity. As an illustration, the PRM800K datasets \citep{lightman2023let}, which were painstakingly labeled by experienced annotators, still exhibit around 20\% annotation errors\footnote{\url{https://github.com/openai/prm800k/issues/12\#issuecomment-1728491852}}. Given these issues, it is necessary to investigate reinforcement learning algorithms that demonstrate resilience to noisy or imperfect reward signals. We anticipate that alignment strategies like \textbf{WEAK-TO-STRONG} \citep{burns2023weak} could significantly reshape the paradigm of learning algorithms.

\paragraph{Reward Function} The reward function constitutes the principal mechanism supplying feedback during training. In reinforcement learning, this role is typically assumed by a neural reward model. We identify three pivotal avenues for developing reward models: 1) \textbf{Improving the generalization capacity of reward models.} The reward model should extend robustly to out-of-distribution prompts and more complex outputs; failing to achieve this may result in reinforcement learning primarily maintaining the existing output distribution of large language models rather than fostering substantive advances; 2) \textbf{Capturing and communicating the uncertainty of the reward model.} Proper representation of uncertainty can potentially serve as a conduit between a limited reward model and weak-to-strong algorithmic frameworks; 3) \textbf{Constructing high-quality process reward models efficiently}, thus enabling detailed, granular feedback that informs the stepwise reasoning process \citep{lightman2023let,wang2023math}.

\section{Conclusion, Limitation, and Future Work} 

This work introduces DeepSeekMath, a model that establishes state-of-the-art results among open-source systems on the MATH benchmark and narrows the gap to proprietary solutions. DeepSeekMath~is based on DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5 7B, trained further over 500B tokens; notably, 120B of those are mathematics-related tokens gathered from Common Crawl. Our comprehensive ablation analysis reveals that content scraped from web sources represents an underutilized reservoir of valuable mathematical material, whereas the contribution from arXiv datasets proved less impactful than anticipated. We present Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a modified approach to Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), capable of advancing mathematical reasoning performance with reduced memory requirements. Experimental findings demonstrate that even when DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B achieves strong benchmark outcomes, GRPO continues to be beneficial. Additionally, we propose a unified framework for interpreting various reinforcement learning techniques and outline several avenues for the advancement of reinforcement learning efficiency.

While DeepSeekMath~records strong performance on quantitative reasoning assessments, its effectiveness remains limited on geometric reasoning and formal proof tasks relative to proprietary models. For example, preliminary evaluations reveal challenges in addressing triangle and ellipse questions, suggesting a possible imbalance in data selection during pre-training and fine-tuning phases. Moreover, owing to the model’s limited parameter count, DeepSeekMath~falls short of GPT-4 in scenarios that demand few-shot learning; GPT-4 leverages such contexts to improve, whereas DeepSeekMath~displays comparable results across zero-shot and few-shot conditions. As future work, we plan to enhance our engineered data selection strategies to yield an even higher-quality pre-training corpus. We also aim to further explore the research directions introduced in Section \ref{sec:effec_rl} to enable more effective reinforcement learning in large language models.
