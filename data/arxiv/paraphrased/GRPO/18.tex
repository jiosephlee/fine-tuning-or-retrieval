\title{DeepSeekMath: Pushing the Limits of Mathematical Reasoning in Open Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
Mathematical reasoning represents a formidable hurdle for language models, owing to its intricate and highly structured characteristics. In this study, we present DeepSeekMath~7B, an extension of DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B that undergoes continued pre-training with 120B tokens centered on mathematical content drawn from Common Crawl, alongside both natural language and programming code corpora. Remarkably, DeepSeekMath~7B obtains a 51.7\% result on the MATH benchmark at the competition level, accomplishing this without the use of external toolkits or ensemble methods, thus nearing the scores of models like Gemini-Ultra and GPT-4. Additionally, employing self-consistency across 64 samples yields a 60.9\% score on MATH. The enhanced mathematical reasoning performance of DeepSeekMath~can be attributed to two principal elements: First, we leverage the vast availability of web-based data using a carefully designed data selection pipeline. Second, we propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an adaptation of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which augments reasoning performance in mathematics while also improving the memory efficiency associated with PPO.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLM) have dramatically transformed mathematical reasoning in artificial intelligence, leading to notable progress on quantitative reasoning benchmarks \citep{MATH} as well as on geometry reasoning evaluations \citep{trinh2024solving}. These models have also shown substantial value in supporting human problem-solvers tackling sophisticated mathematical challenges \citep{tao}. Nonetheless, prominent systems such as GPT-4 \citep{gpt4} and Gemini-Ultra \citep{gemini} remain proprietary, with available open-source alternatives still exhibiting a considerable gap in performance.

This paper presents DeepSeekMath, a specialized language model tailored for mathematical reasoning that achieves substantially better results than previous open-source models and approaches the performance of GPT-4 on standardized academic evaluations. Central to this advancement is the development of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a comprehensive and carefully curated pre-training dataset containing 120B math-focused tokens. The construction of this corpus begins with leveraging a fastText-based classifier \citep{joulin2016fasttext} to filter relevant data from the Common Crawl (CC). Initially, the classifier is trained on positive samples drawn from OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}, contrasted with a wide-ranging selection of unrelated web content as negative samples. This preliminary classifier is used to extract further candidate texts from CC, after which the data undergoes a round of refinement through human annotation. The refined corpus is then used to retrain and enhance the classifier for improved accuracy. Empirical assessment demonstrates the efficacy of our dataset: DeepSeekMath-Base 7B attains 64.2\% on GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} and 36.2\% on the high-level MATH benchmark \citep{MATH}, surpassing the performance of Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}. Moreover, due to the multilingual composition of the DeepSeekMath Corpus, we observe performance gains on Chinese mathematical tasks \citep{wei2023cmath,agieval}. We suggest that our methodology for handling mathematical data sets a valuable precedent for subsequent research, and that considerable potential for further progress remains.

DeepSeekMath-Base is built upon DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B \citep{deepseek-coder}, as we have observed that initializing from a code-oriented pretrained model yields better outcomes than beginning with a general-purpose LLM. Additionally, our findings reveal that mathematical training enhances performance not only in mathematical tasks but also on benchmarks such as MMLU \citep{mmlu} and BBH \citep{bbh}, suggesting that the model’s general reasoning skills are strengthened alongside its mathematical competence.

Subsequent to pre-training, we further refine DeepSeekMath-Base through mathematical instruction tuning using datasets featuring chain-of-thought reasoning \citep{cot}, program-of-thought methodologies \citep{pot,pal}, and tool-based reasoning techniques \citep{tora}. The resulting model, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, surpasses all other 7B models and rivals even the largest 70B open-source instruction-tuned systems.

In addition, we propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an RL algorithm inspired by Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman2017proximal}. Unlike traditional PPO, GRPO dispenses with the critic network and instead infers the baseline from the relative scores within each group, which greatly reduces the computational demand. Leveraging only a subset of English instruction tuning data, GRPO delivers marked improvements over the already strong DeepSeekMath-Instruct model, achieving notable gains on both in-domain (GSM8K: 82.9\% $\rightarrow$ 88.2\%, MATH: 46.8\% $\rightarrow$ 51.7\%) and out-of-domain tasks (e.g., CMATH: 84.6\% $\rightarrow$ 88.8\%) during reinforcement learning.

We also establish a comprehensive framework to interpret diverse approaches—such as Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RFT) \citep{yuan2023scaling}, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \citep{dpo}, PPO, and GRPO—under a unified lens. Our analysis shows that these techniques can all be regarded as direct or simplified forms of reinforcement learning. Extensive empirical studies are conducted to examine key aspects of this paradigm, including comparisons between online and offline learning, outcome- versus process-based supervision, and single-turn versus iterative reinforcement learning, among others. Finally, we elucidate the mechanisms by which our RL strategies enhance the performance of instruction-tuned models and outline promising avenues for developing more effective RL techniques within this unifying paradigm.

\subsection{Contributions}
This work advances scalable math pre-training and provides an in-depth investigation of reinforcement learning methods.

\textbf{Math Pre-Training at Scale}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item We demonstrate that the publicly available Common Crawl dataset contains significant resources for mathematical applications. Through a carefully constructed data selection pipeline, we developed the DeepSeekMath Corpus, a high-quality collection consisting of 120B tokens, curated from web sources with mathematical content. This dataset surpasses that of Minerva \citep{minerva} in scale by nearly sevenfold and is approximately nine times larger than OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}.

    \item DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, our pre-trained foundational model, exhibits performance on par with the substantially larger Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}. This result underscores that model size is not the sole determinant of mathematical reasoning proficiency—a smaller architecture with high-quality pre-training can also reach competitive outcomes.

    \item We report on our math-specific training investigations, revealing that models benefit from code training preceding math training, with observable improvements in solving mathematical tasks both independently and with tool augmentation. This finding provides a partial response to the debated issue: \textit{does code training enhance reasoning?}—suggesting a positive effect, at least within the context of mathematical problem-solving.

    \item Contrary to widespread practices that involve training on arXiv materials for math-related endeavors, our experiments show that incorporating arXiv papers yields no substantial gain across the mathematical benchmarks considered in this study.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Exploration and Analysis of Reinforcement Learning}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item We propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a novel reinforcement learning framework that operates efficiently by eliminating the critic model and instead infers the baseline from group-based score estimates. This approach greatly diminishes computational requirements during training, offering considerable resource savings over Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
    \item We provide evidence that applying GRPO substantially boosts the performance of our instruction-tuned model, DeepSeekMath-Instruct, leveraging only instruction-tuning datasets. Additionally, we notice performance gains on out-of-domain tasks during the reinforcement learning process.
    \item We present a comprehensive framework for understanding a range of methods including RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO. Our thorough experimental analysis—spanning topics such as online versus offline optimization, differences between outcome and process supervision, and comparisons between single-turn and iterative reinforcement learning—delves into the core factors that shape this framework.
    \item Leveraging our unified perspective, we analyze the underlying mechanisms that make reinforcement learning successful, and outline several promising avenues for achieving more effective reinforcement learning strategies for LLMs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Math Pre-Training}

\subsection{Data Collection and Decontamination} In this part, we describe the procedure used to build the DeepSeekMath Corpus from the Common Crawl dataset. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:data_collect}, we detail an iterative framework designed to methodically amass a substantial mathematical corpus, commencing from a seed dataset (such as a small but carefully curated mathematics collection). Importantly, this strategy may also be adapted for data collection in other areas like code.

Initially, our seed data is sourced from OpenWebMath \citep{openwebmath}, a well-curated assembly of mathematical content from the web. This initial corpus serves as training data for a fastText model \citep{joulin2016fasttext}, enabling us to identify further web pages resembling OpenWebMath in mathematical focus. In practice, we randomly draw 500,000 entries from the seed corpus as positive samples, alongside another 500,000 Common Crawl web pages as negative samples. Training is conducted with a public fastText implementation,\footnote{\url{https://fasttext.cc}} using a vector size of 256, a learning rate set at 0.1, word n-gram length capped at 3, a word occurrence minimum of 3, and a total of 3 epochs. To address redundancy in Common Crawl, we perform deduplication at the URL level as well as near-duplicate filtering, ultimately narrowing the dataset to 40B unique HTML documents. Mathematical content is then retrieved from this deduplicated web data using our trained fastText model. To ensure quality, retrieved pages are ranked by their fastText-predicted relevance, and only the highest-ranking pages are retained. We determine the volume of high-quality content to keep by running pre-training experiments on samples comprising the top 40B, 80B, 120B, and 160B tokens. For the first round, we opt to select the top 40B tokens for retention.

Following the initial round of data collection, a substantial number of mathematical web pages remain unacquired, primarily due to the limited diversity in the positive examples used to train the fastText model. To enhance the representativeness of the seed corpus and refine the fastText model, we incorporate additional mathematical web sources. The full Common Crawl dataset is partitioned into non-overlapping domains, where each domain comprises all web pages with the same base URL. For each domain, we determine the proportion of pages gathered in the first collection cycle. Domains in which more than 10\% of web pages have already been collected are tagged as math-related (such as \url{mathoverflow.net}).

We then proceed to manually annotate URLs corresponding to mathematical content within these math-related domains (for instance, \url{mathoverflow.net/questions}). Previously uncollected web pages linked to these annotated URLs are subsequently incorporated into the seed corpus. This process generates additional positive training examples, allowing the fastText model to improve its capacity to identify mathematical web pages in subsequent collection rounds. Ultimately, after four cycles of data acquisition, we assemble a collection of 35.5M mathematical web pages, comprising 120B tokens. By the fourth round, we observe that nearly 98\% of the data had already been retrieved in the third cycle, prompting the termination of further data collection.

In order to prevent benchmark leakage, we adopt the strategy described in \cite{deepseek-coder} by excluding web pages that contain either questions or answers drawn from English mathematical evaluation datasets such as GSM8K \citep{gsm8k} and MATH \citep{MATH}, as well as Chinese benchmarks like CMATH \citep{wei2023cmath} and AGIEval \citep{agieval}. Our filtering protocol stipulates that any text segment containing a 10-gram substring that exactly matches a sequence from the benchmark datasets is excised from the math training corpus. For benchmark contents shorter than 10 grams but at least 3 grams long, we remove contaminated web pages through exact substring matching.

\subsection{Validating the Quality of the DeepSeekMath~Corpus}

To assess the efficacy of the DeepSeekMath~Corpus, we conduct pre-training experiments in comparison with recent mathematical corpora:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{MathPile} \citep{mathpile}: a composite corpus (8.9B tokens) compiled from resources including textbooks, Wikipedia, ProofWiki, CommonCrawl, StackExchange, and arXiv, with the preponderance (over 85\%) originating from arXiv;
    \item \textbf{OpenWebMath} \citep{openwebmath}: a subset of CommonCrawl processed to extract mathematical content, amassing 13.6B tokens;
    \item \textbf{Proof-Pile-2} \citep{llemma}: a mathematical corpus encompassing OpenWebMath, AlgebraicStack (10.3B tokens of math-related code), and arXiv papers (28.0B tokens). For experiments with Proof-Pile-2, we adopt the arXiv:Web:Code ratio of 2:4:1 in accordance with \cite{llemma}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Training Setting}
\label{sec:quality-policy}
We conduct mathematical training using a general-purpose pre-trained language model with 1.3 billion parameters, which adopts the architecture utilized in DeepSeek LLMs \citep{deepseek-llm}—hereafter referred to as DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B. For each mathematical dataset, we fine-tune a distinct model for 150 billion tokens. All training procedures employ the HAI-LLM \citep{haillm} platform, recognized for its efficiency and lightweight design. Mirroring the training regimen of DeepSeek LLMs, we select the AdamW optimizer \citep{adamW}, configuring it with $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.95$, and $\mathrm{weight\_decay}=0.1$. Our learning rate schedule is multi-phased: the rate climbs to its maximum following 2,000 steps of warmup, then falls to 31.6\% of the maximum at 80\% training completion, and ultimately reduces to 10.0\% at the 90\% mark. The highest learning rate is set at 5.3e-4. Training is conducted with batches of 4 million tokens, each batch possessing a context length of 4K.

\subsection{Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-Base 7B}

This section details DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, a foundational model demonstrating robust mathematical reasoning proficiency. The initialization employs DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B \citep{deepseek-coder}, followed by training on 500B tokens. The training dataset composition is as follows: DeepSeekMath Corpus accounts for 56\%, AlgebraicStack for 4\%, arXiv supplies 10\%, 20\% is sourced from Github code repositories, and the final 10\% consists of natural language text from Common Crawl, incorporating both English and Chinese. Training follows the configuration set out in Section \ref{sec:quality-policy}, with the primary exceptions being an increased maximum learning rate of 4.2e-4 and a batch size set to 10M tokens.

A thorough evaluation is carried out to analyze the mathematical proficiency of DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, considering its competence at producing self-contained mathematical solutions without aid, leveraging external tools for problem solving, and formal theorem proving tasks. Additionally, we present an overview of the model's broader skill set, evaluating its capabilities in natural language comprehension, logical reasoning, and coding.

\paragraph{Mathematical Problem Solving with Step-by-Step Reasoning}

The model's ability to resolve mathematical tasks is measured using few-shot chain-of-thought prompting \citep{cot}, spanning eight diverse benchmarks presented in both English and Chinese. These datasets comprise quantitative reasoning challenges (including GSM8K \citep{gsm8k}, MATH \citep{MATH}, and CMATH \citep{wei2023cmath}), as well as multiple-choice formats such as MMLU-STEM \citep{mmlu} and Gaokao-MathQA \citep{agieval}, collectively evaluating mathematical understanding across topics ranging from elementary mathematics to college-level problems.

As illustrated in Table \ref{tab:base_math_cot}, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B achieves the highest scores across all eight benchmarks when compared to open-source base models, such as the widely adopted Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} and the recently introduced Llemma 34B \citep{llemma}, which was specifically math-trained using Proof-Pile-2 \citep{llemma}. Of particular note is DeepSeekMath-Base's performance on the competitive MATH dataset, where it exceeds the accuracy of all existing open-source base models by more than 10\% absolute, and even outperforms Minerva 540B \citep{minerva}—a proprietary base model built upon PaLM \citep{palm} and specialized with additional mathematical data, which is over 77 times larger in scale.

\paragraph{Mathematical Problem Solving with Tool Use} We assess the capacity for program-aided mathematical reasoning on GSM8K and MATH utilizing few-shot program-of-thought prompting \citep{pot,pal}. As displayed in Table \ref{tab:base_math_pot_proof}, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B establishes new state-of-the-art results among open models, outperforming the previous leader, Llemma 34B.

\paragraph{Formal Mathematics}

Automated formalization of mathematical proofs is increasingly valuable for ensuring correctness and boosting productivity in mathematical work. To evaluate this, we test DeepSeekMath-Base 7B on the informal-to-formal proof generation task described in \citep{dsp_proof}, wherein the objective is to synthesize a formal proof using an informal description, its formal equivalent, and a corresponding informal proof. We benchmark this capability using miniF2F \citep{minif2f}, a dataset focusing on formalizations of Olympiad-level problems, and prompt the model with a few examples to produce Isabelle-formatted proofs for each question. In alignment with \cite{dsp_proof}, we have models produce high-level proof outlines, and then leverage the automated prover Sledgehammer \citep{sledgehammer} to complete any missing segments. For evaluation in natural language comprehension, reasoning, and programming, we test models on MMLU \citep{mmlu} for language understanding, BBH \citep{bbh} for complex reasoning, and HumanEval \citep{codex} along with MBPP \citep{mbpp} for code generation. Table \ref{tab:understanding_reasoning_code} shows that DeepSeekMath-Base 7B delivers substantial gains on MMLU and BBH compared to its predecessor, DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 \citep{deepseek-coder}, underscoring the benefits of extensive math-related training for both understanding and reasoning capabilities. Furthermore, through the integration of code-related tokens in continued training, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B successfully preserves the coding proficiency of DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 on both coding benchmarks. Overall, DeepSeekMath-Base 7B markedly surpasses the general-purpose Mistral 7B \citep{mistral} on all three reasoning and programming tasks.

\section{Supervised Fine-Tuning}

\subsection{SFT Data Curation}

We assemble an instruction-tuning dataset for mathematics, incorporating problems in both English and Chinese that span multiple domains and difficulty levels. Each problem is matched with a solution written in chain-of-thought (CoT) \citep{cot}, program-of-thought (PoT) \citep{pot,pal}, or tool-integrated reasoning style \citep{tora}. The dataset consists of a total of 776K training samples.

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{English mathematical datasets}: We provide tool-integrated annotations for problems from GSM8K and MATH, and include selections from MathInstruct \citep{MathInstruct} and the Lila-OOD training set \citep{lila}, where answers follow the CoT or PoT formats. The English portion encompasses a wide array of mathematical areas, including but not limited to algebra, probability, number theory, calculus, and geometry.
    \item \textbf{Chinese mathematical datasets}: We gather a collection of K-12 math questions in Chinese, distributed over 76 specific topics such as linear equations, and annotate the answers in both CoT and tool-integrated formats.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B}

Here, we detail the development of DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B, which is subjected to instruction-based mathematical tuning on top of DeepSeekMath-Base. Training instances are randomly concatenated to utilize up to 4K tokens of context. The model is optimized for 500 training steps with a batch size of 256 and a fixed learning rate of 5e-5.

For evaluation, we assess the mathematical reasoning capabilities of various models—both with and without external tools—using four quantitative reasoning benchmarks available in both English and Chinese. We compare our model’s results to those of the top-performing models at the time:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]

These models are intended for a wide range of applications and have typically been subjected to various alignment methods.

\item \textbf{Open-source models} featured in our analysis encompass both general-purpose and mathematics-enhanced models: (1) DeepSeek-LLM-Chat 67B \citep{deepseek-llm}, (2) Qwen 72B \citep{qwen}, (3) SeaLLM-v2 7B \citep{seallm}, and (4) ChatGLM3 6B \citep{chatglm3}. As displayed in Table \ref{tab:sft_rl_math}, in a scenario where tool usage is not permitted, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B delivers robust step-by-step reasoning performance. On the MATH dataset, representative of competition-grade tasks, our model outperforms all evaluated open-source alternatives and most closed-source competitors (such as Inflection-2 and Gemini Pro) by an absolute margin of at least 9\%. This remains the case even when compared to models with significantly larger parameter counts (e.g., Qwen 72B) or those with dedicated math RL training (such as WizardMath-v1.1 7B). Although DeepSeekMath-Instruct matches the performance of proprietary Chinese models GLM-4 and Baichuan-3 on MATH, it is still outperformed by GPT-4 and Gemini Ultra.

In evaluation setups that allow combining natural language and programmatic tool use for task resolution, DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B attains nearly 60\% accuracy on the MATH benchmark, achieving a new state-of-the-art among open-source models. Across additional benchmarks, our model is comparable to DeepSeek-LLM-Chat 67B, the preceding leading model with a much larger scale.

\section{Reinforcement Learning}

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization} Reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated significant utility in enhancing LLMs' mathematical reasoning following the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) phase \citep{wang2023math, wizardmath}. This section details our novel RL strategy, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which is both computationally efficient and effective.

\subsubsection{From PPO to GRPO} Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman2017proximal} stands as a prominent actor-critic RL framework, often used for the RL fine-tuning of LLMs \citep{ouyang2022training}. PPO's optimization centers on maximizing the surrogate objective below:

\begin{equation}
\footnotesize
    \mathcal{J}_{PPO}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}{[q \sim P(Q), o \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}(O|q)]} \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|} \min \left[ \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})} A_{t}, \text{clip} \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_{t} | q, o_{<t})}, 1 - \varepsilon,

Here, $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$ represent the current and previous policy models, respectively, while $q$ and $o$ denote questions and outputs sampled both from the dataset of questions and the historical policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. The parameter $\varepsilon$ serves as a clipping hyper-parameter, introduced in PPO, with the intent of providing stability during the training process. The term $A_t$ refers to the advantage, estimated using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) \citep{gae}, leveraging the reward signals $\{r_{\ge t}\}$ and predictions from a value function $V_{\psi}$. Consequently, PPO necessitates training a value function in tandem with the policy, and to counteract reward model over-optimization, it is standard practice to include a per-token KL penalty, derived from a reference model, into the reward for each token \citep{ouyang2022training}, i.e.,

\begin{equation}
    r_{t} = r_\varphi(q, o_{\le t}) - \beta \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{t}|q, o_{<t})}{\pi_{ref}(o_{t}|q, o_{<t})},
\label{eq:PPO-reward}
\end{equation}

where $r_\varphi$ stands for the reward model, $\pi_{ref}$ denotes the reference model (commonly the original SFT model), and $\beta$ specifies the weight of the KL penalty term.

Training a value function alongside the policy in PPO typically involves using an additional model that is of a similar scale to the policy, which imposes a notable burden in terms of both computation and memory resources. Moreover, the value function is utilized as a baseline in the computation of the advantage during RL updates, aiming to reduce variance. However, in the context of LLMs, rewards are typically only assigned to the final token by the reward model, potentially creating challenges in fitting a reliable value function across all tokens. To overcome this, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:grpo}, we introduce Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which eliminates the need for an explicit value function as in PPO. Instead, GRPO utilizes the mean reward from several sampled responses, all generated for the same question, to form a baseline. Specifically, for any given question $q$, GRPO samples a set of outputs $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ using the previous policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$, then seeks to maximize the objective given by:

\begin{equation}
\footnotesize
\begin{split}

\subsubsection{Outcome Supervision RL with GRPO} Given a specific question $q$, the approach involves sampling a set of $G$ candidate responses $\{o_1, o_2, \cdots, o_G\}$ from the previous policy model $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. Each of these outputs is evaluated by a reward model, which produces $G$ scores, $\mathbf{r} = \{r_1, r_2, \cdots, r_G\}$. These reward values are then standardized within the group by subtracting their mean and dividing by their standard deviation. For outcome supervision, only the normalized reward at the completion of each output $o_i$ is considered, assigning this value as the advantage $\hat{A}_{i,t}$ for every token $t$ in $o_i$, formally $\hat{A}_{i, t} = \widetilde{r}_i = \frac{r_i- {\rm mean}(\mathbf{r})}{{\rm std}(\mathbf{r})}$. The policy is optimized via the objective described in equation (\ref{eq:GRPO-obj}).

To address this, we implement an iterative RL approach with GRPO. \subsection{Training and Evaluating DeepSeekMath-RL}

Our RL experiments are centered on DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. For the RL training phase, we use chain-of-thought questions from GSM8K and MATH as drawn from the SFT corpus, yielding approximately 144K questions. Other SFT questions are omitted in order to more accurately assess RL effects on benchmarks that remain unseen throughout RL. We assemble the reward model training set according to the methodology outlined in \citep{wang2023math}. The initial reward model is fine-tuned from DeepSeekMath-Base 7B, employing a learning rate of 2e-5. In the GRPO framework, the policy model uses a learning rate of 1e-6, and the KL divergence coefficient is set at 0.04. For each prompt, $64$ responses are sampled. The maximum output length is fixed at 1024 tokens and the batch size is set at 1024. The policy model is updated a single time after every round of exploration. Evaluation of DeepSeekMath-RL 7B utilizes the same benchmarks as DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. For DeepSeekMath-RL 7B, GSM8K and MATH with chain-of-thought solutions are classified as in-domain, while all other benchmarks are considered out-of-domain.

Table \ref{tab:sft_rl_math} presents the results of both open-source and closed-source models, evaluating their performance with chain-of-thought and tool-integrated reasoning approaches on benchmarks in English and Chinese. Our analysis reveals the following key points: 1) Using chain-of-thought reasoning, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B achieves 88.2\% accuracy on GSM8K and 51.7\% on MATH, outperforming every open-source model in the 7B to 70B parameter range as well as most closed-source alternatives. 2) Notably, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B is exclusively fine-tuned using chain-of-thought-style instruction data from GSM8K and MATH, initializing from DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B. Despite the narrow focus of its instruction tuning data, DeepSeekMath-RL 7B demonstrates consistently superior performance relative to DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B on all evaluation criteria, highlighting the value introduced by reinforcement learning.

\section{Discussion}
This section provides insights gained from both pre-training and reinforcement learning experiments.

\subsection{Lessons Learnt in Pre-Training}

We begin by discussing observations related to pre-training. Except when specified otherwise, the training configurations follow those described in Section \ref{sec:quality-policy}. Additionally, references to the DeepSeekMath Corpus within this discussion denote an 89B-token dataset constructed during the second data collection iteration.

\subsubsection{Code Training Benefits Mathematical Reasoning}

The notion that code-focused training enhances reasoning abilities has gained popularity, although supporting evidence remains limited. Here, we offer preliminary findings in the mathematical context: code pre-training facilitates models' capabilities for mathematical reasoning, independent of whether external tools are involved.

To investigate the influence of code training on mathematical reasoning, we explored both two-stage and single-stage training paradigms:

\textbf{Two-Stage Training}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Code Training for 400B Tokens $\rightarrow$ Math Training for 150B Tokens}: DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B undergoes an initial phase of training on 400B code tokens, which is then succeeded by an additional 150B tokens specifically focusing on math.
    \item \textbf{General Training for 400B Tokens $\rightarrow$ Math Training for 150B Tokens}: For comparative purposes, we replace code tokens with general tokens—sampled from a comprehensive general dataset curated by DeepSeek-AI—during the first phase, in order to analyze whether exposure to code, as opposed to general text, provides a unique advantage in fostering mathematical reasoning skills.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{One-Stage Training}

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Math Training for 150B Tokens}: DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B is trained solely on 150B math tokens in a single stage.
    \item \textbf{Training on a mixture of 400B Code Tokens and 150B Math Tokens}: Observations indicate that when math training follows code training in sequence, the model’s code-related abilities are negatively affected. Therefore, we explore if jointly presenting code and math tokens during a unified training phase could preserve code performance while still enhancing mathematical reasoning, thereby minimizing catastrophic forgetting.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results}
Tables \ref{tab:code-math} and \ref{tab:code-math-general-eval} summarize the impact of various training paradigms on downstream tasks.

Both two-stage and one-stage approaches demonstrate that code-focused pretraining markedly strengthens program-based mathematical problem solving. As detailed in Table \ref{tab:code-math}, initiating training with code tokens before transitioning to math-specific data yields noticeable gains in handling benchmarks such as GSM8K and MATH via Python programs, with additional improvements attained through subsequent math fine-tuning. Notably, when code and math tokens are combined within a single-stage curriculum, the risk of catastrophic forgetting seen in sequential (two-stage) methods is considerably reduced, leading to stronger code competency (Table \ref{tab:code-math-general-eval}) as well as program-assisted mathematical performance (Table \ref{tab:code-math}).

Furthermore, code pretraining advances mathematical reasoning skills even without reliance on external tools. In the two-stage protocol, the inclusion of code tokens during the initial stage offers measurable gains and sets a stronger foundation for subsequent math training, resulting in peak model performance. However, an integrated (mixed) code-math curriculum for one-stage training appears to impair pure mathematical reasoning in the absence of tool usage. One plausible explanation is that DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B may lack sufficient model capacity to effectively absorb and leverage both code and mathematical inputs simultaneously.

\subsubsection{ArXiv Papers Appear Unhelpful for Enhancing Mathematical Reasoning}

Although arXiv papers constitute a standard part of pre-training datasets for mathematics-focused models \citep{minerva,gpt-f,llemma,mathpile}, there has been little comprehensive evaluation of their effectiveness in fostering mathematical reasoning. Somewhat surprisingly, our experimental findings suggest that arXiv papers do not substantially enhance models' mathematical reasoning skills. We assessed this by employing models of various scales—including DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B \citep{deepseek-coder}—using distinct versions of arXiv corpora processed through different data preparation pipelines:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{MathPile} \citep{mathpile}: a cleaned and filtered dataset amounting to 8.9B tokens, with over 85\% comprised of scientific arXiv articles;
    \item \textbf{ArXiv-RedPajama} \citep{redpajama}: a 28.0B-token collection containing all arXiv LaTeX files, stripped of elements such as preambles, macros, comments, and bibliographies.
\end{itemize}

Our experiments involved independently training DeepSeek-LLM 1.3B (for 150B tokens) and DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B (for 40B tokens) on each of these arXiv datasets. Results indicate that exposure to arXiv material alone fails to yield measurable gains in mathematical reasoning abilities; in some cases, performance across various mathematical assessments even declines. This trend persists across benchmarks representing a range of mathematical difficulties: from quantitative reasoning tasks such as GSM8K and MATH (see Table \ref{tab:arxiv-cot}) and multiple-choice exams like MMLU-STEM (Table \ref{tab:arxiv-cot}) to formal mathematics challenges typified by miniF2F (Table \ref{tab:arxiv_atp}).

Nonetheless, our observations come with notable caveats. Specifically, our study did not address:

\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt]
    \item How arXiv-derived tokens might influence specialized math tasks outside the scope of our benchmarks—for example, tasks involving the conversion of formal statements to informal language;
    \item The potential impact when arXiv data is mixed with other training corpora;
    \item Whether arXiv content could become beneficial for mathematical reasoning when training much larger model architectures.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, more comprehensive studies are warranted to probe these aspects, which we propose to leave for future investigation.

\subsection{Insights of Reinforcement Learning}
\subsubsection{Toward a Unified Framework} Here, we introduce a general framework for analyzing diverse training methodologies such as SFT, RFT, DPO, PPO, and GRPO, and further run experiments to identify influential elements within this unified structure. Broadly, the gradient with respect to parameters $\theta$ for each training approach can be formulated as:

\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{J}_{\textcolor{red}{\mathcal{A}}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\underbrace{(q,o) \sim \textcolor{red}{\mathcal{D}}}_{Data \ Source}]\left( \frac{1}{|o|} \sum_{t=1}^{|o|}  \underbrace{GC_{{\mathcal{A}}}(q, o, t, \textcolor{red}{\pi_{{rf}}})}_{Gradient \ Coefficient}  \nabla_{\theta}\log \pi_{\theta}(o_t | q, o_{<t})\right).
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}

This framework is defined by three central elements: 1) the \textit{Data Source $\mathcal{D}$}, which specifies the dataset employed for training; 2) the \textit{Reward Function $\pi_{{rf}}$}, providing the reward signal that guides training; and 3) the \textit{Algorithm $\mathcal{A}$}, which leverages the training examples and corresponding rewards to calculate the gradient coefficient $GC$, thereby influencing the degree of penalization or reinforcement applied to each instance. Within this unified perspective, we examine multiple prominent approaches:

\begin{itemize}
    \item  \textbf{Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)}: SFT updates a pretrained model by adapting it to human-curated datasets through additional supervised training.
    \item \textbf{Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning (RFT)}: Building upon SFT, RFT involves an extra stage where outputs generated by the SFT model for selected prompts are filtered according to answer correctness, and the filtered responses are used for further model fine-tuning.
    \item \textbf{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)}: DPO further optimizes the SFT model, employing a pairwise loss computed on augmented samples produced by the SFT model to better align with preference signals.
    \item \textbf{Online Rejection Sampling Fine-tuning (Online RFT)}: Unlike standard RFT, Online RFT starts from an SFT-initialized policy and progressively updates it through fine-tuning on outputs generated by the live (i.e., current) version of the policy model.
    \item \textbf{PPO/GRPO}: These approaches initialize from the SFT-trained model as the policy and enhance it using reinforcement learning, relying on samples drawn from the live policy during training.
\end{itemize}

Table \ref{tab:data_policy} provides an overview of the various method components. For a comprehensive discussion of the derivation process, see Appendix \ref{app:analysis-rl}.

\paragraph{Observation about Data Source} We categorize the sources of training data as either online or offline sampling. Online sampling refers to generating data during the active training process by leveraging the evolving policy model, whereas offline sampling utilizes data produced by the initial SFT model. RFT and DPO utilize offline sampling strategies, whereas Online RFT and GRPO adopt online sampling.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:rl-analysis}, our analysis reveals that Online RFT achieves markedly superior results compared to RFT on both benchmarks. In the initial training phases, the performance of Online RFT closely matches that of RFT, but as training advances, Online RFT secures a clear lead, underscoring the effectiveness of online approaches. This outcome is anticipated: early on, the policy model and the SFT model are similar, leading to marginal variations in sampled data. As training progresses, the differences in data sampled from the current policy become more pronounced, and the benefits of collecting data in real-time are amplified.

\paragraph{Observation about Gradient Coefficient} The model parameters are updated using a gradient coefficient derived from the reward signal. In our experimental setup, the reward function takes two forms: `Rule' and `Model'. The 'Rule' approach assesses the response quality based on correctness, whereas 'Model' employs a learned reward model trained on labels produced by rule-based assessments to evaluate responses. Equations \ref{eq:GC-RFT} and \ref{eq:GC-GRPO} delineate a crucial distinction: GRPO dynamically modifies its gradient coefficient in accordance with the reward value assigned by the reward model. This mechanism enables it to apply differentiated reinforcement or penalty, reflecting the response's score magnitude. Conversely, Online RFT does not implement this granularity—it refrains from penalizing incorrect outputs and provides equal reinforcement strength to all responses judged as correct.

As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:rl-analysis}, GRPO outperforms online RFT, underscoring the effectiveness of modifying positive and negative gradient coefficients. Moreover, the combination GRPO+PS delivers better results than GRPO+OS, suggesting that employing fine-grained, step-sensitive gradient coefficients is advantageous. In addition, we investigate iterative RL by carrying out two cycles in our experiments. The results in Figure \ref{fig:iter-rl} demonstrate that iterative RL leads to substantial performance gains, with the most noticeable improvement occurring during the initial iteration.

\subsubsection{Why RL Works?} This study applies reinforcement learning to a subset of instruction tuning data, leading to significant gains over the instruction-tuned baseline model. To further elucidate the reasons behind RL’s effectiveness, we assess Pass@K and Maj@K accuracies of both Instruct and RL-optimized models on two benchmarks. Figure \ref{fig:maj-pass} shows that RL enhances Maj@K but not Pass@K, implying that RL contributes to making the output distribution more reliable overall; \textbf{in other words, the performance boost appears to result from promoting the correct answer into the TopK rather than from strengthening basic modeling abilities.} Similarly, \citep{wang2023making} pointed out a \textbf{misalignment problem} within reasoning tasks in SFT models, and further works \citep{yuan2023rrhf,song2023preference,wang2023making} have found that a range of preference alignment strategies can bolster SFT model performance in reasoning scenarios.

\subsubsection{How to Achieve More Effective RL?}
\label{sec:effec_rl}
Our experiments establish that RL is highly effective for mathematical reasoning challenges. We also introduce a unified framework that encompasses a range of representative training techniques, categorizing them all as forms of direct or simplified RL. Equation \ref{eq:objective} summarizes this paradigm, highlighting three foundational components: Data Source, Algorithm, and Reward Function. We outline potential avenues for advancing these elements.

\paragraph{Data Source} The data source provides the fundamental basis for any training approach. Specifically in RL contexts, the data source refers to unlabeled queries paired with outputs sampled from the current policy model. In our work, we rely solely on questions from the instruction tuning phase and utilize a basic nucleus sampling method to produce model outputs. We posit that this choice might explain why our RL setup primarily boosts Maj@K performance. Moving forward, we intend to apply our RL pipeline to prompts outside the distribution seen during training, and we will experiment with \textbf{more sophisticated sampling (decoding) techniques}, such as those informed by tree-search algorithms \citep{yao2023tree}. In addition, the adoption of \textbf{efficient inference methods} \citep{xia-etal-2023-speculative,leviathan2023fast,kwon2023efficient,xia2024unlocking}, which govern how effectively policy models explore, will be critically important.

\paragraph{Algorithms} Algorithms utilize both data and the reward signal to compute the gradient coefficient, which is then used to update the parameters of the model. Referring to Equation \ref{eq:objective}, existing approaches, to a large degree, \textbf{TRUST} the reward signal entirely when adjusting the conditional probability of generating a specific token. Nonetheless, it is not feasible to guarantee the constant reliability of the reward signal, particularly when tackling highly complex tasks. For instance, the PRM800K dataset \citep{lightman2023let}, despite rigorous annotation by skilled annotators, still includes about 20\% erroneous labels\footnote{\url{https://github.com/openai/prm800k/issues/12\#issuecomment-1728491852}}. Motivated by these challenges, our investigation centers on reinforcement learning algorithms designed to tolerate noisy reward signals. We are convinced that alignment approaches categorized as \textbf{WEAK-TO-STRONG} \citep{burns2023weak} have the potential to revolutionize existing learning paradigms.

\paragraph{Reward Function} The reward function constitutes the primary training signal in RL and is typically implemented as a neural reward model. We identify three critical avenues for advancing reward models: 1) \textbf{Improving the reward model’s capacity to generalize.} For reinforcement learning to truly enhance the core abilities of LLMs, the reward model must reliably handle both out-of-distribution queries and sophisticated decoding behaviors, or else it may merely preserve current distributions; 2) \textbf{Capturing and representing reward model uncertainty.} Such uncertainty could serve as a conceptual connector between underperforming reward models and weak-to-strong learning frameworks; 3) \textbf{Constructing high-quality process reward models efficiently,} capable of delivering granular training feedback throughout the reasoning trajectory \citep{lightman2023let,wang2023math}.

\section{Conclusion, Limitation, and Future Work} 

This work introduces DeepSeekMath, a model that achieves superior results relative to all publicly available models on the MATH benchmark, and demonstrates performance close to that of proprietary systems. DeepSeekMath~is based on the DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5 7B architecture and is further developed through 500B tokens of continual training, with a substantial portion---120B math-specific tokens---collected from Common Crawl. Our detailed ablation experiments suggest that content derived from web pages serves as a promising resource for high-quality mathematical data, while arXiv-based data contributes less than anticipated. Additionally, we propose Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), an adaptation of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which enhances mathematical reasoning performance while consuming less memory. Experimental analysis indicates that even when DeepSeekMath-Instruct 7B already performs strongly on benchmarks, GRPO leads to further improvements. We also articulate a unified perspective for understanding various methodologies in this domain, and outline several prospective avenues for optimizing reinforcement learning approaches.

Despite DeepSeekMath~displaying strong results in benchmarks of quantitative reasoning, its effectiveness is comparatively limited in areas such as geometry and theorem proving when set against closed-source models. As observed during our initial testing, the model encounters difficulties with problems involving triangles and ellipses, highlighting possible data selection biases introduced during both pre-training and fine-tuning phases. The limited model size also places DeepSeekMath~behind GPT-4 in few-shot learning scenarios; whereas GPT-4 benefits from few-shot prompts, DeepSeekMath~shows minimal difference between zero-shot and few-shot performance. Moving forward, we intend to refine our data selection procedures to build an even more robust pre-training corpus, and to pursue the opportunities discussed in Section~\ref{sec:effec_rl} for advancing reinforcement learning in large language models.
