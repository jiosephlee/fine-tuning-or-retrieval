\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and demonstrate some degree of reasoning ability, exerting accurate and targeted control over their outputs remains challenging due to their purely unsupervised training process. Conventional techniques to enhance model steerability involve gathering human assessments of the comparative quality of model outputs and then adjusting the LM to favor these preferences, most commonly through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). However, RLHF involves a complicated and frequently unstable workflow: it first entails constructing a reward model that captures the provided human preferences, and subsequently involves tuning the large, unsupervised LM with reinforcement learning so as to maximize the predicted reward, all while attempting to avoid substantial divergence from the base model. In this work, we propose a novel way to parameterize the reward model within the RLHF setup, which allows direct recovery of the corresponding optimal policy via a closed-form expression. This reformulation enables the standard RLHF objective to be addressed using a straightforward classification loss. We introduce the resulting approach as \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), which is both efficient and robust, and dispenses with the necessity for language model sampling during fine-tuning or extensive hyperparameter searches. Empirically, we find that DPO can adapt LMs to conform with human preferences at least as well as, if not better than, established methods. Importantly, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in managing sentiment in generated text, while delivering equal or improved output quality for tasks such as summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while being easier to implement and requiring less computational overhead.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Unsupervised large language models (LMs) that are trained on extensive corpora have demonstrated unexpected competencies~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama, bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models are exposed to data reflecting a diverse range of human intentions, priorities, and proficiencies. As a result, some of the behaviors and skills learned from such data might be undesirable for the models to replicate. For instance, while we want an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors in order to effectively correct them, we also aim for the model to preferentially emulate the (possibly infrequent) instances of expert-level code found within its training distribution when generating solutions. Similarly, it is useful for a language model to be \textit{informed} about prevalent misconceptions held by half the population, yet undesirable for the model to propagate such misunderstandings in half of its responses to relevant queries. Thus, being able to distinguish and elicit the model’s \emph{preferred actions and answers} from its broad spectrum of \textit{internalized knowledge and skills} is a fundamental challenge for ensuring that AI systems are reliable, high-performing, and governable \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although common approaches leverage reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human judgments, this work demonstrates that the objective function used by RL-based preference learning can, in fact, be minimized exactly via a straightforward binary cross-entropy formulation, thus offering a more streamlined approach to preference modeling.

Broadly, prevailing techniques inject desirable properties into language models through carefully selected human preference datasets that typify safety and utility. This process of preference modeling follows an initial, large-scale, unsupervised pre-training phase conducted on vast textual resources. Although supervised fine-tuning with high-quality human-annotated responses is the most direct strategy, the most effective paradigm has proven to be reinforcement learning from human (or AI-generated) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF works by learning a reward model from human-preference data, then employing RL to adjust the model’s policy so that it generates outputs with high assigned rewards, all while constraining deviation from the original language model. While RLHF has enabled remarkable improvements in dialogue and code generation, its implementation is substantially more elaborate than supervised learning, necessitating the training of multiple LMs and repeated policy sampling during training, leading to high computational overhead.

This work presents a method for optimizing language models to reflect human preferences without resorting to explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We introduce \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO)}, an approach that implicitly pursues the same objective as RLHF algorithms—specifically, maximizing reward subject to a KL-divergence regularization—yet offers ease of implementation and straightforward training. Conceptually, DPO modifies the log-likelihood ratio in favor of human-preferred outputs over less-preferred ones, while leveraging a per-instance, adaptive weighting mechanism. This weighting counteracts model collapse that may arise if one naively optimizes the probability ratio alone. DPO, like prior techniques, employs a formal preference model (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess the alignment between a candidate reward function and collected preference data. Distinct from traditional pipelines—which utilize the preference model to form a loss for reward model training, subsequently used to optimize a policy—DPO re-parameterizes the preference loss directly as a function of the policy itself. When supplied with a dataset encoding human preferences among model outputs, DPO enables direct policy optimization via a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy for an implicit reward inferred from preference information.

The central innovation of this study is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an intuitive, reinforcement learning–free framework for preference-based language model training. Empirical results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses the effectiveness of prevalent approaches, such as RLHF methods employing PPO, in a range of preference-based tasks—including sentiment control, summarization, and conversational modeling—using models containing up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models have demonstrated the capacity to perform certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language} or using a small number of examples as prompts \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, their effectiveness on downstream tasks and alignment with user intentions can be greatly enhanced by additional fine-tuning on collections of instructions paired with human-authored completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, referred to as `instruction-tuning,' enables large language models to better generalize to new, previously unseen instructions, and tends to improve their overall practicality \citep{chung2022scaling}. Although instruction tuning has proven effective, it is often more feasible to obtain \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality than to curate datasets of expert examples. Consequently, follow-up research has further refined large language models using datasets comprised of human preference judgments, thereby advancing abilities in areas such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and following instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. Typically, these approaches begin by training a neural reward function to fit the collected preference data via models like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and subsequently fine-tune the language model to maximize this learned reward, employing reinforcement learning techniques such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related methods \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Another closely associated research direction uses LLMs—already instruction-tuned with human feedback—to automatically generate more synthetic preference data targeting dimensions such as safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, using only minimal human input via textual guidelines for the model’s evaluations. These strategies illustrate the merging of two separate research traditions: the use of reinforcement learning to train language models for various tasks~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and broader frameworks for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. While leveraging relative human preferences is an appealing direction, the process of fine-tuning large language models via reinforcement learning introduces considerable practical obstacles; this paper introduces a theoretically grounded methodology for optimizing over relative preferences without the need for RL.

Beyond the domain of language, the task of learning policies from preference data has been explored extensively within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, resulting in a variety of methodological advancements. A prominent example is the contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}), which involves learning from preferences or ordinal rankings over actions rather than direct reward signals. In situations where absolute rewards are unavailable, analyses of CDBs replace the concept of an optimal policy with that of a \textit{von Neumann winner}: a policy that is expected to perform at least as well as any competing policy in head-to-head matchups at least 50\% of the time \citep{dudik2015contextual}. It is important to note that, in the CDB paradigm, preference feedback is typically provided online, whereas learning from human preferences most often relies on a fixed, offline set of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) operates by leveraging binary preferences generated according to an unknown underlying ‘scoring’ mechanism rather than from explicit rewards \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Numerous PbRL techniques have been introduced, some capable of utilizing off-policy preference datasets; however, these methods generally necessitate the explicit construction of an implicit scoring (reward) model prior to policy optimization \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. Contrasting with these approaches, we introduce a direct, single-stage policy optimization method that aligns the policy with observed preferences.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF methodology as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further elaborated in subsequent work (\citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}). The RLHF process is commonly structured into three core stages: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) preference collection and reward model training; and 3) reinforcement learning optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: The RLHF framework typically initiates by applying supervised learning to adapt a pre-trained language model for the specific target application (such as dialogue or summarization), utilizing a high-quality labeled dataset to produce the fine-tuned model $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modeling Phase}: During the subsequent phase, the fine-tuned model is prompted with input examples $x$ to generate response pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. These output pairs are evaluated by human annotators, who indicate their preference between the two responses. The preference is denoted as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, signifying that $y_w$ is favored over $y_l$ for the given prompt, with $y_w$ and $y_l$ representing the preferred and non-preferred answers, respectively. The assumption is that preferences are guided by an underlying, latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$, to which we do not have direct access. To capture human preference data, several probabilistic models have been proposed, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being widely adopted (though more general models like Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are equally applicable if preferences over multiple ranked outputs are available). The BT formulation expresses the human preference probability distribution $p^*$ as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given a fixed collection of pairwise comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ that have been drawn according to $p^*$, one can define a parametric reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$, and fit its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. By interpreting this as a binary classification problem, the negative log-likelihood loss is expressed as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ is the standard logistic sigmoid. When working with LMs, the architecture for $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is typically initialized using the parameters from the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. A single linear head is then added atop the transformer’s final hidden state to output a scalar reward prediction \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To reduce reward function variance, previous approaches apply reward normalization such that  $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the RL fine-tuning step, the trained reward model is used to evaluate and guide the policy of the language model. In line with earlier research~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the objective function is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ tunes the penalty for departing from the reference distribution $\pi_\text{ref}$, which is the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. In standard implementations, the initial policy $\pi_\theta$ is set to $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The KL constraint is essential to keep the policy sufficiently close to the distribution over which the reward model is considered accurate, as well as to promote diversity in outputs and prevent convergence to overly narrow or degenerate solutions. Given the non-differentiability of text generation, reinforcement learning is used to optimize this objective. The typical strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involves defining the composite reward ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$, and maximizing it using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Faced with the difficulties inherent in applying conventional reinforcement learning algorithms at the scale of large language model fine-tuning, our objective is to establish a streamlined method for direct policy optimization using preference data. Distinct from standard RLHF pipelines that first fit a reward model before conducting RL-based policy optimization, our method is based on a specific reward parameterization, which allows us to directly derive the optimal policy analytically, eliminating the need for an RL training phase. As detailed below, our central contribution is an exact mapping from reward functions to their corresponding optimal policies, permitting the reparameterization of the loss: instead of defining it over rewards, we directly optimize the policy. This change-of-variables perspective makes it unnecessary to separately train a reward model, while still leveraging established preference models such as Bradley-Terry. \textbf{Derivation of the DPO Objective.} We begin from the same reinforcement learning (RL) objective outlined in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, considering a generic reward function $r$. In line with previous studies~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, one can directly show that the solution optimizing the KL-regularized reward maximization in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} is given by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ denotes the normalization constant (partition function). A full derivation is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation1}. When employing the MLE estimator $r_\phi$ for the true reward function $r^*$, estimating $Z(x)$ remains computationally challenging~\citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, which impedes the practical use of this expression. Nevertheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be algebraically manipulated to isolate the reward as a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the undetermined partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Taking logarithms on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging terms yields:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This alternative parameterization can be applied to the actual reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, under the Bradley-Terry model, only the difference between rewards for two outputs is relevant, i.e., ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r^*(x,y)$ into the Bradley-Terry preference model (Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}), we observe that the partition function cancels, enabling us to write the human preference likelihood solely in terms of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Thus, the RLHF-optimal policy $\pi^*$ with the Bradley-Terry structure fulfills:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

Details of this derivation are included in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is presented for the Bradley-Terry model, parallel formulations can be derived for broader preference models, such as those of Plackett-Luce~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as elaborated in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

Having now rewritten the preference data likelihood in terms of the optimal policy, rather than the reward function, we can specify a maximum likelihood objective for any parametrized policy $\pi_\theta$. Drawing an analogy to the reward modeling formulation in Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}, our objective for training the policy is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

In this approach, we estimate an implicit reward by employing an alternative parameterization such that the optimal policy directly corresponds to $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, because our method effectively reparameterizes the Bradley-Terry model, it inherits certain theoretical guarantees, including consistency provided the preference data distribution satisfies appropriate conditions \cite{bong2022generalized}. We elaborate on these theoretical underpinnings of DPO and compare them with related methodologies in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{What is the effect of the DPO update?} To gain mechanistic insight into DPO, it is informative to inspect the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The gradient with respect to $\theta$ is given by:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater magnitude when the reward model's preference is incorrect}\left[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{raise probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{lower probability of $y_l$}\right]\right],
\end{multline*}

where the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ is determined by both the parameterized model $\pi_\theta$ and a reference $\pi_\text{ref}$ (see Section~\ref{sec:theory} for more details). Conceptually, this gradient pushes the model to assign higher probability to preferred outputs $y_w$ while reducing probability on less-preferred outputs $y_l$. Critically, each sample's influence is modulated according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ mistakenly favors dispreferred completions, with the scaling set by $\beta$—reflecting both the model's misordering and the KL penalty's severity. Empirically, we observe that this specific weighting is crucial, as a simpler version of the algorithm that omits this weighting factor can lead to undesirable collapse of the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{Overview of DPO.} The standard DPO workflow consists of the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate completions $y_1, y_2$ sampled from $\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$, then assign human preference labels to construct an offline dataset of preferences $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) Train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, conditioned on the chosen $\pi_\text{ref}$, dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and specified $\beta$. In practical applications, it is advantageous to make use of existing, publicly available preference datasets, rather than generating new samples and collecting corresponding human feedback. These datasets are generally produced using $\pi^\text{SFT}$, so, when feasible, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$. In cases where $\pi^\text{SFT}$ is unavailable, we instead initialize $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, that is, by setting ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This initialization approach reduces the discrepancy between the unknown true reference distribution and the practical $\pi_\text{ref}$ utilized by DPO. Further implementation specifics and choices of hyperparameters are discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} This section provides a deeper interpretation of the DPO approach, establishes theoretical justifications, and compares the strengths of DPO to limitations seen in actor-critic algorithms for RLHF, such as PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO can unify both reward modeling and policy learning within a single maximum likelihood objective, avoiding the need for explicit reward function fitting or the use of reinforcement learning to train the policy. The optimization target in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} can be seen as an instance of the Bradley-Terry model, with the reward function given by $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. Here, training the parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$ is mathematically equivalent—under an appropriate change of variables—to optimizing a reward model as shown in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}. In this section, we further develop the theoretical underpinnings of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not restrict the space of representable reward models, and establish that the optimal policy can still be precisely recovered. We begin by introducing an equivalence relationship among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are said to be equivalent if and only if there exists some function $f$ such that $r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)$.     
\end{definition}

This definition readily implies that the relation is indeed an equivalence relation, resulting in a partition of the space of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. We now present two supporting lemmas:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce framework, including the Bradley-Terry model, any pair of reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class yield identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions from the same equivalence class will generate the same optimal policy in the corresponding constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}

We provide straightforward proofs in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma reflects a classical identifiability limitation of the Plackett-Luce model family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. This lack of identifiability typically necessitates the adoption of additional constraints to ensure well-defined maximum likelihood estimates from Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that all rewards within an equivalence class yield the same optimal policy; consequently, our ultimate goal is to recover any representative reward function from the optimal class. We establish the following theorem in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Provided certain mild conditions, every reward equivalence class that is consistent with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically the Bradley-Terry) models admits a representation of the form ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ denotes some model and $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$ is a reference model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Take an arbitrary reward function $r(x, y)$, and consider the induced optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ as defined in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We aim to demonstrate that there exists a reward function within the equivalence class of $r$ that can be represented via the above reparameterization. To this end, let us introduce the following projection operator:
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ normalizes the reward function using the log-partition function of $\pi_r$. Since the normalization term depends solely on $x$, $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ remains in the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. Finally, by substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} (which holds universally for reward functions), it follows that $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Thus, the projection $f$ yields a representative of the equivalence class of $r$ in the specified form, establishing that the proposed reparameterization entails no loss of generality in the reward model.
\end{sproof}

An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it precisely identifies which member of each equivalence class of reward functions the DPO reparameterization chooses—specifically, the reward function that fulfills:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, as determined by Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparameterization}} = 1,
\end{equation}

That is, $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a legitimate probability distribution, with non-negative probabilities summing to one. Moreover, in light of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it becomes evident that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function for the optimal policy derived from the reward function $r(x, y)$. The central innovation of the DPO algorithm is to enforce particular constraints on the broadly under-determined Plackett-Luce (and specifically, Bradley-Terry) class of preference models. By doing so, we retain the expressive capacity of the reward model class, while simultaneously rendering the optimal policy in Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy} analytically solvable for any prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework can further be leveraged to analyze and explain sources of instability found in conventional actor-critic algorithms for RLHF, such as PPO. Adhering to the standard RLHF workflow, we concentrate on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section \ref{section:prelims}. There is a natural connection here to the control-as-inference perspective \cite{levine2018reinforcement} applied to the constrained RL setup in \ref{eq:RL}. Assuming a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, the typical approach involves minimizing the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ represents the optimal policy, as established by Eq. \ref{eq:optimum_model}, under the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. This yields, after some algebraic manipulation, the following objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

The objective considered here is identical to that optimized in earlier research 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which utilized the DPO-equivalent reward for the class of rewards $r_{\phi}$. Within this framework, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be seen as the soft value function corresponding to the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this normalization factor does not influence the optimality of the resulting solution, omitting it would result in a policy gradient with potentially high variance, thereby destabilizing the learning process. One way to address this normalization is by introducing a learned value function, but this approach often introduces additional optimization challenges. Previous work has instead often employed normalization with respect to a human completion baseline, effectively a one-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization term. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that operates independently of any explicit baselines. 

\section{Experiments}
Here, we provide an empirical assessment of DPO’s effectiveness in learning policies directly from preference data. We begin by investigating, in a controlled text generation context, the efficiency with which DPO manages the trade-off between maximizing rewards and minimizing KL-divergence from the reference policy, and compare its performance to standard preference optimization methods like PPO. Subsequently, we test DPO on larger-scale models and more challenging RLHF tasks such as summarization and dialogue. Our findings indicate that, even with minimal hyperparameter adjustment, DPO generally matches or exceeds the performance of robust baselines, including RLHF using PPO, as well as outperforming the strategy of selecting the best trajectory among $N$ samples based on a learned reward. Prior to discussing these findings, we outline our experimental protocol; further information is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study examines three distinct open-ended text generation challenges. Across all experimental conditions, methods are trained to optimize a policy using a dataset of preferences denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. For \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ corresponds to the opening segment of a movie review sampled from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, with the policy tasked to generate $y$ exhibiting positive sentiment. To facilitate systematic assessment, we synthetically construct preference pairs from model generations, employing a pre-trained sentiment classifier such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, we continue training GPT-2-large on the training portion of the IMDB reviews until model convergence (additional methodological details provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum entry, and the policy is required to produce a summary $y$ capturing its main ideas. In line with earlier research, we utilize the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, coupled with the set of human preferences compiled by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. We implement an SFT model that has been further tuned on human-created summaries of forum posts\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} by leveraging the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} toolkit for RLHF. The preferences from humans were obtained by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using outputs from a distinct but similarly-trained SFT model. Lastly, for the \textbf{single-turn dialogue} setting, $x$ represents a user prompt, ranging from scientific inquiries to personal advice requests. Here, the policy’s objective is to craft a helpful and engaging response $y$; for evaluation, we use the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which includes 170,000 conversations between a person and an automated assistant. Each conversation concludes with two assistant responses (produced by a large, but unspecified, language model), accompanied by human preference annotations identifying the favored reply. As there is no pre-existing SFT model for this domain, we fine-tune a general-purpose language model solely on the preferred completions to establish our SFT baseline.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two distinct evaluation strategies in our experiments. To investigate each algorithm’s capacity for optimizing the constrained reward maximization objective, we focus on the controlled sentiment generation scenario, where the performance of each method is assessed by plotting the frontier of realized reward versus KL-divergence from the reference policy. This is feasible due to the availability of the ground-truth reward signal, given by a sentiment classifier. In contrast, for real-world applications where the reward function is not directly accessible, we rely on \textit{win rate} metrics relative to a baseline policy. Here, we use GPT-4 to stand in for human raters, assessing summary quality for summarization and helpfulness for single-turn dialogue tasks. For summarization, the test set’s reference summaries serve as the baseline, while in dialogue tasks, the dataset’s preferred responses are used as the comparator. Prior literature indicates that language models can surpass traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}; nevertheless, we supplement our analysis with a human evaluation to substantiate our choice of GPT-4 as a judge, detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings show a high correlation between GPT-4 and human judgments, with agreement rates matching or exceeding those between different human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, our study benchmarks a range of established techniques for aligning language models with human preferences. For the summarization task, we use zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, and for dialogue, 2-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We additionally compare with the \textbf{SFT} model, alongside \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves supervised fine-tuning using the selected output $y_w$—sourced from either the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a general language model (for dialogue). We also assess the \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural} approach, a pseudo-supervised strategy that encourages the model to maximize likelihood for $y_w$ and \textit{minimize} it for $y_l$, modulated by an optional scaling parameter $\alpha\in[0,1]$ on the unlikelihood term. The evaluation further includes \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, which utilizes a reward model trained on preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle version leveraging the true reward function available in the sentiment-controlled environment. For PPO-GT in sentiment tasks, we explore both a standard implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified variant that includes reward normalization and refined hyperparameters to enhance efficacy; these modifications are similarly applied to PPO with learned rewards. Finally, the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline is included, which involves sampling $N$ outputs from the SFT (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) model and selecting the top-scoring completion per a learned reward model. While this strategy effectively separates reward model quality from PPO optimization, it is computationally burdensome for larger $N$, since it necessitates generating $N$ candidate completions per test input.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The objective of reward maximization under a KL constraint, as implemented in standard RLHF approaches, seeks to enhance reward while simultaneously preventing the learned policy from diverging excessively from the reference policy. Thus, an appropriate evaluation of different algorithms must jointly consider both the attained reward and the magnitude of KL divergence; an improvement in reward that comes at the cost of a substantially greater KL is not inherently preferable. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, we illustrate the tradeoff curve between reward and KL for several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each method, we conduct multiple training trials, systematically varying the policy conservativeness through different hyperparameter choices (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO, $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood, and random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 experimental runs. At intervals of 100 training steps, continuing until the models converge, we assess the policies on a designated set of evaluation prompts, calculating both the mean reward (as determined by the true reward function) and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{This denotes the aggregate over timesteps of the KL-divergence at each step.} relative to the reference policy, i.e., $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. The results demonstrate that DPO attains a substantially superior reward-KL frontier, reaching higher reward with comparatively modest KL divergence. This finding stands out for a couple of reasons: firstly, although DPO and PPO are both optimizing for the same objective, DPO realizes this objective much more efficiently—its position on the reward/KL spectrum consistently surpasses PPO. Secondly, even in scenarios where PPO has direct access to the actual reward signal (PPO-GT), DPO still achieves a more favorable reward-KL balance.

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the effectiveness of DPO for fine-tuning on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue. In the context of summarization, automatic metrics like ROUGE have been shown to have weak alignment with human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}, and earlier studies demonstrated that optimizing LMs with PPO guided by human preferences can yield superior summaries. Our evaluation compares several approaches by generating model outputs on the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and measuring the mean win rate relative to reference completions within this set. For every method, outputs are sampled at temperatures ranging from 0.0 to 1.0, with corresponding win rates presented in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). All models—including DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT—are fine-tuned starting from the same GPT-J SFT checkpoint\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results indicate that DPO attains an approximate 61\% win rate at a sampling temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO, which achieves around 57\% at its optimal temperature (also 0.0). DPO further demonstrates a greater peak win rate than the best-of-$N$ baseline. It is important to note that we conducted minimal tuning of DPO's $\beta$ hyperparameter; thus, these findings may not reflect DPO’s full capabilities. Additionally, DPO exhibits much higher resilience to variations in sampling temperature compared to PPO, whose performance may fall back to that of the underlying GPT-J base model as temperature increases. Preferred-FT shows little improvement over the initial SFT model. In Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, we supplement these results with a direct human evaluation between DPO and PPO, where DPO completions sampled at temperature 0.25 were preferred 58\% of the time over PPO samples at temperature 0.

For single-turn dialogue evaluation, we assess the various approaches using the portion of the Anthropic HH test split \citep{bai2022training} that includes a single exchange between a human and an assistant. To gauge GPT-4 performance, we use the preferred completions within the test set as ground truth references for win rate calculations across different methods. Since there is no established SFT baseline for this scenario, our procedure begins with the pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model; we apply Preferred-FT to train a reference model using the selected completions, ensuring that generated completions stay within the model’s distribution, before proceeding with DPO training. Additionally, we benchmark against the best completion out of 128 Preferred-FT samples (having observed that the Best of $N$ approach plateaus at $N=128$ for this dataset; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}), as well as a 2-shot prompting setup with the Pythia-2.8B base model. DPO consistently matches or surpasses the best-performing temperature setting for each alternative. We further analyze an RLHF model, trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}}, sourced from a reputable implementation\footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, yet we are unable to identify prompt or temperature parameters that yield superior results compared to the base Pythia-2.8B model. Given findings from TL;DR and the shared reward optimization objective, we treat Best of 128 as an approximate stand-in for PPO-level outcomes. In summary, DPO emerges as the only computationally efficient strategy that outperforms the preferred completions on the Anthropic HH benchmark, and it achieves performance on par with or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} further demonstrates that DPO achieves optimal results in relatively few training iterations.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To further investigate how PPO and DPO handle distributional changes, we assess the policies derived from our Reddit TL;DR summarization study on a distinct dataset—specifically, news articles found in the test set of the CNN/DailyMail corpus \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}. For this evaluation, we employ the optimal sampling temperatures identified on TL;DR (0 and 0.25). The corresponding results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. Using the same GPT-4 (C) prompt as in the TL;DR experiment—with ``forum post'' replaced by ``news article''—we calculate the GPT-4 win rates against the respective reference summaries from the datasets. Notably, DPO maintains a substantial advantage over PPO on this unseen distribution. These findings offer preliminary support that DPO-trained policies can achieve generalization performance comparable to or better than PPO, even though DPO does not leverage the additional unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts utilized by PPO.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
We implement a human subject study to assess the consistency of GPT-4's ratings by drawing on outputs from the TL;DR summarization experiment and utilizing two separate GPT-4 prompts. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt asks solely which summary best encapsulates the critical information in a post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt requests an evaluation not only on informativeness but also on conciseness, motivated by our observation that GPT-4 using the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt is more inclined than humans to favor longer, more repetitive summaries. Complete versions of both prompts are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We undertake three comparative evaluations, spanning the highest-performing (DPO, temp. 0.25), lowest-performing (PPO, temp. 1.0), and an intermediate (SFT, temp. 0.25) method to ensure a variety of summary qualities. Each method is benchmarked against greedily-sampled PPO, the strongest PPO configuration. The results indicate that, across both prompts, GPT-4's choices align with human raters at rates similar to inter-human agreement, indicating that GPT-4 serves as a suitable proxy for human evaluation (note: due to a limited pool of human raters, multiple judgments were obtained only for the DPO and PPO-1 pairings). Moreover, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt generally yields win rates that more closely mirror human preferences; thus, it is adopted for the main experiments in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information about the human evaluation protocol, including the rater interface and participant roster, is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning constitutes an effective and scalable approach for developing language models that are both capable and well-aligned. In this work, we presented DPO, a straightforward method that facilitates training language models from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning techniques. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit a conventional RL framework and applying standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a correspondence between language model policies and reward functions, enabling direct optimization for human preferences through a simple cross-entropy loss. This approach eliminates the need for reinforcement learning while maintaining generality. Remarkably, DPO matches or surpasses the performance of established RLHF algorithms, such as those utilizing PPO, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO lowers the entry barrier for training language models using human preferences.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Several avenues for further investigation emerge from our findings. One important question is how well the DPO-trained policy extrapolates to out-of-distribution scenarios, in comparison with methods that optimize explicit reward functions. Preliminary evidence indicates that DPO may achieve generalization on par with PPO-based strategies, but more thorough evaluation is warranted. For instance, it remains to be seen whether self-labeling during DPO training can leverage unlabeled prompts as effectively. Additionally, the behavior of reward over-optimization in the direct preference optimization context requires examination, particularly in light of the slight reduction in performance observed in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right, which may be illustrative of this phenomenon. While our current experiments focus on models up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to much larger, state-of-the-art models represents a promising direction for future exploration. In terms of assessment, our results indicate that GPT-4's computed win rates are prompt-sensitive; future studies could explore optimal strategies for eliciting reliable judgments from automated evaluators. Moreover, there is significant potential for applying DPO beyond language modeling, such as in the training of generative models across different data modalities.
