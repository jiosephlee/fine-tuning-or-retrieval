\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Although large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive world knowledge and demonstrate certain reasoning capabilities, exercising fine-grained control over their outputs remains challenging due to the lack of supervision during training. Current strategies to improve this controllability typically involve collecting human judgments on the comparative quality of model outputs and subsequently adjusting the unsupervised LM through fine-tuning to better align with these human preferences, frequently leveraging reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Nevertheless, RLHF presents notable complexity and instability, requiring an initial stage where a reward model is trained to represent human preferences, followed by reinforcement learning to adjust the LM in a manner that maximizes this learned reward while maintaining proximity to the original model's behavior. In this work, we propose an alternative parameterization for the reward model used in RLHF, which facilitates the derivation of the optimal policy in closed form. This enables the RLHF task to be reformulated as a straightforward classification problem. Our proposed method, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), delivers stable and strong performance with minimal computational overhead, removing the necessity for LM sampling during the fine-tuning process and extensive hyperparameter searches. Empirical results indicate that DPO can adapt LMs to human preferences at least as effectively as leading existing techniques. Remarkably, DPO surpasses PPO-based RLHF in managing the sentiment expressed in generated text and performs as well as or better in tasks like summarization and single-turn dialogue, all while greatly simplifying implementation and training procedures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) trained on massive datasets have demonstrated unexpected proficiencies~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Nevertheless, these models are trained on human-produced data that reflects a vast spectrum of objectives, priorities, and competencies. Not all of these traits are suitable for replication; for instance, although it is advantageous for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} prevalent programming errors in order to provide corrections, we typically wish the model to preferentially generate code that mirrors the (sometimes infrequent) examples of strong programming skill found in its corpus. Likewise, we may require our language model to be \textit{cognizant} of widely held misconceptions—such as one endorsed by 50\% of people—but we would decidedly prefer the model not to propagate this falsehood in half of its responses to relevant queries. Essentially, it is vital to distinguish and select the model's \emph{targeted behaviors and outputs} from the broader landscape of its \textit{acquired knowledge and capacities}, ensuring the development of AI systems that are reliable, effective, and amenable to control \citep{ouyang2022training}. Although standard approaches often employ reinforcement learning (RL) to align LMs with human values, we will demonstrate that the standard RL objective in these settings can actually be minimized precisely using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, thus streamlining the entire preference optimization process.

Broadly speaking, prevailing strategies endow language models with desirable qualities by utilizing carefully curated human preference datasets, encapsulating the traits and outputs that users deem trustworthy and useful. This process of preference alignment typically follows a foundational phase of extensive unsupervised training on expansive textual corpora. While the most direct method for preference modeling is to perform supervised fine-tuning on exemplary human-generated responses, the dominant paradigm is reinforcement learning from human or artificial feedback (RLHF/RLAIF; \citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF first trains a reward model on annotated human preferences and then leverages RL techniques to adjust a language model’s policy, aiming to maximize the expected reward while restricting deviation from its pre-trained state. RLHF has yielded models with notable conversational and programming performance, but its workflow introduces considerable complexity compared to purely supervised methods, necessitating the training of multiple LMs and iterative sampling during policy optimization, leading to substantial computational expense.

In this work, we introduce a method for optimizing language models to conform to human preferences directly, circumventing the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning approaches. We present \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO)}, an algorithm that inherently targets the same objective as conventional RLHF techniques—namely, reward maximization under a KL-divergence constraint—yet offers simplicity in both implementation and training. At a high level, DPO modifies the log probability ratio between preferred and non-preferred outputs, integrating a sample-dependent importance weighting mechanism that mitigates the model collapse observed with more naive probability ratio objectives. DPO, akin to previous approaches, leverages a theoretical preference structure (for instance, the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to assess the compatibility between a proposed reward function and observed preference data. Distinctly, while other frameworks employ this preference model to specify a loss function for reward model training—subsequently using this learned reward to update the policy—DPO reformulates the loss in terms of the policy itself via a variable substitution. When provided with human-annotated preference pairs for model outputs, DPO enables direct policy optimization using a straightforward binary cross-entropy loss, yielding an optimal policy corresponding to an implicit reward function inferred from the preference annotations.

The principal contribution of this study is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that eliminates the need for reinforcement learning in training language models from preference signals. Empirical results demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses established approaches, such as PPO-based RLHF, across a range of tasks—including sentiment control, summarization, and conversation—on language models with scales up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Progressively larger self-supervised language models have demonstrated an ability to address certain tasks in a zero-shot manner \citep{radford2019language}, or with minimal in-context examples using few-shot prompting techniques \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nevertheless, these models exhibit marked improvements on downstream tasks and in aligning with user expectations when further refined on curated datasets containing instructions paired with human-authored responses \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, termed `instruction-tuning,' enables large language models to handle instructions not present in the tuning data, thereby enhancing general applicability and user satisfaction \citep{chung2022scaling}. While instruction tuning has achieved notable gains, collecting \textit{relative} judgments—where annotators compare the quality of responses—is generally more tractable than obtaining full expert demonstrations. For this reason, subsequent research has focused on refining language models with datasets derived from human preference comparisons, yielding superior results in domains such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, story generation \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and instruction adherence \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches involve first training a neural reward model to reflect observed preferences, frequently employing models like the Bradley-Terry framework \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}, and subsequently optimizing the language model using reinforcement learning algorithms—including REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or related variants \citep{ramamurthy2023is}—to maximize this learned reward. In parallel, closely aligned research leverages instruction-tuned LLMs, refined with human feedback, to autonomously generate further synthetic preference data aimed at specific criteria such as safety and harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying primarily on high-level text rubrics as weak human supervision for annotation. Collectively, these methods integrate progress from two distinct but related research streams: one focused on reinforcement learning-based training of language models for diverse objectives~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and the other centered on frameworks for learning directly from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. Although utilizing relative human preference signals is attractive in principle, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models presents substantial practical difficulties; the present work introduces a theoretically motivated strategy to optimize language models with respect to relative preferences, circumventing the need for reinforcement learning.

Beyond linguistic applications, the problem of learning policies from preferences has been explored within both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, leading to a variety of methodological innovations. In contextual bandit scenarios, where decisions are guided by preferences or action rankings in lieu of reward signals, the task is formalized as a contextual dueling bandit (CDB; \cite{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}). When explicit rewards are unavailable, theoretical analysis for CDBs adopts the concept of a \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that, on expectation, achieves at least a 50\% win rate against every alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. Notably, CDB frameworks typically require preferences to be supplied interactively during training, whereas human preference learning generally proceeds from a predetermined, finite collection of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. On a related front, \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL) employs binary preferences, which are assumed to be the output of an underlying, unknown `scoring' function rather than direct reward observations \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Although numerous PbRL algorithms have been introduced—including strategies for leveraging off-policy preference datasets—most share the two-stage design of first estimating this latent scoring function (or reward model) and then optimizing the policy with respect to it \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our approach introduces a unified, single-step policy optimization procedure that seeks to directly maximize preference satisfaction.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We outline the RLHF pipeline as articulated by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and further developed in subsequent works \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which generally unfolds in three main stages: (1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); (2) sampling responses for preference collection and reward modeling; and (3) reinforcement learning-based optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: The RLHF workflow typically initiates by further training a pre-existing language model on carefully curated, task-specific datasets—using standard supervised learning—to obtain a policy denoted as $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: Next, the fine-tuned model is prompted with input $x$ and generates answer pairs $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$, which are then presented to human annotators. Annotators express a preference between the two responses, recorded as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$, where $y_w$ stands for the preferred and $y_l$ the less favored completion of the pair $(y_1, y_2)$. While the actual reward function $r^*(y, x)$ underpinning these choices is unobserved, preferences are assumed to stem from such a latent model. Several statistical models can be used to represent preference data, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being particularly prevalent (though alternative frameworks like the Plackett-Luce model \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} are applicable when multiple answers are ranked). The BT model expresses the probability distribution over human preferences $p^*$ as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Given a fixed set of comparison data $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ drawn from $p^*$, we introduce a parameterized reward function $r_{\phi}(x, y)$, with its parameters determined through maximum likelihood estimation. Treating the task as a binary classification, the negative log-likelihood objective is formulated as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. Within language modeling, the architecture for $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ is commonly constructed by extending the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$ with a single linear output layer atop the final transformer block, outputting a scalar reward estimate \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To decrease reward variance, previous studies standardize the output so that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the subsequent reinforcement learning phase, the trained reward model serves as the signal guiding language model updates. Building on established literature~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the objective can be written as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ regulates how much the new policy $\pi_\theta$ is allowed to diverge from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, specifically the initial SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. In practical settings, $\pi_\theta$ is initialized with the parameters of $\pi^\text{SFT}$. This KL-divergence constraint is critical to ensure the policy does not stray excessively from the data distribution where the reward model is reliable, and to preserve diversity in outputs, thereby preventing the model from collapsing to a narrow set of high-reward outputs. Since the discrete sequence generation process is inherently non-differentiable, reinforcement learning methods are employed to optimize this objective. Standard practices \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} involve defining the reward as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$, with optimization typically performed using PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Addressing the complexities inherent in applying reinforcement learning algorithms to large-scale tasks like language model adaptation, we aim to develop a straightforward policy optimization framework that relies directly on preference data. In contrast to earlier RLHF pipelines, which first fit a reward function and subsequently optimize it using RL methods, our technique employs a particular reward model parameterization that admits a closed-form solution for the corresponding optimal policy—eliminating the need for a reinforcement learning loop. As we elaborate in the following section, the central idea is to exploit an explicit mapping from reward functions to their associated optimal policies. This mapping allows us to reformulate the loss—originally defined over reward functions—directly in terms of policies. Through this change-of-variables approach, we sidestep the need to train a separate, explicit reward network, yet still optimize

\textbf{Derivation of the DPO Objective.} Our starting point is the standard RL objective, as formulated in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, using a general reward function $r$. Building on previous work~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, one can readily verify that the solution optimizing the KL-regularized reward maximization problem (Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}) is given by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

with $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ serving as the normalization constant. For detailed derivation steps, refer to Appendix~\ref{app:derivation1}. Direct computation of the partition function $Z(x)$ becomes computationally infeasible even when the reward function $r^*$ is estimated via MLE as $r_{\phi}$ \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, presenting practical limitations for this formulation. Nonetheless, Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} can be reformulated so the reward function is a function of the optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the partition function $Z(\cdot)$. Specifically, by taking logarithms on both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and rearranging terms, we find:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This representation also holds for the true reward $r^*$ and its associated optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model leverages only the difference in reward values between two responses; that is, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. By inserting the form for $r^*(x, y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference probability as formulated in Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry}, the normalization constants cancel, leading to a preference probability dependent solely on the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Thus, for the Bradley-Terry model, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

Further steps for this derivation are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} is presented using the Bradley-Terry framework, similar derivations extend to the broader class of Plackett-Luce models~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as elaborated in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With this formulation of the human preference probability in terms of the optimal policy, it becomes possible to express a maximum likelihood training objective directly for a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. In direct analogy with the reward-based modeling approach (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the policy learning objective is formulated as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

In this approach, we learn an implicit reward through a different parameterization, where the resulting optimal policy coincides with $\pi_\theta$. Furthermore, because this method is tantamount to fitting a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits certain theoretical guarantees, notably consistency under appropriate assumptions regarding the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. Section~\ref{sec:theory} elaborates further on the theoretical attributes of DPO and situates it with respect to related research.

\textbf{What effect does the DPO update have?} To gain mechanistic insights into DPO, one can examine the gradient of the loss function $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. This gradient with respect to the model parameters $\theta$ is as follows:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{higher weight when reward estimate is wrong}\bigg[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{increase likelihood of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{decrease likelihood of $y_l$}\bigg]\bigg],
\end{multline*}

Here, $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$ represents the reward that is implicitly specified by the policy $\pi_\theta$ in relation to the reference $\pi_\text{ref}$ (as discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). Conceptually, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ is structured to promote the probability of selecting preferred completions $y_w$ while diminishing the probability of less favored completions $y_l$. The influence of each example is modulated by the extent to which the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta$ incorrectly rates the less-preferred completions higher, scaled by $\beta$, thus reflecting both the misordering and the influence of the KL regularization term. Our empirical results highlight that this weighting scheme is critical: removing the weighting factor, as in a simplistic variant of the algorithm, can lead to model collapse (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO framework.} The typical DPO workflow consists of: 1) Drawing samples $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$ for each prompt $x$, labeling them according to human preferences to assemble an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$, and 2) training the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$, using the fixed $\pi_\text{ref}$, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and the selected $\beta$. 
In practical applications, researchers often rely on publicly available preference datasets rather than collecting new samples or human preference annotations. Since such datasets are typically generated using $\pi^\text{SFT}$, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ when it is present. Conversely, if $\pi^\text{SFT}$ cannot be accessed, $\pi_\text{ref}$ is initialized by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically, ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This method helps address discrepancies between the intractable true reference distribution and the $\pi_\text{ref}$ employed in DPO. Additional implementation and hyperparameter specifics are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} In this section, we provide an in-depth interpretation of the DPO methodology, establish theoretical support, and discuss how DPO overcomes some of the shortcomings associated with actor-critic RLHF algorithms (e.g., PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO effectively eliminates the need for both explicit reward function fitting and reinforcement learning during policy optimization by utilizing a single maximum likelihood-based objective. It is noteworthy that the objective in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} can be viewed as a Bradley-Terry model with rewards specified as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. The training procedure for our parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$ is, therefore, equivalent—under a change of variables—to optimizing a reward model as in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model}. In this section, we develop the theoretical foundation for this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not reduce the expressive capacity of the family of reward models learned, and prove that it allows for precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation among reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y)-r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.
\end{definition}

This definition naturally gives rise to an equivalence relation, which organizes the set of reward functions into disjoint equivalence classes. The following lemmas capture key properties of these classes:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence}
Within the Plackett-Luce preference framework—including the special case of Bradley-Terry—any pair of reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class will induce identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions from a common equivalence class will yield the same optimal policy in the associated constrained RL setting.
\end{lemma}

Since the proofs are straightforward, they are presented in Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma highlights a well-documented identifiability issue associated with the Plackett-Luce family \cite{plackett1975analysis}. As a result of this ambiguity, it is customary to enforce additional constraints to ensure identifiability in maximum likelihood estimation, as discussed in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma asserts that every reward function within a given class leads to the same optimal policy, and thus, when addressing our main objective, it suffices to recover any representative from the optimal equivalence class. The subsequent theorem, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this insight:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Assuming mild regularity conditions, any equivalence class of reward functions compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically, Bradley-Terry) models admits a representation of the form ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$ for some probability model $\pi(y\mid x)$ and a fixed reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ denote an arbitrary reward function, with its associated optimal policy $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ defined via Eq. \ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that a reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can always be cast in the reparameterized form mentioned above. To this end, define the mapping $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
Here, $f$ adjusts $r(x, y)$ by normalizing it with the logarithm of the partition function under $\pi_r$ and $\pi_\text{ref}$. Importantly, the subtracted term depends only on $x$, ensuring that $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ remains within the equivalence class of $r(x, y)$. By substituting $r$ with the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq}—a valid representation for any reward function—we see that $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Thus, $f$ produces an element of the same equivalence class with the required structure, confirming that the proposed reparameterization is fully general for our purposes.
\end{sproof}

An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it uniquely identifies, within each equivalence class, the reward function chosen by the DPO reparameterization—specifically, the reward function that fulfills the condition:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

which ensures that $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a properly normalized probability distribution (i.e., non-negative values that sum to one). Notably, referencing Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, we observe that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} corresponds to the partition function associated with the optimal policy defined by the reward $r(x, y)$. The principal contribution of the DPO method is to enforce specific constraints within the underdetermined Plackett-Luce (and, more particularly, Bradley-Terry) preference model family. By doing so, the model space for representable reward functions remains unchanged, while ensuring that the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} remains analytically tractable for any given prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework also facilitates the analysis of instability issues encountered in standard actor-critic approaches commonly applied to RLHF, such as PPO. Focusing on the RL fine-tuning stage described in Section~\ref{section:prelims}, we can relate this to the control as inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} within the context of the constrained RL problem in \ref{eq:RL}. If we consider a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, our goal is to minimize $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid \mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ represents the optimal policy derived from Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model} and the associated reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through some algebraic manipulation, this yields the following optimization problem:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

The objective considered here aligns with that used in earlier studies 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, which similarly employ the DPO-equivalent reward for the reward class $r_{\phi}$. Within this context, the normalization component in $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be viewed as the soft value function associated with the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this normalization does not change the optimal solution, omitting it can lead to high-variance policy gradient estimates, potentially destabilizing learning. While introducing a learned value function could address the normalization, optimizing such a value function poses its own challenges. Alternatively, prior research has normalized rewards by utilizing a human completion baseline, which effectively provides a single-sample Monte Carlo estimate for normalization. In contrast, the DPO reparameterization introduces a reward function that eliminates the need for any baselines.

\section{Experiments}
In this section, we conduct an empirical analysis of DPO's capacity to train policies directly from preference data. Initially, using a controlled text generation environment, we investigate how effectively DPO balances reward maximization against the KL-divergence constraint with the reference policy, especially in comparison to prevalent preference optimization methods such as PPO. Subsequently, we assess DPO’s performance when scaling to larger models and tackling more complex RLHF scenarios, including summarization and conversational tasks. Our findings indicate that DPO often matches or outperforms robust baselines like RLHF with PPO and the selection of the best from $N$ generated samples under a learned reward model, all with minimal hyperparameter tuning. Details regarding our experimental protocols are outlined next, with further information provided in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} We investigate three distinct open-ended text generation tasks in our empirical studies. Across all tasks, each algorithm derives a policy from a dataset of preferences, denoted as $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the \textbf{controlled sentiment generation} task, $x$ represents the beginning portion of a movie review sourced from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective for the policy is to generate a continuation $y$ with a positive tone. To facilitate systematic evaluation, we create preference pairs over generated text using an existing sentiment classifier, ensuring that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For the SFT baseline, we perform fine-tuning of GPT-2-large on the training set of IMDb reviews until it converges (see additional details in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ is a Reddit forum post, and the task is to generate a summary $y$ that captures the key points. Consistent with previous research, we adopt the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, paired with human preference annotations from \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. Our SFT baseline is a model fine-tuned on summaries composed by humans,\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF toolkit. The human preference data was originally collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} using outputs from a different, yet comparably trained, SFT model. The final task, \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, features $x$ as a user prompt, which may range from scientific inquiries to personal advice requests. Here, the policy is required to produce an informative and engaging reply $y$; we utilize the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, encompassing 170,000 interactions between users and an AI assistant. Each dialogue concludes with a pair of model-generated responses, annotated with a preference indicating the human’s favored answer. Since there is no available pre-trained SFT model in this scenario, we construct the SFT model by fine-tuning a general-purpose language model on the set of preferred responses only.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We adopt two distinct strategies for assessing our algorithms. To systematically compare their abilities in optimizing the constrained reward maximization objective, we first examine the performance frontier—characterized by the trade-off between achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy—in a controlled sentiment generation environment. This is feasible because the experiment provides access to the underlying (ground-truth) reward function, namely, a sentiment classifier. However, in practical settings, the true reward function is generally unavailable. Consequently, we resort to measuring each algorithm’s \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy, utilizing GPT-4 as a stand-in for human evaluators to judge summary quality in summarization and response helpfulness in single-turn dialogue tasks. In the summarization context, the baseline is constituted by the reference summaries from the test split; in dialogue, the baseline corresponds to the human-preferred responses within the test data. Although prior research indicates that language models can outperform traditional metrics as automatic evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, we additionally carry out a human evaluation to substantiate the reliability of GPT-4 as an evaluation tool, as elaborated in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings demonstrate a strong correlation between GPT-4 and human raters, with human-to-GPT-4 agreement often equaling or surpassing the inter-annotator consistency among humans.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, our evaluation encompasses multiple established techniques for aligning language model outputs with human preferences. For summarization, we implement zero-shot prompting using \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j}, while for dialogue, we employ two-shot prompting with \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia}. We further include models trained via supervised fine-tuning (\textbf{SFT}), along with \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which undergoes fine-tuning on selected completions $y_w$—these are sourced from either SFT outputs (in the controlled sentiment and summarization tasks) or from a generic LM (for single-turn dialogue). Another method in our comparison is \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, which encourages the model to increase the likelihood of $y_w$ while penalizing the probability assigned to $y_l$, regulated by an adjustable coefficient $\alpha \in [0,1]$ applied to the unlikelihood component. Additionally, we evaluate \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal}, where rewards are learned from preference-based annotations, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, which has oracle access to the ground-truth reward in the controlled sentiment setup. In sentiment generation experiments, PPO-GT is instantiated both with an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} and a modified version, the latter featuring reward normalization and enhanced hyperparameter tuning for better results—these improvements are also adopted for standard PPO with learned reward models. We also consider the \textbf{Best of $N$} heuristic, which involves sampling $N$ candidate responses from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT in dialogue) and selecting the top-ranked response based on a reward model trained on preference data. Despite its strong performance, this approach is computationally intensive, as it necessitates generating $N$ completions for each test instance.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

Standard RLHF algorithms employ a reward maximization objective that is constrained by KL-divergence to ensure the policy does not diverge excessively from a reference policy, thereby balancing reward optimization against policy conservatism. Consequently, algorithmic comparisons should consider both the attained reward and the extent of KL divergence; obtaining marginally higher rewards at the cost of substantially larger KL may not be favorable. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, we depict the reward-KL tradeoff frontier for several algorithms applied to the sentiment task. Each algorithm is subjected to multiple training runs, each with a distinct policy conservativeness hyperparameter (target KL $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$ for PPO; $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$ for DPO; $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood training; and different random seeds for preferred-FT), resulting in a total of 22 runs. At intervals of 100 training steps, up to convergence, we assess each trained policy on a suite of test prompts, measuring both the mean reward (according to the true reward function) and the average sequence-level KL\footnote{That is, the sum of the per-timestep KL-divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$. The results indicate that DPO yields the most favorable frontier, attaining superior rewards while maintaining low KL values. This finding is significant for several reasons: not only do DPO and PPO target the same objective, but DPO consistently achieves greater efficiency, strictly outperforming PPO in the reward/KL tradeoff. Furthermore, DPO surpasses PPO even when PPO is provided with access to ground truth rewards (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the fine-tuning capabilities of DPO in the contexts of summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks. In summarization, standard automated metrics like ROUGE have been shown to correlate weakly with actual human judgments~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous studies demonstrated that leveraging PPO to fine-tune language models based on human preferences leads to more satisfactory summaries. To compare various approaches, we generate completions using the test portion of the TL;DR summarization dataset and measure the mean win rate against reference completions from the same test set. For each method, completions are generated across a temperature range between 0.0 and 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT are all applied to the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} for fine-tuning. The results indicate that DPO attains a win rate of around 61\% at a temperature of 0.0, surpassing PPO, which achieves about 57\% at its optimal temperature setting of 0.0. Furthermore, DPO secures a higher peak win rate than the best of $N$ method. It is important to mention that DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter was not extensively tuned, suggesting that current results may not reflect DPO’s full capacity. Additionally, DPO demonstrates significantly greater robustness to varying sampling temperatures, whereas PPO’s performance can diminish to the level of the base GPT-J model under higher temperatures. Preferred-FT, on the other hand, does not show substantial gains over the initial SFT model. A direct comparison between DPO and PPO using human assessments is provided in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, revealing that DPO samples at a temperature of 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time compared to PPO samples at a temperature of 0.

For single-turn dialogue evaluation, we assess various approaches on the single-step human-assistant interaction subset from the Anthropic HH dataset’s test split \citep{bai2022training}. The evaluation with GPT-4 relies on the test’s preferred completions as a reference, calculating win rates across methods. Since a standard SFT model is not available for this benchmark, we initialize with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model, train a reference using Preferred-FT to align with the distribution of selected completions, and subsequently apply DPO training. Additionally, we benchmark against the best completion out of 128 generated via Preferred-FT (noting that performance for the Best of $N$ baseline saturates at $N = 128$, as detailed in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) and a 2-shot prompt version of the Pythia-2.8B base model. Across optimal temperature settings for each approach, DPO consistently matches or surpasses their top results. We further assess an RLHF model, which is trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} provided by a recognized source \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}; however, we are unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature where its performance exceeds the Pythia-2.8B base. Drawing from our TL;DR findings and the shared reward function of these techniques, we regard the Best of 128 baseline as a rough surrogate for PPO-level outcomes. In sum, DPO stands out as the only method that is computationally efficient while achieving improvements over the preferred Anthropic HH completions and delivers results comparable to or better than the resource-intensive Best of 128 baseline. Moreover, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO reaches optimal performance rapidly.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To more thoroughly assess how PPO and DPO respond to shifts in input distribution, we tested both policies—trained during our Reddit TL;DR summarization study—on a new dataset: news articles from the test portion of the CNN/DailyMail corpus \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}. For these evaluations, we applied the optimal sampling temperatures identified in the TL;DR experiments (0 and 0.25). The outcomes are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. To determine performance, we calculated the GPT-4 win rate relative to the gold-standard summaries provided in the datasets, adapting our previous GPT-4 (C) prompt for TL;DR by replacing the phrase ``forum post'' with ``news article.'' Remarkably, DPO remains consistently ahead of PPO by a wide margin on this unfamiliar distribution. These findings indicate that, despite not utilizing the extra unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts available to PPO, DPO policies exhibit comparable or superior generalization capacity under distribution shift.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
A human evaluation was conducted to ascertain the trustworthiness of GPT-4’s assessments, utilizing TL;DR summarization experiment outcomes and two distinct prompt formulations. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt requests GPT-4 to identify the summary that more effectively captures the essential information from the post. In contrast, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt also incorporates an explicit preference for conciseness; we opted to investigate this prompt since, under the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt, GPT-4 exhibited a bias for lengthier, more repetitive summaries than human evaluators typically prefer. Complete prompt texts are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. Three comparative analyses were performed: the top-scoring system (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing (PPO, temp. 1.0), and a method of intermediate quality (SFT, temp. 0.25), each contrasted against PPO with greedy sampling (the best temperature setting for PPO). Our results reveal that GPT-4’s judgments, regardless of prompt, align with human opinions at rates similar to inter-human agreement, suggesting GPT-4 functions as a suitable surrogate for human evaluators (noting that multiple human ratings were gathered only for the DPO and PPO-1 pairings due to resource constraints). On the whole, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates most closely mirroring human judgment; thus, we rely on this prompt for the principal results reported in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. For further information concerning the human evaluation setup, including details on the interface used and the volunteer raters, see Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning offers a robust and scalable approach for developing effective and aligned language models. In this work, we present DPO, a straightforward framework for training language models using preference data, eliminating the need for reinforcement learning. Instead of adapting the preference learning task to fit the traditional RL framework and utilizing standard RL techniques, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward structures. This allows language models to be optimized according to human preferences via a standard cross-entropy loss, bypassing both reinforcement learning and any loss in general applicability. DPO achieves comparable or superior results to existing RLHF approaches, such as those leveraging PPO, with almost no hyperparameter tuning required. Consequently, DPO significantly lowers the complexity of preference-based language model training.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Work.} Several avenues for further investigation emerge from our findings. One critical area is the examination of how DPO-trained policies perform on out-of-distribution data, particularly in comparison with methods that explicitly learn a reward function. Preliminary experiments indicate that DPO generalizes on par with PPO-based approaches, but more systematic exploration is necessary. Additionally, it is worth studying whether strategies such as self-labeling with the DPO policy can efficiently exploit unlabeled prompt data. Another open question concerns the dynamics of reward over-optimization in the DPO paradigm, and whether the marginal decline in performance noted in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right can be attributed to this phenomenon. Moreover, our assessments currently extend up to models with 6B parameters; investigating the application of DPO to substantially larger, cutting-edge models remains a compelling research direction. In terms of evaluation, our observations suggest that GPT-4-derived win rates are sensitive to prompt wording, highlighting the need for future studies on optimizing automated evaluation protocols. Beyond language model training, DPO holds promise for a wide range of use cases, including the optimization of generative models in domains other than language.
