\title{Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs) acquire extensive general knowledge and some degree of reasoning, but shaping their outputs with precision remains challenging due to the lack of supervision in their training regimes. Current approaches to enhance model steerability often rely on collecting human judgments regarding the relative quality of model outputs and then adjusting the LM accordingly, typically through reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Yet, RLHF is often complicated and prone to instability, involving the training of a reward model to capture human preferences, followed by fine-tuning the LM via reinforcement learning to maximize predicted reward, all while constraining the divergence from the original LM. In this work, we propose a novel reward model parameterization within RLHF that permits direct derivation of the associated optimal policy in closed form, thereby enabling a solution to the traditional RLHF optimization using a straightforward classification objective. This approach, termed \textit{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO), is robust, efficient, and computationally inexpensive, removing the necessity for LM sampling during fine-tuning as well as extensive hyperparameter searches. Our empirical findings indicate that DPO can fine-tune LMs to match or outperform existing preference alignment strategies. In particular, DPO surpasses RLHF with PPO in sentiment control tasks and achieves comparable or superior performance in summarization and single-turn dialogue scenarios, all while substantially reducing implementation and training complexity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Large-scale unsupervised language models (LMs), when trained on massive datasets, develop an array of unexpected capabilities~\citep{chowdhery2022palm, brown2020language, touvron2023llama,bubeck2023sparks}. Despite this, the data used for their training is authored by humans with diverse intentions, expertise, and motivations. Some of these human-derived patterns may be undesirable for the model to replicate; for instance, while it is beneficial for an AI coding assistant to \textit{recognize} typical programming errors for the purpose of correction, we would still prefer its code generation to reflect the (potentially infrequent) instances of expert-level programming within its corpus. In a similar vein, while it is important that a language model can \textit{identify} a widespread misconception held by half the population, we certainly do not want the model to repeat this misconception as fact in half of its responses concerning the topic. This highlights the importance of distinguishing a model’s \emph{target responses and behavior} from its broad \textit{repertoire of knowledge and skills}, a critical requirement for ensuring that AI systems are reliable, effective, and governable~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Although current approaches generally direct LMs toward human-preferred outputs via reinforcement learning (RL), we demonstrate that the RL-based objective employed by these techniques can be directly and precisely optimized through a straightforward binary cross-entropy objective, leading to a considerable simplification of the preference learning workflow.

In broad terms, these approaches encourage LMs to adopt favorable behaviors by leveraging carefully constructed datasets reflecting human preferences about safe and useful outputs. This process of preference learning is introduced following a foundational phase in which the model undergoes extensive unsupervised pre-training on a comprehensive text corpus. While a naive strategy for preference learning involves supervised fine-tuning on high-quality human responses, the leading techniques fall under the category of reinforcement learning from human (or artificial) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF;~\citep{christiano2017deep,bai2022constitutional}). RLHF entails fitting a reward model to a collection of annotated human preferences, and subsequently employing RL to adjust the language model policy so that it generates outputs deemed highly rewarding, without straying significantly from the pretrained parameters. Although this procedure yields LMs with remarkable proficiency in conversation and programming, it introduces substantial complexity beyond standard supervised learning—it requires the training of multiple models and necessitates online sampling from the policy during optimization, ultimately resulting in considerable computational overhead.

This paper presents a novel approach to aligning language model outputs with human preferences, eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning frameworks. We introduce \textit{{Direct Preference Optimization} (DPO)}, an algorithm that implicitly seeks the same objective as standard RLHF approaches—maximizing reward while constraining KL-divergence—but with significantly reduced implementation and training complexity. At a high level, DPO works by boosting the log-probability ratio between preferred and non-preferred outputs, while integrating a dynamically calculated, sample-specific importance weight. This importance weight is crucial in mitigating model collapse observed with more naive probability-ratio based objectives. DPO, like prior algorithms, leverages a theoretical preference model (e.g., the Bradley-Terry model; \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis}) to quantify the agreement between any reward function and actual human preference observations. In contrast to previous techniques that construct a preference-based loss to fit a reward model and subsequently train the policy to maximize it, DPO applies a variable substitution to reformulate the preference loss directly as a function of the policy. This enables direct optimization of a policy using a binary cross-entropy objective over human preference data, effectively yielding the policy that is optimal with respect to a reward function that is implicitly consistent with the observed preferences.

The primary contribution of this work is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a straightforward, reinforcement-learning-free method for training language models using preference data. Through empirical studies, we demonstrate that DPO matches or surpasses state-of-the-art methods, including PPO-based RLHF, across tasks like sentiment adjustment, summarization, and conversational modeling, and is scalable to language models with up to 6B parameters.

\section{Related Work}

Self-supervised language models have demonstrated the ability to solve certain tasks without any fine-tuning, either through zero-shot completion \citep{radford2019language} or by responding to a limited number of prompts, as in few-shot learning \citep{gpt3,megatron,chowdhery2022palm}. Nonetheless, adapting these models to downstream applications and aligning them more closely with user goals can be accomplished by additional fine-tuning on curated datasets consisting of instructions paired with human-generated completions \citep{mishra-etal-2022-cross,sanh2022multitask,chung2022scaling,thoppilan2022lamda}. This process, referred to as `instruction-tuning,’ not only enhances language models' capacity to handle instructions that differ from those in the training data but also generally improves user interaction and applicability \citep{chung2022scaling}. In practice, it is often more straightforward to collect \textit{relative} human assessments of response quality than to compile expert-annotated demonstrations; as a result, later research has focused on adjusting LLMs using human preference datasets, leading to advances in domains such as translation \citep{kreutzer-etal-2018-reliability}, summarization \citep{stiennon2022learning,ziegler2020finetuning}, creative story-telling \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, and adherence to instructions \citep{ouyang2022training,ramamurthy2023is}. These approaches initially involve training a neural reward model to align with the human preferences data—typically employing preference modeling frameworks like the Bradley-Terry model \citep{bradley1952rankanalysis}—and subsequently fine-tuning the language model with reinforcement learning, using algorithms such as REINFORCE \citep{williams1992reinforce}, proximal policy optimization (PPO; \cite{schulman2017proximal}), or their derivatives \citep{ramamurthy2023is}. Related methodologies utilize LLMs, already instruction-tuned with human feedback, to produce synthetic preference annotations for specific qualities like safety or harmlessness \citep{bai2022constitutional}, relying primarily on minimal human oversight through rubric-based evaluations of model outputs. Collectively, these techniques embody an intersection of two significant research trajectories: training language models with reinforcement learning to fulfill varied goals~\citep{Ranzato2015SequenceLT,paulus2018a,wu2018learning}, and devising general strategies for learning from human preferences \citep{christiano2017deep,kupcsik2018learning}. While utilizing comparative human feedback is attractive, applying reinforcement learning to fine-tune large language models still presents substantial practical obstacles. This paper offers a theoretically-grounded alternative for optimizing according to relative preferences that does not depend on reinforcement learning.

Beyond language-focused applications, learning policies from preferences has been explored extensively in both bandit and reinforcement learning frameworks, with multiple methodologies introduced in the literature. Within contextual bandit settings, learning from preferences or rankings over actions—rather than from reward signals—is referred to as the contextual dueling bandit (CDB) problem \citep{yue2012karmed,dudik2015contextual}. When absolute rewards are unavailable, CDB analysis replaces the concept of an optimal policy with the \textit{von Neumann winner}, defined as a policy that achieves an expected win rate of at least 50\% against any alternative policy \citep{dudik2015contextual}. However, whereas CDBs involve the acquisition of preference feedback in an online fashion, preference-based policy learning from human feedback is more commonly conducted using a static, offline collection of preference-labeled action pairs \citep{yan2022human}. Likewise, in \textit{preference-based RL} (PbRL), the learning process is driven by binary preferences originating from an unknown underlying `scoring' function, as opposed to direct reward signals \citep{BusaFekete2014,ruiz2023dueling}. Numerous algorithms have been developed for PbRL—including those that leverage off-policy preference data—but these methods typically require explicit estimation of the underlying scoring or reward function before policy optimization can proceed \citep{jain2013learning,BusaFekete2014,christiano2017deep,sadigh2017active,kupcsik2018learning}. In contrast, our approach introduces a unified policy learning procedure that directly adjusts the policy to conform to observed preferences without an explicit intermediate reward modeling step.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{section:prelims}

We summarize the RLHF workflow as described by \citeauthor{ziegler2020finetuning} and subsequently adopted in works such as \citep{stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. This pipeline generally consists of three sequential components: 1) supervised fine-tuning (SFT); 2) collection of preference data and reward modeling; and 3) reinforcement learning-based policy optimization.

\textbf{SFT}: The RLHF process is typically initiated by adapting a pre-trained language model to specific tasks (e.g., dialogue, summarization) through supervised training on curated, high-quality datasets. This step yields an initial model, denoted by $\pi^\text{SFT}$.

\textbf{Reward Modelling Phase}: Subsequently, prompts $x$ are provided to the SFT model, which generates two candidate completions $(y_1, y_2)\sim \pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$. Human evaluators then indicate their preference between these candidates, recorded as $y_w\succ y_l \mid x$—where $y_w$ is the chosen response and $y_l$ the less preferred one. These preference judgments are assumed to be governed by a latent reward function $r^*(y, x)$ that is unobserved. A variety of statistical models exist to capture this preference data, with the Bradley-Terry (BT) model \cite{bradley1952rankanalysis} being a commonly used formulation. More general models such as Plackett-Luce \citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual} can also be incorporated when multi-way rankings are available. Within the BT framework, the probability distribution over human preferences, $p^*$, is modeled as:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bradley-terry}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right)}{\exp\left(r^*(x, y_1)\right) + \exp\left(r^*(x, y_2)\right)}.
\end{equation}

Suppose we are given a fixed dataset of pairwise comparisons $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$ sampled according to $p^*$. In this setting, one can introduce a parametric reward model $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ and estimate its parameters through maximum likelihood estimation. Treating the task as a binary classification, the objective corresponds to minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward_model}
    \mathcal{L}_R(r_{\phi}, \mathcal{D}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[\log \sigma(r_{\phi}(x, y_w)- r_{\phi}(x, y_l))\bigr]
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes the logistic sigmoid function. When applied to language models, the reward network $r_{\phi}(x, y)$ typically starts from the SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}(y \mid x)$, and appends a linear head to the final transformer output, producing a scalar that quantifies reward \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}. To promote stability and reduce reward variance, earlier approaches standardize the reward predictions, enforcing that $\mathbb{E}_{x,y\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[r_\phi(x, y)\right] = 0$ for all $x$.

\textbf{RL Fine-Tuning Phase}: In the reinforcement learning stage, this learned reward model serves as the supervisory signal for updating the language model. Following established methods~\citep{jaques2017sequence, jaques2020human}, the optimization objective is formulated as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:RL}
\max_{\pi_{\theta}}  \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D}, y\sim \pi_{\theta}(y \mid x)}\bigl[r_{\phi}(x, y)\bigr] - \beta\mathbb{D}_{\textrm{KL}}\bigl[\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\bigr],
\end{equation}

where $\beta$ modulates how far the learned policy can diverge from the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$, usually set to the pretrained SFT model $\pi^\text{SFT}$. In practical applications, the policy $\pi_\theta$ is also initialized from $\pi^\text{SFT}$. The KL-divergence term is essential: it acts to limit the policy's drift from the distribution for which the reward model remains trustworthy, helps maintain diverse outputs, and guards against collapse onto a few high-reward responses. Since text generation is inherently discrete, this optimization is not differentiable and thus is tackled using reinforcement learning algorithms. The typical strategy \citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training} defines the reward function as ${r(x, y) = r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta (\log \pi_{\theta}(y\mid x) - \log \pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x))}$, maximizing it with PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal}.

\section{Direct Preference Optimization}\label{sec:DPO}

Recognizing the difficulties inherent in scaling reinforcement learning to large models, such as those used in language model fine-tuning, we aim to formulate a more direct method for preference-based policy optimization. In contrast to RLHF pipelines, which first fit a reward model and then employ RL to optimize it, our method exploits a specific reward parameterization that allows the optimal policy to be analytically characterized, thus removing the necessity for an explicit RL loop. As detailed below, our central observation is that there is an exact correspondence between reward functions and their optimal policies, enabling a reparameterization of the reward-based objective in terms of policies. This change of variables circumvents training a separate, explicit reward model, yet retains compatibility with widely-used models of preference such as the Bradley-Terry framework. Ultimately,

\textbf{Deriving the DPO objective.} We begin with the standard reinforcement learning objective used in previous literature, as stated in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL}, considering a generic reward function $r$. Building upon prior works~\citep{peters2007reinforcement, peng2019advantage, korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, it can be readily demonstrated that the optimal policy for the KL-regularized reward maximization problem in Eq.~\ref{eq:RL} has the following form:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:op_policy}
    \pi_r(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right),
\end{equation}

where $Z(x) =\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)$ denotes the normalization constant (partition function). A full derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{app:derivation1}. Even when substituting $r^*$ with its MLE approximation $r_\phi$, computation of the partition function $Z(x)$ remains computationally burdensome \citep{korbak2022reinforcement, go2023aligning}, thus hindering the practical deployment of this policy formulation. Nevertheless, we can manipulate Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} to express the reward in relation to its associated optimal policy $\pi_r$, the reference distribution $\pi_\text{ref}$, and the unknown normalization $Z(\cdot)$. By applying the logarithm to both sides of Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} and simplifying algebraically, we get:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_eq}
    r(x,y) =\beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x).
\end{equation}

This alternative formulation can be employed for the true reward $r^*$ and its corresponding optimal policy $\pi^*$. Importantly, the Bradley-Terry model considers only reward differences between a pair of completions, specifically, ${p^*(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma(r^*(x, y_1) - r^*(x, y_2))}$. Substituting the expression for $r^*(x,y)$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} into the preference model Eq.~\ref{eq:bradley-terry} leads to a cancellation of the partition function, enabling us to write human preference probabilities solely as a function of the optimal policy $\pi^*$ and the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. As a result, the optimal RLHF policy $\pi^*$ under the Bradley-Terry preference model satisfies:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
    p^*(y_1\succ y_2 \mid x)=\frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_2\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_1\mid x)}\right)}
\end{equation}

Details of this derivation are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation2}. Although Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} leverages the Bradley-Terry framework, an analogous development is possible for more general preference models, such as the Plackett-Luce family~\citep{plackett1975analysis, luce2012individual}, as detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:plackett_luce_models}.

With this preference probability now expressed in terms of the optimal policy rather than the reward model, it becomes possible to construct a maximum likelihood objective for any parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$. Mirroring the logic of the reward modeling setup (see Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model}), the resulting policy training objective is:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimum_model}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_\text{ref}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

In this formulation, we approximate an implicit reward function via an alternative parameterization, where the corresponding optimal policy aligns with $\pi_\theta$. Additionally, as this approach mirrors a reparameterized Bradley-Terry model, it inherits advantageous theoretical properties, such as consistency under appropriate assumptions regarding the distribution of preference data \cite{bong2022generalized}. Further theoretical analysis of DPO and its comparison to related methodologies is presented in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\textbf{How does the DPO update operate?} To elucidate the mechanism underlying DPO, we consider the gradient of the objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$. The derivative with respect to the parameters $\theta$ is expressed as:

\begin{multline*}\label{eq:gradient}
    \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\pi_\theta;\pi_\text{ref}) = \\ -\beta\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \Bigg[\underbrace{\sigma(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta (x, y_w))}_\text{greater emphasis when reward estimation is incorrect}\Big[\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log \pi(y_w \mid x)}_\text{raise probability of $y_w$} - \underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log\pi(y_l \mid x)}_\text{lower probability of $y_l$}\Big]\Bigg],
\end{multline*}

where the implicit reward $\hat{r}_\theta(x, y)$ is defined as $\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$, determined by the language model $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\pi_\text{ref}$ (with further discussion in Section~\ref{sec:theory}). At an intuitive level, the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ acts to make the preferred completion $y_w$ more probable while reducing the likelihood of the less preferred $y_l$. Crucially, each training example is weighted according to how much the implicit reward model $\hat{r}_\theta$ overestimates the dispreferred completion relative to the preferred one, scaled by $\beta$—that is, proportional to the degree of misranking by the reward model and reflecting the influence of the KL constraint. Our empirical findings highlight the significance of this weighting: omitting the weighting factor leads to undesirable degeneration of the language model (see Appendix Table~\ref{tab:unlikelihood_generations}).

\textbf{DPO overview.} The standard DPO workflow comprises the following steps: 1) For each prompt $x$, generate sample completions $y_1, y_2 \sim \pi_\text{ref}(\cdot \mid x)$, annotate them with human preference data to form an offline preference dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$; 2) train the language model $\pi_\theta$ by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}$ using the specified $\pi_\text{ref}$, constructed dataset $\mathcal{D}$, and target $\beta$. 
In real-world scenarios, practitioners typically prefer to utilize existing public preference datasets rather than create new samples and collect additional human labels. Since such datasets are typically drawn using $\pi^\text{SFT}$, we set $\pi_\text{ref} = \pi^\text{SFT}$ when it is accessible. Conversely, if $\pi^\text{SFT}$ cannot be obtained, we determine $\pi_\text{ref}$ by maximizing the likelihood over the preferred completions ${(x, y_w)}$, specifically: ${\pi_\text{ref} = \argmax_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{x, y_w \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\log \pi(y_w \mid x)\right]}$. This approach helps address potential distribution mismatch between the unavailable true reference distribution and the $\pi_\text{ref}$ employed in DPO. Additional specifics regarding implementation and chosen hyperparameters are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

\section{Theoretical Analysis of DPO} This section elaborates on theoretical interpretations of the DPO methodology, supplying foundational analysis and connecting DPO’s strengths to the challenges observed in actor-critic RLHF algorithms (e.g., PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}).

\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model} DPO circumvents both the explicit reward model training and reinforcement learning by unifying them into a single maximum likelihood training objective. Observe that the loss function given in Eq. \ref{eq:main_eq} can be seen as a Bradley-Terry model, where the reward is parameterized as $r^*(x, y) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)}$. The optimization of the parameterized model $\pi_{\theta}$ therefore mirrors the optimization of the reward model described in Eq. \ref{eq:reward_model} when an appropriate change of variables is applied. In this section, we develop the formal underpinnings of this reparameterization, demonstrate that it does not limit the expressiveness of the learned reward functions, and prove that it enables the precise recovery of the optimal policy. We start by introducing an equivalence relation for reward functions.

\begin{definition}
Two reward functions $r(x, y)$ and $r'(x, y)$ are considered equivalent if and only if there exists a function $f$ such that ${r(x, y) - r'(x, y) = f(x)}$.
\end{definition}

This definition naturally constitutes an equivalence relation, thereby organizing the collection of reward functions into distinct equivalence classes. The following lemmas summarize key properties:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_prefrence} Within the Plackett-Luce framework, including the Bradley-Terry model as a special case, reward functions belonging to the same equivalence class result in identical preference distributions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:same_policy}
    Any two reward functions that are members of the same equivalence class yield the same optimal policy when solving the constrained RL problem.
\end{lemma}

The verification of these lemmas is direct and therefore omitted here, with details deferred to Appendix \ref{app:lemma1}. The first lemma illustrates a classical identifiability issue present in the Plackett-Luce family of models \cite{plackett1975analysis}: parameters are not uniquely determined without further restrictions. As a consequence, practitioners typically incorporate additional constraints to secure uniqueness in the maximum likelihood estimates from Eq.~\ref{eq:reward_model} \cite{bong2022generalized}. The second lemma establishes that the optimal policy is invariant within an equivalence class of reward functions, implying that for the purposes of optimization, it suffices to identify any representative from the class corresponding to the optimum. The following theorem, proven in Appendix~\ref{app:thm1}, formalizes this insight:

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
    Provided mild technical conditions, every equivalence class of reward functions compatible with the Plackett-Luce (and specifically, Bradley-Terry) models can be parameterized by the form ${r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}$, where $\pi(y\mid x)$ is a model and $\pi_\text{ref}(y \mid x)$ is a chosen reference model.
\end{theorem}

\begin{sproof}
    Let $r(x, y)$ be a reward function with its associated optimal model $\pi_r(y \mid x)$ defined by Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}. We demonstrate that any reward function from the equivalence class of $r$ can be expressed using the specified parameterization. Define the projection operator $f$ by
\begin{equation}
    f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = r(x, y) - \beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)
\end{equation}
This operator $f$ normalizes the reward by subtracting the logarithm of the partition function (with respect to $\pi_\text{ref}$). Because this normalization depends solely on $x$, $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y)$ is another reward function in the same equivalence class as $r(x, y)$. Substituting the right-hand side of Eq.~\ref{eq:main_eq} for $r$, which is valid for any reward, gives $f(r; \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_r(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. In other words, $f$ provides an explicit representative of the equivalence class with the desired structure, confirming that the proposed parameterization is general enough to cover all relevant cases without any loss of expressivity.
\end{sproof}

An alternative interpretation of Theorem~\ref{thm:main} is that it uniquely identifies, within each equivalence class, the specific reward function that the DPO reparameterization yields—namely, the reward function that fulfills:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lag_p}
     \sum_{y}\underbrace{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x, y)\right)}_{=\pi(y\mid x)\text{, using Thm.~\ref{thm:main} reparam.}} = 1,
\end{equation}

This condition ensures that $\pi(y\mid x)$ constitutes a legitimate probability distribution (i.e., all probabilities are non-negative and sum to one). Building upon Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy}, it becomes apparent that Eq.~\ref{eq:lag_p} represents the partition function for the optimal policy that emerges from the reward function $r(x, y)$. The central conceptual contribution of the DPO algorithm lies in imposing particular constraints on the otherwise underdetermined Plackett-Luce (including the Bradley-Terry model as a special case) family of preference models. These constraints maintain the full expressive capacity of the reward model class, while simultaneously ensuring the optimal policy in Eq.~\ref{eq:op_policy} is analytically computable for every prompt $x$.

\subsection{Instability of Actor-Critic Algorithms} Our framework additionally provides a lens through which to analyze sources of instability in popular actor-critic methods for RLHF, such as PPO. In this context, we adhere to the RLHF workflow, specifically examining the RL fine-tuning phase detailed in Section~\ref{section:prelims}. Connections can be drawn to the control-as-inference paradigm \cite{levine2018reinforcement} as it applies to the constrained RL problem defined in \ref{eq:RL}. Let us suppose a parameterized policy $\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)$, and consider minimizing the divergence $\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x) \mid\mid \pi^*(y\mid x)]$, where $\pi^*$ denotes the optimal policy from Eq.~\ref{eq:optimum_model} as determined by the reward function $r_{\phi}(y, x)$. Through algebraic manipulation, this minimization is equivalent to the following objective:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:AC}
    \max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}\bigg[\underbrace{r_{\phi}(x, y) -\beta\log\sum_{y}\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r_{\phi}(x, y)\right)}_{f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)} - \underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{KL}}\bigg]
\end{equation}

The objective being optimized here aligns with those explored in previous studies 
\citep{ziegler2020finetuning, stiennon2022learning, bai2022training, ouyang2022training}, utilizing the DPO-equivalent reward associated with the reward class $r_{\phi}$. In this context, the normalization factor within $f(r_{\phi}, \pi_\text{ref}, \beta)$ can be seen as representing the soft value function for the reference policy $\pi_\text{ref}$. Although this normalization does not alter the solution's optimality, omitting it may result in a policy gradient with elevated variance, which can destabilize learning. Addressing the normalization with a learned value function is possible, but this approach introduces further optimization challenges. Another strategy employed in earlier work involves normalizing rewards relative to a human completion baseline, functioning as a one-sample Monte Carlo approximation of the normalization constant. By contrast, the DPO reparameterization produces a reward function that obviates the need for such baselines.

\section{Experiments}
We empirically assess DPO in its capacity to train policies based solely on preference data. Initially, within a controlled text generation environment, we examine DPO's efficiency in balancing reward maximization against the minimization of KL-divergence from the reference policy, benchmarking its performance against widely used preference optimization methods like PPO. Subsequently, we extend our evaluation to encompass larger model architectures and more challenging RLHF scenarios, such as summarization and dialogue tasks. Our findings indicate that, even with minimal hyperparameter tuning, DPO consistently matches or outperforms competitive baselines—including RLHF with PPO and the best-of-$N$ sampling approach based on a learned reward. We first outline our experimental methodology; further specifics are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:exp_details}.

\textbf{Tasks.} Our study investigates three distinct open-domain text generation tasks. Across all tasks, models aim to learn a policy using a preference dataset $\mathcal{D}=\bigl\{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$. In the context of \textbf{controlled sentiment generation}, $x$ consists of a movie review prefix from the IMDb dataset \cite{maas-EtAl:2011:ACL-HLT2011}, and the objective is to generate $y$ expressing positive sentiment. To ensure a consistent evaluation framework, we synthesize preference pairs between generated continuations using a pre-trained sentiment classifier, selecting pairs such that $p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_w)>p(\text{positive}\mid x,y_l)$. For SFT, GPT-2-large is further trained on the IMDB dataset's training reviews until model convergence (with more information provided in App~\ref{app:sentiment_details}). In the \textbf{summarization} task, $x$ represents a Reddit forum post, and the policy must produce a summary $y$ encapsulating the key points. This setup draws on the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset \citep{volske-etal-2017-tl}, as well as human preference annotations provided by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning}. For this task, the SFT baseline is a model adapted to human-created forum post summaries\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}} using the TRLX \citep{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115} RLHF library. The preference data were collected by \citeauthor{stiennon2022learning} from completions sampled from another SFT model with a similar training regime. The third scenario, \textbf{single-turn dialogue}, defines $x$ as a user prompt—ranging from scientific inquiries to personal requests—and requires the policy to generate a useful, engaging reply $y$. We employ the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dialogue dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which comprises 170k conversations involving a human and an AI assistant. Each conversation is concluded with two model-generated responses and an accompanying human preference annotation identifying the favored completion. As no pre-trained SFT model exists for this dataset, we construct the SFT model by fine-tuning a general-purpose language model exclusively on the preferred responses.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We employ two primary strategies for evaluating our methods. To assess how effectively each algorithm maximizes the constrained reward objective, we analyze their performance in the controlled sentiment generation context by plotting the trade-off frontier between achieved reward and KL-divergence from the reference policy. This assessment is possible because the actual reward function—a sentiment classifier—is accessible in this scenario. Conversely, since such ground-truth reward functions are unavailable in practical applications, we instead measure each algorithm's \textit{win rate} relative to a baseline policy. In this real-world setting, GPT-4 serves as an automated proxy for human evaluation, judging summary quality in summarization and helpfulness in single-turn dialogue tasks. For summarization, baseline comparisons use reference summaries from the test set, while for dialogue, the comparison is made to the dataset’s preferred responses. Prior research indicates that large language models often surpass traditional metrics as automated evaluators \citep{Chen2023ExploringTU}, but to substantiate our reliance on GPT-4 for evaluation, we present a human subject study in Sec.~\ref{sec:human-judgments}. Our findings reveal a strong correspondence between GPT-4's judgments and those of human raters, with human-GPT-4 agreement matching or exceeding typical agreement levels among human annotators.

\textbf{Methods.} Beyond DPO, our evaluation covers a range of established techniques for aligning language models with human preferences. As simple baselines, we test zero-shot prompting with \textbf{GPT-J} \citep{gpt-j} on summarization tasks, and two-shot prompting using \textbf{Pythia-2.8B} \citep{biderman2023pythia} in dialogue scenarios. Further, we consider the \textbf{SFT} model and also \textbf{Preferred-FT}, which involves supervised fine-tuning on the chosen completion $y_w$, with $y_w$ coming from either the SFT model (for controlled sentiment and summarization) or a generic LM (in dialogue). Another approach, \textbf{Unlikelihood}~\citep{welleck2019neural}, seeks to increase the probability of $y_w$ while reducing that of $y_l$, incorporating an optional weighting factor $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the unlikelihood term. Additionally, we experiment with \textbf{PPO} \citep{schulman2017proximal} utilizing a reward model trained from preference data, and \textbf{PPO-GT}, an oracle method which optimizes directly with the ground-truth reward in the sentiment-controlled setting. Our sentiment studies use two PPO-GT variants: an off-the-shelf implementation \cite{leandro_von_werra_2023_7790115}, and a customized version featuring normalized rewards and additional hyperparameter tuning to boost effectiveness—these enhancements are also applied to the learned-reward PPO. Lastly, we assess the \textbf{Best of $N$} baseline, where $N$ responses are sampled from the SFT model (or Preferred-FT for dialogue) and the highest reward-scoring completion, determined by a learned reward model, is selected. Although this method often achieves strong results by separating reward modeling from PPO optimization, it incurs significant computational costs, as generating $N$ completions per query at test time becomes burdensome for even modest $N$.

\subsection{How well can DPO optimize the RLHF objective?}

The RLHF framework typically employs a reward maximization objective constrained by a KL divergence term, which serves to control reward optimization while preventing substantial divergence of the learned policy from the reference policy. Consequently, fair comparison between algorithms necessitates consideration of both attained reward and the associated KL divergence; marginally higher rewards are not advantageous if they incur considerably greater KL values. In Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main}, we present the tradeoff curve between reward and KL divergence across several algorithms within the sentiment analysis context. For each method, we conduct multiple experiments, varying the hyperparameters that determine policy conservativeness: for PPO, we test target KL values $\in\{3,6,9,12\}$; for other approaches, we use $\beta \in \{0.05,0.1,1,5\}$, and $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.1,0.5,1\}$ for unlikelihood training; for preferred-FT, we employ different random seeds. Altogether, this produces 22 distinct runs. Every 100 training steps, up until convergence, each resulting policy is evaluated on a designated set of test prompts, with both the mean reward under the actual reward function and the mean sequence-level KL\footnote{Defined here as the aggregate of timestep-wise KL-divergences.} relative to the reference policy $\text{KL}\left(\pi\mid \mid \pi_\text{ref}\right)$ recorded. Our findings show that DPO consistently yields the most favorable frontier, attaining superior reward with comparatively low KL divergence. This outcome is especially striking for two reasons. Firstly, despite DPO and PPO seeking to optimize the identical objective, DPO demonstrates markedly greater efficiency, strictly outperforming PPO on the reward/KL spectrum. Secondly, DPO surpasses PPO in defining the reward-KL frontier, \emph{even when PPO is provided with access to ground truth rewards} (PPO-GT).

\subsection{Can DPO scale to real preference datasets?}
\label{sec:dpo-real-datasets}
We proceed to assess the ability of DPO to fine-tune on tasks involving summarization and single-turn dialogue using real preference data. It is well established that automatic evaluation metrics like ROUGE often show weak alignment with human judgments in summarization tasks~\citep{stiennon2022learning}. Previous studies have demonstrated that employing PPO for fine-tuning language models with human preference feedback yields more effective summary generation. To compare methods, we generate samples from the test set of the TL;DR summarization dataset and calculate the mean win rate against the test reference completions. Sampling is performed across a range of temperatures from 0.0 to 1.0, with the resulting win rates illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:frontier-tldr-main} (right). DPO, PPO, and Preferred-FT are all applied to further fine-tune the identical GPT-J SFT model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft}}. Our results show DPO achieving a win rate of about 61\% at a temperature of 0.0, outperforming PPO, which attains approximately 57\% at its optimal temperature setting. Furthermore, DPO attains a higher peak win rate compared to the best of $N$ baseline. It is important to mention that we did not conduct extensive tuning of DPO’s $\beta$ hyperparameter, indicating that the observed results may be a conservative estimate of DPO’s full capabilities. Additionally, DPO demonstrates considerably greater resilience to changes in sampling temperature than PPO, whose win rate can drop to that of the underlying GPT-J model as temperature increases. Preferred-FT does not deliver significant improvements over the initial SFT model. We further directly compare DPO and PPO via human evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:human-judgments}, showing that DPO samples produced at temperature 0.25 were favored 58\% of the time relative to PPO samples at the same temperature.

For single-turn dialogue, we assess various approaches using the portion of the Anthropic HH dataset \citep{bai2022training} test split that includes a single exchange between human and assistant. In GPT-4-based evaluations, we use the human-preferred completions in the test set as references, allowing us to compute the win rates of each method. Since no standardized SFT model exists for this particular task, our pipeline begins with a pre-trained Pythia-2.8B model. We then fine-tune this model with Preferred-FT on the selected completions, ensuring the outputs are in-distribution, followed by DPO training. Our comparisons include the best result among 128 Preferred-FT completions (where the Best of $N$ approach levels off at $N=128$ for this setup; see Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:best-of-n}) as well as a 2-shot prompted variant of the base Pythia-2.8B, observing that DPO achieves equal or superior win rates for optimal temperature values. Additionally, we evaluate an RLHF model trained with PPO on the Anthropic HH dataset \footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B}} sourced from a reputable repository \footnote{\url{https://github.com/CarperAI/trlx/tree/main/examples/hh}}, but are unable to identify any prompt or sampling temperature that results in performance exceeding that of the original Pythia-2.8B. Drawing on our findings from the TL;DR evaluation and the shared reward objective between these methods, we regard Best of 128 as an approximate benchmark for PPO-level results. In sum, DPO emerges as the only method in this study that, while being computationally efficient, surpasses the preferred completions from the Anthropic HH dataset and matches or outperforms the resource-intensive Best of 128 approach. Lastly, Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main} illustrates that DPO attains peak performance after relatively few training iterations.

\subsection{Generalization to a new input distribution}

To more thoroughly assess how PPO and DPO perform under shifts in input distribution, we test the policies trained during our Reddit TL;DR summarization experiment on a distinct domain: the news articles found in the test split of the CNN/DailyMail dataset \citep{nallapati-etal-2016-abstractive}. For these evaluations, we utilize the optimal sampling temperatures previously identified for TL;DR (0 and 0.25). The corresponding results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:ood}. In this setup, we measure the win rate of GPT-4 relative to the ground-truth dataset summaries, applying the same GPT-4 (C) prompt used in the Reddit TL;DR setting, with the term ``forum post'' replaced by ``news article''. On this shifted distribution, DPO maintains a substantial performance advantage over PPO. These findings offer preliminary support for the notion that DPO-trained policies are capable of generalizing to new distributions as effectively as those trained with PPO, despite the fact that DPO does not leverage the extra unlabeled Reddit TL;DR prompts available to PPO.

\subsection{Validating GPT-4 judgments with human judgments}
\label{sec:human-judgments}
To assess the trustworthiness of GPT-4's evaluations, we carry out a human annotation study using outputs from the TL;DR summarization task and two variants of the GPT-4 prompt. The \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} (simple) prompt instructs the model to choose the summary that better encapsulates the key information from the post. The \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} (concise) prompt includes an additional criterion, asking which summary is more succinct, as we observe that the \textbf{GPT-4 (S)} prompt inclines GPT-4 to favor summaries that are longer and more redundant than what humans generally prefer. Full prompt details are available in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. We conduct three pairwise evaluations: the best-performing method (DPO, temp. 0.25), the lowest-performing (PPO, temp. 1.0), and a mid-range approach (SFT, temp. 0.25), ensuring a range of output qualities. Each is compared against the PPO model with greedy decoding (its optimal sampling temperature). Results indicate that, regardless of prompt, GPT-4's preferences tend to match human choices at a rate similar to human-human agreement, supporting the validity of GPT-4 as a surrogate for human assessment. (Due to the limited pool of human annotators, multiple human ratings are obtained only for the DPO and PPO-1 comparisons.) Overall, the \textbf{GPT-4 (C)} prompt yields win rates that are more aligned with human evaluations, leading us to adopt this prompt for the main experiments reported in Section~\ref{sec:dpo-real-datasets}. Further information about the human study, such as the web interface and a roster of participating volunteers, is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:human-study}.

\section{Discussion}
Preference-based learning provides an effective and scalable approach to developing capable and aligned language models. In this work, we have presented DPO, a straightforward training framework that enables learning from preferences without relying on reinforcement learning techniques. Instead of transforming the preference learning problem to fit within the conventional RL paradigm and using standard RL algorithms, DPO establishes a direct correspondence between language model policies and reward functions. This allows the model to be optimized to reflect human preferences using only a standard cross-entropy loss, bypassing the need for reinforcement learning and without sacrificing generality. Remarkably, DPO achieves results comparable to, or exceeding, existing RLHF techniques such as those utilizing PPO, with minimal hyperparameter adjustment. As a result, DPO significantly lowers the entry barrier for preference-based language model training.

\textbf{Limitations \& Future Directions.} Our findings prompt several avenues for further research. For instance, how well does a DPO-trained policy generalize to out-of-distribution data compared to models trained with an explicit reward function? Preliminary findings indicate that DPO’s generalization is on par with PPO-based models, but this area warrants deeper investigation. Another question is whether self-labeling during DPO training can leverage unlabeled prompts as effectively as existing methods. Additionally, the nature of reward over-optimization within DPO, as well as the minor dip in performance noted in Figure~\ref{fig:dialogue-main}-right, remains to be fully understood—does this represent a case of over-optimization? While our current evaluation includes models with up to 6B parameters, scaling DPO to cutting-edge, significantly larger architectures represents a promising research trajectory. In terms of evaluation methodologies, we observed that GPT-4-derived win rates are sensitive to prompt variation; refining approaches for obtaining reliable, high-quality assessments from automated evaluators is a potential direction for subsequent work. Beyond preference-based language model training, DPO’s applicability extends to generative modeling across a variety of modalities, highlighting its broader potential.
